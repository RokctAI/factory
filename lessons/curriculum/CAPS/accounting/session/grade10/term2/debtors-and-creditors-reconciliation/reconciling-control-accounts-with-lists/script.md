# Part 1 — Expert

The control account and its list of balances must agree — that has been the standing law since term one. Today we do the work that law exists for: the month the two records DISAGREE, and the disciplined procedure that finds every error, corrects each record on its own side, and brings both to the single true figure. Reconciliation is a named topic in every grade from here to matric; the method you build today is the method you will still be using in the final examination.

## Subtopic: Two Records, Two Kinds of Error

Recall the architecture, because the whole method hangs on it. The Debtors Control account in the general ledger is built from JOURNAL COLUMN TOTALS, posted monthly. The list of debtors is built from the PERSONAL ACCOUNTS, posted line by line, daily. Two independent journeys from the same source documents — which is why agreement certifies both, and disagreement proves an error on at least one journey.

The diagnostic question for every error found: WHERE did it happen — in the totals' journey, or in the pages' journey? An error inside a journal TOTAL — a column undercast or overcast, a total posted wrongly to control — corrupts the CONTROL account, while the pages, posted from correct individual lines, remain right. Correct the control. An error in POSTING TO A PAGE — a line posted to the wrong person, a transposed amount on a page, a line never posted at all — corrupts the LIST, while control, fed by correct totals, remains right. Correct the list. And the third family: an error in the ORIGINAL LINE, made before totalling — a wrong amount on the invoice entry itself — flows into BOTH records and must be corrected in both.

That classification — totals-side, pages-side, or both — is the entire intellectual content of reconciliation. Everything after is careful arithmetic.

One discipline before we begin: neither record is automatically the right one. Learners instinctively trust the control account and "fix" the list toward it. Wrong — either record can carry the error; only the evidence decides. The true figure emerges when both records, independently corrected, MEET.

Pause here — questions on the two journeys and the three error families are with you now. Classify by asking where the error happened.

## Subtopic: A Worked Reconciliation — the Debtors Side

Month end at Thabo's Trading. Debtors Control shows a balance of R7 440 debit. The list of debtors totals R7 130. A difference of R310 — and an investigation. Four findings emerge; classify and treat each.

Finding one: the Debtors Journal's sales column was UNDERCAST by R200 — the column adds to R200 more than was totalled. Where did this happen? In the totals' journey: the personal pages were posted from the correct individual lines, but control received a total R200 too small. Treatment: correct the CONTROL — debit Debtors Control R200. Control moves to R7 640.

Finding two: a bad debt of R140, written off in the General Journal, was posted to the debtor's personal account — but the GJ's control column was overlooked, and Debtors Control never received it. Totals' journey again: control still carries a debt the pages have already buried. Treatment: credit CONTROL R140. Control moves to R7 500.

Finding three: an invoice for R550 to T. Osman was correctly entered in the DJ — so it reached the totals and control — but was never posted to his personal page. Pages' journey: the list is missing a debtor's debt. Treatment: correct the LIST — add R550 to Osman's balance. List moves to R7 680.

Finding four: an invoice of R350 to P. Peters was posted to her page as R530 — digits transposed on the page only; the journal line and totals were correct. Pages' journey. Treatment: reduce the list by R180. List moves to R7 500.

And the meeting: control, corrected, R7 500. List, corrected, R7 500. Agreement — and only now do we know the true figure at all: neither original number was right. Set the working out exactly this way in the exam: a control-correction statement starting at R7 440, and a list-correction statement starting at R7 130, each adjustment named, both ending on the same line.

Stop for this section's questions now — the four findings, re-examined: classify, treat, and track both records to the meeting point.

## Subtopic: The Creditors Mirror and the Supplier's Statement

The creditors side runs the identical method in the mirror. Creditors Control is built from the CJ, CAJ, CPJ and GJ totals; the list of creditors from the personal pages. Same three error families, same classification question, same rule: correct each record on its own side, meet in the middle. The only flips are the ones you know: balances sit on the credit side, and the standing question is what WE owe.

One worked touch for the mirror. Creditors Control shows R5 240; the list totals R5 000. Investigation finds the CPJ's creditors column overcast by R90 — the totals journey deducted R90 too much from control... reason it: an overcast payments column means control was DEBITED R90 too much, so control is understated. Correct control: credit — no, carefully: payments debit control; an overcast means too large a debit, so ADD BACK by crediting? No — restore by reversing the excess: credit the excess? The excess debit shrank the balance; restoring means increasing the credit balance: credit Creditors Control R90 — control moves to R5 330. And an invoice from B. Bell for R330 never posted to her page: list plus R330 — R5 330. Meeting achieved. Notice how the mirror demands you re-derive each direction from first principles rather than copying the debtors treatment — examiners set exactly this trap.

Beyond the internal lock, the creditors side carries an external one: the supplier's monthly STATEMENT — their record of our account — reconciled against their page in our books. Differences arise from timing (our payment in transit, their invoice not yet received) and from errors on either side. The statement reconciliation is a Grade 11 and 12 mainstay; meet it here as the same principle extended: independent records, compared on schedule, differences explained item by item.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — mirror reasoning throughout: derive each correction's direction; never transplant it.

## Subtopic: The Method, Compressed — and Why It Matters

The full procedure, exam-ready. One: state both uncorrected figures and the difference. Two: classify every finding — totals journey, pages journey, or original line — by asking where the error physically happened. Three: correct the control account for totals-side errors, in a labelled statement; correct the list for pages-side errors, likewise. Four: both statements must end on the SAME figure — the true balance; if they do not, a finding was misclassified or an arithmetic slip made — re-examine before moving on. Five: the corrected figure is what flows to the trial balance and, later, the statements.

The error fingerprints from the posting lesson serve here too: a difference matching a single amount suggests an unposted item; divisible by nine, a transposition; divisible by two, a wrong side. Read the difference before hunting.

Why does this topic carry such weight across the grades? Because reconciliation is internal control made routine. Errors are inevitable — the system's honesty lies not in never erring but in CATCHING errors monthly, while documents are fresh and memories young. A business that reconciles debtors, creditors and — from next year — the bank statement every month is a business whose books can be trusted between audits. And the habit examines beautifully: every scenario is a difference, a set of findings, and a demand for classified, directional corrections.

The final questions of this part are with you now: full reconciliations, both sides — classify, correct, meet, and state the true figure.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same investigation as a story — because a reconciliation is really a small detective case: two witnesses, two different answers, and the truth waiting for whoever asks the right questions.

## Subtopic: Two Witnesses, Different Stories

Month end. Thabo asks his books the same question two ways. The street's one line — the control account — answers: the customers owe you R7 440. The pages, added up, answer: R7 130. Three hundred and ten rand apart. Somebody is wrong.

Here is the detective's first discipline: do not pick a favourite witness. The control line is not "more official" than the pages, and the pages are not "closer to the truth" than the line. Each was built along its own road — the line from monthly totals, the pages from daily entries — and either road can carry a pothole. The R310 difference proves at least one error exists; it does not say where.

Second discipline: the difference is a CLUE, not just a problem. Three hundred and ten is an odd little number — probably not one single slip, but a few, tangled. So the detective does what detectives do: goes back through the month's paperwork, item by item, looking for anything that travelled one road but not the other, or travelled wrongly.

And the case will end in a very particular way — not with one witness surrendering to the other, but with BOTH stories corrected until they independently tell the same truth. That meeting point — both witnesses agreeing after correction — is the only number worth writing down.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the setup are coming to you right now: two witnesses, two roads, and what the difference does and does not prove.

## Subtopic: Four Potholes, Each on Its Own Road

The investigation turns up four potholes. For each, ask the only question that matters: WHICH ROAD has the hole — the totals road that feeds the line, or the daily road that feeds the pages?

Pothole one: the sales column in the Debtors Journal was added up R200 short. The pages never saw this — they were posted from the correct lines before the bad total was struck. The totals road is holed: the LINE is R200 too small. Patch the line: up R200.

Pothole two: Naidoo-style bad news — a R140 write-off recorded on the customer's page, but the entry never made it into the control totals. The page has already written the debt off; the line still thinks the money is coming. Totals road again: line down R140. The line now reads R7 500.

Pothole three: an invoice for R550 to Mr Osman went into the journal — so the line knows about it — but nobody carried it to his page. The daily road is holed: the pages are R550 light. Patch the pages: Osman up R550.

Pothole four: Mrs Peters bought for R350, but her page was written up as R530 — two digits danced. The journal line was right, so the line is fine; only her page exaggerates. Pages down R180. The pages now read: 7 130 plus 550 minus 180 — R7 500.

Look at what just happened: the line, patched on its road, reads R7 500. The pages, patched on theirs, read R7 500. Neither witness was bullied into agreeing — each was simply corrected where IT was wrong, and the truth appeared where they met. R7 500 is what the street really owes — a number that neither original answer contained.

Your questions for this part are up now: four new potholes — name the road, patch the right record, and keep both running totals as you go.

## Subtopic: The Meeting in the Middle

Why must the case end with ONE number? Because tomorrow the shop acts on it. The trial balance needs the debtors figure; statements go out to customers; decisions about credit limits lean on the total. A business cannot act on "either 7 440 or 7 130, we're not sure" — the reconciliation converts two unreliable answers into one reliable one, and documents HOW, finding by finding, so anyone can retrace the case.

And hear the quiet lesson under the loud one: the errors were all CAUGHT, within a month, by routine — not by luck, not by a customer complaining, not by an auditor a year later. That is what reconciliation is for. Mistakes are not the enemy; undiscovered mistakes are. A shop that reconciles monthly gives its mistakes a one-month lifespan.

The creditors side is the same case in a mirror: the line of what WE owe against the pages of our suppliers — same potholes possible on both roads, same patching rule, same meeting in the middle. Just re-derive every direction from the mirror's point of view: their balances live on the credit side, and "too much" and "too little" flip accordingly. Never copy a debtors fix across; think it fresh.

And one more witness arrives in the post each month: the supplier's own statement of your account. A third road, built entirely outside your shop — compared against your pages, differences explained one by one: your payment still travelling, their invoice not yet arrived, or a genuine error on either side. Three independent records of one relationship; that is how trading partners stay friends.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: run a full case — two witnesses, several potholes, both roads patched, one meeting point stated. Close the file like a detective: every difference explained, nothing left unaccounted. Case closed is month closed.
