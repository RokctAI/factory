**Thandi:** Questions from the class! The first one came up three times: if the control account and the list disagree, why can't we just change the list to match the control account and move on?

**Tutor:** Because that assumes the control account is the honest witness, and nothing in the system grants it that status. Control is built from journal totals — and a total can be struck short, struck long, or posted wrongly, exactly as our three hundred rand undercast showed. If you had bent the list toward the uncorrected control figure of nine thousand eight hundred and sixty, you would have destroyed a correct record to match a wrong one, and the true figure of nine thousand nine hundred would never have appeared anywhere. The method's whole power is independence: two records, each corrected only where its OWN journey failed, forced to meet. The meeting is the proof. Shortcut it and you have no proof at all — just one number copied twice.

***

**Thandi:** Next: when a finding lands in front of us, how do we actually DECIDE whether it hits control or the list? A few of us keep guessing.

**Tutor:** Stop looking at the amount and start looking at the journey. Every entry travels two roads from the source document: the line goes into a journal, gets totalled, and the total reaches control; the same line also gets carried, individually, to a personal page. So ask one physical question: at which point on which road did the mistake happen? A column totalled wrongly happens AFTER the lines were correct — pages fine, control poisoned. A line never carried to a page happens after the journal was correct — control fine, list poisoned. A wrong amount written into the original line happens BEFORE both roads split — both poisoned, both corrected. Classify by location, not by instinct, and the treatment writes itself.

***

**Thandi:** Here's the one I loved: you said a difference divisible by nine hints at swapped digits. Why nine? That feels like a magic trick.

**Tutor:** It is arithmetic wearing a magician's cape. Swap two neighbouring digits and the number changes by nine times the difference between those digits. Write four hundred and fifty as five hundred and forty: the tens digit and the hundreds digit traded places, and the change is ninety — nine times the ten that separates a four from a five in those columns. It works for any transposition: seventy-two written as twenty-seven differs by forty-five. So when your difference is ninety, or forty-five, or two hundred and seventy, divide by nine — if it divides cleanly, hunt for a number whose digits could have danced. The fingerprint does not name the culprit, but it tells you which cupboard to search first — and under time pressure, that is half the battle.

***

**Thandi:** Last one: once control and the list finally agree, is the figure guaranteed correct? Or can two agreeing records still both be wrong?

**Tutor:** They can still both be wrong — and knowing that keeps you honest. Agreement proves the two journeys processed the same information consistently; it cannot prove the information itself was true. An error in the original journal line flows down both roads identically, so both records agree on the same wrong number. An invoice never recorded at all leaves no trace on either journey. That is why reconciliation is one layer in a web, not the whole web: document matching catches the unrecorded invoice, the supplier's statement — a record built entirely outside your business — catches what both your internal records missed, and the stocktake tests what no ledger can see. Each check has a blind spot; the system works because the blind spots do not overlap. Agreement is necessary. It is never, by itself, sufficient.
