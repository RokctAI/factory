**Thandi:** Class questions! The big one: when Dube paid her R2 000, a few of us credited Sales — it felt like the shop finally earning its money. Why is that wrong?

**Tutor:** Because the earning and the receiving are two different events, recorded on two different days — and the earning was already booked. The day Dube took the goods, the Debtors Journal recorded Sales of three thousand rand: earned, complete, done. Her payment weeks later settles the PROMISE, not the sale: Bank grows, her page shrinks, and Sales stays untouched — credit it again and the month would claim five thousand rand of sales from a three-thousand-rand transaction. The discipline generalises: in the cash journals, the Debtors and Creditors Control columns exist precisely for money that settles old promises, and money in those columns never visits the trading accounts. Ask of every receipt: is this NEW earning, or an old promise being kept? New earning — Sales. Old promise — the page.

***

**Thandi:** Next: with eleven transactions flying, how do we avoid posting something twice — once from the journal total and once from the personal page?

**Tutor:** By respecting the two destinations' different jobs, which never overlap. The personal page in a subsidiary ledger is DETAIL: it takes each line, daily, so a customer's balance is always current. The general ledger takes TOTALS: one posting per column, monthly. These are two views of the same facts, not two recordings of it — the Debtors Ledger is not part of the double entry; the general ledger alone carries that. Double-counting only happens when someone posts a line to Debtors CONTROL as well as to the page — control receives only the month's column totals, never lines. Hold the map: lines to pages daily; totals to control monthly. And the month-end lock — list against control — would expose the double-count immediately, which is exactly the kind of error it exists to catch.

***

**Thandi:** A sharp one from the back: the trial balance levelled at thirty-two and a half thousand — but if we had routed a slip through the WRONG door entirely, would the scales have caught it?

**Tutor:** Sometimes yes, usually no — and knowing which is the mark of a strong candidate. Route Dube's receipt into the sundries column as, say, rent income, and both sides still move equally: the scales stay level while the books tell a false story — that is the wrong-account error the trial balance is structurally blind to. But the system has other eyes. Dube's page would not have been credited, so the debtors list would exceed control by two thousand rand at month end — the lock screams. Sales analysis would look wrong against the mark-up. And the narrationless oddity would surface the moment anyone traced the document. That is why the month closed with THREE proofs, not one: cross-casts inside each journal, list-against-control per street, and the scales across everything. Layered checks, because every single check has a blind spot.

***

**Thandi:** Last one: the Friday walk sounds wise for Thabo's little shop — but does a big business with computers still do anything like it?

**Tutor:** It does exactly that, at scale and speed — the walk simply wears software. The till count against the day's records becomes automated cash-up reconciliation, exception-flagged. The shelf sample becomes cycle counting: a rotating slice of stock counted daily against the perpetual records. The debtors finger-run becomes an aged analysis printed every Monday, with credit limits enforced by the system itself. The supplier statement check becomes automated reconciliation of statements to the creditors ledger. The principles have not moved a centimetre since Thabo's stoep: reality tested against records, on a schedule, by someone accountable. Computers changed the pace of the walk, never its purpose — and examiners love asking precisely that: the same control, dressed for a bigger business.
