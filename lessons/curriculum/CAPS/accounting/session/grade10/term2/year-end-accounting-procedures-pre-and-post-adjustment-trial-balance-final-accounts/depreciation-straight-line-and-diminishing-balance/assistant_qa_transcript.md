**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! The big one first: depreciation is an expense, but no money leaves the bank. How can something be an expense if nobody pays anything?

**Tutor:** The money left long ago — all R120 000 of it, on purchase day. The question is not WHETHER the bakkie costs the business, but WHICH YEARS carry that cost. Pay-day and expense-day are simply different days: the payment happened once, but the USING happens across five years, and matching charges each year for its share of the using. Think of it as the purchase price arriving in the income statement in instalments — R24 000 a year — long after the cash conversation is over. And that is exactly why the credit side of the entry is Accumulated Depreciation rather than Bank: no cash is moving; value already paid for is being consumed, year by year, and the books are recording the consuming.

***

**Thandi:** Next: why does the Vehicles account stay at R120 000 forever? If the bakkie is honestly worth R96 000 now, why not just write the account down to R96 000?

**Tutor:** Because one account would then be answering two different questions with one number — and losing one of the answers. Keep them separate and the books answer three questions at once: what did we pay — Vehicles, R120 000, the anchor that historical cost protects and the asset register verifies; how much has worn away — Accumulated Depreciation, R24 000 and growing; what remains in the books — the subtraction, R96 000. Write the asset account down directly and the original cost gradually disappears from the ledger; five years on, nobody can tell whether the fleet figure is what was paid or what is left. The pairing preserves the full history in plain sight — and, usefully for you, it is the same discipline as keeping gross, deductions and net apart on a payslip: never collapse a story into one number when the exam will ask for its parts.

***

**Thandi:** This one trips everyone in year two: on diminishing balance, someone calculated twenty percent of R120 000 again and got R24 000. What's the one habit that stops that?

**Tutor:** Write the BASE down before touching the percentage. Diminishing balance means the percentage bites the CARRYING VALUE — and the carrying value has moved since last year. So the working for year two starts with a labelled line: carrying value at the beginning of the year — R96 000. Only then: twenty percent of it — R19 200. Year three: carrying value R76 800, charge R15 360. The learner who slipped skipped the first line and reached for the famous number instead of the current one. One more insurance: check the trend. On diminishing balance every year's charge must be SMALLER than the last — if your year two matches year one, the base was wrong before the multiplication began. Straight-line repeats; diminishing balance shrinks. If your answers don't behave that way, the method and the working have disagreed.

***

**Thandi:** Last one: the September machine got only R900 of depreciation for six months. But we ran it hard from the day it arrived — why should the calendar decide instead of the workload?

**Tutor:** The calendar is how the books MEASURE workload, fairly and checkably. Depreciation charges a year for the service it received; a machine that joined in September could serve at most half this year, however hard those months ran — the other six months of service simply did not exist. So the rate is scaled by time served: R1 800 for a full year, six-twelfths of it — R900 — for half. The months are also the only base everyone can verify: the purchase date sits on the invoice and the register page, while "how hard we ran it" sits in nobody's records at all. Where usage truly is the honest measure, the method itself changes — diminishing balance already loads the charge onto the hard early years. But within a method, time served is the fraction — the same logic as paying a worker from their start date, never from January.
