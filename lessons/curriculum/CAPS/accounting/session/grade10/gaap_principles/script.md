Welcome, student. Today we study the rulebook behind every figure you will ever write in a set of financial statements: the GAAP principles — Generally Accepted Accounting Practice. By the end of this lesson you will name each principle, explain it in one clean sentence, and — the real exam skill — match any scenario to the principle it demonstrates.

## Subtopic: Why Accounting Needs Agreed Rules

Begin with our central scenario. A business bought land for R500 000 in 2019. Today, an estate agent says the land is worth R900 000. Yet in the business's books, the land still appears at R500 000. Is the bookkeeper lazy? Careless? No — the bookkeeper is obeying a principle, and by the end of this lesson you will name it instantly.

First, why do principles exist at all? Imagine every business measured and reported however it pleased. One shop records land at what it hopes to sell it for; another records profit before it is earned; a third mixes the owner's grocery money with the till. Nobody — not the owner, not the bank considering a loan, not SARS — could trust or compare any set of statements.

GAAP is the shared set of rules that makes financial statements trustworthy and comparable. When every accountant follows the same principles, a reader can pick up any business's statements and know exactly what the figures mean.

In Grade 10 you must know six principles: historical cost, prudence, materiality, the business entity rule, going concern, and matching. Exam questions almost never ask you to recite them — they hand you a scenario, like our land example, and ask: which principle is being applied, and why? So for each principle, we will learn the rule, the reason, and the tell-tale signs that identify it in a scenario.

## Subtopic: Historical Cost and Prudence

Historical cost first, because it solves our land mystery. The rule: assets are recorded at their original purchase price — the actual, verifiable amount paid — and not at today's estimated value. The land cost R500 000 in 2019, so R500 000 is what the books show, even if an agent now estimates R900 000.

Why? Because the purchase price is a fact, supported by a source document. Today's value is an opinion that changes with the market and with whoever you ask. Accounting prefers verifiable fact over shifting opinion. So the answer to our opening scenario is: the historical cost principle. The tell-tale sign in any scenario: an asset "is now worth more" but is "still shown at" its original price.

Now prudence, sometimes called conservatism. The rule: never overstate the good news, never understate the bad news. When you must choose between two reasonable figures, choose the one that shows the business in the more cautious light. Anticipate possible losses, but never record profits before they are certain.

You have already met prudence in action without knowing it: writing off a bad debt. The moment we doubt a debtor will pay, we remove that debtor from our assets — we do not keep polishing an asset we may never collect. Provision for bad debts, valuing stock at the lower of cost and selling price when goods are damaged — all prudence. The tell-tale sign: a business recording a possible loss now, or refusing to record a hoped-for gain.

Be careful not to confuse historical cost with prudence — the exam loves this trap. Both can involve values, but historical cost is about sticking to the original purchase price of an asset; prudence is about choosing the cautious estimate when uncertainty exists. Land kept at R500 000 is historical cost. Doubtful debts written down is prudence.

## Subtopic: Materiality and the Business Entity Rule

Materiality next. The rule: information is material if leaving it out or misstating it would change a reader's decision — and material items deserve separate, visible disclosure, while trivial items may be grouped or simplified.

A practical example: a business buys a stapler for R45. Technically a stapler is equipment that lasts years — should we record it as a fixed asset and depreciate it by a few rand every year? Common sense says no. The amount is too small to influence anyone's decisions, so we simply record it as an expense — Stationery or Sundry expenses — and move on. But a R450 000 delivery vehicle? Absolutely a fixed asset, tracked separately. Same logic, different scale. The tell-tale sign of materiality in a scenario: a decision that turns on whether an amount is big enough to matter.

Now the business entity rule — the principle learners meet earliest and quote most often. The rule: the business and its owner are two separate entities in the books. The business records only its own assets, debts, incomes and expenses — never the owner's personal affairs.

When the owner takes cash from the business for personal use, we do not hide it or call it a business expense: we record it as Drawings, a reduction of the owner's equity. When the owner pays their home electricity from the business bank account, that is Drawings too — not an expense of the business. And flip it around: the owner's private car, home and personal savings never appear among the business's assets.

The tell-tale sign: any scenario where the owner's personal money, property or spending brushes against the business's records. The moment you see "the owner took", "the owner's private", "for personal use" — think business entity rule.

## Subtopic: Going Concern, Matching, and the Matching Game

Going concern. The rule: we prepare the books assuming the business will continue operating into the foreseeable future — it is a going concern, not about to close down.

Why does this matter? Because it justifies other practices. We record land at historical cost partly because we intend to keep using it, not sell it tomorrow. We spread the cost of a vehicle over the years it will serve us — which only makes sense if we expect those future years to happen. If a business were closing next month, everything would be valued at what it could fetch in a quick sale. The tell-tale sign: statements prepared "on the assumption the business will continue", or assets valued for continued use rather than closure.

Finally, matching. The rule: incomes and expenses must be recognised in the financial period in which they occur — and the expenses of a period must be matched against the incomes of that same period to calculate a fair profit.

Example: the business pays insurance of R12 000 in March for the year ahead, but its financial year ends in June. Only three months' worth belongs to this year's expenses; the rest is prepaid, belonging to next year. Or wages for the last week of June are only paid in July — matching says they are still June's expense, accrued now. Without matching, profit would bounce around depending on when cash happened to move, instead of reflecting what was truly earned and consumed. The tell-tale sign: amounts being shifted between periods — prepaid, accrued, "belongs to this year".

Now, the promised matching game — five scenarios, five principles. One: land bought for R500 000, now worth R900 000, still shown at R500 000 — historical cost. Two: a business writes off a doubtful debt rather than hoping it gets paid — prudence. Three: a R45 stapler is recorded as an expense instead of a fixed asset — materiality. Four: the owner's home electricity paid from the business account is recorded as Drawings — business entity rule. Five: statements are drawn up assuming the shop will still be trading next year — going concern.

Your self-check for the exam: read the scenario, underline the action taken, and ask "what is the accountant refusing to do, or insisting on doing?" The refusal or insistence points straight at the principle. Practise until each scenario feels like recognising a face.

Class is dismissed.
