**Student:** I keep mixing up historical cost and prudence. Both seem to be about keeping values low?

**Thandi:** They can *look* similar, but the reasons behind them are totally different — and the reason is what the exam wants. Historical cost isn't trying to keep values low at all; it's about sticking to the documented, provable purchase price. If land prices crashed and the land was suddenly worth *less* than R500 000, historical cost alone would still say R500 000! Prudence is the one that cares about direction: it steps in when there's *uncertainty* and says "expect the loss, don't celebrate the gain early." So ask yourself: is the accountant holding onto an old proven price (historical cost), or making a cautious call about something uncertain (prudence)?

**Student:** Who decides what counts as "material"? Is there an official amount, like anything over R100?

**Thandi:** No magic number, and that's on purpose! Materiality is relative to the size of the business and the decision being made. R45 is nothing to a supermarket chain but might genuinely matter to a tiny tuck shop. The test is always: "if this were left out or lumped in somewhere, would a reader of the statements make a different decision?" If no — not material, keep it simple. If yes — material, show it properly. In exam scenarios they make it obvious: a stapler versus a delivery vehicle. Use the common-sense scale, not a fixed rand amount.

**Student:** If the owner IS the business — like a sole trader — how can we pretend they're separate? It's their money anyway!

**Thandi:** Legally, you're right — a sole trader and the business are one person, and the owner can take money whenever they like. But the *books* separate them, and here's why it matters: the owner needs to know if the *business itself* is making a profit. If the owner's groceries and holidays got mixed into the expenses, the profit figure would be meaningless — you couldn't tell a struggling shop from a thriving one with a big-spending owner. So we record personal takings as Drawings, keeping the scoreboard honest. Separate books, not separate people.

**Student:** For matching — why can't we just record expenses when we actually pay them? It seems so much simpler.

**Thandi:** Simpler, yes — but it would make profit tell lies! Imagine paying a full year's insurance of R12 000 in the last month of the financial year. Cash-based thinking dumps all R12 000 into this year, crushing this year's profit, while next year gets eleven months of cover for free and looks amazing. Neither year's profit would be true. Matching fixes this: each year carries exactly the expenses that were *used up* earning that year's income. That's why we get prepaid expenses and accrued expenses at year end — they're the adjustment tools that slide costs into the right year.
