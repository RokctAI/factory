Welcome, student. Today we step back from individual entries and look at the whole machine: the accounting cycle. By the end of this lesson you will be able to arrange the six stages in order — source documents, journals, posting to the ledger, trial balance, final accounts, financial statements — and explain exactly what each stage produces.

## Subtopic: Why the Accounting Cycle Exists

Before we list stages, let us think about the problem the cycle solves. Imagine a sole trader — let us call her T. Mokoena, who runs Mokoena Traders. During June she sells stock for cash, buys stock from suppliers, pays wages, and receives money from debtors. By the end of the month there are dozens of transactions. If she simply kept a shoebox of slips, she could never answer the two questions every owner asks: did I make a profit, and what is my business worth?

The accounting cycle is the fixed route every transaction travels, from the moment it happens to the moment it appears in the financial statements. You already know pieces of this route from Grade 8 and 9: you have written up source documents and entered basic journals. What is new in Grade 10 is seeing the pieces as one connected sequence, and knowing what each stage hands over to the next.

Here is the sequence you must memorise, because every reporting question in Paper 1 walks this exact path: first, source documents. Second, journals. Third, posting to the ledger. Fourth, the trial balance. Fifth, the final accounts. Sixth, the financial statements.

Notice the word cycle. When June is finished, July's transactions start the same journey again. The route repeats every period, which is why accountants call it a cycle and not a line.

A common pitfall right at the start: learners swap the trial balance and the ledger, or put financial statements before final accounts. The order is not decoration — each stage literally cannot happen without the output of the one before it. Let us walk the route stage by stage and prove that to ourselves.

## Subtopic: Stage One and Two — Source Documents and Journals

Stage one is the source document. Every transaction must leave a paper trail, because accounting refuses to record anything without evidence. When Mokoena Traders sells goods for cash on 3 June, the till produces a cash register slip. When the business pays the landlord by electronic transfer, the bank statement and the payment advice are the proof. When goods are bought on credit, the supplier issues an invoice.

What does this stage produce? It produces the evidence — the objective proof of the date, the amount, and the parties involved. No source document, no entry. That is the rule.

Stage two is the journals, also called books of first entry. Here is the key idea: we do not scribble transactions into the ledger one by one as they happen. First we sort them into daily lists by type. Cash coming in — like the 3 June cash sales — goes into the Cash Receipts Journal, the CRJ. Cash going out — the rent payment, wages — goes into the Cash Payments Journal, the CPJ. Small cash payments go through the Petty Cash Journal. In Grade 10 you will add the Debtors Journal and Creditors Journal for credit transactions.

Why sort first? Efficiency and control. If Mokoena Traders makes eighty cash sales in June, we do not want eighty separate ledger entries; we want one column total. The journal gathers similar transactions so that only totals move to the next stage.

What does stage two produce? Chronological, classified records of the month's transactions, with columns totalled at month end — ready for posting.

## Subtopic: Stage Three and Four — Posting to the Ledger and the Trial Balance

Stage three is posting to the general ledger. The journals are organised by date; the ledger is organised by account. The business needs to know the total effect on Bank, on Sales, on Rent Expense — and the ledger account is where all the scattered entries about one item come together.

At the end of June, the bookkeeper posts the journal totals to the ledger accounts using the double-entry principle: every amount is entered on the debit side of one account and the credit side of another. The CRJ's bank column total is debited to Bank; the sales column total is credited to Sales. Each account is then balanced or totalled.

What does this stage produce? A balance on every account — one clean figure telling us, for example, exactly how much cash the business has, and exactly what its total sales for the period are.

Stage four is the trial balance. Now we pause and check our work. We list every ledger account with its balance in one of two columns: debits on the left, credits on the right. Because double entry demands that every debit has an equal credit, the two columns must total the same amount. If they do not, an error was made somewhere in stages two or three, and we must find it before moving on.

Be careful here — this is a favourite exam trap. A balancing trial balance does not prove the books are perfect. If we posted R500 to the wrong account but on the correct side, the trial balance still balances. It checks arithmetic equality, not perfect accuracy.

What does stage four produce? A checked list of all account balances — the raw material for the statements.

## Subtopic: Stage Five and Six — Final Accounts and Financial Statements

Stage five is the final accounts, prepared at the end of the financial year. This is where we work out profit inside the ledger itself. We open the Trading account and transfer Sales and Cost of Sales into it; the difference is the gross profit. The gross profit is carried to the Profit and Loss account, where all the other incomes and expenses — rent income, wages, telephone — are gathered. The balance that remains is the net profit, which is transferred to the Capital account, because the profit belongs to the owner.

What does stage five produce? The gross profit and the net profit, calculated formally through ledger accounts.

Stage six, the destination: the financial statements. These are the polished reports drawn up for the owner and other interested parties. The Statement of Comprehensive Income reports the incomes, expenses and net profit for the period. The Statement of Financial Position reports the assets, equity and liabilities on the final day — the position of the business.

What does stage six produce? Answers. Did Mokoena Traders make a profit in June's financial year? The Statement of Comprehensive Income says how much. What does the business own and owe? The Statement of Financial Position lays it out.

Now let us verify our understanding by running the whole June story through the cycle in one breath. A cash sale happens: the till slip is the source document. The slip's amount is entered in the CRJ. At month end the CRJ totals are posted to Bank and Sales in the ledger. The ledger balances are listed in the trial balance, which must balance. At year end, Sales flows into the Trading account and profit is determined in the final accounts. Finally, that profit appears in the financial statements the owner reads.

Test yourself with this check: for any stage, can you name what it receives from the previous stage and what it hands to the next? If you can do that for all six, you own this cycle — and you own the spine of every Accounting paper you will ever write.
