**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! The most common one: the ledger and the trial balance keep blurring together — aren't they both just lists of accounts?

**Tutor:** They share a cast but perform different jobs. The ledger is where the work happens: every account gathers its own entries, debit side and credit side, until a balance emerges — it is a book of RECORDS. The trial balance records nothing at all: it is a one-page CHECKLIST, copying every ledger balance into a debit or credit column and testing that the two columns agree. Think of the ledger as doing the month's work and the trial balance as marking it. And remember what the marking proves: arithmetic equality only — a list that balances can still hide an amount filed in the wrong account. The ledger works; the trial balance checks; neither replaces the other.

***

**Thandi:** Next: why can't transactions go straight from the source documents into the ledger? The journal stop feels like double handling.

**Tutor:** Picture the month without it. Eighty cash sales, each posted separately: eighty entries in the Bank account, eighty in Sales, one hundred and sixty chances of a slip — and a ledger so cluttered that finding anything becomes archaeology. The journal stop exists to gather: similar transactions queue together, the column is totalled once, and the ledger receives ONE posting for the month's sales instead of eighty. Efficiency is half the reason; control is the other half — a journal organised by date and type means any document can be traced to its line, and any line to its document. The stop that looks like double handling is the stop that makes the whole route fast and findable.

***

**Thandi:** This one wins the trap-question award: if my trial balance balances, my books are correct — true or false?

**Tutor:** False, and knowing WHY it is false is worth marks every year. The trial balance tests one thing: that total debits equal total credits — that every entry kept its double-entry promise arithmetically. Now consider the errors that keep that promise while still being wrong. Post five hundred rand of telephone costs to the Repairs account — correct side, wrong account: the columns still agree. Leave an entire transaction out of the journals — both halves missing equally: the columns still agree. Enter a sale twice — both halves doubled: still agreement. The trial balance is a smoke detector, not a full inspection: when it stays silent you have ruled out one family of errors, never all of them. The other families are caught by different guards — numbered documents, folio trails, and the reconciliations you will meet later in the year.

***

**Thandi:** Last one: do we really run this whole cycle every single day? It sounds exhausting.

**Tutor:** The cycle runs, but its stages keep different clocks — three of them. The daily clock drives the front of the route: source documents written as money moves, journal lines entered while the day is fresh. The monthly clock drives the middle: journal totals posted to the ledger, the trial balance drawn and proven — the books pausing to organise and check themselves. And the yearly clock drives the destination: final accounts weighing the year's trading, financial statements reporting it. So no single day carries the whole route — each day carries its slice, each month its checkpoint, each year its verdict. And then July begins, the fast clock ticks again, and the cycle earns its name.
