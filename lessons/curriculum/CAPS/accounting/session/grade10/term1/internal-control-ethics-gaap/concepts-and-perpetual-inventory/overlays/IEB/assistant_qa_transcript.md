**Thandi:** Class questions! First one: how can the owner and the business be the same person in law, but separate in the books? That sounds like a contradiction.

**Tutor:** It sounds like one until you see that law and bookkeeping answer different questions. The law asks: who can be sued, who must pay the debts? For a sole trader, the answer is the owner, personally — there is no legal wall between Sipho and his shop, and if the shop cannot pay its loan, Sipho's own possessions are on the line. The books ask a different question: how is THE BUSINESS performing? To answer that honestly, the records must fence the shop's money off from the household's — the business entity rule — otherwise school fees and groceries would blur into the shop's expenses and the profit figure would measure the family, not the business. So hold both truths: one person in law, two entities on paper, each for its own good reason.

***

**Thandi:** Next: why does the trade discount just vanish from the books while the cash discount gets its own account? A discount is a discount, surely?

**Tutor:** The difference is timing — whether a recorded amount ever existed to reduce. With a trade discount, the bargaining happens BEFORE anything is recorded: the bulk buyer negotiates, the invoice is written at the lower figure, and that lower figure is simply the price. There is no earlier number in the books to compare against, so there is nothing to record — the discount is invisible by construction. A cash discount is the opposite: the sale was recorded at full price, a real debt stood in the books, and then part of it was forgiven to reward early settlement. Now the books hold a debt that will never fully arrive, and the gap must be explained: discount allowed, an expense, when we forgive our debtors; discount received, an income, when creditors forgive us. Ask one question in any scenario: did a recorded amount shrink afterwards? Yes — record it. No — it was trade discount, and it already vanished into the price.

***

**Thandi:** Here's the one that had the back row debating: if managerial accounting has no rules, what stops the owner from just making the numbers up?

**Tutor:** Nothing stops him — and that is exactly why it works. Managerial accounting is the owner talking to himself about his own future: should I stock lunchboxes, can I afford a helper? If he flatters those numbers, the only person deceived is the one making the decision, and the shop pays the price. Self-interest is the audit. Financial accounting faces the opposite situation: outsiders — the bank, the revenue authority — must rely on numbers they did not prepare, from a business they cannot watch. That is precisely where temptation lives and trust is at stake, so that is where GAAP stands guard. The rulebook follows the risk: rules where strangers must trust, freedom where the audience deceives only itself.

***

**Thandi:** Last question: the perpetual system writes two entries for every single sale. Isn't that double the work for the same information?

**Tutor:** It is more work per sale, and it buys two things the single entry can never provide. First, immediate knowledge: the moment a backpack sells for R450 having cost R270, the books know the R180 gross profit — per sale, per day, per month — while the periodic system knows gross profit only after a stocktake, months later. Second, and greater: an honest shelf. Because every purchase counts stock in and every sale counts cost out, the Trading Stock account always states what SHOULD be on hand — so a physical count becomes a test, and any shortfall surfaces as a named, dated question. Under the periodic system that same shortfall silently disguises itself as sales. So yes — two entries instead of one. In exchange: profit you can see today, and losses that cannot hide. That trade is the bargain of the whole system.
