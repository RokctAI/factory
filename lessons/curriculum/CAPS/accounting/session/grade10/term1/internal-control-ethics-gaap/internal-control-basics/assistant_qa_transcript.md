**Thandi:** Class questions before we wrap up! The one that came in most: division of duties needs three people, but lots of small businesses ARE one person. Does internal control just not apply to them?

**Tutor:** It applies — the toolkit simply shifts weight to the controls that do not need extra hands. A one-person business can still document everything: numbered receipts for every sale, a separate cash tin for the business, daily banking, a notebook ruled off each evening. It can still use independent checks, because the bank statement is an independent record the owner did not write — reconciling the tin and the notebook against the bank is division of duties with the bank as the second person. And the owner can still monitor: a weekly count of stock against what the notebook says should be there. So the principle survives: never let one UNCHECKED stream of information be the only truth. Where a second person is impossible, a second RECORD does the work.

***

**Thandi:** Next: a learner says the three-learner tuck shop is slower — customers queue longer while Lerato ticks the notebook. Isn't there a cost to all this control?

**Tutor:** There is, and honest accounting admits it: controls cost time, sometimes money, and a business can over-control itself into paralysis. The discipline is proportion — match the strength of the control to the size of the risk. A tuck shop handling two hundred rand a week needs a notebook and two counters, not an auditor. A supermarket handling two hundred thousand a day needs much more. The question to ask of any control is: does the risk it removes cost more than the control itself? For cash — small, portable, anonymous and loved by everyone — the answer is almost always yes, controls pay. For the stationery cupboard, a lighter touch is fine. Control is not maximised; it is proportioned.

***

**Thandi:** This one is sharp: if the OWNER is the one setting a bad example — taking from the till, ignoring the rules — who controls the controller?

**Tutor:** The sharpest question in the topic, and the framework's honest answer is: within a small business, largely nothing — which is exactly why the control environment is listed first. Every other component draws its power from the top. Staff follow the example they see, not the policy they are handed. In bigger businesses, outside forces step in — auditors, banks demanding statements, partners, the law — and in later grades you will meet those external checks properly. But for Grade 10, state the principle plainly: a control system is never stronger than the behaviour of the person at the top, and an owner who wants honest staff must first be a visibly honest owner.

***

**Thandi:** Last one: in the exam, learners lose marks on the recommendation part — the fix they suggest. What separates a full-mark recommendation from a zero-mark one?

**Tutor:** Specificity — the recommendation must name who does what, differently, from now on. Zero marks: the business must be more careful with cash. Full marks: two staff members count the till together at closing, record the total, and the takings are banked daily by a person who does not do the recording. Hear the difference: the second version could be handed to the staff tomorrow morning as an instruction, and it attacks the exact weakness the scenario described. Before writing any recommendation, test it with three questions: Who acts? What exactly do they do? Does it close the gap I identified? If any answer is vague, sharpen it before you write.
