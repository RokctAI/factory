**Thandi:** Class questions coming through! First one: if scenarios often fit two or three characteristics, how is anyone supposed to know which single answer the marker wants?

**Tutor:** Usually the marker wants the BEST fit plus a defence, not a lucky guess — and the defence is what separates them. Run each candidate characteristic's definition against the facts and see which one the scenario was clearly built around. The car wash piping runoff into the stream touches leadership and responsible management, yes — someone approved it, resources were managed badly — but the story's engine is profit today at the cost of the environment and community tomorrow, which is sustainability's exact definition. So name sustainability first, argue it with that definition, and then, if marks allow, add the secondary characteristics with their own one-line links. Best fit, argued; near-misses acknowledged. That structure earns marks even when two answers were genuinely defensible.

**Thandi:** Next: leadership and discipline keep blurring together for me. The owner who banks all the takings every day — isn't that both?

**Tutor:** It genuinely is both, and the way to separate them is to ask WHO the action teaches. Discipline is about the doer: rules followed consistently, even under pressure — daily banking, every day, is disciplined regardless of who is watching. Leadership is about the followers: the person at the top setting an example that shapes how everyone below behaves. So the same daily banking is discipline as an act, and becomes leadership because it is the OWNER doing it — staff who see the boss obey her own rules learn that the rules are real. Quick test for scenarios: if the question stresses the person's seniority or the effect on staff, argue leadership; if it stresses consistency and procedure, argue discipline. Stress both, name both, one link each.

**Thandi:** Here's one from the back row: the till-slip refund story — wasn't that just internal control working, not ethics? The record caught the error, after all.

**Tutor:** Sharp question, and the answer is that the story needs both to end well. The till record is pure internal control: a document trail that made the double ring-up checkable. But a record only reports; it does not decide. The manager still chose to pull the slip in front of the customer, admit the shop's error and refund it — that choice is accountability and fairness, and no till roll could make it for him. A dishonest manager with the same record could have shrugged the customer away. That is the two-hands idea from the lesson: control made the honesty provable, ethics made it happen. In an answer, credit both — name the control mechanism, then name the characteristic that used it.

**Thandi:** Last one, and it's cheeky: if ethical shortcuts really destroyed businesses, why do we all know a shop that sells suspicious stock and is still standing?

**Tutor:** Because the loss side of the trade is delayed and uncertain — that is exactly what makes the temptation work. Remember the pattern: a small, certain, immediate gain bought with a large, uncertain, delayed loss. The suspicious shop pockets the certain gain every week, and the loss sits waiting: the first police visit, the first supplier who refuses credit, the first bank that declines a loan because the records cannot explain the stock, the week the community's patience runs out. Some shops get lucky longer than others; none of them can bank on it, insure it or borrow against it. And notice what the shop has already lost even while standing — no credit, no clean records, no growth path. Surviving is not the same as being a good business.
