# Part 1 — Expert

A business can have honest owners, good products and full tills — and still bleed money through error, theft and carelessness. The discipline that stops the bleeding is internal control, and today we master its meaning and its five basic processes: the control environment, risk assessment, control activities, information systems and communication, and monitoring. These five return in every grade of Accounting, attached to cash, stock, debtors and fixed assets — learn them properly once, and they serve you until matric.

## Subtopic: What Internal Control Is — and What It Protects

Definition first, exam-precise: internal control is the system of policies, procedures and checks that a business puts in place to safeguard its assets, ensure the accuracy and reliability of its records, promote efficient operations, and encourage adherence to its policies. Four objectives — assets protected, records accurate, operations efficient, policies followed — and a strong answer can name all four.

Understand what the system is up against. Errors: honest mistakes — a delivery counted wrongly, an amount posted to the wrong account. Fraud: deliberate dishonesty — cash pocketed, stock carried out the back door, invoices invented. Waste: resources leaking without crime — electricity burning overnight, stock expiring unsold. Internal control does not assume employees are criminals; it assumes people are human, and builds a structure where errors surface quickly and dishonesty becomes difficult and detectable.

One principle towers over the rest, so meet it immediately: DIVISION OF DUTIES, also called segregation of duties. No single person should control a transaction from beginning to end. The person who receives cash should not be the person who records it; the person who orders stock should not be the one who receives and also pays for it. Why? Because fraud committed alone must then get past a second pair of eyes to stay hidden — and collusion between two people is far rarer and far riskier than a private temptation. Division of duties converts trust in individuals into confidence in a SYSTEM.

Hold that foundation. The five components we now build are the framework the syllabus names — the architecture inside which division of duties and every other check operates.

## Subtopic: The Five Components of Internal Control

Component one: the CONTROL ENVIRONMENT. The tone at the top — management's own attitude to honesty, discipline and rules. If the owner takes cash from the till for private use, no written procedure will convince staff that rules matter. The control environment is the soil; every other control grows in it or dies in it.

Component two: RISK ASSESSMENT. The business deliberately asks: what can go wrong here? Where does cash change hands? Where can stock disappear? Which records could be manipulated? A risk identified is a risk that can be controlled; the unasked question is the one that costs money. Risk assessment is a habit of management, repeated as the business changes — a new delivery route or a new till point brings new risks.

Component three: CONTROL ACTIVITIES. The concrete rules and checks that answer the risks — the visible machinery of control. These deserve their own subtopic, and get one next.

Component four: INFORMATION SYSTEMS AND COMMUNICATION. Controls only work when accurate information flows to the people who need it, in time to act. The recording system itself — source documents, journals, ledgers — is an information system; so are the staff meeting where procedures are explained and the notice that tells a cashier what to do when the till does not balance. A rule nobody was told about controls nothing.

Component five: MONITORING. Someone checks that the controls exist in practice, not only on paper. Spot checks, surprise cash counts, a manager reviewing the day's documents, the annual stocktake compared against records. Controls decay when nobody watches them — monitoring is the maintenance schedule of the whole system.

Learn the five as a flowing sentence: leadership sets the tone, the business asks what can go wrong, rules and checks answer the risks, information keeps everyone informed, and monitoring proves it all still works.

## Subtopic: Control Activities — the Working Toolkit

Control activities are where exam scenarios live, so build the toolkit item by item, each with its purpose.

Division of duties — already met: split receiving, recording and authorising between different people, so no transaction has a single master.

Authorisation: defined transactions require defined approval — a credit sale above a limit needs the owner's signature; a payment is made only against an approved invoice. Purpose: decisions with consequences pass a responsible eye before money moves.

Documentation: every transaction generates a numbered source document — receipts, invoices, till slips. Purpose two-fold: evidence for the records, and the numbering itself a control — a missing number in the sequence is a question demanding an answer.

Physical safeguards: assets locked, access limited. Cash in a safe, banked daily and promptly; stockrooms locked with named keyholders; fixed assets marked and listed in an asset register. Purpose: opportunity removed is temptation removed.

Independent checks and reconciliation: one record tested against another created independently — the bank statement against the cash journals, the physical stock count against the stock records, the debtors list against the control account. Purpose: two independent records that must agree expose an error or a theft the moment they disagree.

Now apply the toolkit to the three assets Grade 10 scenarios favour. Cash: two staff count the till together at closing; takings banked daily; the person banking is not the person recording; petty cash only against vouchers. Stock: deliveries counted against the delivery note by someone other than the orderer; stockroom locked; regular counts compared with records. Fixed assets: an asset register listing each item, its cost and location; items marked as business property; the register checked physically at intervals.

## Subtopic: Reading a Scenario Like an Examiner

The exam almost never asks you to recite the five components. It hands you a scenario — a business doing something carelessly — and asks three things: identify the weakness, explain the risk, recommend the control. Master that three-move answer here.

Scenario: at a small hardware shop, the same employee orders stock, receives the deliveries, and pays the suppliers. Move one — the weakness, named in control language: no division of duties; one person controls the entire purchasing cycle. Move two — the risk, made concrete: the employee could order goods for private use, or approve payment for goods never delivered, and no second person would notice. Move three — the recommendation, practical and proportionate: separate the functions — one person orders, another receives and checks deliveries against the order, and the owner authorises payments against matched documents.

Note the standard of the recommendation: specific, actionable, and matched to the weakness. "The owner must be more careful" earns nothing; "deliveries must be checked against the order by someone other than the person who ordered" earns full marks.

Second scenario, faster: takings are kept in a drawer and banked whenever the owner has time, sometimes a week later. Weakness: cash not banked promptly, no physical safeguard. Risk: theft and loss, and the records cannot be checked against regular deposits. Control: bank daily, intact, and reconcile deposits against the cash journal.

The component framework organises any answer you are unsure of: ask which of the five is missing — tone, risk-awareness, activities, information, or monitoring — and the weakness names itself. The final questions of this part are with you now: three moves each — weakness, risk, recommendation — in full sentences.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same system, built from a place you have all stood: the school tuck shop. No definitions first — just watch what goes wrong, and let the rules invent themselves.

## Subtopic: The Tuck-Shop Disaster

Imagine your class runs the tuck shop for a term to raise funds. Excitement, stock bought, first break — chaos. One learner, Sipho, does everything: he takes the money, hands over the chips, keeps the float in his pocket, and promises to write it all down after school.

By Friday, the float is short. Is Sipho a thief? Probably not — but here is the uncomfortable truth: NOBODY CAN KNOW. Not even Sipho. Money went into a pocket that also held his own money; sales lived in his memory; nobody counted anything at the start or the end. If he took nothing, he cannot prove it. If he took everything, nobody could prove that either. The system — one person, no records, no counting — has made honesty and theft look identical.

Now replay the week with three small changes. Ayanda takes the money and gives change from a separate cash box. Sipho hands over the stock. Lerato ticks every sale in a notebook, and at closing, Ayanda and Lerato count the box TOGETHER and write the total down. Same learners, same chips — but now the money box matches the notebook or it does not, and either way, everyone knows by Friday break, not Friday month-end. And notice what it did for Sipho: the system now PROTECTS him. When the box balances, his honesty is provable.

That is internal control in one story: not suspicion — protection. Rules that make the honest path visible and the dishonest path difficult. Everything in the formal framework is this tuck shop, scaled up.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the tuck shop are coming to you right now. For each one, ask: could anyone PROVE what happened, or only hope?

## Subtopic: Five Questions Every Business Must Answer

The textbook gives the system five formal names. Forget the names for a minute — they are answers to five plain questions any tuck shop, spaza or supermarket must face.

Question one: WHO SETS THE EXAMPLE? If the teacher in charge borrows from the cash box "till Monday", every learner learns that the box is borrowable. The example at the top decides whether rules are real. That is the control environment.

Question two: WHAT COULD GO WRONG HERE? Sit with the cash box and think like trouble: money and chips both walk past many hands; change can be given wrongly; stock can vanish in the rush. Listing the dangers before they happen — that is risk assessment.

Question three: WHAT RULES STOP IT? Different learners for money and stock; sales ticked as they happen; the box counted by two people; leftover stock counted daily. The practical rules — control activities.

Question four: DOES EVERYONE KNOW WHAT'S HAPPENING? The notebook, the counting sheet, telling Monday's team what Friday's team decided. Information flowing to the people who need it — information and communication.

Question five: WHO CHECKS THAT THE RULES STILL HAPPEN? The treasurer does a surprise count some breaks; the teacher compares a week's notebook against the money banked. Checking the checkers — monitoring.

Say the five questions out loud once — example, dangers, rules, information, checking — and you own the framework. The formal names are just Sunday clothes for these five questions.

Your questions for this part are up now: each describes a tuck-shop moment — match it to the question it answers, then to its formal name.

## Subtopic: Spot the Weakness — Your Turn on Patrol

Last skill, and it is the exam skill: walking into any scenario and SEEING the gap. Train the eye on three quick patrols.

Patrol one: a spaza owner lets her nephew run the shop alone on weekends; he takes payments, restocks the shelves and borrows the calculator to work out his own weekend wages from the tin. Feel the tuck-shop pattern? One person, all the roles, wages self-served from unrecorded cash. The fix costs nothing: a notebook of weekend sales, a counted float at start and close, and wages paid by the owner from counted money.

Patrol two: a shop's storeroom stands open on delivery days because "everyone is busy". What could go wrong needs no imagination. The rule that answers it: the storeroom stays locked; ONE named person receives deliveries and counts them against the delivery note before anything goes on a shelf.

Patrol three: a business writes receipts from a pad with no numbers, "when the customer asks". Without numbers, a missing receipt is invisible; without a receipt for every sale, recorded income is whatever was written down. Numbered receipts for every sale — the number sequence itself becomes the watchdog: a gap in the numbers asks its own question.

Feel your reflex now: in each patrol, you found the gap by asking the five questions in order and stopping where the answer was "nobody" or "no rule". That reflex IS the topic — and it will mark internal-control questions for you all the way to matric.

And here come the final questions of the lesson, right now: three scenarios, three patrols. Find the gap, name the rule that closes it, and — like the tuck shop taught you — remember the rule is there to protect the honest ones. That is a system worth building.
