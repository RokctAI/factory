# Part 1 — Expert

Accounting runs on numbers, but it stands on something older: trust. Today we study the code of ethics that binds every party in the financial environment, and the seven characteristics the syllabus names — leadership, discipline, transparency, accountability, fairness, sustainability and responsible management. Examiners test these the same way they test GAAP: a scenario, and the demand that you name the characteristic at stake and argue it. By the end, you will do exactly that.

## Subtopic: Why Ethics Is Accounting's Foundation

Begin with the uncomfortable fact the whole topic answers: money concentrates temptation. The bookkeeper sees cash daily; the manager approves payments; the owner decides what the records will say. Every financial role carries opportunity, and a business where opportunity meets no ethical boundary will eventually bleed — through theft, through manipulated figures, through corners quietly cut.

Now define the instrument. A code of ethics is a formal statement of the values and standards of behaviour a business commits itself to — a published answer, in advance, to the question "how do we behave when nobody is watching?" It binds ALL parties in the financial environment: owners, managers, accountants and bookkeepers, employees, and in dealings with customers, suppliers and the state.

Why does Accounting specifically demand this? Because financial information only has value if it can be TRUSTED. A bank reading statements, an owner reading a profit figure, SARS reading a return — each depends on the figures being honestly made. The moment records can be bent to convenience, they stop being information and become decoration. Ethics is therefore not a soft subject bolted onto Accounting; it is the precondition for the entire discipline. Every principle you have met — the entity rule, prudence, the disciplines of internal control — assumes people willing to follow rules when following them costs something.

Hold the connection to internal control from the previous lesson: control systems make dishonesty difficult; ethics makes it unwanted. A business needs both, because no control system survives people determined to defeat it, and no ethical culture survives with zero checks. They are the two hands of the same grip.

Pause here — questions on this foundation are coming to you now. They test the definition of a code of ethics, whom it binds, and why trust is the discipline's precondition: answer in those terms.

## Subtopic: The Seven Characteristics, Defined for Marks

The syllabus names seven characteristics. Learn each as a definition plus a one-line business example — that pairing is exactly what earns the marks.

Leadership: those in charge set the ethical example and take responsibility for the organisation's direction. The owner who follows the same rules imposed on staff — banking all cash, never skimming — leads ethically; the tone at the top from internal control is leadership wearing its ethics name.

Discipline: consistent adherence to rules, procedures and standards, even under pressure. The bookkeeper who writes up journals daily, follows the documentation procedure every time, and refuses shortcuts in a busy week shows discipline.

Transparency: openness — decisions and records visible to those entitled to see them. Statements that disclose fully, prices displayed honestly, no hidden charges: stakeholders can see what is actually happening.

Accountability: answering for one's decisions and actions. The manager who explains a loss to the owner rather than burying it, the treasurer who accounts for every rand at the meeting — accountability is reporting back, and accepting consequences.

Fairness: treating all parties justly and without favouritism — employees paid what was agreed, customers charged what was advertised, suppliers paid on time, no advantage taken of the weaker party.

Sustainability: conducting business so that it can endure — using resources responsibly, considering the community and environment, refusing profit today that destroys the base of profit tomorrow.

Responsible management: competent, careful stewardship of the business's resources — planning, controlling waste, insuring assets, making decisions with the business's long-term interest at heart.

Note the overlaps deliberately: leadership feeds discipline; transparency enables accountability. Scenarios often admit more than one characteristic — the skill is choosing the BEST fit and defending it.

Pause here for this section's questions — a characteristic or its example is presented in each. Match definition to name, exactly as paired above.

## Subtopic: Ethics Under Pressure — the Scenarios That Matter

Definitions earn some marks; applied judgement earns the rest. Work three scenarios at examination depth.

Scenario one: the owner instructs the bookkeeper to leave some cash sales out of the records "to reduce tax". Identify every party and every characteristic breached. The owner fails leadership — instructing dishonesty from the top — and fairness, because the missing tax shifts the burden onto honest taxpayers. The bookkeeper who complies fails discipline and accountability, and destroys the one professional asset a bookkeeper has: the reliability of their records. The full answer also states the consequence: records that are false in one place are untrustworthy everywhere, and discovery brings penalties, criminal exposure and the end of the business's credibility with its bank.

Scenario two: a shop notices expired stock and re-prices it to sell quickly without disclosure. Transparency fails — the customer cannot see what is actually being sold; fairness fails — advantage is taken of the buyer's ignorance; responsible management fails too, because the short-term gain trades away customer trust, the business's least replaceable asset.

Scenario three: a business dumps waste in the river behind its premises because disposal is expensive. Sustainability, directly — profit today at the cost of the environment and community that the business depends on tomorrow — with responsible management and leadership implicated in the decision.

Extract the pattern: ethical failures are almost always a trade — a small, certain, immediate gain purchased with a large, uncertain, delayed loss. Trust, once spent, does not restock.

Stop for this section's questions now: each is a scenario in miniature. Name the best-fit characteristic, and be ready to defend the choice against the near-misses.

## Subtopic: The Exam Method for Ethics Questions

Ethics questions reward structure as much as insight. Build the four-move method.

Move one: identify the ethical issue in plain words — what is being done, and to whom? "Cash sales are being deliberately excluded from the records to evade tax."

Move two: name the characteristic — or characteristics — at stake, choosing the strongest fit. State it as a claim: "This breaches transparency and fairness."

Move three: argue the link in one or two sentences, definition to scenario: "Transparency requires records that show what actually happened; excluding sales makes the records deliberately incomplete. Fairness fails because the unpaid tax becomes other taxpayers' burden."

Move four: consequence and correction — what follows if the behaviour continues, and what should happen instead: "The business risks penalties and prosecution, and its records lose all credibility. All sales must be recorded; the code of ethics must be applied from the owner downward."

Discipline yourself on two common errors. First: moralising without naming — "this is wrong and bad" earns nothing; the characteristic must be NAMED and DEFINED in the argument. Second: naming without applying — listing "transparency, accountability, fairness" like a shopping list earns little; each name must be tied to a fact of the scenario.

The final questions of this part are with you now. Run all four moves on each: issue, name, argument, consequence — the full sentence structure, every time.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now let us take ethics out of the textbook and put it back where you already live with it every day — the taxi, the till, the team. You know this material; we are just going to organise what you know.

## Subtopic: The Captain Picture

Think about the best sports team captain you have ever seen — school team, national side, doesn't matter. Watch what makes the team follow them, and you will find all seven characteristics hiding in plain sight.

The captain trains hardest and never asks the team for anything they will not do themselves — that is leadership: the example set from the front. They arrive on time, every practice, whether they feel like it or not — discipline: doing the right thing consistently, not occasionally. When selections are made, the captain explains them openly instead of whispering in corners — transparency. After a lost match, they stand in front of the team and take their share of the blame instead of pointing fingers — accountability. They give the new player the same chance as their best friend — fairness. They refuse to win by injuring opponents or cheating, because next season still has to be played — that is sustainability: never winning today in a way that poisons tomorrow. And they look after the kit, the field, the fixtures — the whole machinery of the team — responsible management.

One captain, seven characteristics, and you have known them all along. A business is a team with money in the middle — same characteristics, higher stakes.

Quick check before we carry on — the questions coming to you right now describe a captain's action. Name the characteristic it shows, straight from the picture.

## Subtopic: Ethics at the Till

Now three moments from ordinary township and mall business life. For each, do not ask "is this wrong?" — you will feel the answer instantly. Ask instead: WHICH characteristic is being tested? That is the exam skill.

Moment one: the cashier gives you change for R50 when you paid with R100, and you are already out the door when you notice. You walk back; the manager checks the till, finds the extra R50, apologises and pays out. Which characteristics passed the test? Accountability — the shop answered for the error; fairness — you got what was yours; and transparency, because the till could be checked at all. Notice how internal control and ethics worked together: the till record made honesty provable.

Moment two: a builder offers a "cash price, no paper" to a customer — cheaper, but no receipt, no record, no comeback if the roof leaks. What is being traded away? Transparency first — the deal is designed to be invisible; accountability second — no record means nobody can be held to anything; and there is unfairness downstream, because untaxed cash work shifts the burden to those who declare honestly.

Moment three: a spaza owner is offered cheap stock that everybody knows fell off a truck. Cheaper stock, better profit, and — think it through — the trade: one bargain today against the shop's whole name in the community tomorrow. Sustainability and responsible management both answer NO, because the shop's real asset is not the stock on the shelf; it is the trust of the street it stands on.

Your questions for this part are up now — three tills, three moments. Name the characteristic under test in each, the way we just did.

## Subtopic: Why Good Ethics Is Good Business

Close with the argument you can carry into any essay question: ethics is not the tax a business pays on top of profit — it is an engine of profit, running quietly.

Run the logic through the spaza again. The shop that gives correct change every single time gets something no advertising can buy: customers who do not check their change. That is trust, and trust brings feet through the door daily. The shop whose owner keeps honest records can show a bank exactly what it earns — and honest records get loans that memory and shoeboxes never will. The shop that pays its bread supplier on time, every time, gets bread on credit in a hard month when others get cash-only terms. Every characteristic converts into an advantage: fairness into loyal customers, transparency into credit, discipline into records that pass any inspection, sustainability into a business that is still standing in ten years.

And the reverse compounds too, like a debt: one discovered dishonesty makes every past figure suspect. Nobody re-trusts a till, or a set of books, halfway.

So when the exam asks you to "discuss the importance of a code of ethics", you have a two-sided answer that beats any list: ethics protects the business from the costs of dishonesty, AND earns the business the profits of being trusted. Name the characteristics as you go, tie each to its business advantage, and you are writing at the top of the mark scheme.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: each gives you a business behaving well or badly — name the characteristic, then name the business consequence that follows. Two names per answer, and the marks are yours. Well played today.
