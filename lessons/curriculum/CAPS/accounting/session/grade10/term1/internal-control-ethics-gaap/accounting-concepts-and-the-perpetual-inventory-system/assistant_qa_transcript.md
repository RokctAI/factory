**Thandi:** Class questions! The big one first: why does every sale need TWO entries under the perpetual system? Writing the R500 sale feels like enough — the R300 cost entry seems like double work.

**Tutor:** It feels like double work until you ask what each entry knows that the other does not. The R500 entry records what the customer paid — income. But the shop also became poorer by one pair of shoes, and those shoes had a cost: three hundred rand of stock walked out the door. If only the sale is recorded, the books believe the shop still owns shoes it has already sold, and nobody can say what any sale actually earned. The second entry — cost of sales out of Trading Stock — keeps the stock account truthful and makes the profit on every single sale visible immediately: five hundred in, three hundred out, two hundred earned. One entry tells you money arrived; two entries tell you whether the business is actually winning.

***

**Thandi:** Next: a learner wrote that trade discount is 'an expense of the seller' and lost the mark. What's the correct way to think about it?

**Tutor:** The correct thought is: trade discount never becomes an amount in the books at all. When a supplier lists a shelf at one thousand rand but invoices the bulk buyer at eight hundred and fifty, the transaction that happened is a sale at eight hundred and fifty — full stop. The listed price was an invitation, not a transaction; nothing was earned and then given away. Contrast the settlement discount: there, a real debt of two thousand rand existed in the books, and accepting nineteen hundred to settle it early means the business genuinely gave up one hundred recorded rand — an expense called discount allowed. The test to write in the margin: was the reduction BEFORE the amount was ever recorded, or did it reduce an amount ALREADY in the books? Before — invisible. Already recorded — it must be accounted for.

***

**Thandi:** This one comes from the fridge picture: if the perpetual system is so much better, why does the periodic system even exist?

**Tutor:** Because the counter has a cost, and for some businesses that cost once outweighed the benefit. Recording the cost of goods at every single sale demands either technology — barcodes, point-of-sale systems — or clerical effort on every transaction. A café selling hundreds of small mixed items with no scanners cannot sensibly write cost of sales per coffee; it counts stock periodically and works backward. Historically, before barcodes, most retailers had no choice. The syllabus teaches the perpetual system because modern tills have made the counter nearly free, and because it is the system with control built in — the one where a stocktake checks rather than discovers. But keep the principle: systems are chosen by weighing information benefits against recording costs, and that trade-off thinking is itself an accounting skill.

***

**Thandi:** Last one: financial versus managerial accounting — will the exam really ask about it, and what's the shortest safe answer?

**Tutor:** It appears regularly, usually for a handful of marks, and the shortest safe answer is the four-contrast frame delivered in full sentences. Financial accounting reports the PAST, follows GAAP, produces standardised statements, and serves parties OUTSIDE the business. Managerial accounting looks to the FUTURE, follows no external rules, takes any useful format, and serves decision-makers INSIDE the business. Then anchor it with one example pair: last year's profit — financial; next year's second branch — managerial. Four contrasts and one example is full marks; a single vague sentence about 'different types of accounting' is none. Learn it as the report card and the game plan, and the formal version writes itself.
