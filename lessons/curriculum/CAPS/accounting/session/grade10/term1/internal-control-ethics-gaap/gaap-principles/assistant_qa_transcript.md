**Thandi:** Class questions! The evergreen one: historical cost and prudence still blur for half the class — both keep values low, so how do we tell them apart under exam pressure?

**Tutor:** Test the direction of the reasoning, not the size of the number. Historical cost is not trying to keep values low at all — it is loyal to the DOCUMENTED price, whichever way the market moves; if land crashed below what was paid, historical cost alone would still show the purchase price, upward modesty and downward stubbornness alike. Prudence only wakes when UNCERTAINTY enters: a doubtful debt, damaged stock, an estimate to choose — and then it commands the cautious side: expect the loss, never the hoped-for gain. So ask the scenario one question: is the accountant holding to an old proven price, or making a careful call about something unsure? Proven price — grandfather, historical cost. Uncertain call — grandmother, prudence.

***

**Thandi:** Next: who actually decides what counts as 'material'? A few learners want a rand amount to memorise.

**Tutor:** No such amount exists, deliberately — materiality is relative to the business and to the decision at hand. Forty-five rand is nothing to a supermarket chain and genuinely something to a one-person tuck shop. The working test is always: if this item were left out or lumped together, would a reader of the statements decide anything differently? No — immaterial; simplify and move on. Yes — material; disclose it properly and separately. In examinations the scale is made obvious — a stapler against a delivery vehicle — precisely so you can apply the test rather than a memorised threshold. Learn the QUESTION, not a number.

***

**Thandi:** This one comes with feeling: the owner IS the business for a sole trader — her money bought the stock! Why must the books pretend they are strangers?

**Tutor:** Legally she is right: a sole trader and the owner are one person, and she may take from the till whenever she pleases. But the BOOKS separate them for a reason that serves her: the profit figure must measure the SHOP. If her groceries, school fees and airtime dissolve into the shop's expenses, the profit line answers no question at all — a thriving shop with a spending owner becomes indistinguishable from a failing shop with a frugal one. Recording her takings as drawings keeps the scoreboard honest, so she can see what her business truly earns — and so can a bank when she needs it. Separate books, not separate lives: the entity rule is bookkeeping's kindness to the owner, disguised as strictness.

***

**Thandi:** Last one: why does matching forbid recording expenses simply when they are paid? Cash-in, cash-out feels so much simpler.

**Tutor:** Simpler, and it makes the profit figure lie. Take insurance of twelve thousand rand paid in March for a full year, with the books closing in June: cash thinking dumps all twelve thousand into this year, crushing this year's profit, while next year enjoys nine months of cover it never paid for — both years misstated by one banking date. Matching pins each cost to the period it SERVED: four months' worth this year, the rest carried as prepaid into next. The same discipline runs the other way with wages owed at year end — worked this year, paid next, but THIS year's expense. Cash timing is an accident; matching makes each year's profit a fair fight between that year's income and that year's costs. That fairness is what the statements exist to deliver.
