**Thandi:** Class questions! First: how do we remember which side of Debtors Control things go on? Half of us are still guessing.

**Tutor:** Stop memorising sides and start asking the account's own question: after this event, do debtors owe us MORE, or LESS? Debtors are an asset — money owed to the business — and assets grow on the debit side. So credit sales, which grow what the street owes, sit on the debit side. Payments received, returns accepted, bad debts written off — every one shrinks what the street owes, so every one sits on the credit side. One question, asked every time, replaces the whole memory game — and it survives exam pressure, because it is reasoning, not recall.

***

**Thandi:** Next — the entry that feels wrong to everyone: the bad debt. Nothing was received, the money is simply GONE — and yet the account goes DOWN as if something good happened?

**Tutor:** The credit side of Debtors Control does not mean good news — it means the street owes us less, for whatever reason. When nine hundred rand is written off, the business stops claiming an asset it will never collect: the claim is removed from debtors, and the loss lands honestly in Bad Debts, an expense. No cash moved; an asset was buried and the funeral recorded. Prudence demands exactly this: the alternative — keeping dead debts on the books — flatters the asset total and misleads everyone who reads it. Less owed is less owed, whether by payment or by burial; the account records the direction, not the mood.

***

**Thandi:** This one is sharp: if the pages and the control line must always agree, why keep both? One of them looks like wasted ink.

**Tutor:** Their value lies precisely in the duplication — because they are built along different roads. The pages take the daily road: each transaction entered person by person as it happens. The control line takes the monthly road: column totals from the journals, posted once. Same facts, two independent journeys. A record kept once can be quietly wrong forever; two records that must agree audit each other automatically at every month end. When the counts match, both are trustworthy; when they differ, the difference itself is the alarm and often the clue — a gap of exactly nine hundred rand points straight at the nine-hundred-rand entry that made one journey and missed the other. That monthly handshake is the backbone of the reconciliation work you will meet in every grade from here to matric.

***

**Thandi:** Last one: do we now have to learn the whole Creditors Control machine from scratch?

**Tutor:** You already own it — stand the debtors machine in front of a mirror. Creditors are the street the business OWES, a liability, so the balance lives on the credit side, and the standing question flips to: do we owe more, or less? Credit purchases grow the debt — credit the account. Payments to suppliers and returns of goods shrink it — debit the account. The architecture is untouched: personal pages in the Creditors Ledger, monthly totals into the control line, and the same handshake — the list of creditors must equal the control balance. Learn one street thoroughly, flip every side, and the second street is free.
