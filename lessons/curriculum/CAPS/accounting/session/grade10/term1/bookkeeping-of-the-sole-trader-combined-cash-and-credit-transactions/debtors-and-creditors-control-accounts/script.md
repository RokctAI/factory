Welcome back, student. Today we build one of the most examinable tools in bookkeeping: the Debtors Control account, with its twin, the Creditors Control account. By the end of this lesson you will post real journal totals into a control account, balance it, and — most importantly — explain what that balance must agree with.

## Subtopic: What a Control Account Is and Why We Need One

Picture a business that sells on credit to forty customers. Each customer — each debtor — has a personal account in a separate book called the Debtors Ledger, showing exactly what that one person owes. Forty debtors, forty little accounts.

Now the owner asks a simple question: how much do all my debtors owe me in total? Adding forty accounts every time would be slow and error-prone. So the general ledger keeps one summary account, the Debtors Control account, which records the totals of everything that happened to debtors as a group. One account, one balance, one answer to the owner's question.

Here is the principle you must hold onto for the whole lesson: whatever is posted to the individual debtors' accounts in detail must be posted to the control account in total. Detail in the Debtors Ledger, totals in the control account — two views of the same facts.

That gives us the built-in check that makes this topic the backbone of reconciliation questions in every grade: at month end, the balance of the Debtors Control account must equal the total of the list of individual debtors' balances. If the summary and the detail disagree, an error exists, and it must be hunted down. This is internal control in action — the books policing themselves.

Which side does what? Debtors are an asset: they owe us money. So the control account increases on the debit side when debtors owe us more, and decreases on the credit side when they owe us less. Keep asking one question for every entry: did this event make debtors owe us more, or less?

## Subtopic: Posting the Debit Side — When Debtors Owe Us More

Let us work with real June figures for our business. The Debtors Journal — the book that records credit sales — has a total of R48 200 for the month. The Cash Receipts Journal's debtors column shows R31 500 received from debtors. The Debtors Allowances Journal shows R2 300 of goods returned by debtors, and we must also write off a debtor's debt of R900 as a bad debt.

Start with the debit side. Suppose the account opened on 1 June with a balance of R15 400 — that is what debtors owed us from May, an asset balance, sitting on the debit side as "Balance b/d".

Now the credit sales. When we sell goods on credit, the debtor owes us more, so the Debtors Control account is debited. We post the total of the Debtors Journal — the whole R48 200 — as one entry on the debit side, with the contra account named Sales (and Cost of Sales handled separately under the perpetual system, but the control account itself receives the selling-price total).

Pause on a classic pitfall: learners sometimes post individual invoices to the control account. Never. Individual invoices go to the personal accounts in the Debtors Ledger. The control account receives only the journal total. That is the entire meaning of the word control.

So far our debit side holds R15 400 plus R48 200. Debtors, as a group, have owed us as much as R63 600 during June. Now we deal with everything that reduced that debt.

## Subtopic: Posting the Credit Side — When Debtors Owe Us Less

Three things in our example made debtors owe us less, so all three are posted on the credit side.

First, payments received. The Cash Receipts Journal has a column dedicated to money received from debtors, and its June total is R31 500. When a debtor pays, their debt shrinks, so we credit Debtors Control with R31 500, contra account Bank.

Second, returns. Some debtors sent goods back or were given an allowance for damaged items — recorded in the Debtors Allowances Journal, total R2 300. A return cancels part of the original credit sale, so the debtor owes us less: credit Debtors Control with R2 300, contra account Debtors Allowances.

Third, the bad debt. One debtor simply cannot pay R900, and the business gives up hope of collecting. We remove the amount from debtors by crediting Debtors Control with R900, and the contra account is Bad Debts, an expense. Note the logic: writing off a debt does not mean the money was received — it means we absorb the loss and stop pretending the asset exists. This is also the prudent thing to do: we must not overstate our assets.

Now balance the account, exactly as you balance any ledger account. Total the debit side: R15 400 plus R48 200 gives R63 600. Total the credit side: R31 500 plus R2 300 plus R900 gives R34 700. The difference — R63 600 minus R34 700 — is R28 900. We write R28 900 on the credit side as "Balance c/d" to make both sides equal, and bring it down on the debit side on 1 July as "Balance b/d".

The debit balance of R28 900 tells the owner: at the end of June, debtors as a group owe the business R28 900.

## Subtopic: The Agreement Check and the Creditors Mirror Image

We said the control account polices the books. Here is how the check works in practice. The bookkeeper takes the Debtors Ledger, lists every individual debtor's balance, and adds the list. That total must come to exactly R28 900 — the same figure as the control account balance.

What if the list totals R29 800 instead? Then somewhere, detail and summary went different ways: perhaps an invoice was posted to a personal account but left out of the journal total, perhaps a receipt was posted twice, perhaps a casting error in a journal column. The disagreement does not tell you what the error is — it tells you that one exists and roughly where to look. This discipline is exactly what Grade 11 and 12 reconciliation questions examine, so build the habit now.

Finally, meet the mirror image: the Creditors Control account. Creditors are people we owe — a liability — so everything flips. The balance sits on the credit side. Credit purchases, from the Creditors Journal, make us owe more: credit the account. Payments to creditors and returns to suppliers make us owe less: debit the account. Its balance must agree with the list of balances from the Creditors Ledger. If you truly understand the debtors version, you get the creditors version free of charge — just reverse every side.

Let us verify our method one last time. For any transaction, ask: does it change what debtors owe us, or what we owe creditors? More owed to us — debit Debtors Control. Less owed to us — credit it. Post totals, never individual entries. Balance the account. Check it against the list. That is the whole machine.
