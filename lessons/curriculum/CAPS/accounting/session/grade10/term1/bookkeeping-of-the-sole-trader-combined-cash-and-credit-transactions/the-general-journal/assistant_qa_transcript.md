**Thandi:** Questions from the class! First: writing off Mrs Jacobs's R900 feels like giving up — the shop just accepts losing money and writes a sad little entry? Why not keep the debt on the books in case she comes back?

**Tutor:** Because the books' first duty is truth, not hope. Keeping the R900 as an asset means the shop's statements claim money it has no realistic prospect of touching — assets overstated, the owner misled, and any bank reading the books deceived alongside him. Prudence — the GAAP principle you met — commands the harder honesty: anticipate the loss the moment it becomes probable. And note that writing off is an accounting decision, not a legal surrender: if Mrs Jacobs reappears next year, money in hand, the recovery is simply recorded then as income — bad debts recovered, a pleasant entry you will meet in later grades. The write-off keeps this year's books honest; it does not forbid next year's good news.

***

**Thandi:** Next: the interest entries confused a few of us because no money moved. If Adams hasn't PAID the R45, how can the shop record income it hasn't received?

**Tutor:** Because income is EARNED, not necessarily received — the same principle that let Sales be credited on invoice day. The credit agreement says overdue accounts bear interest; sixty days passed; the R45 is now legitimately owed. The earning happened — so Interest Income records it, and Adams's page grows by R45, converting the earning into a bigger receivable. When he eventually pays, THAT day's entry is cash in and page down — the receiving, recorded separately from the earning, in the receipts journal where money belongs. Keep the two moments distinct in every topic: the GJ handled the debt growing; the CRJ will handle the cash arriving. One event of earning, one event of receiving, each in its own book on its own day.

***

**Thandi:** This one made the class laugh, but it's serious: if Thabo owns the whole shop, why does taking his own stock home need an entry at all? Surely he can eat his own groceries!

**Tutor:** He can — nobody is policing his supper; the entry is not permission, it is measurement. Remember whose story the books tell: the BUSINESS'S, kept separate from the owner by the entity rule. If stock quietly walks home unrecorded, the books still claim it sits on the shelf — and at stocktake, R350 has vanished with no explanation, indistinguishable from theft. Worse, the shop's profit calculation would eventually absorb the loss as if trading had failed. The drawings entry keeps every story straight: the shelf's record drops honestly, the loss lands nowhere — because it is not a loss, it is the owner collecting part of his equity in groceries. And at cost, always: he took goods, not the profit margin a customer would have paid.

***

**Thandi:** Last one: for the correction entry, a learner asked the practical thing — in the exam, how do I find WHAT the correcting entry should be without getting tangled?

**Tutor:** Use the two-sentence method, written down before any entry. Sentence one: what WAS done — read the error exactly as it happened: Trading Stock was debited R250. Sentence two: what SHOULD have been done — Stationery should have been debited R250. Now compare, leg by leg: the credit leg appears in both sentences unchanged, so it is healthy — leave it. The debit leg differs — Trading Stock stands where Stationery belongs — so the correction moves precisely that amount between precisely those two accounts: debit Stationery, credit Trading Stock, R250, narration stating the correction. Two sentences, one comparison, one entry. The tangle only comes from skipping the sentences and diving at the ledger — write the WAS and the SHOULD, and the entry writes itself.
