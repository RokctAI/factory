# Part 1 — Expert

Bookkeeping proper begins today. The first book of first entry every sole trader keeps is the Cash Receipts Journal — the CRJ — the daily record of every rand the business receives. By the end of this session you will rule the journal correctly, record cash sales under the perpetual inventory system, handle capital and other receipts, and total the journal so that it proves its own accuracy. The format itself carries marks; the discipline carries the whole term.

## Subtopic: The Job of the CRJ and Its Source Documents

Fix the journal's single job in one sentence: the CRJ records ALL money received by the business, in date order, for one month. Money paid out never enters it — payments belong to the Cash Payments Journal. One direction of flow per book; that separation is the first piece of order bookkeeping imposes on a busy month.

No entry without evidence — the standing rule. The CRJ's source documents: the cash register roll, totalled at the end of each day, proves the day's cash sales; a duplicate receipt is issued for money received outside the till — the owner depositing capital, a tenant paying rent; and the duplicate deposit slip proves what went to the bank. Each document's number is recorded in the journal's document column, so any line can be traced back to its proof.

Now the column that confuses learners in week one, mastered here instead: the ANALYSIS OF RECEIPTS column. A business does not sprint to the bank with every rand as it arrives; the day's takings are gathered and banked together — ideally daily, as internal control demands. The analysis column captures each amount AS IT IS RECEIVED during the day; when the collection is banked, the TOTAL of that day's analysis amounts moves into the Bank column as one figure. Analysis answers "what arrived, item by item?"; Bank answers "what was deposited, and when?" On a day with one receipt banked immediately, the two amounts sit on the same line; on a busy Saturday, five analysis amounts become one Bank figure.

Pause here — questions on the journal's purpose, its documents and the analysis column are with you now. Answer with the definitions as stated.

## Subtopic: Recording Cash Sales — Two Truths per Sale

Our working business for the month: Thabo's Trading, selling goods at a mark-up of 25 percent on cost. On the 3rd, the register roll shows cash sales of R1 500.

Recall the perpetual inventory system from the previous lesson: every sale writes two truths — what the customer paid, and what the goods had cost. The CRJ is built for both. Enter the date and the document number — CRR 1. Details: Sales. The R1 500 goes into the analysis column, and — banked the same day — into the Bank column, and into the SALES column.

Now the second truth. At 25 percent on cost, goods selling for R1 500 cost R1 200 — divide the selling price by 1,25 to strip the mark-up. That R1 200 is entered in the COST OF SALES column on the same line. It is not money moving today — no cash left the business at the till — it is the stock leaving, recorded at cost, so the Trading Stock account stays truthful. One line in the CRJ therefore feeds four accounts at month end: Bank, Sales, Cost of Sales, and Trading Stock.

Verify the mark-up arithmetic both ways, the habit that catches errors: R1 200 cost times 1,25 gives R1 500 selling price — consistent. Gross profit on the day: R300, visible immediately, exactly as the perpetual system promises.

Two pitfalls to bury now. First: computing cost of sales as 25 percent OFF the selling price — R1 125 — wrong, because the mark-up was ON COST; always divide by one plus the mark-up rate. Second: leaving the Cost of Sales column empty "because no cash moved" — the column exists precisely to record the non-cash truth of every sale.

Stop for this section's questions now — register-roll amounts and mark-ups. Strip the mark-up by division, and place every figure in its correct column.

## Subtopic: Receipts That Are Not Sales

Money arrives for reasons other than selling stock, and the CRJ receives them all — through the SUNDRY ACCOUNTS column.

On the 5th, the owner, T. Mokoena, deposits R50 000 as additional capital. Receipt 41 is issued. Details: Capital. The amount enters the analysis column, the Bank column — and then, because no dedicated column exists for capital, it goes into the Sundry accounts amount column, with "Capital" written in the sundry details column. The sundry column is the journal's guest room: any receipt without a column of its own lodges there, NAMED, so month-end posting knows exactly which account it belongs to.

On the 12th, the tenant who rents the back room pays R2 400. Receipt 42, details: Rent Income, through analysis, Bank, and Sundries: Rent income. Same machinery.

Discipline point: why do Sales and Cost of Sales earn dedicated columns while Capital and Rent income do not? Frequency. Cash sales happen daily — a dedicated column lets a month of sales be posted to the ledger as ONE total. Capital arrives rarely; each sundry item is posted individually. Columns are efficiency decisions: high-traffic items get their own lane; occasional visitors are handled by name. When you later meet a business with daily commission income, you will widen the journal with a Commission column — the format serves the business, not the other way round.

Note what NEVER appears in a Grade 10 CRJ this term: money received from debtors — that column joins the journal when credit transactions arrive in the coming weeks. Build the format cleanly now and the extension will bolt straight on.

The questions on this section are in front of you now: for each receipt, name the document, the columns struck, and the sundry detail where one is needed.

## Subtopic: Totalling, Cross-Casting and Month-End Discipline

A journal is finished only when it is totalled and PROVEN. At month end, rule off and total every money column. Thabo's Trading, for our four transactions plus a month-end cash sale of R3 000 (cost R2 400) on the 28th: Bank column — R1 500 plus R50 000 plus R2 400 plus R3 000: R56 900. Sales column: R4 500. Cost of Sales: R3 600. Sundries: R52 400.

Now the proof — the CROSS-CAST. The Bank column total must equal the sum of the totals of the columns that feed it: Sales R4 500 plus Sundries R52 400 gives R56 900 — equal to the Bank total. The journal balances internally. Note carefully that Cost of Sales stands OUTSIDE the cross-cast: it is not money received; it is the parallel stock record. Cross-cast before any posting, every month: a journal that does not cross-cast carries an error that would otherwise be laundered into the ledger and hunted for hours later.

The totals then post to the general ledger — the mechanics belong to the posting lesson ahead, but see the destination now: Bank total debited to Bank; Sales total credited to Sales; Cost of Sales total debited to Cost of Sales and credited to Trading Stock; each sundry item posted by name. One month, a handful of postings — the column design paying its rent.

Close with the internal-control thread that runs through this journal: takings banked daily and intact — the analysis column exposes any gap between what arrived and what reached the bank; documents numbered and sequential — a missing receipt number is a question; and the person recording the CRJ ideally not the person handling the cash. The journal is not paperwork after the fact; it is the cash control system in operation.

The final questions of this part are with you now: total, cross-cast, and state what each total will do at month end — precisely as laid out here.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now let us build the same journal again — but starting from a shoebox of paper and a simple need: where did the money come from? No formats first. The format will invent itself.

## Subtopic: The Money-In Diary

Imagine Thabo runs his shop with a simple habit: every time money comes in, he drops the proof in a box — till slips, receipts he wrote out, deposit slips from the bank. By Friday the box is chaos. His sister, who did Accounting, gives him one piece of advice: "Keep a diary. One page a month. Every time money arrives, one line: the date, the paper that proves it, who it came from, how much."

That diary IS the Cash Receipts Journal. Nothing more frightening than that: a money-IN diary, in date order, with proof attached. Money going OUT gets its own separate diary — never mixed, because one question per book keeps every answer findable.

Now watch the diary grow its columns naturally. After a week, Thabo notices he keeps writing the same word: Sales, Sales, Sales. So he gives sales its own column — now he can just drop the number in, and adding up the month takes seconds. But some money is NOT sales — the day he put his own R50 000 savings in, the day the tenant paid rent. Those are visitors, not regulars — so the diary keeps one open column for them, where he writes the amount AND what it was. The regulars get lanes; the visitors get named. That is the whole architecture of the journal: laziness, organised brilliantly.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the diary are coming to you right now. For each amount that arrives, say: regular lane, or named visitor?

## Subtopic: The Double Line — What the Customer Pays and What the Shelf Loses

One more column needs explaining, and it is the cleverest one. When Thabo sells for cash, TWO things happen in the same breath — and only one of them is money.

Saturday: R1 500 in the till from the day's sales. That is the money truth — into the money columns. But walk to the shelf: the goods that left had COST Thabo something. His shop marks everything up by 25 percent on cost, so goods that sold for R1 500 went onto the shelf at R1 200. To find that number, do not take 25 percent off — that is the classic slip. Reverse the mark-up: R1 500 divided by 1,25 equals R1 200. Check it forwards: R1 200 plus a quarter of R1 200 — plus R300 — is R1 500. Lands exactly.

That R1 200 gets its own column on the same line: cost of sales. No cash moved for it today — it is the shelf's diary entry, riding along with the till's. And look what the pair of numbers hands Thabo free of charge: R1 500 in, R1 200 off the shelf — he made R300 gross profit TODAY, and he knows it today, not at year end. That is the perpetual system from last lesson, living inside one journal line.

And the analysis column? That is just the counting-tray: money arrives at different moments through the day — the tray collects each amount as it comes; when Thabo banks the tray, the tray's total becomes one Bank entry. What arrived, then what got banked. Two questions, two columns.

Your questions for this part are up now: each gives a till total and the mark-up. Divide to find the shelf's number, and place both truths on the line.

## Subtopic: The Month-End Handshake

Last skill: proving the month before trusting it. At month end, Thabo adds up every column. And here is the beautiful check hiding in the design: every rand in the Bank column had to come from SOMEWHERE — either the Sales lane or the named-visitors lane. So when he adds Sales total plus Sundries total, the answer MUST equal the Bank total. R4 500 plus R52 400 — R56 900. Bank column: R56 900. The columns shake hands.

If the handshake fails — say the columns give R56 900 but Bank shows R57 200 — the month has a mistake, and the design even tells you what kind: an amount reached the Bank column without being explained in a lane, or a lane amount never made it to Bank. Hunt the difference — R300 — and you will usually find a line with a number on one side and a blank on the other. The journal catches its own errors BEFORE they infect the ledger. (And notice: the cost of sales column sits out of the handshake — it is the shelf's diary, not money, so it never joins the money arithmetic.)

One habit to take from the whole lesson: this diary is also Thabo's protection. Takings banked every day, intact; every receipt numbered; the diary agreeing with the bank's own records. If ever a rand goes missing between the till and the bank, the analysis column points at the exact day. The CRJ is not admin — it is the shop watching over its own cash.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: total the columns, test the handshake, and if it fails, say what kind of error you are hunting. You have just built your first real book of accounts — from a shoebox. That deserves a proud walk home.
