**Thandi:** Class questions before we pack up! The one I heard most: why do assets increase on the debit side but liabilities increase on the credit side? It feels completely random.

**Tutor:** It feels random only until you see the equation as a seesaw. Assets sit on the left of A equals O plus L; owner's equity and liabilities sit on the right. The ledger simply copies that geometry: things on the left of the equation grow on the left side of their accounts — the debit side — and things on the right of the equation grow on the right side — the credit side. Decreases go opposite in each case. So the rule is not a list to memorise; it is one picture. Ask where the item lives in the equation, and the growing side names itself.

***

**Thandi:** Next: with the scooter bought for cash, the equation didn't change at all. So why can't we just leave it out of the books?

**Tutor:** Because the books record events, not merely net effects. Two real events happened: the business gained a scooter worth twenty-four thousand rand, and it lost twenty-four thousand rand of bank money. Skip the entries and your Vehicles account lies, your Bank account lies, and one day a trial balance or a stocktake exposes both. The equation staying still is the consequence of two equal moves cancelling inside assets — proof the recording worked, never a reason to skip it. Remember the order: entries first, equation check after.

***

**Thandi:** Here's a tricky one from the back row: when the business pays wages, whose pocket actually gets smaller?

**Tutor:** Follow the rand. Bank pays out one thousand eight hundred, so assets fall — that part everyone sees. But wages buy no asset in return; the work is consumed the moment it is done. So the loss must land somewhere, and it lands on the owner: the stake the business owes back to the owner is now worth less. That is why expenses live inside owner's equity, on its shrinking side — debit Wages — while the matching credit goes to Bank. And incomes are the same story reversed: every sale quietly grows the owner's stake, which is why income sits on the credit side.

***

**Thandi:** Last one: in a test, what's the fastest safe way to attack a transaction we've never seen before?

**Tutor:** Run the five steps, in order, every time. One: name the two accounts touched. Two: classify each — asset, owner's equity or liability, remembering incomes and expenses live inside owner's equity. Three: decide increase or decrease for each. Four: translate — assets up means debit; owner's equity and liabilities up means credit; decreases opposite. Five: confirm total debits equal total credits so the equation stays level. Speed comes from repetition, not from skipping — the learners who lose marks are the ones who jump straight to step four and guess. Do the five steps aloud in practice this week, and by the assessment they will run silently in about ten seconds.
