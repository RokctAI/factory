**Thandi:** Question time! The most-asked one by far: the owner works in the shop every single day. Why is the R2 000 he takes not simply his wage — a normal expense like any worker's pay?

**Tutor:** Because of who the owner IS to the business. An employee sells their labour to the business at an agreed price — the business receives work, pays a wage, and that wage is a genuine cost of operating. The owner stands on the other side of the counter entirely: the whole profit is already his reward, and the equity is already his money. When he transfers two thousand rand to himself, the business is not buying anything — the owner is collecting part of what is his. That is why drawings bypass the profit calculation and reduce capital directly. And notice the honest consequence you will meet in later years: if the owner wants to measure the shop against a real job, he should compare the shop's profit to the salary he COULD earn elsewhere — but that comparison lives in analysis, never as a fake wage inside the books of a sole trader.

***

**Thandi:** Next: a learner's older sibling has a textbook with a 'Purchases' account for buying stock — but our journal sends it straight to Trading Stock. Who is right?

**Tutor:** Both are right — for different systems, and the system is the choice this syllabus has made. The Purchases account belongs to the periodic inventory system: purchases pile up in their own account all year, and only a stocktake at the end reveals what was sold and what remains. Our syllabus runs the perpetual system: the Trading Stock account is updated at every transaction — loaded at cost when stock is bought, relieved at cost when goods are sold. That is why the CPJ has a Trading Stock column and no Purchases column, and why the CRJ writes cost of sales at every sale. If an older book crosses your path, translate: their Purchases entry is our Trading Stock debit. Same event, different bookkeeping era — and ours is the one the examiners mark.

***

**Thandi:** Here's a sharp one from the back: why restore the petty cash float with exactly the voucher total? Why not just top the tin up to R500 by counting the cash?

**Tutor:** Because the two methods answer to different masters. Topping up by counting the cash trusts the CASH — whatever is missing simply gets refilled, questions never asked; a tin leaking twenty rand a month would be quietly subsidised forever. Restoring by the VOUCHER total trusts the PAPER: the refill equals what the signed slips justify, not a cent more. Now watch the arithmetic police the tin: if vouchers total three hundred and sixty rand, the refill is three hundred and sixty — and the tin only returns to five hundred if the cash on hand was truly one hundred and forty. Any shortfall survives the refill and stands exposed, demanding an explanation. The imprest method turns every restoration into a miniature audit — which is exactly why the system has kept small cash honest for over a century.

***

**Thandi:** Last one: with EFTs and cards everywhere, is petty cash even worth learning? One learner says her mother's shop has no cash tin at all.

**Tutor:** The tin is fading in some businesses, but the PRINCIPLE it teaches is permanent, and the exam tests the principle. Small, frequent, low-value spending exists in every business — and it is precisely the money most likely to leak, because each amount feels too small to police. The imprest design answers that with three ideas that outlive the tin itself: a fixed float so the total at risk is capped; a voucher for every rand so spending explains itself; and restoration matched to vouchers so the records audit the cash automatically. A business running everything off a card applies the same trio — spending limits, slips for every swipe, monthly reconciliation. Master the tin and you have mastered the pattern; the technology underneath it can change as much as it likes.
