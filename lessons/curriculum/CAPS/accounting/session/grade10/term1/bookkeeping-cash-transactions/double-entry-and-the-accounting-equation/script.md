# Part 1 — Expert

Today we go to the very foundation of all accounting: the double-entry principle and the accounting equation. Every journal you will ever write, every ledger you will ever post, every statement you will ever prepare — all of it stands on what we build in this session.

## Subtopic: The Accounting Equation

Let us begin with a statement so simple it looks almost too obvious: everything a business owns was funded by somebody. Either the owner supplied the money, or someone outside the business did.

What the business owns, we call assets — the letter A. Vehicles, equipment, trading stock, money in the bank, debtors. What the owner supplied, we call owner's equity — the letter O. What outsiders supplied, we call liabilities — the letter L. Loans, and creditors we still have to pay.

Because every asset was funded from one of those two sources, we can write the accounting equation: A equals O plus L. Assets equal owner's equity plus liabilities. This is not a rule someone invented to torment learners — it is a statement of fact, true at every moment of a business's life. If it ever stops balancing, the books contain an error. Full stop.

From Grade 8 and 9 you already know that each ledger account has a debit side on the left and a credit side on the right. Here is how the equation connects to those sides: assets increase on the debit side; owner's equity and liabilities increase on the credit side. Decreases go on the opposite side in each case. Keep that mapping in front of you — we are about to use it repeatedly.

And the double-entry principle itself says: every transaction affects at least two accounts, with the total debits always equal to the total credits. Two entries, equal and opposite in effect, keeping the equation in balance. Let us now prove this with four real transactions.

Pause here — questions on the equation and the sides are with you now. State the mapping exactly: what grows on the debit side, what grows on the credit side, and why the equation must always hold.

## Subtopic: Transactions (a) and (b) — Capital and a Cash Asset Swap

Transaction (a): the owner deposits R100 000 into the business bank account as capital.

Think it through before touching debits and credits. What happened to assets? The business bank account grew by R100 000 — assets up. Where did the funding come from? The owner — so owner's equity grew by R100 000 too.

In equation form: A increases by R100 000, O increases by R100 000, L unchanged. Check the balance: the left side grew by R100 000, the right side grew by R100 000. The equation holds.

Now the double entry. Bank is an asset and it increased, so we debit Bank with R100 000. Capital is owner's equity and it increased, so we credit Capital with R100 000. One debit, one credit, equal amounts.

Notice something important about the business entity idea hiding here: the business received the money and the business now owes its existence to the owner. We record the business's view, never the owner's personal view.

Transaction (b): the business buys equipment for R30 000 and pays cash.

What happened? The business gained one asset, equipment, worth R30 000 — but it gave up another asset, money in the bank, of R30 000. This is an asset swap.

In equation form: A increases by R30 000 and decreases by R30 000 at the same time. The net effect on A is zero. O unchanged, L unchanged. The equation stays perfectly balanced — zero equals zero.

Here is the pitfall: learners see "no effect on the equation" and think "no entries needed". Wrong. There are always two entries. Equipment is an asset that increased: debit Equipment R30 000. Bank is an asset that decreased: credit Bank R30 000. Two entries inside A, cancelling each other in the equation, but both fully recorded.

Stop for this section's questions now — the capital deposit and the asset swap, effects and entries both. Reason each answer from what grew and what shrank.

## Subtopic: Transactions (c) and (d) — Bringing in Liabilities

Transaction (c): the business buys trading stock on credit for R12 000.

The business receives stock — an asset — but pays nothing today. Instead, it now owes a supplier. That supplier is a creditor, and creditors are a liability.

Equation effect: A increases by R12 000 because trading stock came in. L increases by R12 000 because creditors grew. O unchanged. Left side up R12 000, right side up R12 000 — balanced.

Double entry: Trading Stock is an asset increasing, so debit Trading Stock R12 000. Creditors Control is a liability increasing, and liabilities increase on the credit side, so credit Creditors Control R12 000.

Transaction (d): the business pays a creditor R5 000.

Now we settle part of that debt. Money leaves the bank: an asset decreases by R5 000. The debt shrinks: a liability decreases by R5 000.

Equation effect: A down R5 000, L down R5 000, O unchanged. Both sides fell by the same amount — still balanced.

Double entry: Creditors Control is a liability decreasing, and decreases go opposite to increases, so debit Creditors Control R5 000. Bank is an asset decreasing: credit Bank R5 000.

Pause and notice the pattern across all four transactions. Every single one produced exactly one debit and one credit of equal size. And every single one left the equation balanced — sometimes both sides moving up together, sometimes both down together, sometimes a shuffle inside one side. That is not luck. That is the design.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — the credit purchase and the payment to the creditor. Name both accounts, both directions, both sides, and confirm the equation's balance.

## Subtopic: Verifying the Whole Picture

Let us now add up everything and verify, the way you should at the end of any accounting-equation question.

Assets: Bank received R100 000, paid out R30 000, paid out R5 000 — leaving R65 000. Equipment R30 000. Trading stock R12 000. Total assets: R107 000.

Owner's equity: capital of R100 000.

Liabilities: creditors of R12 000 minus the R5 000 paid — leaving R7 000.

Now test the equation: does A equal O plus L? O plus L is R100 000 plus R7 000, which is R107 000. Assets are R107 000. The equation balances exactly. If your totals ever disagree, go back transaction by transaction and find the one where your debit and credit were unequal or on the wrong sides.

One more layer before we finish, because Grade 10 will demand it soon: where do incomes and expenses fit? They are not a fourth element — they are movements in owner's equity. Income earns profit for the owner, so income increases O and is recorded on the credit side. Expenses eat into the owner's profit, so expenses decrease O and are recorded on the debit side. When you meet a transaction like "paid wages R2 000", the analysis is: Bank (asset) down R2 000 — credit Bank; owner's equity down R2 000 via the Wages expense — debit Wages.

Your self-check routine for every transaction, from today until matric: one, name the two accounts affected. Two, classify each as asset, owner's equity or liability — remembering incomes and expenses live inside O. Three, decide increase or decrease for each. Four, apply the sides: assets up means debit; O and L up means credit. Five, confirm the equation still balances. Five steps, every time, no exceptions.

Master this and nothing in Accounting will ever be mysterious again — because everything else is just this, repeated.

The final questions of this part are with you now: run the five-step routine on each transaction, ending every answer with the equation check.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same foundation, built the way it actually makes sense — with pockets, jars and a kitchen table. No symbols until the very end.

## Subtopic: The Two-Pocket Rule

Start with something you own — say, your phone. Simple question: where did the money for it come from? Either it was YOUR money — saved birthday money, earnings — or it was SOMEONE ELSE'S money that you must still pay back — borrowed from your brother, owed to a lay-by. There is no third possibility. Everything anyone owns was paid for from one of two pockets: the own-money pocket or the owed-money pocket.

A business is exactly the same, just with bigger numbers. Everything the business OWNS — its bank money, stock, equipment — was funded either from the OWNER'S pocket or from an OUTSIDER'S pocket that must be repaid. Accounting just gives the three piles names: what is owned — assets. The owner's pocket — owner's equity. The owed pocket — liabilities.

And now the famous equation is not a formula at all — it is a sentence you already believe: what the business owns EQUALS the owner's pocket PLUS the owed pocket. A equals O plus L. It cannot stop being true, ever, for the same reason your phone cannot have been paid for out of thin air: every rand of owning has a source, and the sources only come in two kinds.

That is the whole secret of the equation. When the books "balance", they are just confirming that no rand appeared from nowhere and none vanished into nowhere.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the two pockets are coming to you right now: for each item, say what is owned and which pocket paid for it.

## Subtopic: Four Moves on the Kitchen Table

Now play the four transactions like moves on a kitchen table, with the three piles in front of you: OWNED, OWNER'S POCKET, OWED.

Move one: the owner puts R100 000 into the business. The OWNED pile grows by R100 000 — the bank money is real and sitting there. And where did it come from? The owner's pocket — so the OWNER'S POCKET pile grows by R100 000 too. Both sides of the table up by the same amount. Level.

Move two: the business buys equipment for R30 000 cash. Watch carefully — this all happens INSIDE the owned pile: R30 000 of bank money leaves, R30 000 of equipment arrives. The owned pile is worth exactly what it was; the other piles never moved. Level again — but hear this clearly: two real things still happened, and both get written down. The table not moving is the RESULT of two moves that cancel, never a reason to skip the writing.

Move three: stock worth R12 000 arrives on credit. The OWNED pile grows — real stock, on real shelves. But no one paid for it yet, so the OWED pile grows by the same R12 000. Both sides up together. Level.

Move four: the business pays that supplier R5 000. Bank money leaves the OWNED pile; the OWED pile shrinks by the same R5 000. Both sides down together. Level.

Four moves, and the table never tipped once — up together, shuffle inside, up together, down together. That levelness IS double entry: every move touches two places, equally.

Your questions for this part are up now: run each move on the table — which piles, which directions, and is the table still level?

## Subtopic: The Question That Never Fails

Last piece: the one question that answers every transaction you will ever meet: WHICH TWO PLACES, AND WHICH WAY?

Every event touches exactly two places on the table. Train the reflex on wages: the business pays R2 000 in wages. Place one: bank money — owned pile down. Place two — and here is the only new idea: the wages did not buy a THING; they bought work that is already used up. Spending like that eats quietly into the owner's pocket — because everything the business loses without gaining something owned must, in the end, come out of what the owner's stake is worth. So expenses live inside the owner's pocket, on its shrinking side. And incomes live there too, on its growing side — every sale quietly fattens the owner's stake.

That is why the books put expenses on the debit side and incomes on the credit side: they are just the owner's pocket, shrinking and growing, wearing work clothes.

So the full routine, plain words: name the two places. Say which way each moves. Check the table stays level. Then — only then — translate into the book language: owned things up, debit; owner's pocket and owed things up, credit; decreases opposite. The translation is the easy part once the table picture is right; every mark lost in this topic is lost by skipping the picture and guessing the words.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: for each event, two places, two directions, one level table — then the debits and credits will name themselves. This foundation now belongs to you; everything else in Accounting is this game, repeated with better scenery.
