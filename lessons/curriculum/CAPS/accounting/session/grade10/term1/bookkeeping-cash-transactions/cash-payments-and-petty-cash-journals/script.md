# Part 1 — Expert

Money out demands even tighter discipline than money in — paying is easier than earning, and errors in payments cost twice. Today we master the Cash Payments Journal — the CPJ — the book of first entry for all payments from the bank account, and its small companion, the Petty Cash Journal with its imprest system. By the end you will record the standard payments of a sole trader, separate expenses from drawings without hesitation, and run a petty cash float that polices itself.

## Subtopic: The CPJ — Job, Documents and Columns

The CPJ's single job mirrors the CRJ's: it records ALL payments made from the business bank account, in date order, for one month. Receipts never enter it. One direction per book, as before.

The source documents are the proofs of payment: historically the cheque counterfoil, today predominantly the EFT proof of payment, numbered in sequence. The journal's document column carries that number; the NAME column carries the PAYEE — the person or business the money went to. Note the difference from the CRJ, which recorded details of what the money was for; the CPJ names who was paid, because payments are traced payee first.

The money column is Bank — every payment reduces the bank balance, so every line strikes Bank. Analysis columns then sort the traffic: TRADING STOCK, because a trading business buys stock constantly; WAGES, because workers are paid every week; and SUNDRY ACCOUNTS for everything without a dedicated lane — rent expense, equipment, the owner's drawings — each named in the details column. Same architecture as the CRJ: regulars get lanes, visitors get named.

One perpetual-system point before the transactions, because it separates this syllabus from older textbooks: when this business buys stock, the amount goes to the TRADING STOCK column — the asset — not to a "Purchases" account. Under the perpetual inventory system the stock account is updated at every transaction: buying stock loads the shelf account directly at cost. There is no Purchases account in this system; if you inherit one from an older book, retire it today.

Pause here — questions on the CPJ's job, documents and column logic are with you now. Answer with the definitions, and place each item in its lane.

## Subtopic: The Standard Payments — and the Drawings Trap

Run the month for Thabo's Trading, transaction by transaction, columns named aloud.

On the 2nd, stock is bought from Metro Wholesalers for R8 000, paid by EFT 101. Name: Metro Wholesalers. Bank: R8 000. Trading Stock column: R8 000. The shelf account loads at cost — the perpetual system in action on the buying side.

On the 8th, wages of R1 500 are paid, EFT 102. Name: the employee or simply Wages. Bank R1 500; Wages column R1 500. An expense — money consumed keeping the business running — and it will repeat on the 28th: another R1 500 to the same column.

On the 15th, rent of R3 500 is paid to the landlord, EFT 103. No dedicated column — Sundries: R3 500, detail: Rent expense.

On the 20th, a new display fridge is bought for R6 000, EFT 104. Think before writing: is this an expense? No — the fridge is kept for use over years: an ASSET. Sundries R6 000, detail: Equipment. The distinction matters at year end: expenses reduce profit; assets do not — they sit on the books as possessions. Confusing them misstates both profit and the asset position.

On the 25th, the owner takes R2 000 for personal use, EFT 105. Here stands the trap of the whole lesson. This is NOT an expense — the business received nothing for this money; no service, no stock, no benefit. It is DRAWINGS: the owner withdrawing part of their own equity. Sundries R2 000, detail: Drawings. At year end, drawings never touch the profit calculation — they reduce the owner's capital directly. A business that buries drawings inside expenses understates its true profit and lies to itself about performance. The test for every payment: did the BUSINESS receive value? Stock or assets — yes, possessions. Wages, rent — yes, services consumed: expenses. Owner's personal use — no: drawings.

Stop for this section's questions now: classify each payment — asset, expense, or drawings — before naming its column, exactly in that order.

## Subtopic: Totals, the Cross-Cast, and What Posts Where

Month end: rule off, total, prove. Bank column: 8 000 plus 1 500 plus 3 500 plus 6 000 plus 2 000 plus 1 500 — R22 500. Trading Stock: R8 000. Wages: R3 000. Sundries: R11 500.

Cross-cast: the analysis columns must explain the Bank column. Trading Stock 8 000, plus Wages 3 000, plus Sundries 11 500 — R22 500. Equal. The journal proves itself, and only then may it post.

The posting picture, previewing the ledger lesson: Bank's total credits the Bank account — payments reduce the asset. The Trading Stock total debits Trading Stock — the shelf grew. Wages' total debits the Wages expense account. Each sundry item posts individually by name: Rent expense debited, Equipment debited, Drawings debited. One month, six postings, every one traceable to a numbered proof of payment.

And the internal control thread, which examiners attach to every payments question: payments made only against approved documents; EFT references numbered and sequential; the person who approves a payment not the person who captures it; and the bank statement reconciled monthly against the CPJ and CRJ — the independent record testing ours. The CPJ is where a business's money leaks if control is loose; the format's discipline is the first defence.

The questions on this section are in front of you now: total, cross-cast, and state each posting's destination and side.

## Subtopic: The Petty Cash Journal and the Imprest System

A business cannot EFT sixty rand for stamps — small payments need small cash, and small cash needs its own tight system: the petty cash float, recorded in the Petty Cash Journal.

The design is the IMPREST system, and its logic is elegant. The float is fixed — say R500. Every payment from the tin is exchanged for a numbered PETTY CASH VOUCHER stating what, who, how much, signed by the receiver. At any moment, cash in the tin PLUS vouchers must equal exactly R500 — the tin always adds up to its imprest amount, in cash or in paper. At month end, the vouchers are totalled — say stamps R60, taxi fare R85, tea and coffee R120, cleaning materials R95: R360 — and the float is RESTORED with a payment of exactly R360 from the bank, recorded in the CPJ, returning the tin to R500 for the new month.

See the control built into the design. The restoration amount always equals the vouchers, so the vouchers are audited every month by arithmetic. A tin that holds R95 cash and R360 of vouchers balances; a tin with R95 cash and R340 of vouchers is R65 short and says so immediately. The petty cashier — one named person, another division of duties — accounts by voucher for every rand.

The PCJ itself mirrors its big siblings: date, voucher number, details, a total column, and analysis columns for the regular small items — postage, transport, refreshments — with sundries for the rest. Totals post monthly: each expense column to its expense account.

The final questions of this part are with you now: run the tin — vouchers, the balance test, and the restoration amount — precisely by the imprest logic.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Money-out time — and just like the money-in diary, we will let the system build itself from one busy month and a few honest mistakes almost made.

## Subtopic: The Money-Out Diary

Thabo's other diary. Same idea as before, opposite direction: every time money LEAVES the bank account, one line — the date, the payment proof number, WHO was paid, how much. Who, not what, comes first when money goes out — because when you chase a payment later, you chase the person it went to.

And the columns grow from traffic, exactly like last time. What does Thabo pay for constantly? Stock — the shop must stay full: stock gets a lane. Wages — every week, without fail: wages gets a lane. Everything else — the rent, the new fridge, the day he took money for himself — those are visitors: one open column, each named as it enters.

One habit from the money-IN diary carries straight over: the handshake. Every rand that left the bank must be explained by a lane or a named visitor. Add the lanes and the visitors; the sum must equal the Bank column. If the diary says R22 500 left the bank, the explanations must add to R22 500 — to the rand.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the money-out diary are coming to you right now: lane or named visitor, for each payment.

## Subtopic: The Day Thabo Paid Himself

The month's most important line hides in an ordinary moment. On the 25th, Thabo transfers R2 000 to himself — groceries, airtime, life. And as he writes the diary line, he almost writes it in the same breath as the rent: "just another cost of the month."

Stop him there. Ask the question that sorts every payment: what did the SHOP get for this money? For the R8 000 to Metro Wholesalers — the shop got stock; the shelf is fuller. For wages — the shop got a week of work. For rent — a month under a roof. For the fridge — a machine it will use for years. And for the R2 000 to Thabo? The shop got... nothing. No stock, no work, no roof. The OWNER got something — which is completely allowed; it is his business — but it is not a cost of running the shop. It is the owner dipping into his own stake: drawings.

Why does the difference matter so much? Because of the question the books must answer honestly at year end: did the SHOP win this year? If Thabo's personal R2 000 hides among the expenses, the shop looks R2 000 weaker than it truly performed — and a bank reading the books would judge a good shop badly. Keep the owner's pocket and the shop's costs apart, and both stories stay true. Same rule for the fridge, in the other direction: the fridge is not a "cost of the month" either — it is a possession the shop keeps. Costs are what the month CONSUMED: the work, the roof, the electricity. The test, always: what did the shop get, and how long does it last?

Your questions for this part are up now: for each payment, answer the sorting question out loud — what did the shop get? — then name the class.

## Subtopic: The Tin That Always Tells the Truth

Last piece: the small money. Stamps, a taxi to the wholesaler, tea for the shop — nobody EFTs sixty rand. So Thabo keeps a tin, and the tin runs on one beautiful rule: it always starts the month worth R500 — some in cash, the rest in paper.

Here is how the paper works. Anyone who takes money from the tin puts back a signed slip — a voucher: what it was for, how much, signed. So at ANY moment, cash plus slips equals R500. Count the tin any afternoon: R245 in coins and notes, R255 in slips — perfect. R245 in cash and R230 in slips? The tin itself just told you R25 is missing — today, not at year end. The tin audits itself every time someone opens it.

Month end: the slips come out — stamps R60, taxi R85, tea and coffee R120, cleaning stuff R95. Add them: R360. And the refill is not a guess and not "whatever makes it look right" — the refill IS the voucher total: R360 from the bank, through the money-out diary, and the tin stands at R500 again for the new month. Slips explain the spending; the refill matches the slips; the tin returns to its fixed number. Round and round, month after month — that is why it is called the imprest system: the tin is always "pressed" back to the same amount.

One person owns the tin — one name, one set of keys — so every question has an address. Small money, watched as carefully as big money; that is the habit that keeps shops alive.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: count the tin, test cash-plus-slips, and set the refill. If your tin tells the truth, so do you — well worked today.
