**Thandi:** Questions from the class! First up: the cost of sales posting confused a few of us — it posts twice, but the R3 600 was already written in the CRJ. Isn't that three records of the same thing?

**Tutor:** Count the roles, not the appearances. The CRJ line is the SOURCE record — the month's diary entry, where the amount was first captured with its document. At posting time, that one captured amount takes its two ledger positions: a debit to Cost of Sales, because the business consumed R3 600 of stock to earn its sales, and a credit to Trading Stock, because the shelf gave those goods up. Two positions, one amount — that is ordinary double entry, not duplication. The journal is where an amount is born; the ledger is where it lives, on both its sides. Every posting in the system works this way; cost of sales only feels special because BOTH its ledger homes come from one journal column, while for the Bank column the credit partners are scattered across Sales, Capital and Rent Income.

***

**Thandi:** Next one: why do we balance Bank and Trading Stock, but just total the nominal accounts like Sales and Wages? Learners want one rule for everything.

**Tutor:** The rule IS one rule, once you see what each section measures. Balance sheet accounts describe a POSITION — how much money, how much stock, how large the owner's stake — and a position must be stated at a moment: hence balance carried down, brought down, ready for next month. Nominal accounts measure a PERIOD — how much was sold, spent, earned THIS YEAR — so month by month they simply accumulate, like a scoreboard that must keep counting until the final whistle. Balancing a nominal account monthly would be like resetting the scoreboard at half time. At year end the whistle blows: the nominal accounts close into the final accounts, the year's profit is computed, and they start next year at nil. Position accounts endure; period accounts reset. Ask which kind you are holding, and the treatment names itself.

***

**Thandi:** This one came with a groan: a learner's trial balance was out by exactly R7 000, and they re-added every column three times before finding the error. What should they have done first?

**Tutor:** Read the gap before touching the additions — the difference is a fingerprint, and re-adding is the slowest tool in the box. Seven thousand halves neatly to three thousand five hundred: the first suspect is an amount of R3 500 posted to the WRONG SIDE, because a wrong-side posting doubles its own error — it is missing from where it belongs AND intruding where it does not. And there in our own month sits a R3 500 rent payment: post it as a credit instead of a debit and the columns split by exactly seven thousand. The full diagnostic, in order: gap equal to an amount you know — posted once, not twice. Gap divisible by two — wrong side; halve it and hunt that amount. Gap divisible by nine — transposed digits. Only when the fingerprints fail do you re-add. Method first, arithmetic second.

***

**Thandi:** Last one, and it's the wise-sounding one: if the trial balance can miss wrong-account errors and whole missing transactions, why do we celebrate when it balances?

**Tutor:** Because of what it DOES prove, which no other single page proves: that across hundreds of entries, every debit found an equal credit and every column was cast consistently — the arithmetic skeleton of the month is sound. That eliminates an entire species of error before the statements are built. The errors it cannot see are guarded by OTHER layers, each designed for its blind spot: numbered documents catch missing transactions, because a gap in the sequence asks its own question; folio references let any amount be traced when classification is challenged; and next term, control accounts and reconciliations test the books against independent records. Accounting never relies on one check — it layers them, each catching what the others miss. The trial balance is one gate, passed — celebrate it as that, and keep the other gates standing.
