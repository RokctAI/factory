**Thandi:** Class questions! Top of the pile: the analysis column feels like writing the same number twice — it's there AND in the Bank column. Why the double writing?

**Tutor:** Because they answer two different questions, and on busy days the difference is the whole point. The analysis column answers: what arrived, item by item, as the day ran? The Bank column answers: what was actually deposited, and when? On a quiet day with one receipt banked immediately, yes — the same figure sits in both, and it feels redundant. But picture Saturday: five separate amounts collected through the day, banked together that evening as one deposit. Five analysis entries, ONE Bank figure. And now the control: if the five arrivals add to three thousand rand but the deposit shows two thousand eight hundred, the journal itself points at the gap — two hundred rand went missing between the till and the bank, on that exact day. Remove the analysis column and that theft becomes invisible. The double writing is the safety net.

***

**Thandi:** Next — the one everyone dreads: cost of sales. If the mark-up is 25 percent, why is dividing by 1,25 right and taking 25 percent off wrong? They feel like the same thing.

**Tutor:** They feel the same because both use the number 25 — but they measure from different bases, and that changes everything. The mark-up was added ON COST: cost, plus a quarter OF COST, equals selling price. So selling price is one and a quarter times cost — which means going backwards is division: selling price divided by one comma two five. Take our R1 500: divide by 1,25 and cost is R1 200; check forwards — R1 200 plus a quarter of R1 200, which is R300, returns exactly R1 500. Now try the wrong road: 25 percent OFF R1 500 is R1 125 — and check it forwards: R1 125 plus a quarter of R1 125 gives R1 406,25, not R1 500. The forwards check exposes the error every time. Percentages must always come off the base they were put on.

***

**Thandi:** A practical one: a learner asked why money from debtors never appeared in today's journal — surely money from a debtor is money received?

**Tutor:** It is, and it will belong in this very journal — the moment the business HAS debtors. This term's opening weeks deal with cash transactions only: the business sells for cash, so nobody owes it anything yet. When credit sales arrive in the coming lessons, the CRJ grows one more dedicated column — Debtors Control — because receipts from debtors will become daily regulars, and regulars earn lanes. Notice the deeper lesson in that: the journal's format is not a sacred form to memorise; it is built from the business's actual traffic. Learn the logic — regular lanes, named visitors, a bank total explained by its lanes — and every extension bolts on without fear.

***

**Thandi:** Last one: at month end, what actually happens to all these totals? The journal feels like it just... stops.

**Tutor:** It hands over — the totals become the ledger's input, and the column design shows its genius right there. A month of daily sales posts as ONE total to the Sales account; the Bank column posts as one total; cost of sales posts once, moving the month's cost out of Trading Stock. Only the sundry items travel individually, by name — which is exactly why they were named. So thirty days of trading becomes a handful of postings, each traceable back through the journal to a numbered document. The journal is the sorting room; the ledger is the filing cabinet. Next lesson opens that cabinet — and you will find the cross-cast you did today is what guarantees the cabinet balances.
