**Thandi:** Class questions rolling in! First: if the spaza runs perfectly well without books, why should anyone bother formalising at all?

**Tutor:** Because recording buys four things that skill alone cannot. Proof: the day the owner wants a loan to grow, the bank asks for evidence, and unrecorded profit is invisible profit — the answer is no, however well the shop trades. Accuracy: memory leaks small expenses, so the head's profit figure runs high, and decisions built on it wobble. Separation: a notebook and a second tin split the shop's money from the household's, so the profit figure finally measures the BUSINESS. Continuity: records outlive memory — a recorded business can be handed to a daughter, sold, or defended in a dispute. Notice none of these say the trader lacks skill. They say skill plus records beats skill alone.

***

**Thandi:** Next one: the lesson said the spaza owner's profit is overstated because she doesn't pay herself. But she genuinely didn't spend that money — so how is the profit wrong?

**Tutor:** The tin is right; the comparison is wrong. Count only cash and her month shows R1 450 — accurate as a cash count. But think about what that figure quietly includes: a full month of her own daily labour, priced at zero. The supermarket's profit already carries its cashiers' wages as an expense; hers carries none. So the moment she compares her shop to a salaried job, or to any business that pays its workers, she is comparing unlike things. Price her hours honestly — even notionally — and the shop's TRUE earning power appears. The books did not lie; they were silent about something important, and formal recording is how that silence gets a voice.

***

**Thandi:** Here's a thoughtful one from the front: is the stokvel really accounting, or are we just being kind by calling it that?

**Tutor:** Test it against the definitions, not against sentiment. Accounting is the systematic recording of contributions, claims and flows, plus accountability to the people with a stake. The stokvel book records who paid, how much, and when — that is a ledger, often kept flawlessly for decades. The payout roster is a schedule of claims. The treasurer standing before the December meeting, accounting for every rand, is financial reporting under real scrutiny. The penalty rules for missed months are credit control. Every function is present, proportioned to the group's needs. So no kindness is involved — analytically, a stokvel is a financial institution. What formal accounting adds is standardisation: formats a stranger, a bank, or an auditor can read without knowing the aunty who keeps the book.

**Thandi:** Last question: in a test, what's the cleanest way to structure a compare-the-sectors answer so no marks slip?

**Tutor:** Use the four-move method from the lesson, every time. Move one: NAME the concept the question is circling — capital, fixed assets, labour cost. Move two: DEFINE it once, precisely — definitions are the cheapest marks on the paper. Move three: show it living in BOTH columns — one formal example, one informal or indigenous example: the recorded Capital account beside the stokvel savings, the asset register beside the cattle in the kraal. Move four: CLOSE with what recording adds — proof, accuracy, separation or continuity, whichever the scenario points at. Four moves, four sentences if need be. The learners who lose marks give one column and stop; the full comparison is the answer.
