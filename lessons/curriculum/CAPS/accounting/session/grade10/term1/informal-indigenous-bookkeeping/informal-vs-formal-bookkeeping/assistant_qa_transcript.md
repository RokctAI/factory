**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close. First one, and it came in three times: if the spaza owner manages fine without books, why must WE learn all this recording? It feels like extra work for the same answer.

**Tutor:** A fair challenge — and the month-end example is the honest reply. From memory, the owner believed she had made one thousand five hundred rand; the notebook showed one thousand two hundred. Neither laziness nor stupidity caused the gap — memory simply cannot hold a hundred small amounts, so three hundred rand of real expenses vanished from the mental total. Now scale that up: a business with thousands of transactions a month would not be out by three hundred rand, it would be out by thousands, and every decision — what to stock, what to charge, whether to hire — would rest on wrong numbers. Recording is not extra work on top of the business; it is how a business above a certain size knows the truth about itself at all.

***

**Thandi:** Next one: a learner says her mother sells vetkoek and uses the same purse for the business and the house, and it works fine. Is the business entity rule really such a big deal for a small trader?

**Tutor:** It works — until the day a question needs answering. Is the vetkoek business actually profitable, or is it quietly being fed by the household? Nobody can say, because the two moneys are mixed. If she ever wants a loan, or wants to know whether doubling the batch is worth it, or falls ill and someone else must run it for a month, the single purse turns every question into a guess. And notice the cost of fixing it: one separate tin and one notebook — the cheapest business improvement that exists. The entity rule is not bureaucracy; it is the difference between owning a business and owning a busy hobby.

***

**Thandi:** This one made me smile: is calling a stokvel "a bank with a notebook" really accurate, or are we just being kind to tradition?

**Tutor:** It is accurate, and the test is to list what a bank actually does and tick them off. Collects regular deposits — the stokvel does that on strict monthly dates. Keeps a faithful record of every member's position — the treasurer's book does exactly that, and members check it. Enforces rules about missed payments before benefits are paid — that is credit control, alive and well in every stokvel constitution. Reports to its stakeholders — the December accounting, where every rand must be explained. The scale is smaller and the trust is personal rather than institutional, but the FUNCTIONS are identical. We are not being kind; we are being precise. The kindness would be pretending these practices needed rescuing by textbooks.

***

**Thandi:** Last one: in a test, how much detail does a compare-the-sectors answer actually need? Learners never know when to stop writing.

**Tutor:** Use the three-move shape, once per concept, and stop. Move one: define the concept in a single sentence — capital is the owner's resources committed to the business. Move two: one example from each sector — the supermarket's banked five hundred thousand rand; the spaza's three thousand rand in the tin. Move three: one sentence on what recording adds — the formal business can prove its capital to a bank; the informal trader cannot. Three moves, three or four lines, next concept. An answer built that way covers definition, application and insight — which is precisely the mark allocation — without a single wasted sentence.
