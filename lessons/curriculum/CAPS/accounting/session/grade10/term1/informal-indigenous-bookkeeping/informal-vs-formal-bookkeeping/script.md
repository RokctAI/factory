# Part 1 — Expert

Before a single journal is ruled, Accounting asks a grounding question: how do businesses that keep no formal books still manage money successfully — and what do their methods share with ours? Today we compare the bookkeeping of the informal and formal sectors, concept by concept: capital, fixed assets, stock, cost price, labour cost, selling price, income, expenses and profit. Master this comparison and every formal procedure you learn afterwards has a real-world anchor.

## Subtopic: Two Sectors, One Set of Concepts

Define the terrain precisely. The formal sector consists of registered businesses: they keep full accounting records, issue documents for transactions, operate bank accounts, and answer to outside parties — the receiver of revenue, banks, auditors. The informal sector consists of unregistered traders — the spaza shop, the street vendor, the taxi washer — who may keep few or no written records.

Now the claim this lesson proves: both sectors run on IDENTICAL accounting concepts. The spaza owner who has never seen a ledger still commands capital, holds stock, sets selling prices above cost prices, and knows her profit. What differs is not the concepts — it is the RECORDING of them.

Work through the vocabulary with double precision, because these definitions carry marks all year. Capital: the resources the owner puts into the business to start and run it. Fixed assets: possessions kept for use, not for sale — a delivery bicycle, a fridge, a gazebo. Stock: goods bought with the intention of reselling them. Cost price: what the business paid for the goods. Selling price: what the customer pays. Income: amounts earned, chiefly sales. Expenses: amounts consumed in running the business — transport, airtime, rent. Labour cost: wages paid for work done, including the trap we return to later — the owner's own unpaid hours. And profit: income minus expenses — the reason the business exists.

The examinable skill is applying each term across BOTH sectors, so hold a single example in mind throughout: a registered corner supermarket on one side, and a home-run spaza shop on the other.

## Subtopic: The Comparison, Concept by Concept

Execute the comparison like a table with two columns — and quote both columns in any answer.

Capital. The supermarket's owner deposits R500 000 into a business bank account; the amount is recorded in a Capital account. The spaza owner starts with R3 000 saved in cash — possibly from a stokvel payout. Same concept: owner's resources committed to the business. Different form: banked and recorded, versus cash in hand and remembered.

Fixed assets. The supermarket buys shelving, tills and a delivery vehicle — recorded as Equipment and Vehicles, kept for years. The spaza's fixed assets are a fridge and a table. Identical definition — possessions for USE, not resale — regardless of size or paperwork.

Stock. The supermarket receives invoiced deliveries, counts stock, records theft and losses. The spaza owner buys bread, airtime and cooldrinks at a wholesaler. Both hold goods for resale; only one documents the holding.

Cost price versus selling price. The supermarket's system stores both per item; mark-up is policy, set by management. The spaza owner buys a cooldrink for R8 and sells it for R12 — the R4 margin lives in her head, but it is the same calculation: selling price minus cost price.

Labour cost. The supermarket pays recorded wages — and the recording matters, because wages are an expense that reduces profit visibly. The spaza owner often pays herself nothing, so her labour cost is INVISIBLE — which quietly overstates her profit. Name that insight in an exam; it distinguishes answers.

Income, expenses, profit. The supermarket reads them off statements. The spaza owner counts the cash tin at month end: what came in, minus what was spent, is profit — accounting's oldest equation, computed without a single ledger.

Stop for this section's questions now — each presents an item from one of the two columns. Classify it by concept, and be ready to name its counterpart in the other sector.

## Subtopic: Where the Difference Bites — Records and Decisions

If the concepts are identical, why does formality matter? Because records convert knowledge into PROOF and ESTIMATES into decisions. Follow the consequences.

Consequence one: credit and growth. A bank lending money demands evidence — statements, records of income. The supermarket produces them; the spaza owner, however profitable, cannot PROVE her profit, and the loan is refused or shrunk. Unrecorded success is invisible success.

Consequence two: accuracy of profit. Without records, expenses leak from memory: the taxi fare to the wholesaler, the airtime used for orders, the owner's own labour. Each leak inflates apparent profit. The formal system's discipline — every expense recorded when incurred — exists precisely to stop the leaks. When you later meet the matching principle, recognise this as its everyday justification.

Consequence three: separation of business and owner. The spaza's cash tin often doubles as the household purse — school fees taken tonight, restocked next week. Formal bookkeeping enforces the business entity rule: the business's money and the owner's money are recorded apart, so profit measures the BUSINESS, not the household. Mixing them is the single most damaging habit in informal trading, and the single first improvement any informal trader can adopt: a separate tin, and a notebook.

Consequence four: continuity. Records survive; memory does not. A business that lives in one person's head cannot be handed over, sold, or defended in a dispute.

Note the direction of this argument for the examination: we do not say the informal trader is a poor businessperson — the evidence says the opposite. We say formal RECORDING adds proof, accuracy, separation and continuity to business skill that already exists.

The questions on this section are in front of you now. Each describes a consequence of keeping — or not keeping — records: name which of the four gains is at stake.

## Subtopic: Indigenous Practices — the Original Bookkeeping

The syllabus asks you to relate current procedures to indigenous practices — and the relation is direct: indigenous financial practices ARE bookkeeping principles, applied without paper.

The stokvel: members contribute fixed amounts on fixed dates, and the pool pays out in rotation or at year end. Recognise the modern procedures inside it: regular contributions are CAPITAL formation; the member list and payout roster — often meticulously kept — are a LEDGER of contributions and claims; the treasurer reporting to members is FINANCIAL REPORTING and accountability; rules about missed payments are CREDIT CONTROL. A stokvel is a financial institution run on trust, discipline and records proportioned to its needs.

Livestock as wealth: cattle historically served as a store of capital and a measure of value — countable, visible assets, exactly what a Fixed asset or Investments account records today. Communal resource management — grazing rotas, shared harvest stores — implemented ASSET MANAGEMENT and INTERNAL CONTROL: agreed rules governing who may use what, and when, with the community as auditor.

Two conclusions carry the marks. First: modern accounting did not arrive from nowhere — its principles echo practices communities have used for generations, so treat indigenous systems with analytical respect, not nostalgia. Second: the comparison sharpens your grasp of every concept, because seeing capital as a stokvel pool, assets as cattle, and internal control as a grazing rota proves you understand the CONCEPT and not merely its printed form.

Your method for any question from this topic: name the concept, define it once, then show it living in both sectors — one formal example, one informal or indigenous example — and close with what recording adds. The questions on this section are with you now: run exactly that method on each one.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Same territory now, but let us walk it the way you already know it — through the shops on your own street. Nothing in this part is new theory; it is proof that you have understood business your whole life.

## Subtopic: The Shop You Already Understand

Picture the spaza shop nearest your home. The owner — let us say your aunty — has never opened an accounting textbook. And yet, watch what she does.

She started the shop with money she saved up — R3 000, kept safe from a stokvel payout. That starting money? Accounting calls it capital. She owns a fridge for the cooldrinks and a sturdy table for the counter. Things she keeps and uses, not things she sells — fixed assets. On Monday she takes a taxi to the wholesaler and fills two bags: bread, chips, airtime vouchers. Goods bought to sell again — that is stock, and what she paid is the cost price.

Back home, she doesn't sell the chips for what she paid. The packet that cost her R7 sells for R10. Why? She'd tell you straight: "the difference is what feeds us." Accounting nods and calls it the difference between cost price and selling price. The money coming into the tin all week — income. The taxi fare, the electricity for the fridge, the airtime she uses to order — money going out to keep the shop running — expenses.

And at month end she counts the tin. What came in, minus what went out. If it is more, the shop won; if it is less, something must change. That sentence — income minus expenses equals profit — is the beating heart of every financial statement you will ever draw up. Your aunty computes it without writing a word.

So hold this with confidence: you are not learning foreign ideas this year. You are learning to WRITE DOWN ideas that your own community already runs on daily.

## Subtopic: One Month, Two Notebooks

Now let us run one month of that spaza, twice — once in aunty's head, once on paper — and watch what the paper catches.

In her head: "I took R4 500 this month. I spent about R3 000 at the wholesaler. So I made about R1 500." Sounds right. Now the notebook version, honestly, line by line. Sales in the tin: R4 500. Stock bought: R3 000. Taxi to the wholesaler, four trips at R40: R160. Electricity for the fridge, her share: R90. Airtime used for orders: R50. Now the profit line: 4 500 minus 3 000 minus 160 minus 90 minus 50 — R1 200.

The head said R1 500; the notebook says R1 200. Where did R300 go? Nowhere — it was ALWAYS being spent. The head just couldn't hold the small amounts, so they leaked. Multiply that leak by twelve months and the shop believes it made R3 600 more than it did. That is why formal businesses write everything down: not because they are cleverer, but because nobody's memory can hold a hundred small expenses — and decisions made on wrong numbers become wrong decisions.

And one more honest line for the notebook: aunty worked every day and paid herself nothing. The supermarket pays its cashiers, and that wage sits in its books as an expense. If aunty ever wants to compare her shop to a "real job", she must count her own labour too — the notebook makes even that visible.

## Subtopic: Your Grandmother's Ledger

Last stretch — and it is the proudest part of this topic. Formal accounting's big ideas were alive in this country long before textbooks arrived.

Think of a stokvel. Every member pays in, every month, no excuses. Somebody — usually the strictest aunty in the group — keeps the book: who paid, who is behind, whose turn is next. At the December meeting she accounts for every rand. Now put on your new vocabulary: the monthly payments are capital contributions. The book is a ledger. The December meeting is an annual report to stakeholders. The rule that you cannot skip two months and still claim a payout — that is credit control. A stokvel is a bank built from trust and a notebook.

Cattle told the same story for our great-grandparents: wealth you could count standing in the kraal — assets, visible and verifiable, which is exactly what an asset register does in a formal business today. And when a community shared grazing land by agreed turns, with elders checking that nobody overgrazed — that was internal control: rules about who may use resources, and someone trusted to check.

So here is the takeaway to carry into the rest of Accounting: this subject is not importing something foreign. It is formalising something familiar — giving sharper tools to instincts your community already trusts. When the ledgers get technical later this term, remember the strict aunty with the stokvel book. She IS a bookkeeper. You are just getting a better pen.
