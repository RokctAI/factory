# Part 1 — Expert

Cash transactions built the machine; credit transactions now test it. Today the business begins selling on credit — goods now, money later — and two new books of first entry arrive: the Debtors Journal for credit sales, and the Debtors Allowances Journal for goods returned and allowances granted. Alongside them comes a second ledger: the Debtors Ledger, one personal account per customer. Master today's machinery and the debtors side of every reconciliation question through matric stands on it.

## Subtopic: Selling on Credit — the Invoice and the Debtors Journal

Why would a business hand over goods without payment? Because credit sells. Customers buy more, and buy sooner, when they may pay at month end — and a business that refuses credit hands those customers to a competitor who will not. The price of that advantage is RISK and ADMINISTRATION: money owed must be tracked per person, chased on time, and sometimes lost. Today's books are that administration, disciplined.

The transaction's source document is the INVOICE — issued in duplicate, numbered sequentially: original to the customer, duplicate to the bookkeeper. The invoice states the parties, the date, the goods, and the amount owed. It is the birth certificate of a debt: from the moment of issue, the customer is a DEBTOR — an asset of the business, money receivable.

Credit sales are recorded in the DEBTORS JOURNAL — the DJ — a book of first entry as specialised as the CRJ was: it records credit sales of trading stock, and NOTHING else. Its columns, under the perpetual system, will not surprise you: document number, day, debtor's name, folio, SALES — the selling price now owed — and COST OF SALES, the cost of the goods that left the shelf. Two truths per sale, exactly as at the till; the only difference is that the money truth reads "owed" instead of "received".

And each sale must ALSO reach the customer's own record: the Debtors Ledger holds a personal account per debtor, showing that person's full story — what they bought, returned, paid, and currently owe. The DJ feeds both destinations: totals to the general ledger monthly, personal accounts line by line, daily.

Pause here — questions on credit selling, the invoice and the DJ's design are with you now. Answer with the definitions and the two destinations.

## Subtopic: Recording and Posting the Debtors Journal

The month, at Thabo's Trading — mark-up still 25 percent on cost. On the 4th, invoice 61 to L. Dube: goods R2 000; cost — divide by 1,25 — R1 600. On the 10th, invoice 62 to S. Naidoo: R1 500; cost R1 200. On the 18th, invoice 63 to L. Dube again: R1 000; cost R800. Each line: document number, date, the debtor's NAME — names matter now — Sales amount, Cost of Sales amount.

Daily, each sale posts to the personal account: L. Dube's account is debited R2 000 on the 4th — she owes more; asset accounts and debtors grow on the debit side. Naidoo debited R1 500 on the 10th; Dube again R1 000 on the 18th. The folio column records DJ 1 beside each.

Month end: total the columns. Sales R4 500; Cost of Sales R3 600. Now post the totals to the general ledger. Debit DEBTORS CONTROL R4 500 — the account you met in principle: the general ledger's one-line summary of all debtors, which must always equal the sum of the personal accounts. Credit SALES R4 500 — credit sales are still sales; the Sales account does not care when the money arrives. And the perpetual pair: debit Cost of Sales R3 600, credit Trading Stock R3 600 — the shelf gave up goods regardless of how the customer pays.

Check the double entry of the month as a whole: debits — Debtors Control 4 500, Cost of Sales 3 600; credits — Sales 4 500, Trading Stock 3 600. Balanced, 8 100 both sides. And note the timing architecture, because exams probe it: PERSONAL accounts are posted DAILY — a debtor's balance must be current the moment they phone to ask; CONTROL and the other general ledger accounts receive TOTALS MONTHLY. Detail daily, summary monthly.

Stop for this section's questions now: strip mark-ups, write the lines, and post to both ledgers — naming side and timing for each destination.

## Subtopic: Returns, Allowances and the DAJ

Goods come back. On the 12th, L. Dube returns goods invoiced at R500 — wrong size; they are undamaged and return to the shelf. On the 20th, S. Naidoo reports a scratch on an item she is keeping; Thabo grants her R150 off. Two different events, one book: the DEBTORS ALLOWANCES JOURNAL — the DAJ.

The source document is the CREDIT NOTE — numbered, issued to the customer — stating that their debt has been REDUCED. It is the invoice's mirror: where the invoice created debt, the credit note cancels part of it.

Distinguish the two events with precision, because the recording differs. A RETURN: goods physically come back. Dube's R500 return enters the DAJ's Debtors Allowances column at R500 — and because the goods return to stock in saleable condition, the COST OF SALES column also carries their cost: R400. An ALLOWANCE: the customer keeps the goods; only the price adjusts. Naidoo's R150 enters the Debtors Allowances column — and the Cost of Sales column stays EMPTY: no goods moved, so no stock entry exists.

Month-end totals: Debtors Allowances R650; Cost of Sales R400. Post to the general ledger: debit DEBTORS ALLOWANCES R650 — a contra-income account that accumulates the year's returns and allowances, later subtracted from Sales to show turnover honestly; credit DEBTORS CONTROL R650 — debtors as a group owe less. And the stock pair, reversed for the return only: debit TRADING STOCK R400 — the shelf regained goods at cost; credit COST OF SALES R400 — that cost is no longer a cost of anything sold. Daily, the personal accounts: Dube credited R500 on the 12th; Naidoo credited R150 on the 20th.

Why a separate Debtors Allowances account — why not simply debit Sales? Because management needs the truth in two figures, not one: gross sales AND how much came back. A business whose allowances climb from two percent of sales to ten has a quality problem or a description problem, and burying returns inside Sales would hide exactly that signal.

The questions on this section are in front of you now: return or allowance first, then the entries — and let the cost column follow the goods, not the money.

## Subtopic: The Debtors Ledger at Month End

Balance the personal accounts. L. Dube: debits 2 000 and 1 000; credit 500 — balance R2 500 debit. S. Naidoo: debit 1 500; credit 150 — balance R1 350 debit. Each account tells one customer's whole story at a glance, which is the point: when Dube queries her account, the answer is one page, not a month of journals.

Now the month-end discipline that becomes a full topic in later grades: the LIST OF DEBTORS. Write every personal balance in a list and total it: 2 500 plus 1 350 — R3 850. Compare with Debtors Control in the general ledger: debits 4 500, credits 650 — balance R3 850 debit. They agree, and they MUST: control carries the totals of exactly the journals whose lines built the personal accounts. Any disagreement is an error with an address — a line posted to the wrong person, a total miscast — and the comparison finds it monthly, not eventually.

Close with the internal control that keeps credit selling safe, because scenario questions live here. Before credit is granted: screen the customer and set a CREDIT LIMIT per debtor. Monthly: send each debtor a STATEMENT — their personal account's story, mailed — so debts stay visible and disputes surface early. Watch the AGE of debts: the older a debt, the colder; follow up the moment terms are exceeded. And division of duties, as always: the person recording debtors should not be the person receiving their payments. Credit is a privilege the business extends and manages — never a courtesy it forgets.

The final questions of this part are with you now: balance the accounts, build the list, test it against control, and name the control measure each scenario begs for.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same week again, but from the counter — where selling on credit starts with a face you know, and a promise.

## Subtopic: The Exercise-Book of Names

Every township shop that ever gave credit had one: the exercise book under the counter. A page for each person. Mrs Dube's page. The Naidoos' page. Buy now, pay month end — and the shopkeeper writes it down, because memory is generous and business cannot afford generous.

Thabo's shop has grown into that book, properly. When Mrs Dube takes R2 000 of goods on trust, two papers make it official: she gets an INVOICE — the promise, in writing, numbered — and her PAGE gets the entry: owes R2 000 more. That page-per-person book is the Debtors Ledger, and it answers the question the counter actually hears: "Thabo, how much do I owe you?" One page, one glance, done.

But Thabo needs a second view of the same truth. At month end he does not want to page through forty names to know what the WHOLE street owes him — he wants one number. So the day's promises also flow, as monthly totals, into one summary account in his main cabinet: Debtors Control — the whole street on one line. Pages for the people, one line for the street — and here is the beautiful lock: add up all the pages, and the total MUST equal the street's one line. Same promises, counted two ways. If the two counts ever differ, a promise got lost or doubled somewhere — and Thabo knows to hunt before the month closes.

And the shelf still tells its truth: goods that left on a promise still left. Cost of sales rides along with every credit sale, exactly as it did for cash — the shelf does not care how the customer pays.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the book of names are coming to you right now: the page, the street-line, and the lock between them.

## Subtopic: The Week Mrs Dube Changed Her Mind

Real credit weeks have reverse gear. Wednesday: Mrs Dube is back — the R500 shirt is the wrong size, unworn, tags on. Friday: Mr Naidoo phones — the kettle he bought has a scratch; he will keep it, but wants something off. Two different stories, and the books tell them differently.

The shirt COMES BACK. So two things must un-happen. Her debt shrinks: her page is credited R500 — she owes less — and the paper making it official is a CREDIT NOTE: the anti-invoice, numbered, "your debt is reduced". And the shirt goes BACK ON THE SHELF: the stock account regains it at what it had cost — R400 — and that R400 stops being a cost of sales, because that sale, in part, unsold itself. Money story and shelf story, both reversed.

The kettle STAYS with Mr Naidoo. Only the PRICE changes: his page is credited R150 — a credit note again — but the shelf? Untouched. No goods moved, so no stock entry. This is the whole return-versus-allowance distinction in one pair of stories: follow the GOODS to know whether the cost column writes. Goods back — cost comes back. Goods stay — price moves alone.

Why do these reversals get their own book — the Debtors Allowances Journal — instead of minus-lines scribbled in the sales book? Because Thabo needs to SEE the coming-back separately. Sales of R4 500 with R650 coming back is a very different shop from sales of R3 850 with nothing returned — same net number, different story. The first shop might have a sizing problem, a quality problem, an over-promising problem. Separate books keep separate truths visible.

Your questions for this part are up now: for each story, follow the goods first — then write both pages, and only then the shelf.

## Subtopic: Keeping the Names Good

Last stretch: the habits that keep the book of names from becoming a book of losses.

Before a name gets a page at all: decide how much trust it carries. That is a CREDIT LIMIT — Mrs Dube, good for R3 000; a brand-new customer, maybe R500 to start. Limits are not insults; they are the reason the shop can keep saying yes.

Every month end, each name gets its STATEMENT: a copy of their page — bought this, returned that, owe so much. Statements do two quiet jobs: they remind politely, and they invite corrections early — "Thabo, I never bought that kettle" is a conversation you want in week one, not month six.

And watch the calendar on every debt. Money owed for thirty days is normal; for ninety, it is drifting toward being a gift. The older the promise, the harder the chase — so the chase starts the day a promise goes overdue, kindly and every month, in writing.

One more habit, borrowed straight from the tuck shop: the person who writes the pages should not be the person who takes the payments. Two sets of hands, and every rand that moves between them leaves a paper behind.

Do all of this, and credit becomes what it should be: the engine that grows the shop, with brakes that work. And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: each scenario is a name, a promise, or a drift — name the habit that answers it. Then close the book gently; you have run a whole credit month, both ledgers, forwards and backwards. Respect.
