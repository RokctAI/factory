# Part 1 — Expert

Last session the business extended trust; today it receives trust. Buying on credit brings the mirror image of everything you mastered for debtors: the Creditors Journal for credit purchases, the Creditors Allowances Journal for goods sent back, a Creditors Ledger with one personal account per supplier, and a Creditors Control account summarising them all. The machinery is familiar; the direction reverses. Command the reversal and the whole credit system — both sides — is yours.

## Subtopic: Buying on Credit — the Invoice Received and the CJ

When Thabo's Trading buys stock on credit from Metro Wholesalers, the same event wears two names in two sets of books. In METRO'S books, Thabo is a debtor — money receivable. In THABO'S books — the books we keep — Metro is a CREDITOR: a liability, money the business owes. Same invoice, opposite sides. Fix that mirror firmly; it untangles every confusion this topic offers.

The source document is the INVOICE RECEIVED — Metro's original invoice, arriving with the goods. It carries Metro's own number, so the business renumbers it into its own sequence for filing and reference. Before it is recorded at all, internal control demands a matching ritual we will detail later: does this invoice agree with what was ordered and what actually arrived?

Credit purchases are recorded in the CREDITORS JOURNAL — the CJ. Its column design follows the traffic, as journal design always does: a CREDITORS CONTROL column for the total owed per invoice — every line strikes it — and analysis columns for what the money was owed FOR: TRADING STOCK, the constant traffic; STATIONERY, frequent enough to earn a lane in most businesses; EQUIPMENT and SUNDRY ACCOUNTS for the rest. Note what is ABSENT, deliberately: no cost of sales column. Buying loads the shelf at cost — one truth, not two; the two-truths pattern belongs to SELLING, where goods leave at cost while money arrives at selling price.

Pause here — questions on the mirror, the invoice received and the CJ's design are with you now. Answer from the direction of the debt: who owes whom?

## Subtopic: Recording and Posting the Creditors Journal

The month's purchases. On the 3rd, trading stock from Metro Wholesalers, R9 000 — renumbered invoice 71. On the 9th, stationery from PNA Suppliers, R400 — invoice 72. On the 15th, display shelving from ShelfCo, R3 000 — invoice 73: shelving is kept for use, an ASSET — Equipment, not stock and not an expense lane.

Each line: date, invoice number, the CREDITOR'S NAME, the total into Creditors Control, and the same amount into its analysis column. Daily, each purchase posts to the supplier's personal account in the CREDITORS LEDGER — and here the mirror bites for the first time: Metro's personal account is CREDITED R9 000. Creditors are liabilities; liabilities grow on the credit side. Where a debtor's page grew on the debit side because they owed US more, a creditor's page grows on the credit side because WE owe more.

Month end: total and cross-cast. Creditors Control column: R12 400. Analysis columns: Trading Stock 9 000, Stationery 400, Equipment 3 000 — sum 12 400. The journal proves itself, then posts. Credit CREDITORS CONTROL R12 400 in the general ledger — the group liability grew. Debit each analysis total to its account: Trading Stock R9 000 — the shelf loaded at cost; Stationery R400 — an expense of running the business; Equipment R3 000 — an asset acquired. Debits 12 400, credit 12 400. Balanced.

Compare the architecture with the DJ deliberately: same timing — personal accounts daily, control by monthly total; same lock — the list of personal balances must equal control; opposite sides throughout — because the debt now points out of the business, not in.

Stop for this section's questions now: record, post to both ledgers, and name the side each entry takes — reasoning from asset up, expense up, liability up.

## Subtopic: Returns to Suppliers — the Debit Note and the CAJ

Goods go back the other way too. On the 11th, a box of stock from Metro arrives damaged: R800 worth, returned. On the 18th, the stationery from PNA proves to be the wrong type: R100 returned. The business does not wait for the supplier's paperwork — it ISSUES its own document: the DEBIT NOTE, numbered, stating that the supplier's account is being debited — reduced — by the value returned, and why. The supplier answers in due course with their credit note confirming; the debit note is our record on our timeline.

The book is the CREDITORS ALLOWANCES JOURNAL — the CAJ — mirroring the CJ column for column: Creditors Control, Trading Stock, Stationery, Equipment, Sundries. Line one: debit note 21, Metro, Creditors Control R800, Trading Stock R800. Line two: debit note 22, PNA, Creditors Control R100, Stationery R100.

Month end totals: Creditors Control R900; Trading Stock R800; Stationery R100. Posting reverses the CJ's directions exactly: DEBIT Creditors Control R900 — the group liability shrank; CREDIT Trading Stock R800 — the shelf gave the damaged goods back; CREDIT Stationery R100. Daily, the personal accounts: Metro's page debited R800 on the 11th; PNA's page debited R100 on the 18th — we owe each of them less.

Notice the pleasing symmetry with the debtors side, and the one structural difference worth stating in an exam: returns to suppliers carry NO cost-of-sales adjustment — the goods had been bought at cost and return at cost; only selling ever creates the two-truths pair. And notice what the return does NOT touch: no money moved. Returns adjust the DEBT; payment is a separate event, in a separate journal, on a separate day.

The questions on this section are in front of you now: debit note or credit note first — who issues which — then the entries, both ledgers, both timings.

## Subtopic: The Creditors Ledger, the List, and the Discipline of Paying

Balance the personal accounts. Metro Wholesalers: credit 9 000, debit 800 — balance R8 200 CREDIT: the business owes Metro R8 200. PNA Suppliers: credit 400, debit 100 — R300 credit. ShelfCo: R3 000 credit. Credit balances, all — the natural state of a creditor's account, mirroring the debit balances that were natural for debtors.

The month-end lock: list the creditors — 8 200, 300, 3 000 — total R11 500. Creditors Control: credit 12 400, debit 900 — balance R11 500 credit. Agreement, by construction; disagreement, by error — and the monthly comparison catches it while it is one month old.

Now the discipline that separates businesses that thrive on credit from businesses that drown in it. First, the THREE-DOCUMENT MATCH before any invoice is recorded or paid: the ORDER — what we asked for; the DELIVERY NOTE — what actually arrived, checked and signed at receiving; the INVOICE — what the supplier claims. All three must agree; an invoice matching no order, or billing goods never delivered, is a question, not a payment. Second, PAY ON TERMS — not early, not late. Late payment burns the relationship and the credit record; needlessly early payment burns cash the business could use all month. Third, watch for SETTLEMENT DISCOUNTS — suppliers rewarding prompt payment — a topic that grows later, but the habit of reading terms starts now. Fourth, the standing rule: the person recording creditors is not the person authorising payments.

Creditors are not merely amounts owed — they are the business's cheapest financing and its most important relationships. The books you built today are how both are kept in good repair.

The final questions of this part are with you now: balance the pages, build the list, run the lock — and for each control scenario, name the matching discipline.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now cross the counter. All of last lesson, you were the shop trusting customers. Today you are the customer being trusted — and the whole system flips like a reflection.

## Subtopic: The Other Side of the Counter

Remember Mrs Dube's page in Thabo's exercise book? Today Thabo finds out what it feels like to BE Mrs Dube. When he takes R9 000 of stock from Metro Wholesalers on credit, somewhere in Metro's office, there is a page with THABO'S name on it. He is a name in someone else's book now.

But Thabo cannot manage his shop by wondering what other people's books say about him. He keeps his own book about THEM: a page for Metro, a page for PNA, a page for ShelfCo — everyone the shop owes. That book is the Creditors Ledger, and the summary line — the whole "we owe" world in one number — is Creditors Control.

Feel the flip in your hands. In the debtors book, a bigger balance meant MORE COMING IN someday — an asset, growing on the debit side. In the creditors book, a bigger balance means MORE GOING OUT someday — a debt, growing on the credit side. Same machinery, mirrored: pages per name, one summary line, daily detail, monthly totals, and the lock — pages adding up to the line — standing guard on this side too.

One more flip to notice: when Thabo buys stock, only ONE truth gets written — the shelf loads at cost, R9 000, done. No second number. The two-numbers-per-line dance belonged to SELLING, where goods left at cost while the customer owed selling price. Buying is simpler; enjoy it.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the flip are coming to you right now: for each amount, say whose book, whose page, and which way the debt points.

## Subtopic: The Month of Buying on Trust

Walk the month like a story. Early in the month, the big stock order from Metro: R9 000, goods on the shelf, nothing paid — Metro's page grows by R9 000; the summary line grows too. Midway, small things: R400 of stationery from PNA — till rolls, price stickers, the paper the shop runs on. And the new display shelving from ShelfCo, R3 000 — pause on that one: shelving is not stock (it will never be sold) and not stationery (it will not be used up this month); it is a POSSESSION the shop keeps — equipment. Three purchases, three pages, three different homes for the analysis amounts — stock, expense, asset — all owed, none paid.

Then the box from Metro comes up damaged: R800 of stock, unsellable. Watch what Thabo does — he does not phone and hope. He sends the box back WITH A LETTER THAT HAS NUMBERS: the debit note. "We are reducing what we owe you by R800 — here is why." His own document, his own number, written on his own timeline, into his own books: Metro's page shrinks by R800, the shelf gives up the damaged goods at their cost. When PNA's stationery turns out to be the wrong type, same move: back it goes, debit note, R100 off their page.

Month end, the pages read: Metro R8 200, PNA R300, ShelfCo R3 000. The list: R11 500. The summary line: R11 500. The lock clicks, this side of the counter too.

Your questions for this part are up now: sort each purchase to its home — stock, expense or asset — then run the pages and the lock.

## Subtopic: Paying Like a Pro

Last skill, and it is the one that keeps a shop's name golden: how to owe money WELL.

Rule one: never pay an invoice on its own say-so. Before Metro's invoice earns a cent, three papers must tell one story: the ORDER — what Thabo asked for; the DELIVERY NOTE — what the driver actually offloaded, counted and signed for at the door; and the INVOICE — what Metro says he owes. Three papers, one story: pay. Any mismatch — invoiced for ten boxes, received nine — is a debit note conversation, not a payment. This one habit stops a business paying for goods it never got, which happens to careless businesses more often than anyone admits.

Rule two: respect the calendar, both directions. Pay LATE and the shop's name tarnishes — the next order comes slower, the terms get harsher, and in a small trading community, word travels. Pay TOO EARLY and the shop starves itself of cash it was entitled to hold all month — credit from suppliers is free financing; leaving it unused is leaving a gift on the table. On terms, on time, exactly.

Rule three: keep the friendship professional. The monthly statement Metro sends? Check it against Metro's page in Thabo's own book — two records, independently kept, must agree; where they differ, the documents decide. Queries raised young are queries solved friendly.

Owing money well is a skill: matched papers, a respected calendar, reconciled pages. Do it right, and suppliers fight to serve you — which is worth more than any discount.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: match the papers, read the calendar, settle the differences — the pro's checklist, question by question. Both sides of the counter now belong to you.
