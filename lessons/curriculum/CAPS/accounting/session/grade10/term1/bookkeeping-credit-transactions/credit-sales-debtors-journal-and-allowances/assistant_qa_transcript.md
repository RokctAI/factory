**Thandi:** Class questions! The one that filled my inbox: why does Sales get credited on the day of the credit sale, when the money might only arrive next month — or never?

**Tutor:** Because the SALE and the PAYMENT are two different events, and accounting records each in its own moment. On invoice day, the business did its part completely: goods delivered, price agreed, debt created. That is when the sale was EARNED — so Sales records it then, and the debtor's page records who owes for it. Payment day, when it comes, is a different event entirely: cash in, debt down — Bank and the debtor's page, no Sales entry at all. Recording the earning separately from the receiving is what lets the books answer two different questions honestly: how much did we sell, and how much are we owed? And your instinct about 'never' is exactly the right worry — a debt that dies becomes a bad debt, written off as an expense. The system you learned today is precisely what makes that loss visible instead of silent.

***

**Thandi:** Next: a learner asks why the shop bothers with the control account at all — the personal pages already hold every fact, so isn't the summary just extra writing?

**Tutor:** It is extra writing that buys two things the pages alone cannot give. First, speed at the top: the owner and the trial balance need ONE debtors figure — what the whole street owes — without paging through forty accounts. Control answers instantly, because it was built from the same journal totals. Second, and more valuable: the CHECK. The pages are built line by line, daily; control is built from monthly totals — two routes from the same journals. Routes that must agree, and are compared every month. A line posted to the wrong page, a total miscast — the comparison exposes it while the month is still warm. Remove the control account and errors in forty pages have nowhere to be caught until a customer complains. The summary is not duplication; it is the second witness.

**Thandi:** Here's the tangled one: returns versus allowances. Both reduce what the customer owes, both use a credit note — so why does one touch the stock records and the other not?

**Tutor:** Untangle it by asking what physically happened to the goods — the money side is identical, the goods side is not. A return: the shirt comes back, saleable. The shelf has genuinely regained an asset, so Trading Stock rises by its cost, and that cost stops being a cost of sales — the sale partly un-happened, in money AND in goods. An allowance: the scratched kettle stays with the customer. The business owns nothing more than before; only the price of the completed sale was renegotiated. Write a stock entry there and the books would claim a kettle standing on a shelf where no kettle stands — and the next stocktake would report a phantom surplus. So let the credit note handle the money in both cases, and let the cost column obey the goods alone. Follow the goods — the rule survives every variation the examiners dress it in.

**Thandi:** Last one, from the practical corner: Mrs Dube phones to query her balance. Which book does the shop open — and what if she disagrees with it?

**Tutor:** Open her PAGE — the personal account in the Debtors Ledger — because it holds her complete story in date order: invoice sixty-one for two thousand, the five-hundred-rand return on the twelfth, invoice sixty-three, balance two thousand five hundred. One page, one conversation. If she disagrees, the page's folio references settle it: every line points to a numbered document — an invoice she signed for, a credit note she received. Dispute a line, fetch its paper. That is why documents are numbered and why the monthly statement exists: it mails her a copy of that very page, so disagreements surface while the papers are young. A well-kept debtors system never argues from memory — it argues from the page, and the page argues from the documents.
