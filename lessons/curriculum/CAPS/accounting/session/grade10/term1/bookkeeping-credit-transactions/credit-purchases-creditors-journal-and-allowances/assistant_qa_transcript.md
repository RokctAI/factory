**Thandi:** Question time! The one that tripped half the class: the DEBIT note reduces what we owe — but reducing a debt sounds like a credit-y, kind thing. Why is it called a DEBIT note?

**Tutor:** Name the account it acts on, and the name explains itself. The supplier — Metro — lives in our books as a creditor: a liability with a CREDIT balance. To make that balance smaller, we enter on the opposite side — a DEBIT to Metro's account. The debit note is simply the document announcing that debit: "we are debiting your account by eight hundred rand, for the damaged goods returned." The supplier, whose books show the mirror image — us as a debtor with a debit balance — answers with THEIR credit note, crediting our account on their side. Same event, two books, opposite sides, each document named for the entry it makes in the sender's own ledger. Whenever a document name confuses you, ask: whose book, which account, which side?

***

**Thandi:** Next: why does the business renumber the supplier's invoice? It already has Metro's number printed on it — renumbering feels like busywork.

**Tutor:** Metro's number serves Metro's filing; it does nothing for ours. Imagine the month's invoices from ten suppliers, each numbered in its own sequence — Metro at four thousand something, PNA in the nine hundreds, ShelfCo starting from one. File by THEIR numbers and our records have no order at all: gaps mean nothing, sequence means nothing. Renumbering into our own unbroken sequence — seventy-one, seventy-two, seventy-three — restores the control we built for every other document: a missing number in OUR sequence is a missing document, instantly visible. The supplier's number is not discarded — it is recorded alongside for cross-reference when we query Metro. Two numbers, two jobs: theirs for their book, ours for our discipline.

***

**Thandi:** This one is practical: the delivery driver is standing at the door with boxes, and everyone is busy. Why is the delivery note such a big deal in the lesson — it's just a signature, surely?

**Tutor:** That signature is the single moment the business can ever verify what physically arrived. Before signing, the goods are the supplier's claim; after signing, they are our acknowledged receipt — and every later document stands on it. Sign for ten boxes having counted none, and when the invoice bills ten but the storeroom holds nine, the argument is already lost: our own signature says ten arrived. Count first, sign for what was counted, note shortages ON the delivery note before the driver leaves — then the three-document match has a truthful middle witness, and any dispute resolves from paper, in our favour when we are right. Thirty seconds of counting at the door protects every rand the invoice will later demand. Busy is precisely when businesses get billed for phantom boxes.

***

**Thandi:** Last one: the lesson said supplier credit is 'free financing'. That sounds too good to be true — where's the catch?

**Tutor:** The catch is discipline, which is why the lesson paired the gift with rules. For thirty days, the business trades with Metro's money: stock on the shelf, selling and earning, while the R9 000 stays in our bank. Genuinely free — IF the terms are honoured. Miss the date, and the price appears: interest on overdue accounts in some agreements, tightened terms on the next order, a cooling relationship, a name that travels badly in the trade. And there is an opportunity cost even in virtue: pay needlessly early and the financing you were freely given goes unused. So the skill is exact: take the full term, never a day more, reconcile the statement monthly so no dispute ambushes payment day. Free financing is real — it is simply rented with reliability instead of interest.
