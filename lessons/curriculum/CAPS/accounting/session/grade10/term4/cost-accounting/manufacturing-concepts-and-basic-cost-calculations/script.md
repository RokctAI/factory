# Part 1 — Expert

Until now, Thabo's Trading BOUGHT its goods finished and sold them marked up. Term four opens the factory door: what changes when a business MAKES what it sells? Cost accounting answers the maker's first question — what does one unit actually cost to produce? — and Grade 10 lays the vocabulary and the basic calculations: direct and indirect materials and labour, factory overheads, prime cost, fixed and variable behaviour, and work-in-progress. Master the sorting; the arithmetic is short once every cost is in its right bin.

## Subtopic: Direct and Indirect — the First Sorting

Our example for the topic: Thabo opens a small workshop sewing school shirts — four hundred shirts in an ordinary month. Every cost of making them lands in one of the bins we now define, and the first sorting question is: can this cost be traced to ONE SPECIFIC shirt?

DIRECT MATERIALS: materials that become part of the product and are traceable to each unit — the fabric, R40 of it per shirt. Cut the cloth for shirt number 217 and you know exactly which shirt carries that cost. DIRECT LABOUR: the wages of those whose hands make the product — the machinist paid R25 per shirt sewn. Again, traceable: her work IS the shirt.

Now the INDIRECT family — real costs of making, but spread over ALL the shirts, traceable to none in particular. Indirect materials: thread, needles, machine oil — consumed in production, but nobody can say how much thread went into shirt 217. Indirect labour: the supervisor who watches the whole floor — her wage serves every shirt equally and no shirt specifically. And the other factory costs: the workshop's rent, its electricity, the depreciation on the sewing machines. All of these indirect production costs gather under one name: FACTORY OVERHEADS.

The test, always: trace it to one unit? Direct. Serves production in general? Indirect — overheads. And note the boundary of the factory: the SHOP'S costs — the sales assistant, the shop's rent — are not production costs at all; cost accounting fences the factory and counts only what happens inside the fence.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: the sorting test, applied cost by cost — direct material, direct labour, or overheads — and what stays outside the fence.

## Subtopic: Prime Cost, Overheads and the Cost of One Shirt

Now assemble the month's production cost, layer by layer, the way the formula sheet expects.

Layer one: PRIME COST — the direct costs alone: direct materials plus direct labour. The month: fabric, four hundred shirts at R40 — R16 000; machinist, four hundred at R25 — R10 000. Prime cost: R26 000. The name earns itself: prime, first, the costs that touch the product directly.

Layer two: add the FACTORY OVERHEADS. The month's indirect production costs: workshop rent R3 000; supervisor R2 000; electricity R600; thread and needles R400 — R6 000 in total. TOTAL COST OF PRODUCTION: prime cost R26 000 plus overheads R6 000 — R32 000.

Layer three: the cost of ONE shirt — the UNIT COST: total cost of production over units produced: 32 000 over 400 — R80 per shirt. And now every pricing decision has its floor: sell at R100 and each shirt clears R20; sell below R80 and every sale funds a loss. The unit cost is to the maker what the cost price was to the trader — the number beneath the mark-up.

One more concept the syllabus names: WORK-IN-PROGRESS. At month end, some shirts stand half-sewn — cut fabric, unfinished seams. They are no longer raw material, not yet finished goods: they are work-in-progress, production's halfway house, and their cost sits in the factory until completion carries them out. Grade 10 needs the concept and its place; the full ledger treatment waits for Grade 11.

Stop for this section's questions now — the three layers computed in order: prime, total, unit — and work-in-progress placed between raw and finished.

## Subtopic: Fixed and Variable — the Second Sorting

The first sorting asked WHERE a cost attaches; the second asks HOW A COST BEHAVES when production rises or falls — and this distinction drives every planning decision.

VARIABLE COSTS move WITH output, in step: make more shirts, buy more fabric, pay for more sewing. Fabric at R40 and machinist at R25 are variable: four hundred shirts cost 26 000 in variables; five hundred shirts, 32 500. Per UNIT, though, the variable cost stays constant — every shirt carries its own R65, first shirt or five-hundredth.

FIXED COSTS ignore output within the month: the workshop's rent is R3 000 whether the floor sews one shirt or five hundred; the supervisor's wage likewise. Our overheads — R6 000 — are broadly the fixed family (electricity has a variable element; Grade 10 may treat it simply). Total fixed cost: constant. Per UNIT, fixed cost FALLS as output rises — the same R6 000 spread over more shirts.

Watch the consequence at the unit level. Four hundred shirts: variable R65 plus fixed spread R15 — unit cost R80. Five hundred shirts: variable still R65, but 6 000 over 500 is R12 — unit cost R77. Producing more made each shirt CHEAPER — not by cutting any cost, but by spreading the fixed ones wider. There stands the logic of scale, in one workshop: busy factories out-price idle ones because their rent is thinner per unit.

And the reverse warns: a quiet month at two hundred shirts carries R30 of fixed cost per unit — R95 total — and the comfortable R100 price suddenly clears almost nothing. Fixed costs are the reason quiet months hurt more than they seem.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — behaviour sorting and the unit-cost effect: compute at different volumes, and explain why the unit cost moved.

## Subtopic: The Calculations, Formalised

Gather the topic into its exam shapes.

Shape one: CLASSIFICATION — a list of costs, each to its bin: direct material, direct labour, indirect material, indirect labour, other overheads; then, across the other axis, fixed or variable. Every cost carries BOTH labels: fabric is direct AND variable; workshop rent is overhead AND fixed; the supervisor is indirect labour AND fixed. Two sortings, one table — answer both dimensions when asked.

Shape two: the COST STATEMENT in layers: direct materials plus direct labour — prime cost; plus factory overheads — total cost of production; over units — unit cost. Labelled lines, in that order, every time: 16 000 plus 10 000 — 26 000; plus 6 000 — 32 000; over 400 — R80.

Shape three: the WHAT-IF — recompute at a new volume, respecting behaviour: variables scale, fixeds stand. At 500: 20 000 plus 12 500 — prime 32 500; plus 6 000 — 38 500; over 500 — R77. The error the marker hunts: scaling the fixed costs with volume — multiplying the rent by five-fourths because output rose — which contradicts the very definition of fixed.

Shape four: the DECISION comment: given unit cost and price, advise — the four-move anatomy from term three travels here intact: state, judge, explain, recommend. Unit cost R95 in a quiet month against a R100 price: state the thin margin, judge it risky, explain the fixed-cost spread, recommend building volume or trimming overheads.

Why does this matter beyond the factory? Because next year's manufacturing accounts, and every business decision about pricing, outsourcing and scale, stand on today's two sortings. Where a cost ATTACHES tells you what a product truly costs; how it BEHAVES tells you what changing plans will do.

The final questions of this part are with you now: all four shapes — classify on both axes, build the statement, run the what-if, and advise.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same factory from the kitchen — because everyone who has ever cooked for a family gathering already understands manufacturing costs; they just haven't priced the pots yet.

## Subtopic: Cooking for the Wedding

Your aunt takes an order: four hundred vetkoek for a wedding. Watch her costs sort THEMSELVES into the famous bins.

The flour and the oil that go INTO each vetkoek — she can tell you to the cent what each one carries: that's direct material, the fabric of the vetkoek. The cousin she pays per batch to fry — his hands make the product: direct labour. Now the quieter costs: the gas bottle that heats everything — which vetkoek did the gas cook? All of them; none in particular. The washing-up soap, the wear on her big pots, the neighbour she pays to watch the pots while she takes orders — all real costs of making, none traceable to one vetkoek. That whole family is the overheads: the costs of KEEPING THE KITCHEN COOKING, as opposed to the costs inside each vetkoek.

And the sharp line she draws without thinking: her taxi fare to DELIVER the order isn't a cooking cost at all — the kitchen fence stops at the kitchen door. Selling and delivering live outside it.

One question sorts everything, hers and Thabo's alike: can I point at ONE unit and say "this cost is yours"? Yes — direct. No, it serves the whole kitchen — overhead. Sort with that question and the vocabulary is done.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the wedding order are coming to you right now: sort each cost with the pointing question, and mind the kitchen fence.

## Subtopic: What One Vetkoek Costs

Now the aunt's real question — the one every maker must answer before naming a price: what does ONE cost me to make?

She stacks the costs in three layers, exactly like the workshop. First, what's inside each unit: flour and oil, say R2 per vetkoek, and the cousin's frying, R1 — R3 of direct cost each; four hundred units: R1 200 of prime cost — the FIRST layer, the costs that touch the product. Second, the kitchen's running costs for the job: gas R300, pot-wear R60, the pot-watcher R240 — R600 of overheads. Total cost of the order: R1 800. Third, the division that answers everything: R1 800 over four hundred — R4,50 per vetkoek.

Now she can price with open eyes: sell at R6 and each unit clears R1,50; the whole order clears R600. Price at R4 because a competitor did — and every single vetkoek sells at a 50-cent loss that FEELS like business but is quietly eating her savings. The unit cost is the floor under the price; a maker who doesn't know it isn't pricing, only guessing.

And the half-made tray at day's end — mixed dough, not yet fried? That's her work-in-progress: no longer flour, not yet product, its costs waiting in the kitchen overnight. Every maker has that tray; accounting just gives it a name and keeps its costs counted.

Your questions for this part are up now: build the three layers for a new order — prime, total, per-unit — and find the floor under the price.

## Subtopic: The Rent Doesn't Care How Busy You Are

Last idea — the one that separates makers who survive from makers who wonder where the money went: some costs SCALE with the work, and some costs stand still and must be fed regardless.

The flour scales: double the vetkoek, double the flour, rand for rand — but each unit's share never changes: R3 of direct cost per vetkoek, always. The gas bottle and the pot-watcher don't scale the same way — and the kitchen RENT, if she rented one, wouldn't budge at all: R600 of standing costs for the month, one order or five.

Now watch the standing costs do their quiet arithmetic on the unit. Busy month, four hundred units: R600 spread over 400 — R1,50 each; unit cost R4,50. Great month, six hundred units: the same R600 spreads to R1 each — unit cost R4. She cut no cost, bargained with nobody — busyness itself made every unit cheaper. That's why big bakeries undercut home kitchens: their ovens' rent is sliced thinner per loaf.

And the warning in reverse: a slow month, two hundred units, and the R600 lands R3 on each — unit cost R6, the whole margin gone at the old price. The rent doesn't care how busy you are — which is exactly why YOU must care how busy you are. Standing costs are a drumbeat: match your volume to it or it plays you out of business.

That's the whole topic, honestly: sort costs by where they attach, sort them by how they behave, stack the layers, divide — and read what volume does to the floor under your price.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: scale an order up and down, keep the standing costs standing, and explain — in one clear sentence — why the unit cost moved. Next lesson closes the year: budgets, the art of writing the future down before spending it.
