**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! First: the supervisor gets paid for working on production all day — why is her wage an OVERHEAD when the machinist's is direct labour? Both work in the same room!

**Tutor:** The room is the same; the traceability is not. The machinist's hands touch specific shirts — she is paid R25 per shirt sewn, and shirt 217 carries her R25 as surely as it carries its fabric. The supervisor's attention spreads across the whole floor: she watches four hundred shirts being made, and no accountant can honestly say how much of her R2 000 belongs to any one of them. Direct labour is the labour IN the product; indirect labour is the labour AROUND the production. And notice the test never mentions job titles or importance — a supervisor matters enormously; she is simply untraceable per unit. If the workshop one day paid her per shirt inspected, that portion would become traceable and the classification would follow the facts. Classification follows traceability, not status.

***

**Thandi:** Next: why do we bother splitting prime cost from overheads at all? In the end everything gets added into the total anyway — why the ceremony of layers?

**Tutor:** Because the layers answer different management questions before the total answers the pricing one. Prime cost is the pure product cost — what one unit demands in materials and hands — and it behaves predictably: double the output, double the prime. Watch it per unit across months, and any movement means the product itself changed: fabric prices rose, or the sewing slowed. Overheads are the factory's standing establishment, and their diseases are different: rent renegotiations, electricity creep, a second supervisor hired too soon. Mix the layers into one number and both diagnoses blur — you would see the unit cost rise without knowing whether the product or the establishment is at fault. The same logic as term three's three-floor income statement: subtotals exist because problems live at specific layers. Add everything up only AFTER the layers have told their separate stories.

***

**Thandi:** This one caused a debate: electricity in the workshop — the lesson called it broadly fixed, but surely sewing machines use more power when they sew more? Which is it?

**Tutor:** Both — and your debate is the correct answer. Real electricity is a MIXED cost: a standing portion — the lights, the iron heating up each morning — that burns whether one shirt or five hundred gets sewn, plus a variable sliver that tracks the machines' running hours. Grade 10 simplifies it into the fixed family because the standing portion dominates in a small workshop and because the syllabus wants the two pure behaviours mastered first. But hold your instinct, because it is the mature truth: costs sit on a SPECTRUM from purely variable (fabric) to purely fixed (rent), with mixed costs between, and later grades split them formally into their two parts. For now, the exam will label costs cleanly or follow the textbook convention — answer with the convention, and if a question invites discussion, earn the extra mark by noting the mixed nature exactly as you just did. Doubting a tidy classification for a good reason is not confusion; it is accounting.

***

**Thandi:** Last one: the busy-month magic — unit cost falling from R80 to R77 just by making more. If volume makes everything cheaper, why doesn't every factory simply produce as much as possible?

**Tutor:** Because the magic has three limits, and running past any of them turns it into a trap. Limit one: DEMAND — the unit cost falls only on shirts that SELL; five hundred made and four hundred sold leaves a hundred shirts of cost sitting on the shelf as stock, and if they never sell, the "cheaper" units became pure loss. Limit two: CAPACITY — the workshop's machines, hours and floor space carry a ceiling, and pushing beyond it doesn't spread the fixed costs; it CREATES new ones — overtime at the multipliers you studied, a second machine, a bigger workshop — a step upward in the standing costs that resets the whole arithmetic. Limit three: QUALITY — a floor racing to maximise volume starts producing rejects, and a rejected shirt carries full cost and zero price. So the honest principle is: produce as much as you can SELL, within capacity, at quality — the fixed-cost spread rewards fullness, not recklessness. The drumbeat image ends the year well: match your volume to the drumbeat — don't try to outrun the drum.
