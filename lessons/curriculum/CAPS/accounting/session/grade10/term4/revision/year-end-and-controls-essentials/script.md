# Part 1 — Expert

The first revision session rebuilt the recording machine; this one revises what happens when the year ENDS — and what guards the machine all year. Four stations: the adjustments that make the figures true; the final accounts and the two statements that report them; the percentages and ratios that read the results; and the guard-rails — internal control, ethics, GAAP — together with the cost and budget arithmetic that closed the year. One business carries every figure: Thabo's Trading, year ended the twenty-eighth of February, and every number will balance on the way through.

## Subtopic: The Adjustments That Make the Year True

The trial balance at year-end is honest about the past but wrong about the year, because money's calendar and earning's calendar disagree. Adjustments repair the difference — the MATCHING principle in action: this year's statement must carry this year's income and this year's expenses, no more, no less.

Run Thabo's four. DEPRECIATION: the vehicle cost R40 000, straight-line at twenty percent — R8 000 for the year. The equipment cost R10 000, diminishing balance at ten percent, accumulated depreciation R1 000 — so ten percent of the carrying value of R9 000: R900. Total depreciation R8 900, an expense with no cash movement, and the accumulated depreciation accounts grow by the same amounts. ACCRUED EXPENSE: water and electricity paid R2 400, but February's account of R400 is unpaid — expense becomes R2 800, and R400 stands as a current liability. PREPAID EXPENSE: insurance paid R1 800 includes R300 covering next year — expense becomes R1 500, and R300 stands as a current asset. ACCRUED INCOME: rent income received R5 500 at R500 a month, one month outstanding — income becomes R6 000, and R500 stands as a current asset.

Each adjustment is the same two-sided move: correct the income or expense to the TRUE year figure, and park the difference on the statement of financial position as something owed or something owned.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: both depreciation methods computed, and the four timing repairs with their balance-sheet homes.

## Subtopic: From Final Accounts to the Two Statements

Year-end closes the books in a fixed sequence. The TRADING ACCOUNT gathers sales against cost of sales: sales R120 000, cost of sales R80 000 — gross profit R40 000, and check the policy: fifty percent mark-up on cost, eighty thousand times one comma five. The PROFIT AND LOSS ACCOUNT takes the gross profit, adds other income — rent income R6 000 — and absorbs the operating expenses: wages 18 000, water and electricity 2 800, insurance 1 500, depreciation 8 900 — R31 200 in all. Net profit: 40 000 plus 6 000 minus 31 200 — R14 800, closed away to Capital. Then drawings of R6 800 close against Capital too, and the POST-CLOSING trial balance carries only balance-sheet accounts into the new year.

The statements dress the same figures formally. The STATEMENT OF COMPREHENSIVE INCOME tells the year as a story: sales down to gross profit, down to net profit R14 800. The STATEMENT OF FINANCIAL POSITION photographs midnight on the last day: non-current assets at carrying value R70 300 — land and buildings 38 200, vehicles 40 000 less accumulated depreciation 16 000, equipment 10 000 less 1 900; current assets R15 400 — trading stock 8 000, debtors 4 500, bank 2 100, prepaid insurance 300, accrued income 500. Total assets R85 700. Against them: owner's equity — opening 70 000 plus profit 14 800 less drawings 6 800 — R78 000; current liabilities R7 700 — creditors 7 300 plus accrued expenses 400. Total R85 700. The photograph balances.

Stop for this section's questions now — the closing sequence in order, net profit built, and both sides of the photograph totalled.

## Subtopic: The Gauges — Reading the Results

Statements report; ratios INTERPRET — and every comment needs a comparison: against last year, against the policy, against the danger line.

Profitability first. Gross profit on cost: 40 000 over 80 000 — fifty percent, exactly the mark-up policy, so no stock leaked unrecorded. Gross profit on sales: 40 000 over 120 000 — thirty-three comma three percent. Net profit on sales: 14 800 over 120 000 — twelve comma three percent: of every hundred rand through the till, about twelve stayed as profit after all costs.

Liquidity next — can the near bills be paid? CURRENT RATIO: current assets 15 400 over current liabilities 7 700 — two to one: two rand of near money for every rand of near debt, comfortable. ACID TEST strips out stock, the slow asset: 7 400 over 7 700 — nought comma nine six to one, just under one to one: without selling a single item, the shop can ALMOST cover its immediate debts — acceptable, worth watching.

SOLVENCY: total assets 85 700 against total liabilities 7 700 — the business could pay everything owed eleven times over; failure is nowhere near. And the owner's question, RETURN ON EQUITY: net profit 14 800 over average equity — 70 000 opening, 78 000 closing, average 74 000 — twenty percent. Compare it to what a bank pays on savings: several times better, and the difference is the reward for effort and risk. The technique for every comment: figure, comparison, judgement — never a number left naked.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — each gauge computed, and each one commented with its comparison.

## Subtopic: Guards, Rules and the Cost of One Unit

What kept the figures believable all year? Three layers. INTERNAL CONTROL: division of duties — the one who records is not the one who banks; documents for every movement, numbered and kept; authorisation before money leaves; physical safeguards and regular independent checks — stock counted, cash reconciled. Every control answers a named risk, and exam scenarios reward naming both. ETHICS: the bookkeeper's figures must carry integrity, objectivity, confidentiality and competence — because a control system run by a dishonest hand protects nothing. GAAP: the agreed rules that make one business readable by another — historical cost, recording at what was paid; prudence, anticipating losses but never profits; matching, the engine behind every adjustment; going concern, valuing the business as continuing; business entity, the owner and the business as separate persons; materiality, disclosing what would change a reader's decision.

And the year's last arithmetic, revised in one breath. COST: making 1 000 shirts took direct materials R6 000 and direct labour R4 000 — prime cost R10 000; overheads R2 000; total R12 000; unit cost R12. Selling at R15 leaves R3 per shirt. BUDGET: next month written in advance — receipts 9 000, payments 7 500, surplus 1 500, opening balance 1 000, closing R2 500 — the cash-only rule holding: no depreciation, credit collections counted when they arrive.

The final questions of this part are with you now: controls matched to risks, principles matched to scenarios, and the unit cost and budget skeleton computed clean.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the year-end from the stoep of the shop — because closing a year is something every household already understands: the honest stocktake before the new start.

## Subtopic: Tidying the Room Before the Photo

Year-end is photo day: one picture of the business, taken at midnight on the last day, that outsiders will trust. But before any photo, you tidy the room — and that is all adjustments are.

The bakkie standing in the yard is not worth what it cost three years ago; the books must admit the wearing — that is depreciation, R8 900 of honesty this year, no cash moving, just truth arriving. The electricity used in February but billed in March belongs to THIS year's picture — pull it in, R400 still owed. The insurance paid ahead for next year does not belong in this picture — push R300 out, it is cover the shop still OWNS. The tenant's last month of rent, earned but not yet received — pull it in, R500 owed to us. Four movements, one principle: the photo must show what this year truly used and truly earned, not what the bank account happened to see.

And feel why anyone bothers: an untidied photo flatters or slanders. Skip depreciation and the shop looks richer than it is; skip the owed electricity and expenses look smaller than they were. The neat photo is not for the photographer — it is for everyone who must trust the picture: the bank, the taxman, the owner's own decisions.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the tidying are coming to you right now: each adjustment named, and where its leftover piece stands in the photo.

## Subtopic: The Report Card and the Health Check

Two documents come out of photo day, and they answer two different questions a parent would ask about a child.

The REPORT CARD — the statement of comprehensive income — answers: how did the year GO? Read Thabo's like a story with three floors. Top floor: bought for eighty, sold for a hundred and twenty — the buying-and-selling game earned R40 000. Middle floor: add the tenant's rent, R6 000. Bottom floor: subtract everything it cost to keep the doors open — wages, lights, cover, wearing — R31 200 — and the year's true mark is R14 800. One page, and the owner knows whether the effort was worth it.

The HEALTH CHECK — the ratios — answers: how is the body DOING underneath the mark? Money for the month's bills: two rand ready for every rand owed — healthy. Without selling stock: ninety-six cents for every rand — just short, keep an eye. If everything ended tomorrow: assets cover debts eleven times — no danger. And the owner's own question: the money left in the business earned twenty out of every hundred rand — far better than the bank pays, so the sweat was worth it. Numbers alone say nothing; numbers COMPARED say everything. Every comment in the exam is the same little sentence: here is the figure, here is what I compared it to, here is my judgement.

Your questions for this part are up now: the three floors of the report card, and the health check read out loud with comparisons.

## Subtopic: Locks on the Doors, and What One Vetkoek Costs

Last stretch — the guards and the two street arithmetics.

The guards first, as a story. A tuck shop where one person takes the money, counts the money, records the money and banks the money is not a system; it is an invitation. The fixes are old and simple: split the jobs so no hand touches money end to end; a slip for every movement, numbered so a missing one shouts; nothing paid without a second nod; the stock counted by someone who does not sell it. And under the locks, the person: rules cannot save books kept by a dishonest hand, which is why the ethics list — honesty, fairness, keeping confidences, doing the job properly — is not decoration. It is the foundation the locks stand on.

Then the vetkoek arithmetic, the year's most useful street skill. To know what ONE vetkoek costs, gather everything the batch consumed: flour and oil R6 000, the hands that made them R4 000, the stall and the gas R2 000 — twelve thousand rand for a thousand vetkoek — twelve rand each. Sell at fifteen and every one carries three rand home. Price without knowing the twelve, and you might work all year to go backwards. And the last habit: before the month starts, write it down — what should come in, what must go out, what survives. Nine thousand in, seven and a half out, purse grows to two and a half. The year taught many formats; underneath them all it taught three street laws: guard the money with split hands and slips, know what one unit truly costs, and write the month before living it.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: the weak tuck shop repaired, one vetkoek costed, and the month written in advance. Finish them and the year's whole toolkit — recording, closing, reading, guarding, costing, planning — is revised and yours.
