**Thandi:** Revision questions from the class! First one: if the trial balance can't catch wrong-account postings anyway, why do we bother drawing it up every single month?

**Tutor:** Because a test that catches MOST errors cheaply is worth running before the tests that catch the rest expensively. The trial balance costs an hour and instantly exposes the whole family of one-sided mistakes: a debit posted without its credit, unequal amounts in a pair, a casting error inside an account, a balance carried down wrongly. Those are the commonest errors in real bookkeeping, and the scale finds them the same day. What survives the scale — wrong-account postings, complete omissions, compensating errors — is exactly what the OTHER guards exist for: control accounts catch wrong-page and wrong-total errors, reconciliations catch what the business never recorded, and source-document checks catch what never entered the books at all. The system is layered deliberately: each check is imperfect alone and formidable together. An examiner who asks "what does trial balance agreement prove?" is testing whether you see the layers — answer that it proves the equality of the double entry, and name what it cannot see, and you have shown the whole design.

**Thandi:** Next: cost of sales confuses people every year — why does a SALE make us record an expense at the same time?

**Tutor:** Because under the perpetual system every sale is two events happening in one moment, and the books refuse to tell only half the story. Event one: the customer's money or promise comes in — sales, an income, measured at the selling price. Event two: goods leave the shelf forever — and the shelf is an ASSET called trading stock, so the business just lost an asset whose cost must be recorded as an expense, cost of sales, measured at COST price. Two prices for one bundle of goods, because the two events answer different questions: what did we earn, and what did it cost us to earn it. Hold them apart and the gross profit falls out by simple subtraction — the month's sales of nine thousand against cost of sales of six thousand is gross profit of three thousand, exactly the fifty percent mark-up on cost the business intended. Learners who merge the two prices, or forget the second entry on returns, break that chain. The habit that saves you: every movement of stock gets two lines — one at selling price, one at cost — in both directions, sales and returns alike.

**Thandi:** This one's sharp: my aunt's shop keeps just one notebook and she says it works fine. What breaks first when a business skips the full system?

**Tutor:** Credit breaks first, and it breaks quietly. One notebook records what happened well enough while everything is cash — money in the tin, stock on the shelf, no promises outstanding. The moment the shop sells on trust, it needs to know PER CUSTOMER who owes what, and needs a second, independent total to check those pages against — because memory inflates goodwill and customers' memories deflate debt, and without records the difference becomes a quarrel instead of a lookup. The next break is invisibility of loss: with no cost-of-sales record, stolen or wasted stock simply vanishes into "the money feels small this month" — the one-notebook shop cannot distinguish theft from thin sales, so it cannot fix either. And the third break comes from outside: the day the aunt wants a loan, a supplier account or a partner, someone entitled to ask will ask for proof, and a single notebook proves nothing because nothing checks it. The full system is not bureaucracy — it is exactly the set of records that let a business trade on trust, see its losses, and be believed.

**Thandi:** Last one: in the exam, the control account question gives a balance, a list that disagrees, and a pile of errors. What's the method that never fails?

**Tutor:** Two columns and one question per error. Head one column "control account", the other "list of debtors", and write the given balances at the top. Then take each error in turn and ask only: which SOURCE did this error touch? An error in a journal total — an undercast debtors journal, a receipts column added wrongly — flowed into the control account, so correct the control column. An error in an individual posting — a payment on the wrong customer's page, an invoice never posted to the personal account — damaged the list, so correct the list column. Some errors touch both, such as an invoice completely omitted from the journal: then both columns move. Work with signs, not stories: increases plus, decreases minus, and never reverse a correction because the wording sounds negative. When every error is placed, total both columns — they must meet, and that shared figure is the TRUE amount owed by debtors. If they refuse to meet, one error is in the wrong column; re-ask the source question for each. It is a sorting exercise wearing a scary mask — and the method marks come from showing each correction in its column, not from the final figure alone.
