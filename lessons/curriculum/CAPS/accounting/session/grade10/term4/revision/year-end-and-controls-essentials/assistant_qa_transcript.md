**Thandi:** Revision questions coming in! First: adjustments never involve cash — so if no money moved, why do the figures change at all? It feels like inventing numbers.

**Tutor:** It is the opposite of inventing — it is refusing to let the bank account tell the whole story, because the bank account is a witness with a narrow view. Cash records answer one question only: when did money move? The year's TRUE question is different: what did this year consume, and what did it earn? Those calendars disagree constantly — electricity used in February is billed in March; insurance paid in one lump covers months that belong to next year; the tenant's rent is earned before it arrives; the bakkie wears out a little every trip with no invoice ever arriving. If the statements followed cash alone, February's electricity would belong to next year and the bakkie would stay forever young — figures anyone could see are false. Each adjustment brings a documented, calculable truth into the year it belongs to: the R400 owed is on the municipal account, the R300 prepaid is on the policy, the R8 900 of wear follows a stated method and rate. Matching is the principle's name, and the test for any adjustment is a question, not a feeling: did THIS year use it, or earn it? If yes, it enters this year's figures — whatever the bank saw.

**Thandi:** Next one: why does the diminishing balance method use carrying value when straight-line uses cost? Learners mix those two up every single year.

**Tutor:** Hold the picture each method paints, and the base chooses itself. Straight-line says: this asset gives the same service every year of its life, so its cost is sliced evenly — twenty percent of COST is the same R8 000 slice every year until the asset is written down. Diminishing balance says something different: this asset loses most of its value early — true of vehicles and technology — so the charge should be big at first and shrink each year. The only way a fixed percentage can produce a shrinking charge is to work on a shrinking base, and the base that shrinks is the CARRYING VALUE — cost minus all depreciation already accumulated. Thabo's equipment: ten percent of the R9 000 carrying value gives R900 this year; next year the carrying value is R8 100, so the charge falls to R810. Apply ten percent to cost instead and you have accidentally done straight-line at a different rate — the classic exam error. The habit that prevents it: before calculating, write the base next to the method — straight-line, cost; diminishing, carrying value — and if an asset was bought partway through the year, multiply by the months owned over twelve. Base first, months second, calculator last.

**Thandi:** This one's from the back row: the acid test came out at nought comma nine six to one. Is that a fail? It's below one, and below one sounds like failing.

**Tutor:** Below one is a fact; failing is a judgement, and the judgement needs context the number alone does not carry. What 0,96 to one says precisely: if every current liability knocked on the door at once, the shop could cover ninety-six cents of each rand without selling any stock. Whether that is dangerous depends on three things a good comment weighs. First, direction: if last year read 0,7 and this year 0,96, liquidity is strengthening — the same figure with the opposite meaning to a slide from 1,4. Second, the nature of the stock: a shop whose stock sells within days can lean on it safely, so a ratio just under one with fast-moving stock is comfortable; a business with slow, seasonal stock needs more cover. Third, the timing of the debts: creditors due in ninety days are a different threat from an overdraft repayable on demand. So the exam sentence is never "below one, therefore bad" — it is figure, comparison, judgement: the acid test is 0,96 to one, close to the safe benchmark of one and up from last year, so immediate debts are nearly covered without stock — acceptable, monitor it. That earns the interpretation marks the bare number forfeits.

**Thandi:** Last question: internal control, ethics and GAAP get revised together — but in the exam, how do I know WHICH of the three a scenario is actually asking about?

**Tutor:** Listen for what is broken, because each of the three guards a different thing. If the scenario describes money or stock at RISK — one person handling cash end to end, no documents, no counts, no authorisation — it is an internal control question, and the answer names the risk and the specific control that answers it: division of duties, numbered documents, independent checks. If the scenario describes a PERSON facing a temptation or a pressure — asked to hide a figure, tempted to share a customer's information, covering for a friend — it is an ethics question, and the answer names the characteristic under threat: integrity, objectivity, confidentiality, competence — and states what the person should do. If the scenario describes a RECORDING CHOICE — which value to record an asset at, whether to anticipate a profit, whether the owner's holiday may go through the business — it is a GAAP question, and the answer names the principle: historical cost, prudence, business entity, matching, going concern, materiality. Broken safeguards, tested character, or disputed recording rule — sort the scenario into one of those three sentences first, and the mark scheme's language follows almost word for word.
