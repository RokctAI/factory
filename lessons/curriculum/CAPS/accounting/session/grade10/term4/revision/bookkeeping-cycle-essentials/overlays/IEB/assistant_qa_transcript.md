**Thandi:** Revision questions from the class! First: with eight transactions and six journals, some of us still freeze on WHICH journal. Is there a fail-safe way to choose under exam pressure?

**Tutor:** There is, and it is the same two questions the lesson kept repeating — asked in order, every time, with no shortcuts. Question one: did CASH move, or only a promise? If cash moved, you are in one of the two cash journals, and the direction settles it — in is the CRJ, out is the CPJ. Done. If only a promise moved, question two: which way is the value travelling, and is it the original deal or a reversal? Value out to a customer on trust — Debtors Journal; that customer sending goods back — Debtors Allowances. Value in from a supplier on trust — Creditors Journal; goods going back to the supplier — Creditors Allowances. And if the transaction is none of these — a correction, interest charged, a write-off — the General Journal is the catch-all, and its very strangeness is the clue. Freezing happens when learners try to remember the ANSWER for each transaction type; the cure is remembering the two QUESTIONS instead. Eight transactions or eighty, the questions never change.

***

**Thandi:** Next: why does the CRJ carry the cost of sales next to a cash sale? Recording R6 000 of cost inside a RECEIPTS journal feels backwards.

**Tutor:** It feels backwards until you see that one event genuinely has two stories. When the till rings R7 500, two things happened in the same second: money arrived, and goods worth R6 000 left the shelf for good. The perpetual system insists on telling both stories immediately — sale recorded, cost of sales recorded, stock reduced — so that at any moment the stock account claims exactly what the shelf should hold. Park the cost story for later and your books spend the whole month believing in stock that already walked out the door. The cost column in the CRJ is not a payment — no cash left — it is the shelf's side of the sale, travelling with it. And it hands you a free checking tool: the mark-up. Cost 6 000 times one comma two five gives sales of 7 500 — if the two columns stop obeying the policy, either an error crept in or stock is leaving without being recorded. Two stories, told together, checking each other: that is the perpetual system's whole personality.

***

**Thandi:** Third one: the trial balance balanced, but the lesson still called it an imperfect test. If it can miss errors anyway, why do we bother with it?

**Tutor:** Because a test that catches MOST errors cheaply is worth running even though it cannot catch all of them. The trial balance instantly exposes the mechanical failures — a debit posted without its credit, unequal amounts in a pair, an addition slip inside an account. Those are the commonest accidents in bookkeeping, and one scan of two totals finds them. What it cannot see are the errors that keep the pairs equal: the right amount in the wrong account, a transaction omitted entirely, or two errors cancelling each other. That is not a reason to drop the test — it is the reason the system LAYERS its tests. The trial balance checks the pairing; the control accounts check the debtors and creditors against their own pages; reconciliations check the bank against its statement; the physical stock count checks the shelf against the books. Each net has different holes, and the holes do not line up. An error that slips through one net swims into the next. No single check is perfect; the overlap is what makes the system hard to fool.

***

**Thandi:** Last one: exam technique. When a question gives us a control balance and a customer list that DON'T agree, plus a handful of errors — where do we start?

**Tutor:** Start by drawing two columns and refusing to touch any number until each error has an address. Column one is the control account — it is disturbed only by errors in JOURNAL TOTALS: a miscast column, a total posted twice, a journal total posted to the wrong side. Column two is the list of individual balances — disturbed only by errors on the CUSTOMER PAGES: a receipt on the wrong page, an invoice never posted to a page, a balance copied wrongly onto the list. Read each given error slowly and ask one question: did this happen in a journal total, or on a page? Write its correction in that column only — never both, unless the error genuinely lives in both places. Then total each column: corrected control on one side, corrected list on the other. If you addressed every error correctly, the two figures meet — and that meeting is your own proof, sitting inside your answer before any marking happens. Learners lose marks by correcting a page error in the control account, so let the address do the deciding, not instinct. Two columns, one question per error, and the counts walk toward each other.
