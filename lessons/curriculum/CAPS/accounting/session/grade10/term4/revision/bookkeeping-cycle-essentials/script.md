# Part 1 — Expert

Examinations reward the learner who sees the year as ONE machine, not ten separate topics. This revision session rebuilds the recording machine end to end: the accounting equation that governs every entry, the six journals that capture a month, the posting that gathers the month into the ledger, the trial balance that proves it, and the control accounts that check it. One small month of transactions runs through the whole machine, every figure balancing on the way — the same month the examiner will give you, only with different numbers.

## Subtopic: The Equation Behind Every Entry

Everything rests on one sentence: ASSETS equal OWNER'S EQUITY plus LIABILITIES. What the business OWNS is financed by what the owner put in and what outsiders lent — always, at every moment, after every transaction. And because the equation must stay level, every transaction has TWO effects: a debit and a credit, equal in amount. That is DOUBLE ENTRY — not a rule invented for teachers, but the arithmetic of the equation defending itself.

Hold the debit-credit compass for the five account types. ASSETS live on the debit side: they increase debit, decrease credit. EXPENSES and DRAWINGS behave like assets — increase debit. OWNER'S EQUITY, LIABILITIES and INCOME live on the credit side: they increase credit, decrease debit. Every entry the year threw at you obeys this compass; when unsure, ask two questions — WHICH two accounts, and WHICH one increases.

Test it on the month we will carry through this whole session: Thabo's Trading. The owner deposits R10 000 capital. Bank — an asset — increases: debit Bank R10 000. Capital — owner's equity — increases: credit Capital R10 000. The equation after one entry: assets R10 000 equal equity R10 000 plus liabilities nought. Level. Every transaction that follows, however elaborate, is this same move wearing different clothes.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: the equation stated, the compass applied, and the double effect named for a capital deposit.

## Subtopic: Six Journals, One Month

The journals are the diaries — each transaction type has its own book and its own source document. Cash IN goes to the CASH RECEIPTS JOURNAL from receipts and cash register tapes. Cash OUT goes to the CASH PAYMENTS JOURNAL from EFT records and cheque counterfoils. Credit SALES go to the DEBTORS JOURNAL from duplicate invoices; goods a debtor returns go to the DEBTORS ALLOWANCES JOURNAL from credit notes. Credit PURCHASES go to the CREDITORS JOURNAL from original invoices; returns to suppliers go to the CREDITORS ALLOWANCES JOURNAL from debit notes. And everything that fits no diary — corrections, write-offs, interest charged — goes to the GENERAL JOURNAL.

Now record Thabo's month. Cash sales R6 900, cost of sales R4 600 — the CRJ carries both, because the perpetual system records the sale AND the shelf's loss together; check the mark-up: 4 600 times one comma five is 6 900. A credit sale to Mrs Dube, R2 400, cost R1 600 — Debtors Journal. Stock bought for cash R5 000, rent R1 500, wages R800 — CPJ. Stock bought on credit from the wholesaler, R3 000 — Creditors Journal. Mrs Dube returns goods of R300, cost R200 — Debtors Allowances Journal, credit note issued. She then pays R1 000 on her account — CRJ. And Thabo pays the wholesaler R2 000 — CPJ.

Eight transactions, six doors, every one chosen by two facts only: cash or credit, and which direction the value moves.

Stop for this section's questions now — documents matched to journals, and the month's transactions posted to the right doors.

## Subtopic: Posting, Balancing and the Trial Balance That Proves It

Month-end gathers the diaries into the GENERAL LEDGER — totals for the big columns, item by item for sundries — and then every account is balanced. Follow the month home. Bank: in came 10 000 plus 6 900 plus 1 000 — R17 900; out went 5 000 plus 1 500 plus 800 plus 2 000 — R9 300; balance R8 600 debit. Trading stock: in 5 000 plus 3 000 plus the R200 cost of the return; out 4 600 plus 1 600 as cost of sales; balance R2 000 debit. Debtors control: 2 400 owed, less the 300 allowance, less the 1 000 payment — R1 100 debit. Creditors control: 3 000 owed less 2 000 paid — R1 000 credit. Sales: 6 900 plus 2 400 less returns 300 — R9 000 credit. Cost of sales: 4 600 plus 1 600 less 200 — R6 000 debit. Rent R1 500, wages R800, capital R10 000.

Now the TRIAL BALANCE — the month on a scale. Debits: bank 8 600, trading stock 2 000, debtors control 1 100, cost of sales 6 000, rent 1 500, wages 800 — R20 000. Credits: capital 10 000, sales 9 000, creditors control 1 000 — R20 000. The scale is level, and notice what that proves and what it does not: equal totals prove the double entry held; they cannot see an entry posted to the WRONG account, or a transaction left out on both sides. The trial balance is a strong test, not a perfect one — which is exactly why the next layer of checking exists.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — the balances computed, the trial balance totalled, and its blind spots named.

## Subtopic: Control Accounts — the Second Count

The ledger holds ONE account called Debtors Control; the debtors ledger holds a PAGE PER CUSTOMER. Same information, counted twice, from different sources — the control account built from JOURNAL TOTALS, the individual accounts built from INDIVIDUAL POSTINGS. At month-end the list of individual balances must equal the control balance, and when the two counts of the same street disagree, an error exists and its hiding place is already half-found.

Run the check on Thabo's month. Control account: debit side, credit sales 2 400; credit side, allowances 300 and receipts 1 000; balance R1 100. The list: Mrs Dube alone — invoice 2 400, less credit note 300, less payment 1 000 — R1 100. Agreement. The creditors side mirrors everything: control credited with purchases 3 000, debited with payments 2 000, balance R1 000 — and the wholesaler's account in the creditors ledger says the same R1 000.

What the agreement catches: a receipt posted to the wrong customer's page changes the list but not the control; a journal total miscast changes the control but not the list; an invoice never posted to the individual account leaves the list short. Each disagreement points at its own kind of error — and that is internal control in miniature: two independent records, built by different routes, forced to face each other monthly. The examiner's favourite question here gives you a control balance and a list that differ, plus four errors; correct each record and watch them meet.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the two counts distinguished, the agreement performed, and errors matched to the record they disturb.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the whole machine again, on the kitchen table — because the year's bookkeeping is a spaza shop's ordinary week, written down properly.

## Subtopic: One Slip, Six Doors

Picture the shoebox every small shop keeps: every slip of paper from the month thrown in — till tapes, invoices, credit notes, EFT stubs. Bookkeeping begins as a SORTING job. Six boxes stand on the table, and every slip belongs in exactly one. Money came IN — receipts box. Money went OUT — payments box. Sold on trust — invoice-out box. Customer brought goods back — credit-note box. Bought on trust — invoice-in box. Sent goods back to the supplier — debit-note box. And the odd slip that fits nowhere — the catch-all box at the end.

Two questions sort every slip: did cash move, or a promise? And which way did the value travel? Thabo's till tape of R6 900 — cash in. Mrs Dube's invoice of R2 400 — a promise, value out to her. The wholesaler's invoice of R3 000 — a promise, value in from him. Sort the month and the shoebox chaos becomes six tidy piles — and each pile becomes one journal, already totalled, ready for the ledger. The system the year taught is nothing more mysterious than that sorting, done with discipline, every single month.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the sorting are coming to you right now: slips to boxes, and the two questions that never fail.

## Subtopic: The Month That Adds Up Twice

Why post it all into a ledger? Because the shoebox answers "what happened" but never "where do we STAND". The ledger regroups the month by THING instead of by date: one page for the bank, one for the stock, one for what customers owe, one for what the shop owes. Ask the bank page and it answers R8 600 in one line. Ask the stock page — R2 000 still on the shelf. Ask Mrs Dube's page — R1 100 still promised.

Then comes the weigh-in. Every entry all month was a pair — something debited, something credited, equal amounts. So if the machine was fed honestly, all the debit balances heaped on one side of the scale and all the credit balances on the other must WEIGH THE SAME. Thabo's month: twenty thousand on the left, twenty thousand on the right. Level scale, honest month.

And one street-smart caution: a level scale proves the pairs were equal — it cannot smell a pair written into the wrong pages, or a slip that never left someone's pocket. A thief who steals a slip before the shoebox steals invisibly, as far as the scale knows. The scale is the first guard, not the only one — which is why the shop counts the street twice.

Your questions for this part are up now: what the ledger answers that the shoebox cannot, and what the level scale proves and misses.

## Subtopic: Counting the Street Twice

The second guard. The shop keeps an exercise book with a page for every customer who buys on trust — Mrs Dube's page, and everyone else's. Separately, the main books keep ONE line: total owed by all customers. Two records of the same street, built from different papers — the exercise book from each slip as it happens, the total line from the month's journal totals.

Month-end, the shop adds up every page in the exercise book and puts the sum next to the total line. They must agree — Thabo's month: eleven hundred rand, both counts. When they do not, nobody panics and nobody guesses: the DIFFERENCE itself gives directions. Wrote a payment on the wrong customer's page? The exercise book is wrong, the total line right. Added a journal column wrongly? The total line is wrong, the pages right. Two witnesses who never compared notes, telling the same story — that is what makes the books BELIEVABLE, to the owner, to the bank, to anyone entitled to ask.

Step back and see the year whole: sort the slips, write the diaries, gather the ledger, weigh the trial balance, count the street twice. Five habits, one machine — and every examination question is the machine running with different slips. And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: the two records named, the agreement performed once more, and each error sent to the record it damages. Master the machine, and the marks follow it home.
