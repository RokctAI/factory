**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close the year! First one: a budget is built on guesses about the future — and guesses go wrong. Why would any serious business stake real decisions on numbers everybody KNOWS are estimates?

**Tutor:** Because the alternative to an estimate is not certainty — it is blindness. Nobody is ever choosing between a perfect forecast and an imperfect one; the choice is between an explicit plan that can be checked and no plan at all. And a budget keeps its value even when it is wrong: if November's sales land at twenty-seven thousand against a budgeted thirty, the budget did not fail — it manufactured the exact question "where did three thousand go?" within weeks of the problem starting, instead of at year-end when the damage has compounded. Estimates also sharpen with use: this year's variances teach next year's budget. A business run on written guesses corrected monthly beats a business run on no numbers, every single time. The discipline was never about predicting perfectly; it is about writing down what you expect so that reality has something to argue with.

**Thandi:** Next: the lathe costs R9 000 in November's cash budget — but the depreciation lesson taught us to spread an asset's cost over its working years. Which treatment is right?

**Tutor:** Both — because they answer different questions, and keeping them apart is real mastery. The cash budget asks: what money LEAVES in November? Nine thousand rand, in one payment — and November's bank balance must survive that fact, which is why the full amount sits in that column. The income statement asks a different question: what did THIS YEAR consume of the lathe's working life? A slice — the depreciation — spread across its years by matching. Same machine, two calendars: the cash calendar records the buying; the earning calendar records the using. And notice that the two documents never borrow each other's items: depreciation appears in the statement but never in the cash budget, because no rand moves when value wears away; the purchase price hits the cash budget in full but reaches the income statement only in slices. Learners who let the calendars blur write depreciation into cash budgets — the classic error. Hold onto the question being asked, and the placement answers itself.

***

**Thandi:** This one from the back row: the lesson says arrange an overdraft IN ADVANCE if the budget shows a shortfall. Why would the bank care whether you ask before or after the money runs out?

**Tutor:** Because the timing of the request tells the bank who is asking. Walk in during September with a budget showing a planned R4 000 dip in November, and you demonstrate precisely what a lender wants to see: you understand your own cash flows, the shortfall is temporary, its cause is a productive asset, and the recovery is already on paper in December. The bank prices that as low risk — a modest facility, agreed calmly, on decent terms. Walk in during November with the account already empty and debit orders bouncing, and the identical shortfall tells the opposite story: a business that never saw its own dip coming, asking under pressure, with a crisis as its opening evidence. Emergency money is expensive money — higher rates, harder conditions, sometimes a flat refusal. A budget converts borrowing from a confession into a proposal. That one practical habit — showing the bank the dip before living through it — is worth more than any interest-rate table.

***

**Thandi:** Last question of the whole year: we've covered journals, ledgers, statements, ratios, costs, budgets... if one habit from Grade 10 Accounting should follow us for life, which one is it?

**Tutor:** Write money down — before, during and after. That is the entire subject folded into one habit, and every topic this year was a variation on it. Before: the budget, so that spending follows a plan instead of a mood. During: the source documents and the journals, so that every rand's movement leaves a trace memory cannot bend. After: the statements and the checking — trial balances, reconciliations, variances — so that the records face reality and reality answers back. Do that with a corner shop, a stokvel, a salary or a city, and three things follow: you catch problems while they are small, you can prove your honesty to anyone entitled to ask, and your decisions rest on figures instead of feelings. The formats will grow — new statements, new standards, software doing the posting — but the habit underneath never changes. Money that is written down can be trusted, questioned and steered. Money that is not simply disappears — and takes businesses, families and governments down with it. Keep the habit; the rest of accounting is commentary.
