# Part 1 — Expert

Everything this year recorded the PAST — transactions that happened, statements of what resulted. The year's final topic turns the books around to face forward: BUDGETING, the discipline of writing the future down before spending it. Grade 10 asks for the concepts — cash budget, capital budget, zero-based budget, medium-term and long-term budgets — and for basic calculations, which centre on the cash budget: the month-by-month forecast of money in, money out, and the balance that survives. One worked quarter today, and the year's toolkit is complete.

## Subtopic: Writing the Future Down

First, fix the difference in kind. A financial statement REPORTS: it is drawn after the fact, from records, and its numbers are verifiable. A BUDGET ESTIMATES: it is drawn before the fact, from plans and expectations, and its numbers are targets. The two meet at year end, when actual figures stand beside budgeted ones and every difference — every VARIANCE — demands an explanation: sales below budget, why; electricity above, why. The budget is the plan; the comparison is the control.

Now the family of budgets, each answering its own question. The CASH BUDGET — the workhorse: month by month, what money will COME IN, what must GO OUT, and what balance remains — answering the survival question: can we pay our way each month? The CAPITAL BUDGET: the plan for buying long-term assets — machines, vehicles, equipment — large sums, long lives, planned separately from daily running. Then the horizons: MEDIUM-TERM budgets plan roughly one to three years ahead — expansion, staffing, product changes; LONG-TERM budgets carry the strategy beyond that — new premises, major growth. The further the horizon, the broader the strokes: next month is budgeted in rands; five years out, in directions.

And one METHOD with its own name: ZERO-BASED budgeting. The lazy method — call it incremental — copies last year's budget and adds a percentage. Zero-based refuses the copy: every line starts at NOTHING and must justify its whole amount afresh — why does this cost exist, what does it buy, what happens without it? More work, and the reward is that dead costs die instead of riding along year after year.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: statement against budget, the family members matched to their questions, and zero-based against the copy-and-add habit.

## Subtopic: The Cash Budget, Built

Now build the workhorse: Thabo's next quarter — January, February, March — one column per month, and the fixed skeleton: RECEIPTS, then PAYMENTS, then the arithmetic of survival.

The receipts, and the estimate behind them: sales run R20 000 a month, sixty percent for cash, forty on credit collected the FOLLOWING month. January's receipts: cash sales R12 000, plus the collection of December's credit sales — forty percent of December's R20 000 — R8 000. Total in: R20 000. February and March repeat the pattern while sales hold steady. Note the discipline: the cash budget records money when it ARRIVES, not when it is earned — a credit sale enters the budget a month late, when the debtor pays. Accrual thinking belongs to the statements; the cash budget thinks in cash only.

The payments: stock purchases R12 000 a month, paid in the month; wages R3 000; rent R1 500 — R16 500 of routine outflow. And one planned exception: in February, a new sewing machine, R6 000 — a CAPITAL item, planned in the capital budget, LANDING in the cash budget in its purchase month, because the cash budget must see every rand that leaves, whatever its purpose.

The survival arithmetic, month by month: receipts minus payments — the month's SURPLUS or DEFICIT; add the OPENING bank balance; the result is the CLOSING balance, which becomes next month's opening. January: in 20 000, out 16 500 — surplus 3 500; opening 2 000; closing R5 500. February: surplus 3 500 minus the machine's 6 000 — deficit 2 500; opening 5 500; closing R3 000. March: surplus 3 500; closing R6 500.

Stop for this section's questions now — the skeleton and the quarter: receipts with the collection lag, payments with the capital landing, and the rolling balances.

## Subtopic: Reading the Budget — the Decisions Inside

A budget's worth is in the decisions it enables BEFORE the money moves; read the quarter as its owner should.

Reading one: February survives — but barely by design. The machine's R6 000 lands in a month that only generates R3 500 of surplus; the balance dips to R3 000. Seen in ADVANCE, that dip is manageable — it was planned onto the calendar. Seen too late — machine bought in a weak month on impulse — it could have emptied the account. The budget's first gift is timing: big purchases go where the balances can carry them. Had the dip gone negative, the budget offers choices while they are still cheap: delay the machine a month, or arrange an overdraft IN ADVANCE — banks price desperation dearly and preparation kindly.

Reading two: the credit-sales lag is a permanent tax on timing. Forty percent of every month's earning arrives a month late — the shop EARNS R20 000 in January but only holds R20 000 of it because December fed the pipeline. If sales GROW, the lag bites: a bumper month's cash arrives after its costs were paid. Cash budgets expose what income statements hide: profit and cash keep different calendars — the lesson the statements topic promised, now visible in a planning tool.

Reading three: the budget is also the guard. When March ACTUALLY happens, its real figures stand beside these estimates, and every variance asks its question: purchases above budget — price rise, or leakage? Sales below — market, or service slipping? Variance by variance, the budget converts surprises into investigations. A business without a budget meets its problems as emergencies; a budgeted business meets them as variances, months smaller.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — read the quarter: the February decision, the lag's effect, and variances as early warnings.

## Subtopic: The Wider Family and the Year's Close

Place the remaining family members with worked meaning. The CAPITAL BUDGET behind February's machine: a schedule of intended asset purchases — the machine now, perhaps a second one next year, a bakkie the year after — each with its amount, its timing, and its funding: from savings, or borrowed. It feeds the cash budget its landing dates, and it disciplines growth: assets bought by plan, not appetite. The MEDIUM-TERM view: will the workshop expand its range, hire a second machinist, grow sales beyond R20 000 — one to three years of direction, numbers broader. The LONG-TERM view: premises owned instead of rented, a second branch — strategy in outline, refined yearly as it approaches.

And ZERO-BASED, applied where it earns most: the expense lines that renew themselves by habit. The R1 500 rent — is the space right, or paying for unused room? Each subscription, each service — justify from zero or cut. Households and governments alike lose fortunes to lines nobody re-examines; zero-based budgeting is institutional spring-cleaning.

Technique for the exam, four habits. One: respect the cash-only rule — depreciation NEVER enters a cash budget: no rand leaves; and credit sales enter at COLLECTION, not sale. Two: keep the skeleton's order — receipts, payments, surplus or deficit, opening, closing — and roll each closing into the next opening. Three: label estimates with their logic — "40% of December sales" — the working is the mark. Four: comment as a decision-maker: a dip is a timing question, a deficit is a financing question, a variance is an investigation question.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the family placed, the cash-only traps avoided, the quarter read for its decisions.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same topic at the kitchen table on the twenty-fifth of the month — because every household that survives payday to payday already runs a cash budget; tonight it gets its formal clothes.

## Subtopic: The Month Written Before It Happens

Month-end at home. Before the salary lands, somebody sits with a pen and writes the month that HASN'T HAPPENED yet: money coming in — the pay, the cousin repaying his loan; money that must go out — rent, electricity, food, transport, the burial society. And then the only subtraction that matters: does what's coming in cover what's going out, and what's left over?

That page IS a cash budget — the same tool Thabo uses, the same tool the government uses, differing only in zeros. And feel the difference between that page and everything else this year taught: the statements looked BACKWARD — what happened, recorded and proved. The budget looks FORWARD — what we EXPECT, written down so the month can be steered instead of suffered. A report is a mirror; a budget is a headlamp.

Why write it down at all — why not just be careful? Because unwritten plans cannot be checked. When the month ends, the written budget stands there, and the real month stands beside it: food cost R300 more than planned — why? The taxi money ran out in week three — why? The written plan turns "eish, the money just went" into specific questions with findable answers. That check — plan against actual — is the whole discipline of control, and it needs the plan on paper first.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the headlamp are coming to you right now: budget against report, and why the writing-down is the power.

## Subtopic: Watching the Purse Through Three Months

Now run Thabo's quarter like a family watching its purse — because the drama of a cash budget is in the running balance.

January: R20 000 comes in — the till's cash plus the customers from December finally paying. R16 500 goes out — stock, wages, rent. The month puts R3 500 INTO the purse, which opened with R2 000: purse now R5 500. Comfortable.

February — the planned shock: the new sewing machine, R6 000, lands in a month that only adds R3 500. The purse gives back R2 500 and closes at R3 000. Thin — but here is the whole point: Thabo KNEW IT WOULD BE THIN before February began. He chose February, saw the dip on paper, and confirmed the purse could carry it. Compare the cousin who buys the machine on feeling in a lean month and finds the rent bouncing — same machine, same price, ruin instead of a dip. The difference was never the money; it was the LOOKING AHEAD.

March: no shocks, R3 500 in, purse recovers to R6 500 — stronger than the year began. And notice the one sneaky rhythm inside the numbers: forty percent of each month's sales arrives a month LATE, because credit customers pay next month. The shop can be earning beautifully and still feel tight this month — earning and receiving keep different calendars. Families know this one too: month-end work, mid-month pay.

Your questions for this part are up now: run the purse through the quarter — in, out, dip, recovery — and say why February was safe for Thabo and dangerous for the cousin.

## Subtopic: Why Every Rand Must Re-Apply for Its Job

Last idea of the year, and a sharp one: the ZERO-BASED habit. Most budgets are written by copying: last month's list, same lines, maybe ten percent more for prices. Comfortable — and quietly dangerous, because dead costs love the copy. The subscription nobody uses anymore. The delivery arrangement from when the shop was on the other side of town. The airtime bundle sized for a phone somebody replaced. Copied budgets carry their dead forever.

Zero-based budgeting fires the whole list once a year and makes every rand RE-APPLY for its job. Start from nothing: why does this line exist? What does it buy us NOW — not three years ago? What breaks if we cut it? The rent defends itself easily — no premises, no shop. The forgotten subscription stands silent — cut. It is spring-cleaning for money: more effort than copying, and it pays for itself in found rands every single time a household or a business honestly does it.

And now step back, because the year closes here. You can record any transaction through six journals; post and balance a ledger; reconcile the details to their controls; adjust the books to truth; close a year and prove it; dress the results in formal statements; read those statements with nine gauges; cost a product to the unit; and write the future down before spending it. That is not a school list — that is the complete basic toolkit of financial stewardship, the same one that runs spaza shops, stokvels, companies and countries.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: budget families matched to jobs, the purse run once more, and one zero-based interrogation done properly. Finish them, and Grade 10 Accounting is — in the truest sense — accounted for.
