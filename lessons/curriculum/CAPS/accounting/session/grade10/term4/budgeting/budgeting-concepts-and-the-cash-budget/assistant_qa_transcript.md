**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close the year! First: budgets are guesses about the future — and guesses are often wrong. Why do serious businesses stake decisions on numbers everybody KNOWS are estimates?

**Tutor:** Because the alternative to an estimate is not certainty — it is blindness. The choice is never between a perfect forecast and an imperfect one; it is between an explicit plan that can be checked and no plan at all. And the budget's value survives its own errors: if February's sales come in at eighteen thousand against a budgeted twenty, the budget did not fail — it created the very question "why are we two thousand short?" a month after the problem started, instead of at year end when the damage has compounded. Estimates also improve with use: this year's variances teach next year's budget. A business run on guesses corrected monthly beats a business run on no numbers at all, every time. The discipline is not predicting perfectly; it is writing down what you expect, so reality has something to argue with.

***

**Thandi:** Next: the machine costs R6 000 in February's cash budget — but the depreciation lesson taught us to spread an asset's cost over its years. Which is right?

**Tutor:** Both — they answer different questions, and holding them apart is real mastery. The cash budget asks: what money LEAVES in February? Six thousand rand, in one payment — and February's bank balance must survive that fact, which is why the full amount lands in that column. The income statement asks: what did THIS YEAR consume of the machine's working life? A slice — the depreciation — spread over its years by matching. Same machine, two calendars: the cash calendar records the buying; the earning calendar records the using. And notice the two tools never borrow each other's items: depreciation appears in the statement but never in the cash budget, because no rand moves when value wears; the purchase price hits the cash budget in full but reaches the income statement only in slices. Learners who let the two calendars blur put depreciation in cash budgets — the classic error. Keep the question in view and the placement answers itself.

***

**Thandi:** This one from the back: the lesson says arrange an overdraft IN ADVANCE if the budget shows a deficit. Why would a bank care whether you ask before or after the money runs out?

**Tutor:** Because the timing of the ask reveals the quality of the borrower. Arrive in November with a budget showing a planned R2 500 dip in February, and you demonstrate exactly what a lender wants: you see your cash flows ahead, the shortfall is temporary, its cause is a productive asset, and the recovery is on paper in March. The bank prices that as low risk — a modest facility, agreed calmly. Arrive in February with the account already empty and payments bouncing, and the same R2 500 tells the opposite story: a business that did not see its own shortfall coming, asking under pressure, with the bank's first evidence being a crisis. Emergency money is expensive money — higher rates, harder terms, sometimes refusal. The budget converts borrowing from a confession into a proposal. That single practical habit — showing the bank the dip before living it — is worth more than most of the interest rate tables in the world.

***

**Thandi:** Last question of the whole year: we've done journals, ledgers, statements, ratios, costs, budgets... if one habit from Grade 10 Accounting should follow us for life, which is it?

**Tutor:** Write money down — before, during and after. That is the whole subject in one habit, and every topic this year was a variation of it. Before: the budget, so spending follows a plan instead of a mood. During: the source documents and journals, so every rand's movement leaves a trace that memory cannot distort. After: the statements and the checking — trial balances, reconciliations, variances — so the records face reality and reality answers back. Do that with a spaza shop, a stokvel, a salary or a country, and three things follow: you catch problems while they are small, you can prove your honesty to anyone entitled to ask, and your decisions rest on figures rather than feelings. The formats will grow — new statements, new standards, software doing the posting — but the habit underneath never changes. Money that is written down can be trusted, questioned and steered. Money that is not, simply disappears — and takes businesses, families and governments with it. Keep the habit; the rest of accounting is commentary.
