**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! First: our ratios came out at six-to-one and five-comma-three-to-one — miles above the norms of two and one. The textbook norms feel useless if real answers ignore them. What are norms actually FOR?

**Tutor:** Norms are the centre of a healthy range, not a target to hit — and the skill is knowing what each SIDE of the range means. Below the norm, the classic danger: bills may knock faster than assets turn to cash — creditors unpaid, credit reputation damaged, stock deliveries stopped. Above it, the quieter cost: resources standing idle. Our six-to-one says no bill will ever go unpaid this year — excellent — but it also says some fifteen thousand rand of cash is sleeping while a loan upstairs charges twelve percent and shelves could carry stock earning twenty-five. So the norm's real use is as a REFERENCE POINT for the comment: state where the business sits relative to it, then name the consequence of being on that side — danger below, idleness above. A learner who writes "above the norm, therefore good" has read half the gauge. The full reading is: safe, and possibly too comfortable — recommend putting the excess to work: reduce the loan, or invest in stock.

***

**Thandi:** Next: why does return on equity insist on the AVERAGE equity? The year ended at R23 900 — why not just use that?

**Tutor:** Because the profit was earned by the money as it stood THROUGH the year, not as it stood at the last midnight. The equity opened at R20 000, and only gathered its way to R23 900 as profit accumulated — indeed part of the closing figure IS the profit being measured, which makes the closing base partly circular: you would be dividing the profit by itself. The average of opening and closing — R21 950 — approximates the stake that was actually at work across the months, so the rate is honest. Feel the difference in the numbers: on the closing figure the return looks like thirty-three percent; on the opening, thirty-nine and a half; the average lands at thirty-six, between them, and defensible. And practically, for the exam: the formula sheet says average, the marker looks for the "(opening + closing) ÷ 2" working, and the unaveraged base is THE classic lost mark of this ratio. Write the average line first, every time.

***

**Thandi:** This one had us arguing: how can a business be SOLVENT and still go under? If assets cover all debts, what kills it?

**Tutor:** Timing kills it — solvency is a stock of value, but bills are paid with CASH, on dates. Picture the solvent-but-illiquid shop: equipment and property worth double its debts, but the wages fall due Friday and the bank account is empty. Selling equipment takes weeks and forced sales fetch poor prices; the creditors will not wait for an auction. Miss the wages, workers leave; miss the suppliers, deliveries stop; miss the rent, doors close — the business dies of thirst beside a full dam it cannot drink from quickly. That is precisely why BOTH gauge families exist and why the bank reads both: solvency says the last-resort promise is covered; liquidity says the ordinary Friday is covered. And the rescue for such a business is usually structural: convert slow assets into working cash — sell surplus equipment properly, arrange a loan against the property — before the timing gap becomes a default. Value that cannot reach the door in time pays no bills.

**Thandi:** Last one: thirty-six percent sounds unbelievable next to the bank's eight. If businesses really return that, why does anyone leave money in fixed deposits at all?

**Tutor:** Because the two numbers are different SPECIES, and the difference is the whole theory of investing. The deposit's eight is contractual: promised in advance, payable in the worst year, requiring nothing from you. The shop's thirty-six is residual: whatever remains after every risk has taken its chance — computed only afterwards, never promised. Behind our thirty-six stood a year of the owner's unpaid hours, stock that could have spoiled, debtors who could have defaulted, a break-in that never happened — and a real possibility of zero, or of the capital itself shrinking. Many small businesses earn less than the deposit rate; some lose everything. So the deposit buys certainty and sleep; the business buys a CHANCE at multiples, priced in labour and risk. The principle for your exams and your life: the extra return over the safe rate is the wage of risk — demand it when you take risk, and be suspicious of anything promising deposit-safety with business-sized returns. When those two features are advertised together, the number that is usually false is the safety.
