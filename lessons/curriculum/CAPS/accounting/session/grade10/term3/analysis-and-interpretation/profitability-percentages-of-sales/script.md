# Part 1 — Expert

The statements are built; now they must be made to TALK. Analysis converts absolute figures into percentages and ratios that can be compared — against last year, against the policy, against other businesses — and interpretation turns those comparisons into meaningful comment. Today, the profitability family: five percentages, all anchored on the income statement, every one on the examination's formula sheet. The calculations are short; the marks concentrate in choosing the right formula and saying something intelligent about the answer.

## Subtopic: Making the Numbers Talk

Why can the statements not speak for themselves? Because absolute figures carry no verdict. A net profit of R7 900 — is that good? For a spaza table, spectacular; for a supermarket, catastrophic. The figure only gains meaning against a BASE, and profitability analysis chooses the most natural base in trading: SALES. Every profitability percentage asks the same shaped question: out of every hundred rand of sales, how many rand ended up as this?

The toolkit, from the formula sheet: gross profit on sales; gross profit on cost of sales; net profit on sales; operating expenses on sales; operating profit on sales. Each is the named figure, divided by sales — except the second, divided by cost of sales — times one hundred. The figures themselves you built: sales R100 000; gross profit R20 000; operating expenses R16 900; operating profit R9 100; net profit R7 900. The three-layer statement was the preparation; analysis is the harvest.

And the formula sheet discipline from day one: the sheet GIVES you every formula — no formula needs memorising. The examined skills are selection — which formula answers this question — substitution — the right figures in the right places — and interpretation. Learners lose marks not by forgetting formulas but by feeding the wrong figure into a remembered one: operating profit where net profit belongs, cost of sales where sales belongs. Label what you substitute, every time.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: why percentages beat absolutes, the sales base, and the formula sheet's real role.

## Subtopic: The Two Gross Profit Percentages

The first pair of tools measures the trading layer — and the pair is deliberate, because the same gross profit is read against two different bases.

GROSS PROFIT ON SALES: 20 000 over 100 000, times one hundred — twenty percent. Meaning: of every hundred rand that crossed the counter, twenty rand was margin. This is the margin the business LIVES on — every operating expense must be paid out of that twenty.

GROSS PROFIT ON COST OF SALES: 20 000 over 80 000, times one hundred — twenty-five percent. Meaning: for every hundred rand of goods at cost, twenty-five rand was added on. Recognise it: this IS the mark-up — and there stands Thabo's pricing policy, twenty-five percent on cost, confirmed by the year's actual trading. The two percentages are the same profit seen from the two ends of the counter: on cost, it is the mark-up applied; on sales, the margin received.

Now the interpretive power: suppose the policy says twenty-five percent on cost, but the achieved percentage computes to twenty-one. The gap is a FINDING — and its causes are a short, learnable list: goods sold below full price in sales or markdowns; stock lost, broken or stolen after being costed; errors in recording; theft of takings. The percentage cannot name the culprit, but it raises the alarm and sizes the leak — analysis pointing exactly where the control lessons said to look. That achieved-versus-policy comparison is the single most examined interpretation in Grade 10.

Stop for this section's questions now — both gross percentages: compute, name the bases, and read a gap against the policy.

## Subtopic: The Operating Family and Net Profit on Sales

Three more percentages complete the family, and together they retell the three-layer statement in per-hundred form.

OPERATING EXPENSES ON SALES: 16 900 over 100 000 — sixteen comma nine percent. Of every hundred rand of sales, nearly seventeen went to running the shop. This is the cost-burden gauge: watch it across years — a rising percentage means expenses are growing FASTER than sales, the operating leak the three-floor lesson warned about, and the itemised expense list says which line is swelling.

OPERATING PROFIT ON SALES: 9 100 over 100 000 — nine comma one percent. What the operations kept from every hundred, after the running costs. Note the arithmetic kinship: the gross profit percentage, plus other operating income's contribution, minus the expense percentage, lands here — the layers are additive in percentage form, which makes this family self-checking.

NET PROFIT ON SALES: 7 900 over 100 000 — seven comma nine percent. The final keep-rate: out of every hundred rand of sales, seven rand ninety survived everything — cost of goods, running costs, and the loan's interest. The distance between operating and net — nine comma one down to seven comma nine — is the financing bite, isolated.

Read the five as one dashboard: mark-up twenty-five achieved; margin twenty; expenses seventeen; operations keep nine; owner keeps eight. One glance now tells the whole earning story in proportions — and proportions, unlike rand figures, can be compared with any year and any business, which is the entire point.

The questions on this section are in front of you now: compute each percentage, then read the dashboard — where the hundred rand went, layer by layer.

## Subtopic: Interpreting — Comparing to Comment

Percentages alone earn calculation marks; INTERPRETATION marks demand comparison and comment, and comment has a learnable anatomy.

First, the comparison bases. Against LAST YEAR: is the percentage rising or falling — the trend. Against the POLICY: achieved mark-up versus intended — the control check. Against a SIMILAR BUSINESS: are we keeping less of each rand than the shop across the road — the competitive check. A percentage without a comparison is a fact without a meaning; the exam signals the intended base by what it supplies — given last year's figures, compare years; given the policy, test it.

Then the comment's anatomy, four moves. STATE: name the figure and its comparison — "gross profit on sales fell from twenty-three to twenty percent." JUDGE: is this favourable or unfavourable — "a deterioration." EXPLAIN: offer the likely cause from the scenario — "consistent with the markdowns on winter stock mentioned, or stock losses before sale." RECOMMEND: one practical action — "investigate the stock losses; review the markdown policy." State, judge, explain, recommend — four sentences, full marks. Learners who only restate the numbers — "it went down" — earn the first quarter of the marks and abandon the rest.

Last discipline: respect what each percentage CAN say. Gross percentages speak about pricing and stock control — never about expenses. The expense percentage speaks about cost management — never about pricing. Diagnose from the right gauge, and when a scenario shows margin healthy but net thin, say where the leak is NOT before saying where it is: pricing intact, problem downstream. That is reading like an accountant.

The final questions of this part are with you now: full interpretations — compare, then comment in the four-move anatomy, from the correct gauge.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same five tools from the counter — because every shopkeeper already does this in their head: it's the hundred-rand question, asked five ways.

## Subtopic: Out of Every Hundred Rand

Forget the year's totals for a minute. Take ONE hundred-rand note from the till — imagine following it as it breaks up.

A customer hands over R100 for goods. First, the goods themselves must be replaced: R80 goes back to buying stock — that's the cost of the goods at our mark-up. R20 remains: the margin — the twenty rand the shop actually gets to work with from every hundred. Out of that twenty, the running of the shop takes its share: wages, telephone, insurance, the missing stock — nearly R17 of every hundred in our year. And the loan's interest nibbles its bit. When everything has taken its cut, R7,90 of the original hundred remains — the owner's keep, from every single hundred that crossed the counter.

THAT journey — 100 in, 80 replaced, 20 margin, 17 running, 8 kept — is the entire profitability analysis. The five official percentages are just the checkpoint names along the note's journey. Why per hundred instead of the year's totals? Because per-hundred travels: you can compare it with last year when sales were different, with the bigger shop in town, with any business anywhere. Rand totals are trapped in their own year; percentages are portable.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the hundred-rand journey are coming to you right now: where the note breaks, and why per-hundred beats totals.

## Subtopic: The Health Check at the Clinic

Think of analysis as the shop's clinic visit — because that's honestly what it is: vital signs, compared, and read together.

One number alone means nothing at the clinic. "Temperature 37" — fine or fever? You know because it's compared: against the normal range, against YOUR last visit. The shop's percentages work identically. Margin twenty percent — healthy? Compare: last year it was twenty-three — something has slipped. The policy says the mark-up should deliver twenty-five on cost — this year's books achieve twenty-five: the pricing is being followed. The shop across the road keeps nine of every hundred and we keep eight — close, but worth asking why.

And like a nurse, never read one vital alone — read the SET, because the pattern points to the organ. Margin fine but the owner's keep thin? The problem isn't pricing — check the running costs: some expense line is swelling. Margin down but expenses steady? The leak is upstream — markdowns, missing stock, prices quietly slipping. Everything proportionally fine but the keep still small in rand? Then the shop is healthy but small — a growth question, not a sickness.

The percentages are the thermometer and the blood pressure cuff of a business. They never treat anything — but they tell you which organ to examine, and they catch sickness while it's still curable. That's why the bank reads them before lending, and why the owner should read them monthly, not yearly.

Your questions for this part are up now: read sets of vitals — compare, spot the pattern, and name the organ to examine.

## Subtopic: Saying Something That Means Something

Last skill — and it's the one that separates the marks: after calculating, you must SAY something, and "it went down" is not saying something.

Here's the four-line recipe, in home language. Line one, WHAT: "The margin fell from twenty-three to twenty rand out of every hundred." Line two, SO: "That's bad news — the shop works with three rand less from every hundred it takes." Line three, WHY, from the story: "It fits the winter markdowns — goods left below full price — or stock that vanished after costing." Line four, NOW WHAT: "Count the stock against the books this week; reconsider the markdown depth."

What, so, why, now what. Four short lines and the comment is complete — a number, a verdict, a cause, an action. Practise the recipe until it's automatic, because exam interpretation questions are marked line by line against exactly that anatomy, and most learners stall after line one.

One warning about line three: the WHY must come from the SCENARIO, not from imagination. If the question mentions markdowns, cite markdowns; if it mentions a break-in, cite stock loss. Analysis is detective work with supplied clues — the percentages say where to look, the scenario says what was there to find, and your comment connects the two.

And the deepest habit of all: match the gauge to the question. Asked about pricing — the gross percentages. About cost control — expenses on sales. About the whole year's keep — net on sales. The five gauges each answer their own question, and answering from the wrong gauge is the interpretation error the markers see most.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: full four-line comments, gauges matched to questions, causes taken from the story. Next lesson, the other half of analysis: can the shop pay its bills — liquidity, solvency, and the owner's return.
