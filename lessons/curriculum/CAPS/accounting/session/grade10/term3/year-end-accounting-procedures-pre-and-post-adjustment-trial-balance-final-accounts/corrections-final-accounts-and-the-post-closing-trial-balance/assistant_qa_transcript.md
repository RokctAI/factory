**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! First: if a stock SURPLUS is good news, why did the lesson tell us to be suspicious of it? Shouldn't we just be happy?

**Tutor:** Be happy about the R30; be curious about the story. The perpetual system predicted R2 250 on the shelf from a year of priced movements; the shelf held R2 280. Stock cannot appear from nowhere — so either goods arrived without paperwork, or a sale was recorded that never left the shelf, or a return came back unrecorded. Each of those is a broken link in the document chain, and a chain that dropped one link may have dropped others — some in directions that cost money. So the entry is made, because the count is evidence and the books must match fact — but the investigation follows, because the surplus is not a gift; it is a symptom wearing a smile. An accountant who banks the R30 and asks no questions has fixed the number and ignored the control.

***

**Thandi:** Next: for the wages error — R1 050 instead of R1 500 — why the top-up of R450? Wouldn't reversing the whole entry and re-entering R1 500 be cleaner?

**Tutor:** Both end at the same balances; the top-up is better bookkeeping for three reasons. It is one entry instead of two, so half the opportunity for a fresh slip. It leaves an honest audit trail: the ledger shows the original entry, then a dated correction of the difference with its narration — a reader reconstructs exactly what happened and when. And it preserves the principle that corrections address the GAP between what happened and what should have: the R1 050 was not wrong in existence, only in size, so only the missing R450 needs writing. Reversal-and-re-entry belongs to a different disease — an entry that should never have existed at all, or one posted to entirely wrong accounts on both sides. Match the cure to the illness: wrong size, top up; wrong existence, reverse.

***

**Thandi:** This one is sneaky: the telephone-in-Repairs error changed NOTHING in the totals — profit identical before and after the fix. Why does anyone care?

**Tutor:** Because the totals are not the only readers of those accounts. Next week the financial statements list expenses BY NAME, and both lines would have lied — Repairs overstated, Telephone understated. The year after, someone budgets: repairs "rose" this year, so money is set aside for maintenance that never happened, while the telephone budget runs short by March. Ratios, comparisons across years, decisions about which cost is growing — all read the detail, not the total. And there is a control reason with teeth: misfiled expenses are exactly how certain frauds hide — a private cost parked in a busy account no one scrutinises. A business that shrugs at wrong-drawer errors trains itself not to look; the fix costs one journal entry, and what it buys is that every line can be believed. Detail integrity IS the product; the total was never the point.

***

**Thandi:** Last one: why do we draw THREE trial balances — pre-adjustment, post-adjustment, post-closing? Surely the last one is the only one that matters?

**Tutor:** Each certifies a different machine, and a failure at each points to a different culprit. The pre-adjustment trial balance proves the YEAR'S ROUTINE — twelve months of journals and postings — kept its double-entry promise; fail here and the fault lies in the daily books, and you must find it BEFORE adjusting, or it hides among the adjustments forever. The post-adjustment version proves the ADJUSTMENTS themselves were balanced double entries; fail here and the fault is in this week's work, a half-adjustment somewhere. The post-closing version proves the CLOSING TRANSFERS emptied and transferred cleanly, leaving a level equation to open the new year; fail here and the closing is at fault. Skip the middle one and an error could be the year's or the adjustments' — you would not know where to hunt. Three checkpoints, three suspects, and each search stays small. That is the same wisdom as the whole cycle: prove each stage before feeding the next.
