# Part 1 — Expert

Term two closed the year with three adjustments: consumable stores, the stock deficit, and depreciation. Term three completes the family — the TIMING adjustments: expenses owed but unpaid, expenses paid but not yet used, income earned but not yet received, income received but not yet earned, and the loan whose interest quietly joins its own balance. These four-plus-one are the heart of every year-end question from here to matric, and they all obey one master: the matching principle, applied at the year boundary.

## Subtopic: The Timing Problem — Four New Adjustments

The problem in one sentence: money moves on its own schedule, but the financial year ends at midnight on the last day of February — and matching demands that THIS year's statement carry exactly this year's incomes and expenses, regardless of when the cash happened to move.

So four mismatches are possible, and they form a clean two-by-two grid. On the EXPENSE side: an expense already USED but not yet paid — an ACCRUED EXPENSE; and an expense already PAID but not yet used — a PREPAID EXPENSE. On the INCOME side: income already EARNED but not yet received — ACCRUED INCOME; and income already RECEIVED but not yet earned — INCOME RECEIVED IN ADVANCE.

Hold the grid's logic rather than four separate rules. Ask two questions of any straddling amount: does it belong to THIS year or NEXT year — that is the earning-or-using question; and has the cash MOVED yet? Belongs to this year, cash not moved: accrue it in — the statement must carry it even without the cash. Belongs to next year, cash already moved: push it out — the statement must shed it even though the cash sits in this year's records.

And each push or pull leaves a trace on the balance sheet: something owed BY the business or owed TO it, something stored for next year or received on next year's behalf. Every timing adjustment is one income-statement correction plus one balance-sheet creation — always the pair.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: the grid itself. For each case, answer the two questions — whose year, and has the cash moved — before naming the adjustment.

## Subtopic: The Expense Side — Accrued and Prepaid

Case one: the ACCRUED EXPENSE. Thabo's telephone costs run about R100 a month, and eleven months stand paid in the ledger: R1 100. February's account — R100 — only arrives in March. Did this year USE twelve months of telephone? Yes. The adjustment: debit Telephone R100, bringing the expense to the true R1 200 — and credit ACCRUED EXPENSES, a CURRENT LIABILITY: the business owes February's telephone at year end, and the balance sheet must say so. Next year pays the bill and clears the liability; next year's expense is untouched, exactly as matching wants.

Case two: the PREPAID EXPENSE — the mirror. On the first of September Thabo paid the full annual insurance premium: R1 200 for twelve months of cover. By year end — the last of February — only SIX months of that cover have been used: September through February, R600. The other R600 buys March-to-August cover — NEXT year's protection, paid for early. The ledger, though, shows Insurance R1 200, as if this year used it all. The adjustment: credit Insurance R600, leaving the expense at the R600 truly consumed — and debit PREPAID EXPENSES, a CURRENT ASSET: value bought and stored, waiting to serve next year. It is the stationery-cupboard logic applied to time instead of paper: paid-for does not mean used-up.

Notice what both cases share: the expense account ends at what the YEAR USED — R1 200 telephone, R600 insurance — regardless of what the bank account did. The cash's timing becomes a balance-sheet item; the income statement stays true.

Stop for this section's questions now — accrued against prepaid: for each, the corrected expense, the balance-sheet item it creates, and the side that item lives on.

## Subtopic: The Income Side, plus the Interest That Joins the Loan

The income grid mirrors the expense grid — with the directions flipped, so derive each fresh.

ACCRUED INCOME: the tenant renting Thabo's back room at R500 a month has paid only eleven months by year end; February's rent arrives late. Did this year EARN twelve months of rent? Yes — the room was occupied. Adjustment: credit Rent Income R500, lifting it to the true R6 000 — and debit ACCRUED INCOME, a CURRENT ASSET: money owed TO the business, a receivable waiting in the new year.

INCOME RECEIVED IN ADVANCE: the opposite tenant. Suppose instead the tenant, leaving for the holidays, paid THIRTEEN months by year end — February's rent plus March's, in advance. The ledger shows Rent Income R6 500; but only R6 000 was EARNED this year. Adjustment: debit Rent Income R500, trimming it to the earned R6 000 — and credit INCOME RECEIVED IN ADVANCE, a CURRENT LIABILITY. A liability, because the business now OWES the tenant a month of occupation: money taken, service not yet delivered.

And the fifth adjustment: INTEREST CAPITALISED. Thabo carries a bank loan of R10 000 at twelve percent per annum. The year's interest — R1 200 — was not paid in cash; the bank ADDED it to the loan. Adjustment: debit Interest on Loan, an expense of R1 200 — the borrowing cost belongs to this year — and credit the LOAN itself, whose balance rises to R11 200. Capitalised means "added to the capital amount owed": the loan grew by its own interest. Miss this one and two errors happen at once — the expense understated and the liability understated.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — the income mirror and the growing loan: derive each direction from whose year and whose money, never from the expense-side pattern.

## Subtopic: The Balance-Sheet Afterlives and Exam Technique

Line up the five afterlives, because the statements in the coming weeks will collect them. Accrued expenses — current liability. Prepaid expenses — current asset. Accrued income — current asset. Income received in advance — current liability. Interest capitalised — folded into the loan's balance, a non-current liability growing. Two assets, two liabilities, one swelling loan: every timing adjustment files something into the balance sheet, and the statement of financial position lesson will expect you to know each item's address.

The symmetry worth memorising as a check: whatever the adjustment does to profit, its balance-sheet twin does the opposite side. Accruing an expense cuts profit and raises liabilities; prepaying stores an asset and restores profit; accruing income raises profit and raises assets; income in advance trims profit and raises liabilities. Profit and the equation move together — if your adjustment changes one and not the other, half the entry is missing.

Technique, four habits. One: draw the grid — expense or income, then early cash or late cash; classify BEFORE calculating, because every error in this topic is a classification error wearing an arithmetic disguise. Two: monthly rates are the workhorse — divide annual figures by twelve, count months on your fingers, and write the fraction into the working. Three: state each adjustment as its pair — the income-statement line it corrects AND the balance-sheet item it creates; examiners award both, and learners who write only the expense correction leave the second mark on the table. Four: read the paper's verbs — "owing", "outstanding", "still to be received" signal accruals; "paid until", "in advance", "for the period ending after year end" signal the prepaid and in-advance family.

The final questions of this part are with you now: classify by the grid, correct the statement line, file the balance-sheet twin — every case, both halves.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same five adjustments from the kitchen calendar — because every household already lives this topic: the electricity bill that arrives after month end, the school fees paid before the term, the lay-by that grows if you leave it.

## Subtopic: The Year Line and the Four Latecomers

Draw a chalk line on the floor: midnight, last day of February. Everything the shop EARNED and USED on this side of the line belongs to this year's report card; everything on the far side belongs to next year's. Simple — except money refuses to respect the line. Money arrives early, arrives late, gets paid ahead, gets paid behind — and at year end there are always a few amounts standing with one foot on each side.

Only four kinds of straddler exist, and you can sort them all with two questions. Question one: is this about money the shop PAYS (an expense) or money the shop COLLECTS (an income)? Question two: did the money move BEFORE the work-or-use it belongs to, or AFTER? That gives four corners: used-but-not-yet-paid; paid-but-not-yet-used; earned-but-not-yet-collected; collected-but-not-yet-earned.

And here is the comfort: the fix is the same in every corner. Put the amount in the year where the USING or the EARNING happened — that's the report card's side — and let the balance sheet remember whatever the money still owes or is owed. The report card tells the truth about the year; the balance sheet holds the loose ends. Two jobs, cleanly split.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the line and the corners are coming to you right now: sort each straddler with the two questions, corner by corner.

## Subtopic: Bills Still Coming, Cover Paid Ahead

Live in the two expense corners for a moment, because your own home knows them both.

Corner one: the bill that hasn't arrived. Every family knows February's electricity gets billed in March — but the lights burned in FEBRUARY. Thabo's February telephone is the same: R100 of calls made, bill still travelling. The report card must charge this year for what this year USED — twelve months, R1 200 — and the balance sheet writes a sticky note: "we still owe February's R100." That note is a small debt — a liability — and it sits there until March's payment tears it up. The shop used first and pays after; the note remembers.

Corner two: the cover paid ahead. In September, Thabo paid a full year of insurance — R1 200 — the way a family might pay a year of school fees in January. But by the chalk line, only half that protection has been USED: six months, R600. The other R600 is next year's safety, bought early — like six unopened months sitting in the cupboard. So the report card charges only the R600 this year actually used, and the balance sheet stores the other R600 as a little parcel of value — an asset — waiting on the shelf for the new year to unwrap month by month.

Feel the symmetry: one corner leaves a sticky note of debt, the other leaves a parcel on the shelf. Both exist because the money moved on the wrong day — and neither is allowed to distort the report card.

Your questions for this part are up now: bills still travelling and cover bought ahead — say what the year truly used, then name what the balance sheet keeps: note or parcel.

## Subtopic: Rent Owed, Rent Ahead, and the Loan That Grows Quietly

Now the collecting side — the tenant in the back room, R500 a month — and both corners in one story.

If the tenant is a month BEHIND at year end: the room was occupied all twelve months, so the shop EARNED R6 000 — the report card says so even though only R5 500 arrived. The missing R500 becomes an IOU in the shop's favour: an asset, someone else's sticky note, collected in March. But if the tenant paid a month AHEAD — thirteen payments by year end — the report card refuses the extra: only twelve months were earned, R6 000, and the thirteenth payment becomes a debt the SHOP owes: a month of occupation already paid for but not yet delivered. Money in hand can still be a liability — that's the corner learners find strangest, and the tenant's holiday payment makes it plain: the shop holds the money, but the tenant holds the claim.

And the fifth story: the loan that grows quietly. Thabo borrowed R10 000 at twelve percent. This year's interest — R1 200 — was never paid across the counter; the bank simply ADDED it to the loan, the way a lay-by grows if you skip the instalments. Two truths must land in the books at once: the year DID have a borrowing cost — R1 200 on the report card as an expense — and the debt is now BIGGER: R11 200. Nothing moved through the bank account, and that is exactly why this one gets missed: no cash, no slip, no reminder — only the loan statement, quietly larger. Prudence reads the loan statement.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: rent behind, rent ahead, and the growing loan — for each, what the year truly earned or cost, and who ends up holding the sticky note. Master the corners and next week's financial statements will feel like filing, not fighting.
