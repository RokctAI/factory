**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! The one everyone whispered: how can money the tenant already GAVE us be a liability? Money is money — it's in the bank!

**Tutor:** The bank balance and the claim on it are two different facts. The tenant's thirteenth payment put real cash in the business's account — the asset side is true. But ask what the business gave in exchange: nothing yet. A month of occupation is still OWED, and an obligation to deliver something is precisely what a liability is. Test it with the walk-away: if the tenant cancelled tomorrow, that month's money would have to go back — and anything you might have to give back was never yours to call income. So the cash sits in Bank, honestly, while Income Received in Advance stands opposite it, recording who holds the claim. Money received and income earned are different events; this topic exists exactly where they fall in different years.

***

**Thandi:** Next: for the insurance, why four months and not three or five? Walk us through the counting — that's where the marks leak.

**Tutor:** Anchor both ends and count inclusively. The premium bought cover from the first of March; the year ends the last day of June. Count on fingers: March, April, May, June — four months used, eight remaining for July through February. Then the arithmetic follows the count: R1 800 buys twelve months, so R150 a month; four used months cost R600, and R1 200 stands prepaid. The leak happens when learners count the gap between month NAMES — March to June "feels like" three — instead of counting each month of cover actually enjoyed. Write the month names down if you must; the marker sees only that the fraction — four over twelve — appears in your working, and that fraction is where the mark sits.

***

**Thandi:** This one splits the class every year: accrued INCOME is an asset, but accrued EXPENSE is a liability — same word, opposite sides. How do we stop swapping them?

**Tutor:** Stop attaching the answer to the word "accrued" and attach it to the DIRECTION of the debt. Accrued only means "built up but no cash yet" — it cannot tell you who owes whom. So ask that question directly: the tenant's June rent — who owes? The tenant owes US: a claim in our favour, an asset. June's electricity — who owes? WE owe the municipality: a claim against us, a liability. Same timing, opposite arrows. One more anchor if you like pictures: an asset is an arrow pointing money toward the business; a liability points it away. Every timing adjustment ends with one arrow — draw it, and the sides never swap again.

***

**Thandi:** Last one: these five adjustments feel endless when they pile up in one question. Is there an order that keeps them from tangling?

**Tutor:** There is, and it is the grid itself, worked as a routine. First pass: read the adjustments and CLASSIFY each into its corner before touching a number — expense or income, cash early or cash late, plus the loan's no-cash special. Second pass: for each, compute the year's TRUE figure — months counted, rates divided — and write the corrected statement line. Third pass: file each leftover on the balance sheet — note or parcel, asset or liability. Three passes, never interleaved: classification errors happen when you calculate mid-read, and filing errors happen when you calculate all five and then try to remember the corners afterwards. And keep the symmetry check running as you go: five adjustments must yield five balance-sheet items — count them at the end. Four means one is half-done; the count tells you before the marker does.
