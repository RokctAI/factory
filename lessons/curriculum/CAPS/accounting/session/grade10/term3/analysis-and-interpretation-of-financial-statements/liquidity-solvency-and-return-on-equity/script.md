# Part 1 — Expert

Profitability asked how well the business EARNS; today's ratios ask three different survival questions. Liquidity: can the bills of the coming year be paid as they knock? Solvency: do total assets cover total debts at all? And return on owner's equity: was the owner's money better here than anywhere else? Four formulas — current ratio, acid-test ratio, solvency ratio, return on owner's equity — all on the formula sheet, all drawing from the statement of financial position you built, plus one figure from its companion. Calculate, compare, comment: the discipline is unchanged.

## Subtopic: Can the Bills Be Paid? — the Liquidity Ratios

Liquidity lives entirely in the current sections of the statement — the fast piles. The CURRENT RATIO: current assets to current liabilities, expressed as a ratio to one. Ours: current assets R22 700 — inventories 2 450, receivables 4 950, cash 15 300 — against current liabilities R3 800. Divide: five comma nine seven — call it 6 to 1. Meaning: for every rand of debt knocking within the year, six rand of assets are turning into cash to meet it.

But the current ratio has a soft spot: it counts STOCK as if it were nearly cash — and stock must first find a buyer, at full price, in time. So the ACID-TEST RATIO repeats the question without inventories: current assets minus inventories, to current liabilities. Ours: 22 700 less 2 450 — R20 250 — against R3 800: five comma three to one. Even if not one item sold tomorrow, the receivables and cash alone cover the knocking debts more than five times.

The guideline norms — and hold them as GUIDES, not laws: around 2 to 1 for the current ratio, around 1 to 1 for the acid test, are conventionally comfortable. Ours sit far ABOVE the norms — safe, certainly; but here is the mature reading the examiners reward: a very high ratio is not automatically excellent. Fifteen thousand rand sleeping in the bank is money not working — not buying stock that could earn the mark-up, not paying down the loan that charges twelve percent. Liquidity buys safety; EXCESS liquidity quietly costs opportunity. Both directions of comment score.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: both ratios computed as x-to-1, the stock-soft-spot reasoning, and the two-sided reading of a high ratio.

## Subtopic: Solvency — the Total Question

Liquidity examines the coming year; SOLVENCY examines existence itself: if everything owned were measured against everything owed — long and short — would the assets cover the debts? The ratio: TOTAL assets to TOTAL liabilities. Ours: R38 900 against R15 000 — the loan's 11 200 plus the payables' 3 800. Divide: two comma six to one. For every rand owed to anyone on any horizon, two rand sixty of assets stand behind it.

The threshold that matters: one to one. Above it, solvent — the business could, in the last resort, meet every obligation. Below it, INSOLVENT — debts exceed assets, and trading in that state has legal consequences: creditors' money is already partly lost, and continuing to take credit becomes reckless toward them. That is why solvency is the banker's first glance and the auditor's going-concern touchstone: the going concern assumption — carrying assets at their useful values, planning a future — presumes solvency; a failing ratio puts the whole basis of the statements in question.

Keep the two families cleanly apart, because exam questions bait the confusion: liquidity is the SHORT game — current items only, the year's bills against the year's resources; solvency is the WHOLE game — everything against everything. A business can be solvent but illiquid — rich in equipment, unable to pay Friday's wages; or liquid but drifting toward insolvency — cash today, debts compounding beyond assets. Two gauges, two questions; name the right one before commenting.

Stop for this section's questions now — the solvency ratio, the one-to-one threshold, and liquid-versus-solvent scenarios sorted into the right gauge.

## Subtopic: Return on Owner's Equity — Was It Worth It?

The owner left R20 000 standing in the business, watched it grow to R23 900, and drew R4 000 along the way. The final analysis question is the investor's: what rate did that money earn — and could it have earned more elsewhere?

RETURN ON OWNER'S EQUITY: net profit over AVERAGE owner's equity, times one hundred. Why average? Because the equity changed during the year — profit joining, drawings leaving — so the fair base is the mean of the opening and closing figures: 20 000 plus 23 900, over two — R21 950. The return: 7 900 over 21 950, times one hundred — thirty-six percent, near enough.

Now the comparison that gives the figure meaning — because thirty-six percent floating alone says nothing. The alternative: money in a fixed deposit earns, say, eight percent, with almost no risk. The business returned thirty-six — four and a half times the safe rate. Was it worth it? The number says emphatically yes — BUT the full answer weighs RISK: the deposit's eight percent is promised; the business's thirty-six was earned through effort, could have been a loss, and is not guaranteed to repeat. The principle the examiners want stated: a business return SHOULD exceed the safe alternative comfortably, because the owner carries risk and does the work — a return merely equal to the bank's is a warning that the risk is unpaid.

And the linkage to see: this ratio joins the two statements in one fraction — profit from the income statement over equity from the balance sheet. Analysis full circle.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — the average base, the calculation, and the risk-weighed comparison against the safe alternative.

## Subtopic: The Full Dashboard and Technique

Assemble the term's complete instrument panel. From the income statement: mark-up achieved twenty-five; margin twenty; expenses seventeen; operating keep nine; net keep eight — the earning story. From the statement of financial position: current ratio six to one; acid test five comma three to one; solvency two comma six to one — the safety story. Across both: return on equity thirty-six percent — the investor's verdict. Nine gauges; a full health check in one reading.

Technique, five habits. One: express ratios AS ratios — "6 : 1", the answer to one — and percentages as percentages; format itself carries marks. Two: show the substitution with labels — "22 700 / 3 800" under the named formula — the route earns even when arithmetic slips. Three: use the AVERAGE equity for return, and say so; the unaveraged base is the classic error. Four: comment with the four moves — state with comparison, judge, explain from the scenario, recommend — and remember both directions: too low is dangerous, too high can be idle. Five: match gauge to question — bills of the coming months, liquidity; long-term safety, solvency; the owner's decision to stay invested, return on equity; and never answer a liquidity question from a profitability gauge.

One closing perspective: these ratios exist because the statements are read by people DECIDING things — a bank deciding to lend, an owner deciding to stay, a supplier deciding to give credit. Every ratio is a decision, quantified. Learn them that way and the formula sheet becomes a menu of questions you know how to answer.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same three questions from the kitchen table — because every household already runs these exact checks: month-end, the what-if-everything-goes-wrong count, and the was-it-worth-it question.

## Subtopic: The Month-End Test

Every family knows the month-end test. The bills are lining up at the door — rent, electricity, the account at the shop. On the table: this month's incoming money, the grocery stock in the cupboard, and what's owed TO you by your cousin. Question: can what's coming in cover what's knocking?

That's the current ratio — the shop's version just uses bigger words. Knocking within the year: R3 800. On the table within the year: R22 700 — stock, customers' debts, and cash. Six rand available for every one rand knocking. The family sleeps well.

But sharpen it, the way a careful mother does: "the cupboard's food counts, but I can't pay the landlord in rice." Strip out the stock — it must first find a buyer — and re-ask with only the near-cash things: R20 250 against R3 800. Still more than five to one. That's the acid test — the month-end test with the hard-to-sell things removed, the honest version of "can we pay if the stock doesn't move?"

And one more household truth the exam loves: too much sitting money is its own small problem. If R15 000 sleeps under the mattress while the loan charges its twelve percent upstairs, safety has quietly become waste. The healthy household keeps ENOUGH ready — and puts the rest to work.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the month-end test are coming to you right now: both versions, and the too-much-of-a-good-thing reading.

## Subtopic: If Everything Went Wrong Tomorrow

Now the bigger, quieter question families ask in hard times: if we had to settle EVERYTHING — every debt, long and short — by selling everything we own, would there be enough?

Count both sides for the shop. Everything owned: the equipment, the stock, the debts owed to us, the cash — R38 900. Everything owed to anyone, on any timeline: the bank's loan, standing at its grown R11 200, plus the suppliers, the telephone bill, the tenant's early month — R15 000. Divide: two rand sixty owned for every rand owed. That's solvency — and above one-to-one, the answer to the hardest question is still "yes, everyone could be paid."

Feel the difference between the two checks. Month-end liquidity is the SPRINT: this year's bills against this year's resources. Solvency is the WHOLE RACE: everything against everything. And they can disagree — that's why both exist. A family can own a house worth plenty yet miss the rent money this Friday: solvent, but illiquid. Another family can have cash this month while their total debts quietly outgrow everything they own: liquid, and sinking. One check never answers the other's question.

Below one-to-one, the word is insolvent — the debts exceed everything owned — and a business trading in that state is spending money that already belongs to its creditors. That's why the bank looks at this number FIRST: before asking whether you'll pay on time, it asks whether, in the end, you could pay at all.

## Subtopic: Was It Worth the Owner's While?

Last question — the one the owner asks alone, at the end of the year: "My R20 000-odd lived inside this shop all year. Was that the best home for it?"

Run the household version. The money in the shop, averaged over the year: about R21 950. What it produced: R7 900 of profit. That's thirty-six rand earned for every hundred invested — thirty-six percent. Now the comparison every adult makes: the bank down the road pays eight percent on a fixed deposit — guaranteed, no early mornings, no stock counts, no risk. The shop paid four and a half times that.

So — worth it? On the numbers, absolutely. But finish the thought like an accountant, because this is where the marks and the wisdom both live: the bank's eight is PROMISED; the shop's thirty-six was EARNED — with the owner's labour, worry and risk. A bad year could have paid zero, or eaten the capital. That's why a business must beat the bank comfortably, not narrowly: the extra return is the wage of risk. If a shop returns only nine percent while the deposit pays eight, the owner is working a year, carrying all the risk, for one extra rand per hundred — and the honest advice would be: rethink.

And notice what this one ratio quietly did: it joined the two statements — the year's profit from the story page, the owner's stake from the photograph — into a single number that answers a life decision: stay invested, or not.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: compute the return on the average stake, set it against the safe alternative, and give the owner honest advice — numbers first, risk weighed, decision stated. That closes the term's analysis: nine gauges, and you can read them all.
