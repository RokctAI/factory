**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First: how do I decide which SIDE of a reconciliation an item belongs to? Half the class puts every difference on whichever side makes the numbers work.

**Tutor:** Making the numbers work backwards is exactly the habit that fails the variant questions, so replace it with one diagnostic question per item: WHOSE record has not yet caught up with reality — or has recorded reality wrongly? Every difference has an owner. Take the bank version. Reality: you banked R12 700; your books recorded it; the bank has not — so the adjustment sits on the bank statement's side of the bridge. Reality: the bank charged fees; the bank recorded them; your books have not — so your BOOKS must be updated, and that item never appears on the reconciliation statement at all, because the reconciliation carries only genuine timing differences between two correct records. The creditors' version obeys the same law. The late invoice was real goods your ledger missed: ledger side. Your payment on the twenty-ninth was real money the statement missed: statement side. Their posting of another customer's invoice was their error: their side. Notice what this gives you that number-juggling cannot: when a question flips the scenario — an unfavourable bank balance, a statement lower than the ledger — the diagnostic still works, because you are not memorising directions, you are asking who is behind. One refinement completes the method: after assigning every item, both corrected records must land on the same figure, and that figure — not either starting balance — is the truth that goes into the financial statements. If they do not meet, you have either missed an item or put one on the wrong side, and the gap usually tells you which: a difference equal to twice an item means a side error; a difference equal to an item means an omission.

**Mandy:** Next: why is the age analysis such a big deal? A debtor owing R9 600 owes R9 600 whether it is one month old or four — the balance sheet number is identical.

**Tutor:** The balance sheet number is identical; its VALUE is not, and the age analysis is the instrument that measures the difference. A debt is not cash — it is a promise, and promises decay. A debtor within terms is simply a customer; a debtor at ninety days has told you something by their behaviour: they cannot pay, or will not, and every week that passes makes both possibilities more likely and your legal position more awkward. Collection professionals will tell you recovery rates fall steeply with age — which is why the accounting responds with the provision for bad debts, sized by looking hardest at the oldest column: the balance sheet keeps the R9 600 in debtors but honestly concedes, through the provision, that part of it is probably already lost. Prudence in action. There is also a cash flow argument that strong answers state: the aging book must be financed. If customers take ninety days while you pay suppliers in thirty, you fund sixty days of your customers' lives — possibly on your own overdraft, paying real interest so they can enjoy free credit. That is the sense in which a business with slow debtors becomes an unwilling bank. And there is an information argument: the age analysis read as PERCENTAGES over time is an early-warning system. A book drifting from seventy-five percent within terms toward fifty is a credit department losing control months before a single name is written off — trend first, casualties later. So the identical balance sheet figure can describe a healthy book or a dying one, and only the ages distinguish them.

**Mandy:** This one caused an argument: if rolling cash just means banking today's takings as yesterday's, the total banked eventually equals the total received. Where is the actual theft?

**Tutor:** Walk the first week slowly, because the trick hides in the first day and never leaves. Monday the cashier receives R4 000 and keeps it — that is the theft, complete on day one. Tuesday brings R4 300; the cashier banks R4 000 of it labelled as Monday's deposit, and now holds R4 300 short of one day. Wednesday's takings cover Tuesday's label, and so on. At every moment from Monday onward, the business is permanently one day's takings poorer — the missing R4 000 has not been delayed; it has been SPENT, and the rolling exists only to stop the books from noticing. The totals never catch up: the scheme ends either with the cashier repaying — rare — or with the hole surfacing the day receipts dip, a holiday closes the till, or an auditor compares deposit DATES in the cash journal against dates on the bank statement, which is precisely the test that exposes it: every deposit consistently landing a day or two late is the fingerprint. Sometimes the fraud escalates into teeming and lading on the debtors' book: money received from customer A is pocketed and later covered with customer B's payment, which is covered by customer C's — same rolling logic, now leaving a second fingerprint, receipts allocated to the wrong customers' accounts, which is why debtors' reconciliations and customer statements catch it. The controls are the ones the lesson named: the receiver of cash does not bank it, the banker does not reconcile it, deposits go in intact daily, and someone independent reviews the reconciliation monthly — four small rules that make the first Monday impossible.

**Mandy:** Last one: reconciliations feel like something computers do automatically now. My banking app matches transactions by itself — why is this still a grade 12 skill?

**Tutor:** Because the computer performs the MATCHING, and matching was never the skill — judgement about the unmatched is. An automated system pairs thousands of transactions flawlessly and then produces the only thing that matters: the exception list, the items that did not pair. Every question this lesson trained lives on that list. Is this unmatched deposit a timing difference or three weeks stale? Is this unmatched invoice a late delivery or another customer's goods? Is this recurring month-end reversal innocent or window-dressing? Software flags; someone must READ the flags — and fraudsters specifically exploit businesses where nobody does, because an automated reconciliation that no human reviews is the modern version of the lock nobody turns. There is a second reason the skill survives automation: the reconciliation LOGIC is how you audit the automation itself. Systems are configured by people, and a mis-mapped bank feed, a duplicated import or a rule that auto-matches on amount alone will happily reconcile rubbish to rubbish; the person who understands why both corrected records must independently land on the same figure is the person who notices when the system's version of agreement is fake. And third, the deeper truth: reconciliation is tested because it is the purest form of accounting's core discipline — two independent records of the same reality, forced into agreement, with every difference explained and owned. That discipline transfers to everything: control accounts against ledgers, statements against books, budget against actual, physical count against register. Machines will keep doing the arithmetic. The suspicion, the assignment of each difference to its owner, and the decision about what the stale items MEAN — that stays human, and that is what earns the marks.
