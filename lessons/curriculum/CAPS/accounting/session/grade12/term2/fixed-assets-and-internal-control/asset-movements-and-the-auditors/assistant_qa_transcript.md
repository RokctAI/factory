**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First: the age formula — accumulated depreciation divided by the annual charge. Does that still work if the company uses the diminishing balance method instead of straight line?

**Tutor:** Sharp question, and the honest answer is: not cleanly, and knowing why earns you marks. The formula works perfectly under straight line because the annual charge is constant — R90 000 every year — so accumulated depreciation grows like a clock: R630 000 gathered at R90 000 a year can only mean seven years. Under diminishing balance the charge SHRINKS every year — 20 percent of an ever-smaller carrying value — so there is no single annual charge to divide by, and dividing by this year's charge would overstate the age, because earlier years charged more. For diminishing balance you estimate age differently: compare the carrying value to the cost. If a vehicle cost R400 000 and its carrying value is R204 800, ask how many times 80 percent must be applied to 400 000 to reach that figure — once gives 320 000, twice gives 256 000, three times gives 204 800 — so the vehicle is three years old. The examinable point beneath the arithmetic: every age estimate is method-dependent, because depreciation is a MODEL of wear, not a measurement of it. That is also why interpretation answers should say assets are seven years old ON AVERAGE, ACCORDING TO THE DEPRECIATION POLICY — a sentence that signals you know the number's pedigree, and precisely the caution that separates a top answer from a mechanical one.

**Mandy:** Next: why does the disposal leave the fixed asset note at carrying value, but the income statement shows a loss computed against the selling price? It feels like the same event is recorded twice with different numbers.

**Tutor:** It is one event answering two different questions, and the split is exactly right. The fixed asset note has a single job: to reconcile the carrying value of the asset CLASS from the start of the year to the end. When the vehicle leaves, what the class loses is what the class was carrying — cost of R200 000 out of the cost column, accumulated depreciation of R140 000 out of that column, net effect R60 000 of carrying value gone. The note does not care what the buyer paid, because the buyer's money is not part of the asset class; including the R48 000 would break the reconciliation. The income statement answers the other question: how did the SALE turn out? Proceeds of R48 000 against a book value of R60 000 — a loss of R12 000, reported as an expense. Notice the two figures are not rivals; they are complements that always reconcile: carrying value out of the note, less proceeds into the bank, equals the loss in the income statement — 60 000 less 48 000 is 12 000. If you ever find a disposal where note, bank and income statement do not tie together in that triangle, one of the three is wrong. Examiners exploit the triangle constantly: they give you two corners and ask for the third, sometimes hiding the carrying value inside the note's blank cell so you must chain both skills together.

**Mandy:** This one caused an argument: if internal auditors are employees, are their findings not worthless whenever the problem involves their own bosses? Someone said no employee will ever write a report that gets a director in trouble.

**Tutor:** The tension is real — it is the central weakness of internal audit, and the syllabus wants you to see both the weakness and the machinery built to manage it. An internal auditor's salary, promotion and daily working life sit inside the company, so pressure is possible: a finding that embarrasses a powerful manager tests the auditor's courage and the company's culture at once. But worthless is too strong, for three reasons. First, structure: in a well-governed company, internal audit does not report to the managers it audits — it reports to the AUDIT COMMITTEE, a subcommittee of the board that should be dominated by independent non-executive directors. That reporting line exists precisely so a finding can bypass the manager it implicates. Second, scope: the overwhelming majority of internal audit work — fuel logbooks, stock counts, asset verification, payment controls — concerns processes well below board level, where independence is not strained and where the auditor's inside knowledge is an advantage no outsider can match. Third, the backstop: where internal pressure does suppress a finding, the external auditor is the next layer — independent by appointment, reporting to shareholders — and an external auditor who finds internal audit reports being buried has learned something important about the company's control environment, which itself shapes the audit opinion. So the mature statement is: internal audit's value depends on its reporting line and the company's governance, and the King Code's demands of accountability and transparency are what keep that value real. Where governance is rotten, yes — internal audit rots with it, and that is why the annual report's governance pages matter to outside readers.

**Mandy:** Last one: sampling still feels like guessing. If Lindiwe checks ten taxis out of fifteen and finds nothing, how sure is she about the other five — and what should she do if she finds ONE problem?

**Tutor:** Two excellent halves. On the first: with ten of fifteen checked, Lindiwe has direct evidence on two-thirds of the fleet and a defensible basis for a conclusion about the rest, PROVIDED the ten were selected without bias — which is why method matters more than size. If the depots chose which taxis to present, the sample is worthless regardless of number; if she drew randomly, or stratified so the highest-value vehicles were all included, the unchecked five carry low and quantifiable risk. Notice the design trick: sample the big rands with certainty, sample the rest by chance — then what remains unchecked is both small in value and unpredictable in selection, the two properties that make hiding things hard. On the second half: one finding changes everything, because a sample result is not a fact about one taxi — it is an estimate of a RATE. One logbook failure in ten suggests the fleet may have more, so the professional response is never to fix the one and move on. She should extend the sample — check more vehicles, and check the same vehicle's history backwards — to establish whether she has found an isolated slip or a pattern; investigate the cause, because a control that one driver could bypass is a control others can bypass; and report the finding with its projection: at this rate, roughly one vehicle in ten is refuelling unrecorded, costing an estimated amount per month. That projection is what turns a clipboard observation into a management decision — and it is exactly the gap-risk-recommendation structure the examination asks you to write.
