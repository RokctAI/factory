**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First: if FIFO and weighted average give different profits from the same shelf, one of them must be wrong about reality. Which one is TRUE?

**Tutor:** Neither, and the reason goes to what stock valuation actually is. Once identical units bought at different prices are mixed on a shelf, there is no physical fact of the matter about which COST remains — the boxes do not remember their invoices. So accounting does not discover the answer; it CHOOSES a convention and applies it without favour. FIFO's convention — oldest costs leave first — usually mirrors how shops physically rotate stock, which makes its balance sheet value feel current. Weighted average's convention — every unit carries the pooled cost — refuses to pretend anyone knows which units remain, which makes it robust where stock is genuinely blended, like grain in a silo or fuel in a tank. Both obey historical cost; both, applied consistently, charge every rand of cost to some year; and across the life of the business they produce identical total profit — they only slice it differently between years. What WOULD be wrong is a method applied inconsistently, or chosen fresh each year to flatter the result. That is why the question you will actually face is never which method is true, but: given the direction prices moved, explain which method reports the higher profit, and why the business may not switch. Answer that with the one-law logic — closing stock value and cost of sales split one fixed pool — and you cannot be caught out.

**Mandy:** Next: why does the higher closing stock give the HIGHER profit? Instinct says stock sitting unsold is a bad thing, so a bigger stock number feels like it should hurt.

**Tutor:** The instinct confuses two different questions — how much stock is there, and what does the stock VALUE do to the profit equation. Take the equation slowly. Gross profit is sales minus cost of sales, and under either system cost of sales is the cost of goods available minus closing stock. Radebe had R57 300 of fans available. That pool has only two destinations: charged against this year's sales, or standing on the shelf for next year. So the closing stock figure acts as a gate — every rand assigned to the shelf is a rand NOT charged against this year's sales. FIFO put R14 700 on the shelf, so R42 600 went to cost of sales; weighted average put R13 752 on the shelf, so R43 548 was charged. Higher shelf, lower charge, higher profit — mechanically, with no judgement about whether holding stock is wise. Your instinct is about a different and equally worthwhile issue: TOO MUCH stock is bad for the business — cash tied up, storage costs, obsolescence risk — which is what the stock turnover rate and stockholding period measure in the analysis toolkit. Keep the two apart: valuation asks what the shelf is worth; efficiency asks whether the shelf should be that full. A business can have a high stock VALUE and a healthy turnover, or a modest value that is nonetheless dead stock nobody wants.

**Mandy:** This one caused an argument: the lesson said stock must be valued at the lower of cost and net realisable value. Where does that fit with FIFO and weighted average — is prudence a third method?

**Tutor:** Good precision — prudence is not a third method; it is a ceiling that sits ON TOP of whichever method you use. The sequence runs in two steps. Step one, compute cost by your chosen method: FIFO says the 300 fans cost R14 700; weighted average says R13 752. Step two, ask what the stock can actually FETCH — its net realisable value, meaning expected selling price less any costs still needed to make the sale. In the normal case, selling price is far above cost and the ceiling never touches anything: the fans cost around R48 each and sell for R70, so they stand at cost, and prudence is satisfied silently. But suppose a new fan model floods the market and Radebe can only clear the old units at R46 each, spending R3 a unit on advertising to do it. Net realisable value is R43 — below cost. Prudence now bites: the stock must be written DOWN to R43 a unit, and the write-down is an expense this year. The logic is the asymmetry at the heart of prudence: never anticipate a profit — stock is not valued at selling price while it stands on the shelf — but always anticipate a loss the moment it becomes visible. Reporting the stock at a cost you know you cannot recover would overstate assets and this year's profit, deceiving every reader downstream. So in an answer: method first, ceiling second, and mention the write-down as an expense — that order shows you know how the pieces stack.

**Mandy:** Last one: the deficit was only R1 146 on a stockroom worth over thirteen thousand. At what point does a deficit stop being normal shrinkage and start being an internal control problem?

**Tutor:** The honest answer is that the number alone never tells you — the JUDGEMENT comes from three comparisons, and this is exactly how an internal auditor thinks. First, compare to the past: Radebe's deficit is about eight percent of closing stock by units — twenty-five of three hundred. If last year was one percent, something changed, and a rising trend matters far more than any single figure; shrinkage that doubles quietly, month after month, is a control failing in real time. Second, compare to the nature of the stock: boxed desk fans are sealed, bulky and countable, so eight percent walking out is alarming for this stock in a way it might not be for loose sweets, where breakage, spillage and miscounts genuinely occur. High-value, portable, resellable stock — phones, razor blades, batteries — earns tight controls precisely because its shrinkage is rarely innocent. Third, compare to the pattern: shortages concentrated in one storeroom, one shift, one clerk's receiving documents, or spiking when a particular counter is on duty, convert a statistic into a lead. Then act on the ladder of controls: recount independently to eliminate counting error; check the paper trail for unrecorded sales or supplier short-deliveries — theft is not the only explanation, and accusing before eliminating alternatives is both unfair and legally dangerous; tighten access and segregation where the pattern points; and keep writing the deficit off honestly in the meantime, because hiding shrinkage inside cost of sales removes the very signal management needs. The deficit line is not an embarrassment to be minimised on paper — it is an instrument, and it only works if it is measured honestly.
