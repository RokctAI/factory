# Part 1 — Expert

Ask a shop what its stock is worth and the answer sounds simple — until prices start moving. Buy identical heaters at R50 in March, R55 in May, R60 in August and R62 in December, sell most of them, and the ones left on the shelf have no name tags saying which delivery they came from. What are THEY worth? The answer decides cost of sales, gross profit, and the balance sheet in one stroke — and the syllabus offers three defensible answers: specific identification, first-in-first-out, and weighted average. This lesson works all three to the rand on one set of figures, then turns to the discipline around them: validation of stock, the GAAP principles that referee the choice, and the ethics and internal controls that keep the stockroom honest. Inventory valuation is a guaranteed examination question, usually with the arithmetic and the ethics stitched together.

## Subtopic: Three Methods, One Shelf of Stock

First the record-keeping frame. Under the PERPETUAL system, stock is a running account: every purchase goes into Trading Stock, every sale moves cost out to Cost of Sales immediately, and the account claims to know the stock figure at any moment. Under the PERIODIC system, purchases pile up in a Purchases account and nobody computes cost of sales until stock is physically counted at year-end: opening stock plus purchases less closing stock equals cost of sales. Either way, the counted stock must be given a VALUE — and that is where the methods part company.

Method one: SPECIFIC IDENTIFICATION. Each item's actual cost is known and tracked — appropriate where items are few, expensive and distinguishable: vehicles by chassis number, jewellery, machinery. A car dealer values each car on the floor at what THAT car cost. Nobody tracks individual tins of paint this way; the bookkeeping would cost more than the paint.

Method two: FIRST-IN-FIRST-OUT — FIFO. Assume the OLDEST stock is sold first, so the stock remaining on the shelf carries the NEWEST prices. The assumption usually matches physical reality — shops rotate stock so the old sells before it expires — but understand precisely: FIFO is an assumption about COSTS for valuation, not a claim about which physical box left first.

Method three: WEIGHTED AVERAGE. Pool the cost of everything available, divide by the units available, and value every unit — sold or remaining — at that average. One price, no history.

And the referee: GAAP. The HISTORICAL COST principle anchors stock at what it cost, not what it will sell for. PRUDENCE steps in when stock is damaged or obsolete — value at the lower of cost and net realisable value, never anticipating profit. And CONSISTENCY: pick a method and keep it, year after year, because comparability dies the moment a business switches methods to suit its story. A change is permitted only for good reason, disclosed openly.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: perpetual against periodic, the three methods matched to the stock they suit, and the three GAAP principles named to their jobs.

## Subtopic: FIFO Worked to the Rand

Dlamini Stores sells one model of paraffin heater. The year's stock story, spoken slowly: opening stock 200 units that cost R50 each — R10 000. Purchases: May, 400 units at R55 — R22 000; August, 300 units at R60 — R18 000; December, 100 units at R62 — R6 200. Total available: 1 000 units costing R56 200. During the year 750 units were sold at R90 each — sales of R67 500 — leaving 250 units on the shelf at year-end.

FIFO values those 250 remaining units at the NEWEST prices, counted backwards from the last delivery. The December delivery supplies the first 100 units at R62 — R6 200. Still 150 units to account for, drawn from the August delivery at R60 — R9 000. Closing stock: 6 200 plus 9 000 — R15 200.

Cost of sales follows by subtraction: cost of everything available, R56 200, less what remains, R15 200 — R41 000. Gross profit: sales of R67 500 less R41 000 — R26 500.

Notice what FIFO did in a year of RISING prices: it left the newest, most expensive costs on the balance sheet and sent the oldest, cheapest costs to cost of sales. Consequence one: closing stock sits close to current replacement prices — the balance sheet is up to date. Consequence two: cost of sales is low, so gross profit is HIGH — flattering, and taxed accordingly. Reverse the price trend and every consequence reverses with it. Examiners love asking which method shows the higher profit — the answer is always conditional on the direction prices moved, and saying so is the mark.

The layered counting is where marks leak: the 250 units must be built from the last deliveries backwards — 100 at R62 first, THEN 150 at R60. Counting forward from the opening stock, or mixing layers, is the classic error.

Stop for this section's questions now — the 250 units layered from the newest deliveries, cost of sales by subtraction, and the rising-price consequences stated.

## Subtopic: Weighted Average Worked to the Rand

Same shelf, same year, second answer. The weighted average method refuses to guess which units remain. Instead: pool everything. Total cost available, R56 200; total units available, 1 000. Average cost per unit: 56 200 divided by 1 000 — R56,20.

Closing stock: 250 units at R56,20 — R14 050. Cost of sales: 750 units sold at R56,20 — R42 150. Check the pair the way an examiner would: 14 050 plus 42 150 is exactly R56 200, the full pool. If your two figures do not rebuild the pool, one of them is wrong.

Gross profit: 67 500 less 42 150 — R25 350.

Now set the two methods side by side, because the comparison IS the understanding. FIFO closing stock R15 200 against weighted average R14 050 — a difference of R1 150. FIFO gross profit R26 500 against R25 350 — the same R1 150. Not a coincidence, a law: whatever value sits in closing stock has, by that exact amount, been kept OUT of cost of sales. Value the shelf higher and the year looks more profitable; value it lower and profit shrinks. Stock valuation is profit measurement — the same rands, standing on the shelf or standing in the profit line.

Two cautions. WEIGHTED average, not simple average: never average the four PRICES — 50, 55, 60 and 62 average to R56,75, the wrong answer, because it ignores how many units arrived at each price. Weight by units, always. And carriage on purchases joins the cost of stock — if a delivery costs R2 per unit to transport, that R2 is part of the unit's cost in the pool, not an expense on its own.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — the pool averaged by units, the closing-stock-plus-cost-of-sales rebuild, and the R1 150 explained as one law.

## Subtopic: Validation, Ethics and Control of Stock

Valuation assumed one quiet fact: that 250 units actually exist. VALIDATION is the discipline of checking. A physical stock count at year-end — and cyclical counts through the year — sets the book figure against the floor figure. Dlamini's records say 250 heaters; the count finds 235. Fifteen units are MISSING — at the weighted average cost of R56,20, a trading stock deficit of R843, written off as an expense. The deficit is not a rounding nuisance; it is information. Theft, breakage, unrecorded sales, or a receiving clerk signing for deliveries that never arrived — the deficit is where every one of those stories first surfaces.

The INTERNAL CONTROLS that keep the number small, each pinned to the risk it answers. Division of duties: the person ordering stock, the person receiving it, the person keeping the records and the person counting must not be one person — a buyer who also receives and records can invent deliveries indefinitely. Documentation: pre-numbered orders, delivery notes checked against invoices, so every unit that entered can be traced. Physical security: locked storerooms, restricted access, cameras at dispatch. Independent counts: counters who are not the storeman, rotating and unannounced. And management review: deficits tracked month by month, because a rising trend is a control failing in real time.

Then the ETHICS the syllabus attaches, sharpened by the valuation methods themselves. Because closing stock value flows straight into profit, stock is a favourite instrument of manipulation: OVERSTATE the count or the value, and profit inflates — flattering managers chasing bonuses, deceiving lenders and shareholders. UNDERSTATE it, and profit shrinks — understating tax owed to SARS. Switching valuation methods in a convenient year is the polite version of the same sin, which is exactly why CONSISTENCY is a GAAP principle and why a method change must be disclosed. An accountant instructed to nudge the stock figure is being instructed to lie in rands — and the professional answer is refusal, whatever the seniority of the voice instructing.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the deficit computed and read as information, controls pinned to risks, and stock manipulation traced from shelf to profit line.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same topic from a tuck-shop cooldrink fridge — because every valuation argument in this lesson happens in miniature the day prices go up at the wholesaler.

## Subtopic: The Fridge That Ate the Profits

Zanele runs a tuck shop. Cooldrinks cost her R10 each at the wholesaler in January, and she has been selling them at R15 — five rand profit a can, life is simple. Then winter comes, the wholesaler's price climbs to R12, and Zanele's fridge becomes a philosophy problem.

She has 30 cans in the fridge. Some were bought at R10, some at R12, and cold cans do not carry receipts. Her cousin asks a simple bookkeeping question: Zanele, what is the stock in your fridge WORTH? And Zanele realises she has three honest options. She could say: I know exactly which can is which — true for her three tubs of expensive ice cream with dates on the lids, hopeless for thirty identical cans. She could say: I always sell the oldest cans first — the front row is old stock, so whatever is LEFT must be the newest, the R12 cans. Or she could shrug and say: they cost me R10 and R12 in some mixture — call every can the average of what the whole batch cost.

Hold onto why the question matters at all. Whatever value Zanele puts on those 30 cans decides how much profit she made — because cans in the fridge are cost she has NOT yet used up, and cans sold are cost she has. The fridge and the profit line share one pot of rands. Value the fridge high, and this year's profit rises with it. Value it low, profit falls. Same fridge, same cans — the method chooses where the rands stand.

Questions on this piece are with you now: the three honest options named, which stock suits each, and why the fridge's value and the profit are one decision.

## Subtopic: Two Honest Answers to One Question

Run Zanele's numbers, small enough to hold in your head. She started the season with nothing, bought 40 cans at R10 — R400 — then 20 cans at R12 — R240. Available: 60 cans that cost R640. She sold 30 cans at R15 each — R450 in the till. Thirty cans remain.

Answer one, first-in-first-out. The oldest cans sold first, so the fridge holds the newest: all 20 of the R12 cans — R240 — plus 10 of the R10 cans — R100. Fridge: R340. Cost of the cans she sold: R640 less R340 — R300. Profit: R450 less R300 — R150.

Answer two, weighted average. Pool it: R640 across 60 cans is — careful, weight by cans, not by prices — R10,67 a can, near enough. Fridge: 30 cans at R10,67 — R320. Cost of sales: R320. Profit: R450 less R320 — R130.

Two honest methods, R20 apart. Where did the R20 go? Look at the two fridge values: R340 against R320 — the same R20. Whatever the fridge holds, the profit line released. That is the whole mechanism, in a tuck shop.

Now the sentence that earns the interpretation mark, in Zanele's words: prices went UP this season, and the first-in-first-out fridge holds the NEWEST, priciest cans — so it shows the higher stock value and the higher profit. If the wholesaler had CUT prices, the same method would show the lower profit. The method does not create or destroy a single rand across the years — every can's cost gets charged eventually — it only decides WHICH year carries it.

And the rule Zanele must now live by: pick one answer and keep it. A shopkeeper who values the fridge one way in a good year and another way in a lean year is not doing arithmetic anymore; she is telling stories.

Your questions for this part are up now: both answers computed on the 60 cans, the R20 traced fridge-to-profit, and the rising-price sentence said properly.

## Subtopic: When the Count and the Book Disagree

One evening Zanele counts the fridge: 27 cans. Her notebook says 30. Three cans of profit walked off somewhere — a friend helping herself, a can given away and forgotten, a sale that never reached the notebook. At R10,67 a can, that is R32 of stock deficit, written off, gone. Three cans sounds small. Three cans a week is a hundred and fifty-six cans a year — most of a month's profit, drunk by a ghost.

Scale Zanele up to a company with a warehouse and the ghost gets professional. So business builds fences, and each fence answers a specific trick. Different people order, receive, record and count — because one person doing all four can invent deliveries and drink the difference. Papers with numbers on them — orders, delivery notes, invoices — so every can that ever entered can be traced to a signature. Locks and cameras where the stock lives. Counts done by someone OTHER than the person who keeps the stock, on days nobody announced. And a manager who reads the deficit every month, because a small number growing is a fence failing.

Last, the dark side of the fridge, and it is a grade 12 examination favourite. Because the fridge's value flows straight into profit, a dishonest owner has two temptations. Puff the fridge UP — count cans that are not there, value them above cost — and the shop looks more profitable: banks lend, buyers overpay, bonuses get earned on fiction. Squash the fridge DOWN, and profit shrinks — along with the tax owed on it. Both are lies measured in rands, and both are usually caught the same way: by an independent count and a consistent method, which is precisely why the count and the consistency exist.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: the deficit priced and scaled, the fences matched to the tricks, and the two temptations with the controls that catch them. Take the layering and the pooling with you — the examination will hand you a shelf and moving prices, and now you own three honest answers.
