# Part 1 — Expert

A trader buys goods and sells them dearer; the accounting follows the goods through one door and out another. A MANUFACTURER makes the goods, and the accounting must follow flour into dough, dough into product, product into money — which demands its own vocabulary, its own statement, and its own arithmetic. This lesson builds the full grade 12 set: the cost concepts unique to manufacturing; the production cost statement with its notes; the abridged income statement that follows it; unit costs and the break-even point; and the ethics and internal controls of the factory floor. This is Paper 2 territory, and the production cost statement is one of its anchor questions.

## Subtopic: The Language of the Factory

Manufacturing costs sort into three, and every question begins with the sorting.

DIRECT MATERIAL COST — the raw materials that become the product itself: timber in the furniture, fabric in the uniform, flour in the bread. Carriage on raw materials joins their cost. INDIRECT materials — machine oil, cleaning supplies, glue used in small amounts — do not qualify; they are overheads.

DIRECT LABOUR COST — the wages of workers whose hands make the product: the machinist, the assembler, the baker. The supervisor watching six production lines, the security guard, the maintenance team — indirect labour, again overheads.

FACTORY OVERHEAD COST — everything else the factory needs but cannot pin to one unit: factory rent, factory electricity, depreciation on factory machinery, indirect materials and indirect labour, insurance of the plant. The word FACTORY matters: rent of the sales office is not a factory overhead — it is an administration cost, and keeping factory costs apart from office costs is half the topic's discipline.

Direct materials plus direct labour has a name — PRIME COST — the costs that touch the product directly. Add factory overheads and the total is MANUFACTURING cost.

Beyond the factory sit the two operating cost families: ADMINISTRATION COST — office salaries, office rent, stationery, audit fees — and SELLING AND DISTRIBUTION COST — advertising, sales commission, delivery vehicle expenses, bad debts.

And the factory holds three stocks, not one: RAW MATERIALS waiting to be used; WORK-IN-PROGRESS — units started but unfinished, dough half-baked, chairs half-assembled; and FINISHED GOODS ready for sale. Three stock accounts, three closing figures, and the examination moves value between them.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: costs sorted into their three factory families plus two operating ones, prime cost named, and the three stocks separated.

## Subtopic: The Production Cost Statement

The production cost statement answers one question: what did it cost to FINISH goods this year? Build it for Mzansi Textiles, spoken line by line.

Start with DIRECT MATERIALS USED, which has its own note: opening stock of raw materials R80 000, plus purchases of R640 000, plus carriage on purchases of R20 000 — materials available, R740 000 — less closing stock of raw materials R120 000. Direct material cost: R620 000. The subtraction matters: buying materials is not using them; what remains on the shelf has not entered production.

Add DIRECT LABOUR COST: R380 000. PRIME COST: R1 000 000.

Add FACTORY OVERHEAD COST, also carried by a note that gathers indirect materials, indirect labour, factory rent, factory electricity and depreciation on factory plant: R440 000 in total. TOTAL MANUFACTURING COST: R1 440 000.

Now the work-in-progress adjustment, the statement's signature move. The factory began the year with R60 000 of half-finished goods — last year's manufacturing cost, finished this year, so ADD it. It ended with R100 000 still unfinished — this year's cost, not yet finished goods, so SUBTRACT it. COST OF PRODUCTION OF FINISHED GOODS: 1 440 000 plus 60 000 less 100 000 — R1 400 000.

Read the logic, not just the arithmetic: the statement filters a year of spending down to the cost of what was actually COMPLETED. Money spent is not the same as production finished, and work-in-progress is the difference.

One more division while the figures are warm: Mzansi finished 70 000 garments this year. UNIT COST OF PRODUCTION: 1 400 000 divided by 70 000 — R20 per unit. That single number prices the stock, feeds the break-even analysis, and lets management ask the only question that matters: is R20 good?

Stop for this section's questions now — the materials note subtracted properly, prime cost through total manufacturing cost, the work-in-progress adjustment in both directions, and the unit cost.

## Subtopic: From Factory Floor to Income Statement

The factory's output now flows into a trading story, and the manufacturer's income statement picks it up exactly where a trader's would say purchases.

FINISHED GOODS first. Opening stock of finished goods: R90 000 — that is 4 500 garments carried at last year's R20. Add the year's cost of production, R1 400 000 — 70 000 garments. Available for sale: 74 500 garments carrying R1 490 000. Closing stock: 7 000 garments at R20 — R140 000. COST OF SALES: 1 490 000 less 140 000 — R1 350 000, which is 67 500 garments sold.

SALES: 67 500 garments at R32 each — R2 160 000. GROSS PROFIT: 2 160 000 less 1 350 000 — R810 000. Check it as an examiner would: gross profit on cost of sales is 810 000 over 1 350 000 — exactly 60 percent, the mark-up policy confirmed.

Then the operating families, each with its note. ADMINISTRATION COST: office salaries, office rent, stationery, audit fees — R290 000. SELLING AND DISTRIBUTION COST: advertising, commission, delivery expenses, bad debts — R256 000. NET PROFIT: 810 000 less 290 000 less 256 000 — R264 000.

The classification traps live here, so name them. Depreciation splits by USE: factory machinery to factory overheads, office equipment to administration, delivery vehicles to selling and distribution. Rent splits by floor space if the building is shared. Salaries split by role: factory supervisor to overheads, bookkeeper to administration, sales staff to selling. One expense, three possible homes — the ADDRESS depends on what the spending served, and examiners deliberately hand you mixed lists to sort.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — finished goods rolled into cost of sales, the 60 percent confirmed, and mixed expenses delivered to their correct notes.

## Subtopic: Break-Even and the Ethics of Making Things

Management's next question: how many units must we sell before we stop losing money? That is the BREAK-EVEN POINT, and it needs costs sorted a different way — not by department, but by BEHAVIOUR.

VARIABLE COSTS rise with every unit made: materials, direct labour paid per unit, sales commission, packaging. FIXED COSTS arrive regardless of volume: rent, insurance, salaries, depreciation. The split matters because each sold unit contributes the gap between its price and its own variable cost toward the pile of fixed costs — that gap is the CONTRIBUTION per unit.

Take the drill case the way the examination sets it, with given totals. A factory sells its product at R32 a unit; variable costs are R21 a unit; fixed costs for the year are R660 000. Contribution per unit: 32 less 21 — R11. BREAK-EVEN POINT: fixed costs divided by contribution — 660 000 over 11 — 60 000 units. Below 60 000 units the year runs at a loss; at 60 000 it breaks exactly even; every unit beyond contributes R11 of pure profit. If the factory produced and sold 67 500 units, it operates 7 500 units above break-even — a cushion worth stating in every answer, because the interpretation mark asks whether the business is safe, not just where the line is.

Work the formula's levers, because examiners do: raise the price and contribution widens, break-even falls; materials get dearer and contribution narrows, break-even climbs; take on more fixed costs — a new machine, a bigger factory — and the pile to be covered grows. Every business decision moves the line.

Then the factory's ETHICS, which the syllabus lists deliberately. Product QUALITY and product AGE: selling substandard or expired goods — bread past its date, brake pads that fail — endangers customers for margin. RAW MATERIALS sourced honestly: no illegally harvested timber, no undocumented imports; and supporting LOCAL products and suppliers builds the economy that buys your goods. PRICE FIXING: competitors agreeing on prices is illegal collusion against the consumer. THEFT and FRAUD on the factory floor — materials walking out, hours claimed but not worked, production figures inflated. The INTERNAL CONTROLS answer each: raw materials issued only against requisitions, wages tied to clock cards, production counted independently of the people who report it, waste and off-cuts monitored — because materials unaccounted for are the factory's version of a stock deficit.

The final questions of this part are with you now: costs re-sorted by behaviour, break-even computed and cushioned, the levers argued, and each ethical risk paired with its control.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the whole factory again, at kitchen scale — because everything a production cost statement says, Auntie Grace's koeksister business says in one morning.

## Subtopic: Auntie Grace's Koeksister Kitchen

Auntie Grace bakes koeksisters and sells them outside the taxi rank. Watch her kitchen with accounting eyes and the whole factory vocabulary appears before breakfast.

The flour, sugar, oil and syrup that BECOME the koeksister — direct materials. Her niece Palesa, hired to braid dough all morning — direct labour: hands that make the product. But the kitchen needs more than hands and flour: the gas bottle, the rent for the stand, the scouring pads, the oil for the pans that fries a hundred batches — nobody can say which koeksister used which rand of gas. Those are the overheads: real costs, spread over everything, pinned to nothing.

Add it the factory way. Materials plus Palesa's wages — prime cost, the costs touching the product. Plus the kitchen's overheads — the morning's manufacturing cost.

Now look around the kitchen at ten o'clock and see the THREE stocks. In the cupboard: flour and sugar not yet used — raw materials. On the counter: braided dough waiting for the oil, half-made — work-in-progress. In the display box: golden koeksisters ready to sell — finished goods. Three different stages, three different values, and money sitting in every one of them.

And one distinction Auntie Grace refuses to blur: the taxi fare to town to buy stock jars, the notebook she writes orders in — that is not the KITCHEN's cost; that is the business-running cost. Factory costs make the product; office costs run the show; selling costs move the product. Three addresses. Every rand she spends lives at exactly one of them.

Questions on this piece are with you now: her costs sorted direct, indirect and beyond-the-kitchen, and the three stocks found on the counter.

## Subtopic: What One Koeksister Costs

Auntie Grace's grandson, doing grade 12, asks the killer question: Gogo, what does ONE koeksister cost you? She laughs — but the month's numbers can answer.

This month the kitchen used R6 000 of ingredients — used, mind: she bought R7 000 worth, but R1 000 of flour and sugar still sits in the cupboard, and the cupboard's contents cost nothing to THIS month's koeksisters. Palesa's wages: R5 000. Overheads — gas, stand rent, odds and ends: R3 000. Kitchen total: R14 000.

One adjustment, and it is the clever one. On the first of the month there was a tray of braided dough from last month — say R400 worth of last month's spending — which this month finished and fried: count it in. On the thirty-first, another tray of dough sits waiting — R600 of this month's spending that produced nothing sellable yet: leave it out. Finished production this month: 14 000 plus 400 less 600 — R13 800.

The month finished 2 300 koeksisters. So: 13 800 divided by 2 300 — SIX RAND a koeksister. That number is the business in one figure. Selling at R10, she clears R4 of gross margin on each. If the flour price climbs and the unit cost creeps to R7, she sees it immediately — and decides: raise the price, find cheaper flour, or accept a thinner margin. Businesses that never compute their unit cost make that decision blind, usually by accident, usually too late.

And notice what the grandson's question really taught: money SPENT this month and production FINISHED this month are different things. The cupboard and the dough tray are where the difference lives. That is the entire production cost statement, in a kitchen.

Your questions for this part are up now: used against bought, the dough-tray adjustment both ways, and the six-rand unit cost put to work.

## Subtopic: How Many Must She Sell Before She Smiles?

Auntie Grace's last lesson is the one that decides whether the stand survives winter: how many koeksisters must she sell in a month just to cover herself?

Sort her costs by how they BEHAVE, not where they live. Every koeksister she makes eats about R5 of ingredients and packaging — costs that come and go with each unit: variable. And every month, sell or starve, the stand rent takes R4 000 — the cost that arrives like the calendar: fixed.

Each koeksister sells for R10 and carries R5 of its own costs, so each one CONTRIBUTES R5 toward that fixed pile. Now the only division in the topic: four thousand of fixed costs, five rand of contribution — she must sell EIGHT HUNDRED koeksisters a month before the stand has paid for itself. Number 801 is the first one that feeds the family.

Prove it, because proving it is how the formula sticks: 800 sold at R10 is R8 000 in; ingredients for 800 at R5 is R4 000; rent R4 000. Eight thousand in, eight thousand out — zero. Break-even means exactly that: the smile starts at 801.

Now move her levers, one at a time. Syrup prices rise and each koeksister eats R6: contribution drops to R4, and break-even jumps to a thousand — two hundred more sales for the same zero. She raises her price to R11: contribution R6, break-even falls to about 667. She considers a second stand at another rank — R4 000 more fixed rent — and her break-even doubles: growth is wonderful, but the pile to cover grows before the first extra koeksister sells. Every business decision she could make moves that one number, which is why managers compute it before they decide, not after.

And the ethics she will not trade: day-old koeksisters sold as fresh buy one good week and lose the taxi rank forever; agreeing prices with the vetkoek lady next door is collusion, not neighbourliness; and if Palesa's cousin joins and the flour starts vanishing, Auntie Grace counts bags against batches — requisitions and production counts, kitchen-sized.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: the behaviour sort, break-even proved at 800, the levers moved one at a time, and the ethics that keep the stand standing. The examination will dress this factory in bigger numbers — the machinery underneath is Auntie Grace's, exactly.
