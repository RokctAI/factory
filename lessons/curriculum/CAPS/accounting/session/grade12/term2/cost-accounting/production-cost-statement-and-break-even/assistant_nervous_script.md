Hey, it's Mandy — cost accounting looks like a factory full of moving parts, but the whole topic is one river flowing through three pools, plus a single division at the end. Walk it with me.

Pool one: raw materials. Opening stock, plus purchases, plus carriage, MINUS closing stock — what remains on the shelf was not used. That gives direct materials cost. Add direct labour: prime cost. Add factory overheads: total manufacturing cost.

Pool two: work-in-progress. Add the opening half-made goods — last year's money, finished this year. Subtract the closing half-made goods — this year's money, not finished yet. Now you hold the cost of production of FINISHED goods. If the adjustment ever flips in your head, run the extreme test: a factory that finished nothing must show zero, and the formula only gives zero if closing work-in-progress is subtracted.

Pool three: finished goods. Opening stock plus this year's production, minus closing stock — cost of sales, and from there the income statement runs exactly like a trader's.

Then the division: cost of production over units produced equals unit cost. Circle it — it links every part of the question.

Break-even is its own little machine with its own sorting: variable costs per unit against fixed costs in a pile. Contribution is price minus VARIABLE cost only — never use the full unit cost there, that is the classic error. Fixed costs divided by contribution gives the break-even units, and always add the comparison sentence: we produce this many, so we are safely above, or dangerously below.

Classification marks are free marks: factory, office, or selling — ask what the rand was spent FOR, and depreciation follows its asset.

Three pools, one division, one little machine. You've got this!
