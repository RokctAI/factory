**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First: the drawings adjustment. When the owner takes stock home, why does the VAT count AGAINST the business? She owns the shop — surely she can take her own stock without SARS getting involved?

**Tutor:** She can take the stock; what she cannot keep is the tax break that arrived with it. Trace the R4 000 of stock from the beginning. When the business bought it, the supplier charged R600 of VAT, and the business claimed that R600 back as input tax — legitimately, because the rule is that input tax is claimable on goods bought TO MAKE TAXABLE SALES. The state, in effect, advanced the business R600 on the promise that this stock would be sold onward with output tax charged on it. The owner taking it home breaks that promise: the goods exit the business as private consumption, no output tax will ever be charged on them, and the R600 advance loses its justification. So the adjustment charges it back — and notice the deeper principle, which strong answers always surface: the owner, as a CONSUMER, must carry VAT like every other consumer. If she could route her household shopping through the business and strip the VAT off via input claims, she would be the only person in the country consuming tax-free, and every vendor-owner would copy her. The entity concept is quietly at work here too — the owner and the business are separate persons, and goods crossing that line are a transaction with tax consequences, exactly as drawings are a transaction in the capital accounts. The same logic applies when the business takes stock for staff gifts or any private use: consumption without onward sale forfeits the input claim.

**Mandy:** Next: bad debts confuse me in the opposite direction. The business already LOST the money the customer never paid — why does SARS give anything back? It feels like SARS is rewarding the business for weak credit control.

**Tutor:** It is not a reward; it is the correction of an overpayment, and seeing that requires remembering when the output tax was paid across. VAT works on the invoice basis for most vendors: the moment Naledi Building Supplies invoiced that customer, the sale entered the VAT return and the output tax was paid across to SARS — out of the business's own bank account, in anticipation of the customer's payment arriving. The customer then vanished. Total received from that customer: nothing. But SARS is sitting with R900 of tax on a transaction that, economically, never completed — the state has been paid tax on consumption that was never paid for. The bad-debt relief simply unwinds that: the business claims back the R900 it advanced on SARS's behalf, so the fiscus keeps tax only on money actually collected. Test the fairness in both directions. Without the relief, the business would lose the R6 000 of goods AND R900 of tax paid on a ghost — taxed on income it never received. And symmetrically: if the vanished customer later reappears and pays, the business must account for the output tax again in that period, because the consumption is now real and funded. One more precision that earns the calculation credit: the R6 900 written off is INCLUSIVE — the debtor owed the price plus VAT — so the relief is fifteen over one-fifteen of the debt, R900, never fifteen percent of it. Questions set the inclusive figure deliberately to catch the reflex percentage.

**Mandy:** This one caused an argument: someone said poor households pay no VAT because bread and maize meal are zero-rated. Someone else said VAT hits the poor hardest. Who is right?

**Tutor:** Both are holding a real piece of the truth, and the full picture is worth a paragraph in any essay answer. The zero-rating is genuine and deliberate: the basket of basic foodstuffs — brown bread, maize meal, rice, samp, dried beans, lentils, milk, eggs, vegetables, fruit, vegetable oil, tinned pilchards and more — carries no VAT precisely so that the most survival-critical spending of poor households escapes the tax. If a family's trolley held only that basket, their VAT bill would indeed be close to zero. But no household lives on the basket alone: soap, washing powder, candles, paraffin stoves, school shoes, airtime, the transport costs embedded in every price — all standard-rated, all fifteen percent. Now bring in the economists' word REGRESSIVE: a poor household spends essentially everything it earns, so nearly its whole income passes through tills where VAT waits; a wealthy household saves and invests a large slice of income, and saved rands attract no VAT. As a share of income, VAT therefore takes more from the poor than from the rich, zero-rating notwithstanding — the zero-rating softens the regressivity; it does not remove it. That, incidentally, is exactly why the VAT ethics in this lesson carry such weight: when a vendor steals collected VAT, the revenue must be made up through taxes that circle back onto the same households, and the services VAT funds — clinics, grants, school feeding — are the ones poor households lean on hardest. The honest summary sentence: VAT is efficient and broad, softened for the poor by zero-rating, but still regressive in its remaining reach — and that is why both its design and its honest collection are matters of fairness, not just arithmetic.

**Mandy:** Last one: the control account and the output-less-input calculation gave the same R15 300. If they always agree, why must we learn both?

**Tutor:** Because they are the same truth arriving by two independent routes, and the INDEPENDENCE is the entire point — in the exam room and in real business. The computation route — output adjusted, input adjusted, subtract — is analytical: it starts from the period's transactions and derives the liability. The control account route is custodial: it is a ledger account living inside the double-entry system, catching every VAT posting as it happens — each sale's output tax, each purchase's input tax, each payment to SARS — and its balance at any moment says where the slate stands. When both routes land on R15 300, each validates the other; when they disagree, you have found an error before SARS finds it for you — a sale posted without its VAT, an input claim without an invoice, a payment misallocated. Real vendors reconcile exactly this way every period: the VAT return prepared from the records against the VAT control balance in the ledger, differences hunted down before filing, because a return that disagrees with your own ledger is an audit invitation. Questions mirror that reality from both ends: sometimes you are given transactions and asked for the amount payable — the computation; sometimes you are given a partially completed control account and asked for missing figures or the closing balance — the ledger; and the strongest questions give you both with a deliberate discrepancy and ask you to find it. There is also a reading skill in the account itself: a credit balance is a liability, a debit balance is a refund due, and the opening balance is last period's unfinished business — the same slate-reading you use for SARS income tax with companies. Learn one habit and it serves the whole subject: any account between the business and an outside party is a slate, and a slate must always be readable from both ends.
