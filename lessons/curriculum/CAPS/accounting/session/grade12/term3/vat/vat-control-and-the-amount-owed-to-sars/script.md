# Part 1 — Expert

Value-Added Tax runs through every till slip in South Africa, and grade 12 asks the vendor's question: at the end of the period, how much must the business pay over to SARS — or claim back? The machinery from grade 11 returns — output tax, input tax, the fifteen percent, the inclusive and exclusive calculations — and grade 12 adds the complications that make the real computation: returns, bad debts, drawings, discounts, and the VAT control account that gathers them all. Then the part the syllabus refuses to treat as optional: the ethics. VAT money never belongs to the business that holds it, and the frauds around it are theft from every taxpayer. Calculations first, ledger second, conscience third.

## Subtopic: VAT Machinery Revisited

VAT is a tax on consumption, charged at FIFTEEN percent on most goods and services, collected in stages by registered VENDORS. A business must register as a vendor once its taxable turnover passes one million rand a year; smaller businesses may register voluntarily.

The vendor's two streams. OUTPUT TAX: the VAT a vendor charges its customers on sales — collected on behalf of SARS. INPUT TAX: the VAT a vendor itself pays its suppliers on purchases and expenses — claimable back from SARS. The vendor pays over the difference: output less input. That difference is precisely the tax on the VALUE THE VENDOR ADDED — hence the name.

The two calculations, drilled until automatic. From an EXCLUSIVE price — the price before VAT — the tax is fifteen percent: a table priced at R2 000 exclusive carries VAT of R300, invoiced at R2 300. From an INCLUSIVE price — the till-slip price, VAT already inside — the tax is the inclusive amount times fifteen over one hundred and fifteen: R2 300 inclusive times fifteen over one-fifteen gives the same R300. The examiner's favourite error is applying fifteen percent directly to an inclusive amount — R345 instead of R300 — so always ask first: does this figure already contain the VAT?

Not everything carries fifteen percent. ZERO-RATED supplies are taxed at zero percent — basic foodstuffs like brown bread, maize meal, rice, vegetables, fruit, eggs, milk and vegetable oil, kept tax-free to protect poorer households. A vendor selling zero-rated goods charges no output tax yet still claims input tax on its costs. EXEMPT supplies stand outside the system entirely — financial services, residential rent, non-fee public road and rail transport, educational services: no output tax, and no input tax claims either. Zero-rated is in the system at zero; exempt is out of the system — the distinction is a two-mark certainty.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: output against input, both calculations both ways, and zero-rated separated from exempt.

## Subtopic: Output Less Input — the Amount Owed to SARS

Now the grade 12 computation: one VAT period for Jabulani Hardware, a registered vendor, all base figures exclusive of VAT unless said otherwise.

The plain streams first. Sales for the period: R460 000 — output tax at fifteen percent, R69 000. Purchases of stock and taxable expenses: R320 000 — input tax, R48 000. If the story ended there, the amount owed would be R21 000. It never ends there; four adjustments follow, and each one is examinable.

Adjustment one: DEBTORS' ALLOWANCES — customers returned goods and received credit notes totalling R20 000 exclusive. The original sales charged output tax the business will never keep, so output falls by fifteen percent of 20 000 — R3 000.

Adjustment two: DRAWINGS of stock. The owner took stock costing R6 000 exclusive for personal use. The business claimed input tax when it bought that stock, but the goods were consumed, not sold on — so VAT of R900 is charged back, increasing the amount owed. The principle: input credit belongs only to goods used to make taxable sales.

Adjustment three: BAD DEBTS written off — R9 200 INCLUSIVE. The business paid output tax on those sales when invoiced, but the money never came. SARS returns the tax on the lost amount: 9 200 times fifteen over one-fifteen — R1 200 of input tax relief. Note the inclusive calculation: the debt owed included VAT.

Adjustment four, when it appears: DISCOUNTS allowed to customers reduce output tax the same way returns do, and discounts received from suppliers reduce input tax.

Assemble Jabulani's period. Output side: 69 000 less 3 000 for returns, plus 900 for drawings — R66 900. Input side: 48 000 plus 1 200 for bad debts — R49 200. AMOUNT PAYABLE TO SARS: 66 900 less 49 200 — R17 700. Had input exceeded output — a big equipment purchase, or mostly zero-rated sales — the difference would flip into a REFUND receivable from SARS, and examiners flip it regularly.

Stop for this section's questions now — the four adjustments with their directions, the inclusive bad-debt calculation, and the R17 700 assembled.

## Subtopic: The VAT Control Account

The ledger holds the same story in one account: VAT CONTROL — the running slate between the vendor and SARS. Learn it by sides.

The CREDIT side gathers everything owed TO SARS: output tax on sales, and the drawings adjustment. The DEBIT side gathers everything owed BACK to the business: input tax on purchases and expenses, VAT on debtors' allowances, VAT relief on bad debts written off — and every payment made to SARS, because a payment settles part of the slate.

Speak Jabulani's account through the period. It opens with a CREDIT balance of R12 400 — last period's VAT still owed at the start. During the period the business pays SARS R12 400, settling that opening debt: debit VAT Control, credit Bank. Then the period's own entries land: credit side, output tax R69 000 and drawings R900 — R69 900 of new credits. Debit side, input tax R48 000, returns R3 000, bad debts R1 200 — R52 200 of new debits. Balance the account: credits 12 400 plus 69 900 against debits 12 400 plus 52 200 — a CREDIT balance of R17 700. The account agrees with the calculation, as it must — same facts, second route.

Read the balance like every other slate in the subject: a credit balance is a CURRENT LIABILITY — VAT owed to SARS; a debit balance is a CURRENT ASSET — a refund due. And the payment rhythm: most vendors file a return every TWO months on SARS eFiling and pay by the deadline; late filing and late payment draw penalties and interest, which are expenses the income statement must then carry — the price of poor administration, not a cost of trading.

The discipline that makes this account reliable is documentary: a vendor may only claim input tax on a valid TAX INVOICE showing the supplier's VAT registration number. No invoice, no claim — a rule that closes the door on invented input tax, and the first thing a SARS auditor asks to see.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — the sides of the control account, the R17 700 balanced out of the ledger, and the balance read in both directions.

## Subtopic: Ethics, Fraud and the Vendor's Duty

Here is the fact that reframes the whole topic: VAT collected by a vendor is NEVER the vendor's money. From the moment a customer pays R2 300 for a R2 000 table, the R300 belongs to SARS; the vendor merely holds it in passing. A business in cash trouble that dips into collected VAT to pay wages is not borrowing — it is spending money that was never its own, and the law treats it accordingly.

The frauds, named the way examination scenarios name them. UNDER-DECLARING output: sales rung up off the books, cash taken without invoices — the classic till fraud, which understates output tax and steals from the fiscus sale by sale. INFLATING input: claiming VAT on fictitious purchases, on invoices from ghost suppliers, or on private expenses dressed as business costs — each claim a direct extraction from SARS. FAILING TO PAY OVER: charging customers VAT and simply keeping it — collecting tax without being registered is the aggravated version, charging fifteen percent with no intention of paying anything over. And FALSE REFUND claims: engineering a period where input appears to exceed output, then pocketing the refund.

Why it matters beyond the law: VAT is the fiscus's broadest revenue stream — it funds the grants, clinics, schools and roads every scenario in this syllabus depends on. A vendor stealing VAT steals from every household in the country, including the poorest, who pay VAT on almost everything they buy. That is why the syllabus calls VAT honesty an ETHICS topic and not merely a compliance one.

The controls, internal and external. Internally: till sales reconciled to banking daily, sequential invoice numbers with gaps investigated, input claims matched to valid tax invoices, VAT returns prepared and reviewed by different people, and the VAT control account reconciled to the returns filed. Externally: SARS audits, penalties, interest, and criminal prosecution for evasion. And the professional duty the syllabus states plainly: an accountant asked to prepare a false return must refuse — assisting evasion makes the accountant party to the crime, and no employer's instruction changes that.

The final questions of this part are with you now: whose money the R300 is, the four frauds with the control that answers each, and the accountant's duty stated.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same topic from behind the counter of a spaza shop — because VAT makes complete sense the day you realise the shopkeeper is not a taxpayer here; she is an unpaid tax collector.

## Subtopic: The Fifteen Cents That Was Never Yours

MaDlamini runs a general dealer. A customer buys washing powder for R23. Inside that R23, exactly R3 is not MaDlamini's — it never was, not for one second. It is VAT, riding inside the price, and she is holding it for SARS the way a conductor holds the taxi fare for the owner.

That is the whole mental model: every till slip splits invisibly into two piles. The bigger pile — R20 — is the shop's: it covers the stock, the rent, the profit. The small pile — R3 — belongs to the tax man and is just sleeping in her till until payday, which comes every two months.

Learn the two little sums on her calculator, because everything else stands on them. When she knows the BEFORE-tax price and wants the tax: fifteen percent. A price tag of R200 before VAT means R30 of tax and R230 on the shelf. When she knows the SHELF price and wants the tax hiding inside it: times fifteen, divided by one hundred and fifteen. The R23 washing powder: 23 times 15 over 115 — R3. Never take fifteen percent of a shelf price — the tax is inside that number already, and taxing the tax gives the wrong answer every time.

One more thing MaDlamini knows from her own shelves: the brown bread and the maize meal carry NO VAT — the law keeps the poorest basket tax-free, so those are zero-rated. But her airtime, sweets and washing powder all carry the fifteen. And when she pays rent for her backroom flat, no VAT appears at all — residential rent lives outside the system entirely. Zero-rated is inside the system at a rate of nothing; exempt never entered the building.

Questions on this piece are with you now: the two piles in the till, both calculator sums, and the bread separated from the rent.

## Subtopic: Collected In, Paid Out, and What Remains

Every two months MaDlamini settles with SARS, and her whole return is one subtraction with a story on each side.

Side one: the VAT she COLLECTED from customers — on everything that went over the counter at fifteen percent. Say her period's standard-rated sales were R46 000 before tax: she is holding R6 900 of SARS's money.

Side two: the VAT SHE paid — because MaDlamini is also a customer. The wholesaler charged her VAT on stock; the electrician charged VAT on the repair; the delivery bakkie's fuel and parts carried VAT. Suppose those purchases came to R32 000 before tax: she paid out R4 800 of VAT to other vendors. That money SARS owes back to her — she should not carry tax on her way to collecting tax.

Settle: collected 6 900, paid 4 800 — she owes SARS R2 100. Notice what that difference really taxes: she bought for 32 and sold for 46 — the R14 000 of value SHE added — and fifteen percent of 14 000 is exactly the R2 100. The name of the tax is its own explanation.

Then life happens, and each event nudges one side. A customer returns a broken kettle and gets money back: the VAT on it goes back too — collected shrinks. MaDlamini takes cooking oil home off her own shelf: she claimed input VAT on oil that never got sold to anyone — she must give that claim back, so her bill grows. A customer who bought on credit vanishes without paying: MaDlamini handed SARS output tax on money she never received — SARS gives it back, and her bill shrinks. Returns down, drawings up, bad debts down — three nudges, and every examination question is these nudges wearing bigger numbers.

And if in some period she buys a new fridge and her VAT paid outweighs her VAT collected? Then SARS owes HER, and the refund arrives on the same slate. The slate points both ways.

Your questions for this part are up now: the subtraction with its two sides, the value-added check, and the three nudges with their directions.

## Subtopic: Why Cheating VAT Is Stealing Twice

Now the uncomfortable scene the syllabus insists on. MaDlamini's cousin, who also runs a shop, gives her advice one Sunday: keep two tills. Ring the card sales through the machine, let the cash sales vanish, and your VAT bill halves. Everyone does it, he says.

Take his advice apart, because the paper will ask you to. The R3 on that washing powder never belonged to the shop. A customer PAID it, believing it was going to the state; keeping it is not clever bookkeeping — it is stealing money already collected from someone else. And it is stealing twice over: once from SARS, and once from every person who depends on what VAT funds — the clinic queue, the school feeding scheme, the grant on which half the shop's own customers survive. A shopkeeper hiding cash sales is picking the pockets of her own community and calling it business.

The cousin's other tricks are the same theft in different clothes. Inventing invoices from a supplier who does not exist, to inflate the VAT claimed back — that is not hiding income; that is actively taking money OUT of SARS. Charging customers fifteen percent without ever registering as a vendor — collecting a tax you never intend to deliver. And the refund game: cooking the period so SARS appears to owe you.

How do such tricks get caught? Boringly, which is the beauty of it. SARS sees the wholesaler's records — every invoice MaDlamini's supplier issued to her sits in the supplier's own VAT return, so a shop buying R32 000 of stock and declaring R15 000 of sales is a red light on a screen. Invoice numbers run in sequence, and gaps ask questions. Bankings that dwarf declared sales ask louder ones. The two-till shop is not a secret; it is a delayed penalty — with interest, and with a criminal case where the amounts justify it.

So MaDlamini keeps one till, files every two months, claims only what her tax invoices prove, and sleeps at night. The examination sentence version: VAT collected is held in trust for SARS; evasion is theft from the state and the public; and the vendor's controls — honest tills, valid invoices, reconciled returns — protect the business and the country at once.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: whose R3 it was, the cousin's three tricks named as theft, and the boring ways they get caught. Master the slate and the nudges — Paper 2 will set this exact machinery with different numbers.
