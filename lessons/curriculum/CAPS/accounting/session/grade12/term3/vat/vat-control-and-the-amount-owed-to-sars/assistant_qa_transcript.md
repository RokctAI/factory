**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First: the drawings adjustment. When the owner takes stock home, why does the VAT go AGAINST the business? He owns the shop — surely he can take his own stock without SARS getting involved?

**Tutor:** He can take the stock; what he cannot take is the tax break that came with it. Trace the R6 000 of cooking oil from the beginning. When the business bought it, the wholesaler charged R900 of VAT, and the business claimed that R900 back as input tax — legitimately, because the rule is that input tax is claimable on goods bought TO MAKE TAXABLE SALES. The state, in effect, advanced the business R900 on the promise that this oil would be sold onward with output tax charged on it. The owner taking it home breaks that promise: the oil exits the business as private consumption, no output tax will ever be charged on it, and the R900 advance no longer has a justification. So the adjustment charges it back — and notice the deeper principle, which examiners love: the owner, as a CONSUMER, must carry VAT like every other consumer. If he could route his household groceries through the business and strip the VAT off via input claims, he would be the only person in the country eating tax-free, and every vendor-owner would do the same. The entity concept is quietly at work here too — the owner and the business are separate, and goods crossing that line are a transaction with tax consequences, exactly like drawings are a transaction in the capital accounts. Same logic if the business takes stock for staff gifts or private use of any kind: consumption without onward sale forfeits the input claim.

**Mandy:** Next: bad debts confuse me in the opposite direction. The business already LOST the money the customer never paid — why does SARS give anything back? It feels like SARS is rewarding the business for bad credit control.

**Tutor:** It is not a reward; it is a correction of an overpayment, and seeing that requires remembering when the output tax was paid across. VAT works on the invoice basis for most vendors: the moment Jabulani invoiced that customer, the sale entered the VAT return and the output tax was paid across to SARS — out of Jabulani's own bank account, in anticipation of the customer's payment arriving. The customer then vanished. Total received from that customer: nothing. But SARS is sitting with R1 200 of tax on a transaction that, economically, never completed — the state has been paid tax on consumption that was never paid for. The bad-debt relief simply unwinds that: the business claims back the R1 200 it advanced on SARS's behalf, so the fiscus keeps tax only on money actually collected. Test the fairness both ways. Without the relief, the business would lose the R8 000 of goods AND R1 200 of tax paid on a ghost — taxed on income it never received. And symmetrically: if the vanished customer later reappears and pays, the business must charge the output tax again in that period, because the consumption is now real and funded. One more precision that earns the calculation mark: the R9 200 written off is INCLUSIVE — the debtor owed the price plus VAT — so the relief is fifteen over one-fifteen of the debt, R1 200, never fifteen percent of it. Examiners set the inclusive figure deliberately to catch the reflex percentage.

**Mandy:** This one caused an argument: someone said poor households pay no VAT because bread and maize meal are zero-rated. Someone else said VAT hits the poor hardest. Who is right?

**Tutor:** Both are holding a real piece, and the full picture is worth an essay paragraph. The zero-rating is genuine and deliberate: the basket of basic foodstuffs — brown bread, maize meal, rice, samp, dried beans, lentils, milk, eggs, vegetables, fruit, vegetable oil, tinned pilchards and more — carries no VAT precisely so that the most survival-critical spending of poor households escapes the tax. If a family's trolley were only that basket, their VAT bill would indeed be close to zero. But no household lives on the basket alone: soap, washing powder, candles, paraffin appliances, school shoes, airtime, transport costs embedded in every price — all standard-rated, all fifteen percent. Now bring in the economists' word REGRESSIVE: a poor household spends essentially everything it earns, so nearly its whole income passes through tills where VAT waits; a wealthy household saves and invests a large slice of income, and saved rands attract no VAT. As a share of income, VAT therefore takes more from the poor than from the rich, zero-rating notwithstanding — the zero-rating softens the regressivity; it does not remove it. That, incidentally, is exactly why the VAT ethics in this lesson carry such weight: when a vendor steals collected VAT, the revenue is made up through rates and taxes that circle back onto the same households, and the services VAT funds — clinics, grants, school feeding — are the ones poor households lean on hardest. The honest summary sentence: VAT is efficient and broad, softened for the poor by zero-rating, but still regressive in its remaining reach — and that is why both its design and its honest collection are matters of fairness, not just arithmetic.

**Mandy:** Last one: the control account and the output-less-input calculation gave the same R17 700. If they always agree, why does the syllabus make us learn both?

**Tutor:** Because they are the same truth arriving by two independent routes, and the INDEPENDENCE is the entire point — in the examination and in real business. The computation route — output adjusted, input adjusted, subtract — is analytical: it starts from the period's transactions and derives the liability. The control account route is custodial: it is a ledger account living inside the double-entry system, catching every VAT posting as it happens — each sale's output tax, each purchase's input tax, each payment to SARS — and its balance at any moment says where the slate stands. When both routes land on R17 700, each validates the other; when they disagree, you have found an error before SARS finds it for you — a sale posted without its VAT, an input claim without an invoice, a payment misallocated. Real vendors reconcile exactly this way every period: the VAT return prepared from the records against the VAT control balance in the ledger, differences hunted before filing, because a return that disagrees with your own ledger is an audit invitation. The examination mirrors that reality from both ends: sometimes you are given transactions and asked for the amount payable — the computation; sometimes you are given a partially completed control account and asked for missing figures or the closing balance — the ledger; and the strongest questions give you both with a deliberate discrepancy and ask you to find it. There is also a practical reading skill in the account itself: a credit balance is a liability, a debit balance is a refund due, and the opening balance is last period's unfinished business — the same slate-reading you use for SARS income tax with companies. Learn one habit and it serves the whole subject: any account between the business and an outside party is a slate, and a slate must always be readable from both ends.
