# Part 1 — Expert

Everything so far in this course recorded the past. Budgeting turns the machinery around and points it at the FUTURE: what will sales be in August, when will the cash from July's credit sales actually arrive, can the business afford the new equipment in the same month it pays the loan instalment? Grade 12 works with two forecasting documents — the PROJECTED INCOME STATEMENT and the CASH BUDGET — and the syllabus is explicit that the skill being examined is ANALYSIS and INTERPRETATION: reading the forecasts, comparing them to what actually happened, and judging the decisions they reveal. The debtors' collection schedule is the topic's one hard calculation, and it appears in almost every budgeting question ever set.

## Subtopic: Two Forecasts, Two Questions

The two documents answer different questions, and mixing them is the topic's cardinal error.

The PROJECTED INCOME STATEMENT asks: will the business be PROFITABLE next period? It is an ordinary income statement written in advance — projected sales, projected cost of sales, projected expenses — built on the ACCRUAL rules the whole subject uses: income when earned, expenses when incurred. Depreciation appears, because equipment will wear. Bad debts appear, because some credit sales will die. Whether cash moves is irrelevant.

The CASH BUDGET asks: will there be MONEY IN THE BANK? It records only cash, when it actually moves. Receipts: cash sales, collections FROM debtors — on the delay the schedule computes — rent income received, a loan received, capital contributed, interest on the fixed deposit. Payments: cash purchases, payments TO creditors on their own delay, wages, expenses paid, equipment bought, loan instalments, drawings. Receipts less payments gives the month's surplus or shortfall, which rolls the opening bank balance to the closing one, month by month.

Now the items examiners use to test the boundary. DEPRECIATION: projected income statement yes, cash budget never — no rand ever leaves the bank for depreciation. BAD DEBTS: income statement yes; in the cash budget they appear only as money that never arrives inside the collection schedule. A NEW MACHINE: cash budget yes, in full, in the month of payment; income statement no — only its depreciation belongs there. A LOAN RECEIVED: cash budget yes; income statement no — borrowing is not income. DRAWINGS or DIVIDENDS: cash budget yes; income statement no — distributions are not expenses. Discount allowed: income statement yes; and in the cash budget it shows as debtors paying less than face value.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: the two questions stated, accrual against cash, and the boundary items placed on the correct side.

## Subtopic: Debtors' Collections and Creditors' Payments

The collection schedule converts projected CREDIT SALES into projected CASH, and it is pure disciplined arithmetic.

Sindi Traders sells on credit with this collection pattern, learned from experience: 30 percent of a month's credit sales pay in the SAME month; 60 percent pay in the FOLLOWING month; 8 percent pay in the SECOND month after; and 2 percent never pay — bad debts. Credit sales: June R80 000, July R100 000, August R120 000.

Compute AUGUST's collections, layer by layer. From August's own sales: 30 percent of 120 000 — R36 000. From July's sales, now one month old: 60 percent of 100 000 — R60 000. From June's sales, now two months old: 8 percent of 80 000 — R6 400. August collections: 36 000 plus 60 000 plus 6 400 — R102 400. The 2 percent of each month simply never appears — that absence IS the bad debt, and the schedule is where the income statement's bad-debts line and the cash budget's silence meet.

Read the pattern before moving on, because interpretation marks live in it: only 30 percent pays inside the month — Sindi finances seventy percent of every month's credit sales for at least a month. If the pattern worsens — say 20 in the month, 55 following, 15 second month, 10 bad — the same sales deliver less cash, later, and the budget must show the strain. Tightening credit checks, offering early-settlement discounts, or charging interest on arrears are the levers, each with its own cost.

The CREDITORS' side mirrors it. Sindi buys stock on credit and pays in the month AFTER purchase, earning a 5 percent settlement discount for prompt payment. July purchases were R90 000, so August pays 95 percent of 90 000 — R85 500. The R4 500 discount is income earned by paying on time — and a business that starts missing discounts is quietly reporting its own cash trouble.

Stop for this section's questions now — August's three layers totalled, the missing two percent named, and the discounted creditors' payment.

## Subtopic: Building and Reading the Cash Budget

Assemble Sindi's August, spoken as the statement flows.

RECEIPTS. Cash sales: R80 000. Collections from debtors, as computed: R102 400. Rent income from the spare storeroom: R6 000. Total receipts: R188 400.

PAYMENTS. To creditors, with the discount: R85 500. Wages: R34 000. Deposit on new equipment: R25 000. Loan instalment to Ithala Bank, including interest: R9 900. Sundry operating expenses: R14 000. Total payments: R168 400.

The month's result: receipts 188 400 less payments 168 400 — a SURPLUS of R20 000. Sindi opened August with an OVERDRAFT of R4 000, so the closing balance is minus 4 000 plus 20 000 — R16 000 favourable. The budget's last three lines — surplus or shortfall, opening balance, closing balance — chain every month to the next, which is why cash budgets run in linked monthly columns.

Now READ it, because reading is the examinable skill. One: the equipment deposit of R25 000 exceeds the month's surplus — without it August would have shown R45 000 of operating surplus; the business is buying capacity out of one month's cash, and the remaining instalments must appear in coming months. Two: the overdraft clears in August — interest costs stop, a good sign. Three: test the shape of the receipts — nearly 55 percent of August's cash comes from old sales via the collection schedule; a single bad month of sales will echo through two more months of collections, so the budget must be re-run whenever sales projections move. Four: ask the WHAT-IF questions the examiners ask: if the equipment supplier demanded full payment now, or if collections slipped to the weaker pattern, would the overdraft return, and can the facility absorb it?

A shortfall month is not failure — it is INFORMATION, arriving early enough to act: arrange the overdraft before the month, delay the discretionary payment, chase collections, or time the capital purchase differently. That earliness is the entire point of budgeting.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — the budget assembled to R16 000, the three chained lines, and the what-if reasoning.

## Subtopic: Budget Against Actual, and the Ethics of Forecasting

A budget nobody compares to reality is a wish. The discipline is VARIANCE analysis: set the budgeted figure against the actual, name the difference favourable or unfavourable, and — the grade 12 move — explain it and judge it.

September's comparison for Sindi, three lines of it. SALES: budgeted R200 000, actual R164 000 — unfavourable by R36 000. ADVERTISING: budgeted R8 000, actual R2 000 — on its face favourable, R6 000 saved. Read the two TOGETHER and the story flips: the advertising was cut and the sales fell — a FALSE ECONOMY, saving six thousand to lose thirty-six. Spending less than budget is not automatically virtue; the question is what the spending was FOR. WAGES: budgeted R34 000, actual R41 500 — overspent by R7 500. Explanation before judgement: overtime for the stocktake, a wage settlement, or uncontrolled casual hiring? The variance opens the question; the explanation answers it; the judgement follows the explanation, never the raw number.

Then the ETHICS of forecasting, which the syllabus pairs with every budgeting question. PADDING: managers overstating expense budgets so they can underspend and look disciplined — a lie in advance that misallocates the business's resources. UNREALISTIC sales targets: set to impress a bank or motivate staff, they corrupt every downstream figure — collections, purchases, cash — and a loan granted on a fantasy budget is money lent into a lie. BUDGETS AS WEAPONS: slashing a department's numbers without reason, or shifting blame for variances that management's own decisions caused. And the governance point: a budget approved, then quietly overspent on directors' comforts while the training line is frozen, tells you where the business's real priorities live — read the variances for VALUES, not just rands.

Internal control closes the loop: budgets prepared before the period, approved by the right level, compared monthly against actuals, variances explained in writing, and corrective action minuted. That cycle — plan, act, compare, correct — is the managerial version of the reconciliations this subject keeps teaching: two records of one reality, forced to explain their differences.

The final questions of this part are with you now: variances computed and judged in pairs, the false economy named, and padding with its cost.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same topic at kitchen-table scale — because every household that survives December has already done everything this lesson teaches.

## Subtopic: The Month Written Down Before It Happens

Nolwazi supports her family on a salary and a weekend catering side business. At the end of every month she does something her neighbours call fussy and her bank calls rare: she writes down NEXT month before it happens.

Two different lists, and she keeps them apart. The first list asks: will the catering MAKE MONEY next month? Orders she expects, what the ingredients will cost, transport, the gas — and some wear on her big pots, because they will not last forever. That list is her projected income statement: profit on paper, counting things when they are earned or used up, whether or not money moves that month.

The second list asks the harder question: will there be money IN THE ACCOUNT on the right days? Because the school wants fees on the third, the taxi association wants its payment on the first, and the wedding client will only pay her AFTER the wedding on the twenty-sixth. Profitable and broke are different things, and the second list — the cash budget — exists because bills arrive on dates, not on averages.

Watch the items that sit on one list only. The wear on her pots: first list, never the second — no one collects money for wear. The deposit on the new gas stove: second list in full — real money out — but only its wear reaches the first. The R3 000 her brother repays her: second list — money in — but not income; he is returning her own money. And the wedding client who might never pay: a small sad line on the first list, and on the second list simply money that never shows up.

Questions on this piece are with you now: the two lists with their two questions, and each boundary item on its side.

## Subtopic: When Will the Money Actually Arrive?

The catering business sells mostly on trust: schools and churches pay Nolwazi after the function. Experience has taught her the rhythm, and the rhythm IS the calculation.

Of every hundred rand she invoices, thirty comes in the same month, sixty in the next, eight the month after that — and two rand, from hard experience, never comes at all. Her invoices: June R8 000, July R10 000, August R12 000. So what lands in AUGUST? Three layers, oldest last: thirty percent of August's own R12 000 — R3 600. Sixty percent of July's R10 000 — R6 000. Eight percent of June's R8 000 — R640. August's collections: R10 240.

Say what the layers mean, because this is the insight the topic runs on: August's bank balance is mostly JULY's work. Nolwazi caters in July and eats in August — she is her clients' unpaid bank for a month or two on most of every invoice. And the two percent that never comes is not a surprise by the time it fails to arrive; it was budgeted as loss from the start, which is the honest way to plan.

Her suppliers run the mirror rhythm. The wholesaler lets her pay next month, and takes five percent off if she pays on time. July's stock bill was R9 000; in August she pays R8 550 and keeps R450 for punctuality. That R450 is real income earned by discipline — and the first month she misses it, her budget is telling her something is wrong with her cash, not with the wholesaler.

Put August together: R10 240 collected, plus R8 000 of cash orders, minus the wholesaler, the taxi money, the gas, the stove deposit — and the month shows a surplus that turns her small overdraft into a positive balance. The point is not the specific rands: it is that she KNEW IN JULY. Every crisis a budget reveals in advance is a crisis with options; the same crisis discovered on the day has none.

Your questions for this part are up now: the three layers of August, whose work August's money really is, and the discount as a warning light.

## Subtopic: The Plan Meets the Month

End of September. Nolwazi sits with two columns: what she PLANNED, and what HAPPENED. This half-hour is the most valuable in her business month.

Orders: planned R20 000, actual R16 400. Down R3 600 — why? Then her eye catches the next line. Flyers and boosted posts: planned R800, actual nothing — she skipped the advertising to save money. There is her answer, staring back in two rows: she saved eight hundred rand and lost three thousand six hundred of orders. The saving was the most expensive thing she bought all month. Underspending is not automatically winning — it depends what the money was FOR.

Gas: planned R1 400, actual R2 100. Overspent — but September had two extra funerals, which also brought extra orders, so the overspend fed revenue. Judge variances in PAIRS and in stories, never one number at a time.

And because Nolwazi answers only to herself, she can be honest — but scale her up and the ethics arrive. A branch manager who pads the expense budget, asking for more than needed so that underspending looks like skill, is lying to the business in advance. An owner who shows the bank a fantasy sales forecast to get a loan is borrowing against sales that exist only on paper. A company that freezes the training budget while the directors' travel line grows has published its true priorities in numbers. Budgets are where intentions become visible — which is why examiners pair every budgeting calculation with a judgement question, and why the honest comparison of plan against outcome, month after month, is an internal control as real as any lock.

Her cycle, and the syllabus's: write the month before it happens; live it; compare the two columns; explain every gap in words; fix what the explanation demands; and roll the lesson into next month's plan.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: the false economy in her two rows, the paired reading of the gas line, and the padding fraud at company scale. This is the syllabus's final new topic — from here the course turns to bringing everything together for the examinations.
