**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First: the collection schedule always trips me on WHICH month's percentage applies to WHICH sales. Is there a way to set it up so I stop scrambling the layers?

**Tutor:** There is, and it is worth building as a physical habit before the examination. Draw the months as columns and the SALES as rows, then ask of each row: where does this month's money land? Take July's credit sales of R100 000 on the 30-60-8 pattern. Thirty percent lands in July itself, sixty percent lands in August, eight percent in September, and two percent lands nowhere, ever. Do that for every sales row and the answer to any question is simply: read DOWN the column of the month they asked about. August's column collects three entries — thirty percent of August's own sales, sixty percent of July's, eight percent of June's — and the discipline of rows-are-sales, columns-are-cash prevents the classic scramble of applying sixty percent to the wrong month. Two refinements protect the remaining marks. First, direction of time: the same-month percentage applies to the NEWEST sales and the smallest percentage to the OLDEST — if your biggest layer is coming from the oldest month, you have reversed the pattern. Second, completeness check: each sales row's percentages must account for one hundred percent — thirty plus sixty plus eight plus two — and the two percent must appear NOWHERE in the schedule, because bad debts never become cash. Examiners test that specifically by asking how much of June's sales is still expected after August, or what bad debts the pattern implies on the year's sales; both fall out instantly if your table is built by rows. And when the question flips to creditors, the same table works with one row per month's purchases and usually a single payment column — shifted a month, at ninety-five percent if a settlement discount applies.

**Mandy:** Next: why does the syllabus keep insisting the cash budget and the projected income statement are different documents? If both describe the same future month, surely a profitable month must also be a cash-positive month, more or less?

**Tutor:** The whole reason budgeting exists as a topic is that MORE OR LESS fails exactly when it matters. The two documents can and regularly do point in opposite directions in the same month, and each disagreement is a real business situation. Take profitable-but-cash-starved, the dangerous quadrant. Sindi sells R200 000 in a month at healthy margins — the projected income statement glows. But seventy percent of those sales are on credit and collect over the following two months, while her suppliers, wages and the equipment deposit all demand cash NOW: the cash budget for the same month shows a deep shortfall. Businesses die in precisely that month — profitable on paper, unable to pay the wages that keep the doors open — and it is the growth months, when rising sales pump ever more money into debtors and stock, that are the most treacherous. That is why the examiner's scenario of expanding sales plus tightening cash is not a paradox but the expected pattern. Now the reverse quadrant: cash-rich but unprofitable. A month in which a loan lands, or old debtors finally pay, or the owner contributes capital, can show a fat closing balance while trading itself runs at a loss — the cash budget smiles while the income statement warns. Read only the bank balance and you miss the rot. So the two documents are two instruments on one dashboard: profitability is the engine's health, cash is the fuel line, and either can fail independently. The examination boundary questions — depreciation, loans, capital purchases, drawings — are really testing whether you understand WHY the instruments differ, which is the difference between placing items correctly by rule and placing them correctly by understanding.

**Mandy:** This one caused an argument: if the 2 percent never pays, why sell to those customers at all? Someone said the business should only sell for cash and the whole schedule disappears.

**Tutor:** Cash-only would indeed delete the collection schedule, the bad debts and a good part of the bookkeeping — along with most of the sales. The argument is a genuine business trade-off and the syllabus rewards candidates who can weigh it rather than moralise it. Credit is a sales tool: schools, churches, businesses and most large customers simply cannot or will not pay on delivery — their own cash cycles will not allow it — so a supplier who refuses credit refuses their business to a competitor who will take it. The two percent is therefore not waste; it is a COST of accessing the ninety-eight percent, exactly as rent is a cost of having a shop. The management question is never how do we reach zero bad debts — a business with zero bad debts is usually refusing profitable customers out of excessive caution — but whether the cost sits at the level the credit policy accepts. That is what the pattern in the schedule monitors: two percent failing and seventy percent collected within a month is a policy working; watch the drift, because a slide toward ten percent bad and slower layers is credit control failing in real time. And the levers are graded, each with a price: credit checks and limits filter the riskiest customers at the door; early-settlement discounts speed the layers but surrender margin; interest on arrears compensates for late money but can chase customers away; suspension and collection recover some debts at real cost. The examination sentence: bad debts are a planned cost of a credit policy, budgeted at the expected rate, monitored through the collection pattern, and managed — never assumed away.

**Mandy:** Last one: with budget-against-actual, how do I avoid the trap of calling every underspend favourable and every overspend unfavourable? The lesson's advertising example makes me distrust the labels completely.

**Tutor:** Keep the labels but demote them: favourable and unfavourable are the ARITHMETIC verdict — which direction the variance moves the surplus — and the analysis begins, not ends, there. The examiner's marks sit in the three questions you ask after labelling. Question one: is the line CAUSAL or CONSEQUENTIAL? Advertising, maintenance, training and credit control spending are causal — they generate or protect other lines, so an underspend there must be read against what it was supposed to cause. Sindi saved R6 000 of advertising and lost R36 000 of sales: arithmetically favourable, economically a disaster — the false economy. Consequential lines like commission or delivery costs MOVE with sales, so an overspend beside higher sales may be pure health, and the fair test is proportion: commission at five percent of actual sales is on-budget behaviour even if the rand amount overshoots. Question two: is the variance PRICE or QUANTITY? Wages overspent because of a negotiated increase is a different management problem from wages overspent because casual hours ran uncontrolled — same rand variance, different explanation, different action, and answers that separate the two read like management, not marking. Question three: was the BUDGET itself honest? A variance against a padded or fantasy budget measures the lie, not the performance — which is where the ethics content re-enters: padding, targets set to impress lenders, and blame-shifting all corrupt the comparison that internal control depends on. So the disciplined answer pattern for any variance question is: state the figures and the arithmetic label; explain the likely cause, pairing related lines; then judge — favourable, unfavourable, or favourable-looking-but-false — with the reason. Label, explain, judge. That is the full mark allocation in three moves, and it works on every variance the paper can print.
