**Mandy:** Class questions coming in! First: the King Code is not a law — so what actually happens to a company that just ignores it? If nobody can be arrested, why does any board bother?

**Tutor:** Because the enforcement is real even though the police are not involved, and understanding the mechanism is worth marks. Start with the JSE: a listed company must apply the King Code and explain in its annual report how it has done so. Refuse, and the exchange can query, censure, fine, and ultimately end the listing — and losing a listing cuts a company off from the public capital that made it list in the first place. Then comes enforcement by markets rather than regulators: banks price loans on governance risk, institutional investors — the pension funds and unit trusts that hold most listed shares — have their own policies obliging them to vote against badly governed boards, and analysts publicly score governance. A company that shrugs at King pays for it in a lower share price and dearer debt long before any regulator writes a letter. And remember the Code does not float alone: many of its demands shadow duties that ARE law. Fiduciary duty, the requirement that statements fairly present, the prohibition on reckless trading — those live in the Companies Act, and a director who ignored King usually finds they have breached the Act as well. So the honest summary is: King itself persuades and exposes; the listing rules, the investors and the Companies Act punish. For a scenario answer, say both halves — voluntary code, involuntary consequences.

**Mandy:** Next: non-executive directors do not work at the company, some only attend a meeting every couple of months. What can a part-timer really contribute — and why does the lesson treat them as so important?

**Tutor:** Flip the question around: what can a full-timer NOT contribute? An executive director lives inside the company's story — its targets, its friendships, its habits of mind — and that closeness is both their value and their blindness. The non-executive walks in from outside carrying three things no insider can. Distance: they can ask the naive-sounding question that everyone inside stopped asking years ago — why are we still in this loss-making line? Experience from elsewhere: a non-executive who sits on other boards has watched other companies stumble and recognises the early signs. And crucially, independence of livelihood: an executive who challenges the chief executive risks a career; an independent non-executive risks only a directorship they do not depend on, so they can afford to say no. That is why the King Code puts independent non-executives specifically where conflicts concentrate — chairing the board, filling the audit committee, staffing the remuneration committee. Notice each of those rooms decides something executives should never decide about themselves: whether the reporting is honest, and what the executives are paid. The part-time nature is not a weakness to be excused; the detachment IS the contribution. What good governance asks of them in return is genuine engagement — read the papers, probe the numbers, resign loudly if stonewalled — because a passive non-executive gives a board the appearance of oversight without the substance, which is more dangerous than no oversight at all.

**Mandy:** Third one, from the debate we had about the delivery contract: if the director declares that his brother is bidding and then leaves the room, and the brother's quote genuinely is the best — is it actually fine for the brother to win? It still feels wrong to some of us.

**Tutor:** It feels wrong because we instinctively judge the OUTCOME — brother wins contract — but ethics here judges the PROCESS, and that distinction is exactly what this topic wants you to master. Ask what the conflict-of-interest rules are protecting: not the company from ever trading with anyone's relatives — that would be unworkable in a country where business communities are tight — but the DECISION from being bent by private loyalty. Once the director declares the connection and withdraws, the remaining directors compare the quotes on merit, and the choice is protected: nobody in the room owes the winning bidder anything. If the brother's firm offers the best price, delivery record and terms, awarding it the contract is not merely acceptable — refusing purely because of the family tie would itself be unfair to a legitimate bidder and poor stewardship of the company's money. What makes the identical outcome corrupt in the other version is that the safeguard never existed: the interested director stayed in the room, the comparison never happened, and even a good price cannot prove the decision was clean, because nothing protected it. And notice the practical footnote the scenario answers should carry: the declaration must be MINUTED — recorded in the board's minutes — because a safeguard nobody can later verify protects nobody. Process, declared and documented: that is the whole answer.

**Mandy:** Last one: the three layers — controls, internal audit, external audit — sound expensive. All those people checking each other instead of selling anything. How is that not wasted money, especially for a company that trusts its staff?

**Tutor:** Total up what the layers cost, then total up what they buy, and the arithmetic settles it. Start with the direct answer: fraud found late is vastly more expensive than checking done early. A payroll clerk quietly paying a ghost employee for five years costs more than a decade of reconciliations. But the deeper answer is that the layers are not really bought for catching thieves — most people are honest — they are bought for three other things. First, they protect the honest: when the gate money is counted by one person, recorded by a second and banked by a third, an honest treasurer facing a shortfall can PROVE it was not her — without the controls, suspicion lands on everyone and trust inside the business dies. Second, they catch errors, which are far more common than fraud: reconciliations find the duplicated payment and the missed invoice whether or not anyone meant harm. Third — and this is the one that pays for everything — they make the company's word believable to outsiders. The bank lends more cheaply, suppliers extend credit, investors pay more for the shares, precisely because an independent auditor with everything to lose has signed that the statements fairly present. Cheap capital is the reward for verified honesty. As for "we trust our staff" — the controls are why the trust is rational. A business that trusts without checking is not trusting; it is hoping, with other people's money. And one caution for a scenario answer: the layers must stay proportionate — a spaza needs a locked cash tin and a notebook, not an audit committee. Match the checking to the money at risk; that judgement is itself part of good governance.
