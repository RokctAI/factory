**Mandy:** Questions that come up a lot with this topic, before we close! First one: if the King Code is not an Act of Parliament, why would any company actually obey it? What happens to a board that just ignores it?

**Tutor:** The enforcement is real — it just arrives through side doors rather than a courtroom. Door one: the Johannesburg Stock Exchange. Listing on the JSE is a contract, and that contract requires companies to apply the King Code and explain in their reporting how they have done so. A listed company that shrugs at the Code is breaching its listing requirements, and losing a listing means losing access to the public's capital — a commercial death sentence for many companies. Door two: the law is closer than it looks. Many of the Code's demands — directors acting in good faith, declaring personal interests, keeping proper records, preparing statements that fairly present — overlap with duties the Companies Act imposes directly, so a board ignoring the Code is usually breaching statute at the same time. Door three: the market itself. Banks price loans on trust, and investors pay for shares on trust; a company known for opaque reporting and unexplained decisions pays more for capital or cannot raise it at all. And door four: the annual general meeting — shareholders who read a governance report full of evasions can vote the directors out. So the mature examination answer is: the Code is not legislation, but the JSE requires it, the Companies Act echoes much of it, and the capital markets punish those who ignore it. Voluntary in form, compulsory in consequence.

***

**Mandy:** Next one, and it causes arguments every year: the audit committee is made up of directors — but the committee's job is to check on the company that those same directors govern. Isn't that the board checking itself?

**Tutor:** It would be, if the committee were built carelessly — which is exactly why the Code is fussy about who sits on it. The audit committee must be made up of independent non-executive directors: people who sit on the board but hold no job inside the company, draw no salary from its operations, and have no other financial ties to it. Their entire livelihood does not depend on the numbers looking good, and that is the point. Now watch what that composition unlocks. The executives — the chief executive, the financial director — are the people whose performance the financial statements measure, so they are precisely the people who must NOT control the checking of those statements. The audit committee stands between them and the two checking mechanisms. Internal audit reports to the committee, so an executive cannot bury a finding about his own department. The external auditor's appointment, fee and independence are overseen by the committee, so an executive cannot quietly threaten to replace an auditor who asks hard questions. Is it perfect? No — committee members are still human, still appointed through board processes, and a truly rotten board can stack any committee. But the design principle is the one the examiner wants stated: the people being measured must not control the measurement, and the audit committee exists to hold that line inside the board itself.

**Mandy:** Third: confidentiality confuses everyone. The code of conduct says an accountant must keep the employer's information secret — so what happens when the accountant discovers the employer is committing fraud? Does confidentiality mean staying quiet?

**Tutor:** No — and the reasoning is worth owning, because scenario questions test exactly this collision. Confidentiality protects legitimate business information: customer lists, costing structures, strategy, salaries. It exists so that employers can trust their accountants with everything, which is the only way the job can be done. But confidentiality was never designed as a hiding place for crime, and every professional code says so: the duty falls away where the law requires disclosure or where the public interest demands it. Fraud is both. So the accountant who discovers fraud does not choose between ethics and silence — the ethical route has an order of operations. First, raise it inside the company through the proper channels: the financial director, and if the financial director is the problem, the audit committee — that is what the committee is for. Second, if the company's structures smother it, the law protects disclosure to the appropriate authorities; South African law shields employees who report crimes in good faith through proper channels. What the accountant may never do is the two opposite failures: stay silent and become part of the concealment, or run to social media with the company's books, which breaches confidentiality without serving the public interest. In an examination answer, name the collision, then resolve it: confidentiality yields to legal and public-interest disclosure, and the route is internal structures first, authorities second.

**Mandy:** Last one: the director who knows bad results are coming does not sell his own shares — he just mentions it to a friend at a braai, and the friend sells. Has anyone actually done something wrong?

**Tutor:** Both of them have, and this scenario separates candidates who memorised a definition from those who understand it. The wrong in insider trading is not the act of selling — it is USING information the market does not have, so that one side of a trade is playing with marked cards. The director who tips off his friend has taken confidential, price-sensitive information and put it to private use; South African financial markets law treats disclosing inside information improperly, and encouraging another person to trade on it, as offences alongside trading itself. The friend who sells, knowing the information came from inside and was not public, is trading as an insider even though he has never set foot in the company. Notice what this protects: the person who BOUGHT those shares at the braai-tip price, believing the market's public story. Every insider deal has an invisible victim on the other side of it. And complete the answer with consequences, because examiners award marks for them: for the director, dismissal for breach of fiduciary duty, prosecution under the law, and expulsion from a professional body if he belongs to one; for the friend, prosecution too; for the company, a reputation for leaking that raises its cost of capital. One sentence at a braai, three sets of consequences — that is why the rule reaches conversations, not just trades.
