**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First: ROSHE uses profit AFTER tax over equity, but ROTCE uses profit BEFORE tax PLUS interest over everything. Why do the two returns get built from different profit figures? It feels inconsistent.

**Tutor:** It is deliberately inconsistent, because the two ratios stand in different shoes. ROSHE stands in the shareholder's shoes and asks: of the money that can ever reach ME, how much did my equity generate? A shareholder can never touch the interest — that belongs to the bank — and can never touch the tax — that belongs to SARS. So both are stripped out, and what remains, net profit after tax, is measured against the shareholders' own money, average equity: R450 000 over R3 000 000, fifteen percent. ROTCE stands in the business's shoes, before any funder has been paid, and asks: how productive is the TOTAL pot of long-term capital, regardless of who supplied it? To answer fairly you must count the whole output of the engine — including the R80 000 that will go to the bank as interest, and the amount that will go to SARS as tax — because the engine earned all of it. Hence profit before tax plus interest, R700 000, over average capital employed of R4 000 000: seventeen and a half percent. The consistency test is this: each ratio's numerator must contain exactly the income that belongs to the capital in its denominator. Shareholders' profit over shareholders' capital; everyone's profit over everyone's capital. Mix them — after-tax profit over total capital, say — and the ratio answers no one's question. And it is precisely because the two are built on matching foundations that comparing ROTCE to the interest rate gives the clean gearing argument.

**Mandy:** Next: if the market pays 380 cents for a share with a net asset value of 304,5 cents, is the market not simply overpaying? Which number is the share really worth?

**Tutor:** Neither number is THE worth — they are answers to two different questions, and a strong answer says so explicitly. NAV answers a backward-looking, break-up question: if the company stopped trading today and its assets were sold at their book values and its debts settled, each share would collect 304,5 cents. It is anchored in the balance sheet, which records assets at cost less depreciation — not at what they could earn. The market price answers a forward-looking question: what will a rand of this company's future be worth? A buyer at 380 cents is not buying shelves and delivery vehicles; they are buying a claim on every profit the company will ever make. If the company reliably earns 45 cents a share and looks likely to grow, paying a premium over the book value of the assets is perfectly rational — the assets are just the instruments; the music is the earnings. So a premium to NAV usually signals market confidence, and a discount signals doubt: the market believes the assets will not earn their keep, or suspects the book values are optimistic. But note the examiner's subtlety: a discount can also mark an opportunity, which is why takeover bidders hunt for companies trading below NAV — buying the whole company for less than its net assets. The disciplined sentence is: the share trades at a premium of 75,5 cents to NAV, indicating that the market expects future earnings beyond what the balance sheet shows — quote, compare, judge.

**Mandy:** This one caused an argument: the lesson praised Khanya for collecting from debtors in thirty days while paying creditors in fifty. But is deliberately paying your suppliers slowly not exactly the kind of ethics problem this syllabus keeps warning about?

**Tutor:** The argument is worth having, because the line between working-capital skill and abuse is real and the syllabus sits directly on it. Start with what makes the practice legitimate: credit terms are a negotiated part of the price. If Khanya's suppliers granted sixty-day terms — common for large, reliable customers — then paying on day fifty is early, honourable, and smart: the company enjoys free financing that the supplier consciously priced into the relationship. Collecting in thirty while paying in fifty then means the operating cycle funds itself, and no one is wronged. The ethics problem begins where the agreement ends. Paying on day fifty when the agreed terms were thirty is a breach — the company is taking an unagreed, interest-free loan by force, and the cost lands on the supplier, often a smaller business with weaker bargaining power whose own cash flow, wages and survival depend on being paid on time. The syllabus flags the consequences on both sides: the late payer loses discounts, damages its credit rating, and eventually faces cash-on-delivery terms or interest charges; the supplier may fail entirely. So the interpretation discipline is: never praise a long creditors' payment period in the abstract — first ask what the terms were. Within terms, it is efficient treasury management; beyond terms, it is a liquidity confession and an ethics failure at once. That one-sentence caveat is routinely the difference between part marks and full marks on this question.

**Mandy:** Last one: for the annual report task — if the audit opinion is unqualified, the statements are trustworthy, so why must we still read the directors' report and the governance pages at all?

**Tutor:** Because an unqualified opinion certifies far less than people assume, and the gaps are exactly what the other pages cover. The auditor's opinion says one thing: the financial statements fairly present, in all MATERIAL respects, the financial position and results, in terms of the reporting framework. It does not say the company is well run. It does not say the strategy is wise, the directors' pay is deserved, or the company will still prosper next year. A company can march into disaster with spotless statements — the statements would simply present the disaster fairly. So each remaining section answers a question the audit never asked. The directors' report tells you what management DID and intends to do: expansions, dividends, events after year-end — read it as the players' own match report, informative but interested. The governance section tells you whether power is supervised: who sits on the board, whether independent non-executives outnumber insiders, what the audit and remuneration committees did, how the King Code's demands of responsibility, accountability, fairness and transparency are being met. And directors' remuneration read AGAINST performance is the sharpest test in the book: pay rising while earnings per share falls is a question shareholders should stand up and ask at the annual general meeting. The mature model of the annual report is a triangle: the statements say what happened, the audit report says whether you can believe it, and the governance pages say whether the people in charge deserve continued trust. Analysis uses all three corners — and examination scenarios are built precisely on the corners students skip.
