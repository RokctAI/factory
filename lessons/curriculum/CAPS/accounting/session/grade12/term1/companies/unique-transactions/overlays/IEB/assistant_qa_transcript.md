**Mandy:** Class questions before we wrap up! Here is the first: in the buy-back, the retiring shareholder got R9,00 a share. Who decided on R9,00 — and could the company have refused and offered him only the R5,75 average instead?

**Tutor:** The R9,00 is a negotiated price, and understanding that separates the deal from the bookkeeping. When a company repurchases shares it must pay something close to what the shares are actually worth today — otherwise the seller would simply sell to another investor instead, on the JSE if the company is listed, or privately if not. The company cannot impose the R5,75 average on him, because the average is not a value at all; it is a historical record of what the company happened to receive across all its issues. Naledi Limited's shares are worth more than R5,75 now precisely because the company has grown and kept profits back. So the negotiation sets R9,00, and only then does accounting take over with its own question: of this R9,00, how much is a return of contributed capital and how much is a distribution of accumulated profit? The answer is mechanical — R5,75 of capital per share, R3,25 of retained income per share — and it does not depend on what anyone negotiated. Keep the two decisions in separate compartments: directors and the seller decide the PRICE; the recording rule decides the SPLIT. One more safeguard sits behind the whole deal: directors may only go through with a buy-back if the company will still be able to pay its debts afterwards, because handing cash to a departing owner must never come at the expense of the creditors.

***

**Mandy:** Next one: the lesson said the repurchased shares are cancelled. Why destroy them? Buying them at R9,00 and selling them again later at R10,00 sounds like an easy profit for the company.

**Tutor:** It sounds like a profit, but think about who would be on each side of that trade. A company cannot meaningfully trade with itself: when it holds its own shares, it owns a claim on its own assets, which cancels out to nothing — no outsider owes the company anything because of those shares. That is why our approach treats repurchased shares as cancelled and struck from the register: the number of shares in issue simply falls from 800 000 to 760 000. And notice that cancellation already delivers the benefit you are reaching for, without any resale. After the buy-back, the same business is divided among fewer shares, so each remaining shareholder owns a larger fraction of it — earnings per share rises even if total earnings stand still. If the company later needs capital again, it does not dig old certificates out of a drawer; it issues fresh shares at the going price, exactly as Naledi Limited issued at R8,00, and the entry is the ordinary issue entry. So the rule costs the company nothing: destroy the returned slices, and if you ever need money, cut new ones.

**Mandy:** This one comes from three different people, so it clearly bothers everyone: the interest on the Kopano Bank loan never actually left the bank account, but we still recorded an expense of R80 000. How can something be an expense if no money was paid?

**Tutor:** Because expense and payment are two different events, and grade 12 is the year you fully separate them. An expense is incurred when the company consumes something of value; the payment is merely the settling up, which can happen before, after, or — as here — be rolled into another balance entirely. Naledi Limited used Kopano Bank's R800 000 for a full year, and the price of using someone else's money for a year at 10 percent is R80 000, whether or not a payment run happened. The contract says that instead of collecting the interest, the bank ADDS it to the debt — capitalisation. So the expense side is debit Interest on Loan R80 000, exactly as if it were paid, but the other side is credit Loan, not credit Bank. The loan then behaves like a slate that grows: R800 000 plus R80 000, less the R180 000 of instalments, closes at R700 000. Here is a test you can apply anywhere: if the company had NOT borrowed, would its wealth be R80 000 higher at year-end? Yes — so the R80 000 belongs on the income statement. You already accept this logic with depreciation, where no cash ever leaves. Matching what was consumed to the year it was consumed is the principle; the bank account is just one of several places the other leg can land.

**Mandy:** Last question: at the AGM the shareholders vote on everything else — so can they vote themselves a bigger dividend if they think the directors declared too little?

**Tutor:** No — and the reason goes to the heart of who does what in a company. Declaring dividends is a management decision, because it is really a decision about the company's cash and its future: how much must be held back for the new branches, the tax still owing, the loan instalments, the stock the yard must carry. Shareholders, however many votes they hold, do not manage — so the law leaves the declaration in the directors' hands, and no resolution from the floor can conjure a dividend the directors have not declared. What shareholders CAN do is exert the pressure the structure gives them: question the directors at the AGM, vote against re-electing directors whose retention policy they distrust, or sell their shares — and a falling share price is a message directors hear clearly. Notice the flip side, though, which we recorded today: the moment the directors DO declare, the balance of power reverses. Declaration turns a discretionary decision into a legal debt — Naledi Limited's R380 000 final dividend sits under current liabilities as Shareholders for Dividends, and a shareholder can now enforce payment like any other creditor of the company. Before declaration, the shareholders cannot demand a cent; after it, the company cannot take the promise back. That hinge — discretion before, obligation after — is exactly what the phrase "declared but not yet paid" is testing.
