# Part 1 — Expert

A company's year ends in three documents. The statement of comprehensive income tells the profit story. The statement of financial position tells the position on the last day. And the cash flow statement — new territory for grade 12 — answers the question the other two dodge: where did the money actually go? Behind all three stand the year-end adjustments and final accounts carried up from earlier grades, now with company-only entries: income tax, dividends, and retained income. And after the statements comes the audit report, the independent verdict on whether any of it can be believed. Work with one company throughout — Ilanga Limited — and every figure will be traced until it balances.

## Subtopic: Year-End Adjustments and the Final Accounts

The machinery is familiar from grade 11; the additions are the company entries. At year-end, adjustments force the books to obey the MATCHING principle: this year's statements carry this year's income and expenses, no more and no less. Accrued expenses are added, prepaid expenses removed, income receivable brought in, income received in advance pushed out, depreciation charged, trading stock deficits recorded. And the PROVISION FOR BAD DEBTS is adjusted, not recreated: if debtors of R240 000 justify a provision of R12 000 and the provision account stands at R14 000, the ADJUSTMENT is a decrease of R2 000 — only the movement touches the income statement.

Then the final accounts close the year off in three stages. The TRADING account matches sales against cost of sales and delivers gross profit. The PROFIT AND LOSS account absorbs the other incomes and all operating expenses, interest income and interest expense — and, new for companies, the INCOME TAX for the year — delivering net profit after tax. The APPROPRIATION account then answers the only question left: what happens to that profit? Some is distributed as DIVIDENDS — interim already paid, final declared. The remainder rolls into RETAINED INCOME, the accumulated undistributed profits of the company's whole life.

Ilanga's appropriation, in words: net profit after tax of R394 200 comes in. Dividends take R270 000 — an interim dividend of R120 000 paid in August, a final dividend of R150 000 declared on the last day. That leaves R124 200 retained for the year, added to the opening retained income of R215 800 for a closing balance of R340 000. Every one of those figures reappears somewhere in the statements — watch for them.

One reversal deserves its own sentence: accruals, prepayments and receivables from LAST year must be reversed at the start of THIS year, or income and expenses are counted twice. Examiners test it through the ledger accounts.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: the provision adjusted by its movement, the three final accounts in order, and the appropriation traced from profit to retained income.

## Subtopic: The Statement of Comprehensive Income

Now assemble the year into the statement of comprehensive income, spoken from the top.

Sales, after deducting debtors' allowances: R3 600 000. Cost of sales: R2 250 000. GROSS PROFIT: R1 350 000 — and check the relationship, because examiners do: gross profit on cost of sales is 1 350 000 divided by 2 250 000, a mark-up of 60 percent on cost.

Add other operating income of R90 000 — rent income, bad debts recovered, that R2 000 decrease in the provision for bad debts. Gross operating income: R1 440 000. Deduct operating expenses of R840 000 — salaries and wages, directors' fees, audit fees, depreciation of R180 000, sundry running costs. OPERATING PROFIT: R600 000.

Add interest income of R15 000 — profit before interest expense: R615 000. Deduct interest expense of R75 000 — NET PROFIT BEFORE TAX: R540 000. Deduct income tax of R145 800 — 27 percent of the profit — and the bottom line, NET PROFIT AFTER TAX: R394 200. The same R394 200 that entered the appropriation account. The statement and the ledger are one system.

Learn the three profit levels by name — gross profit, operating profit, net profit — because analysis questions quote them and each tests a different skill: buying and pricing, running the operation, financing the operation. And note what does NOT appear here: dividends. Dividends are not an expense; they are a distribution of profit, and they live in the retained income note, never above the profit line. Writing dividends among the expenses is one of the costliest errors in the paper.

Stop for this section's questions now — the statement recited from sales to net profit after tax, the mark-up verified, and dividends kept out of the expenses.

## Subtopic: The Statement of Financial Position and Its Notes

The statement of financial position is the photograph on the last day, and for Ilanga it balances at R3 750 000. Build it aloud, section by section.

ASSETS first. Non-current assets: fixed assets at a carrying value of R2 900 000 — that is cost less accumulated depreciation, detailed in the fixed asset note — plus a financial asset, a fixed deposit of R100 000: R3 000 000. Current assets: inventories R380 000, trade and other receivables R240 000, cash and cash equivalents R130 000: R750 000. TOTAL ASSETS: R3 750 000.

Now the claims on those assets. SHAREHOLDERS' EQUITY: ordinary share capital R2 500 000 and retained income R340 000 — the closing balance the appropriation account just produced — R2 840 000 in total. NON-CURRENT LIABILITIES: the loan from Themba Bank, R500 000, the portion not repayable within the next year. CURRENT LIABILITIES: trade and other payables R232 200, SHAREHOLDERS FOR DIVIDENDS R150 000 — the final dividend declared but unpaid — and SARS income tax R27 800: R410 000. Equity plus liabilities: 2 840 000 plus 500 000 plus 410 000 — R3 750 000. It balances, and it must.

Two company-only current liabilities deserve their arithmetic spoken. Shareholders for dividends is simply the final dividend of R150 000 — declared on the last day, paid early next year. SARS income tax is a slate: the year's charge was R145 800, provisional payments during the year came to R118 000, so R27 800 is still owing. Reverse the numbers and the balance flips to a current asset — read the account, never guess the side.

The NOTES carry the detail the face of the statement summarises: the fixed asset note showing cost, accumulated depreciation and the year's movements for each class; the ordinary share capital note showing authorised shares, issued shares and any issues or buy-backs during the year; the retained income note showing opening balance, net profit after tax, dividends both interim and final, and the closing balance. Examiners ask for the notes as often as the statement.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — the statement built to balance at R3 750 000, the two SARS directions, and the three notes matched to their contents.

## Subtopic: The Cash Flow Statement and the Audit Report

A company can report handsome profits and still fail, because profit is an opinion about matching while cash is a fact about the bank. The CASH FLOW STATEMENT reconciles the two, in three sections.

OPERATING ACTIVITIES. Start with net profit before tax, R540 000, and adjust for entries that never moved cash: add back depreciation of R180 000 and interest expense of R75 000. Cash generated before working capital changes: R795 000. Then the working capital movements: inventories rose by R40 000 — cash out; receivables rose by R25 000 — cash out; payables rose by R32 000 — cash in. Cash generated from operations: R762 000. Now the actual payments: interest paid R75 000, income tax paid R140 000 — last year's balance of R22 000 settled plus this year's provisional payments of R118 000 — and dividends paid R250 000, being last year's final dividend of R130 000 plus this year's interim of R120 000. The final dividend declared this year is NOT here — no cash has moved yet. Net cash from operating activities: R297 000.

INVESTING ACTIVITIES. Fixed assets purchased: R450 000 out. The fixed deposit increased by R20 000: out. Net cash used in investing: R470 000 out. Confirm the fixed asset figure the way examiners expect: opening carrying value R2 630 000 plus additions R450 000 less depreciation R180 000 gives the closing R2 900 000 — no disposals this year.

FINANCING ACTIVITIES. New shares issued for R200 000: in. Loan repaid: R60 000 out. Net cash from financing: R140 000 in.

Sum the three: 297 000 in, 470 000 out, 140 000 in — a net DECREASE in cash of R33 000. Opening cash R163 000, closing cash R130 000 — exactly the figure sitting in current assets. The statement closes the loop or it is wrong.

Then the AUDIT REPORT, the last page of the annual report and the first a careful reader checks. The independent auditor expresses one of three opinions. UNQUALIFIED: the statements fairly present the position — clean. QUALIFIED: fairly presented EXCEPT FOR specific stated matters — read the exceptions. DISCLAIMER or ADVERSE: the auditor cannot express an opinion, or the statements do not fairly present — the alarm bells. The report is addressed to the SHAREHOLDERS, and the syllabus links it to governance: directors prepare the statements, auditors verify them, and an audit fee buys an opinion, never a guarantee sold to the directors.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the three sections summed to the closing bank balance, tax and dividends paid rebuilt from the slates, and the three audit opinions ranked.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same year told the way you would tell it at home — because a set of financial statements is really three answers to the three questions any family asks about money.

## Subtopic: One Year, Three Photographs

Think of a family running a tuck shop for a year. In December, three questions get asked around the table.

Question one: how did we DO this year? That is the statement of comprehensive income — not a photograph but a video, the whole year compressed: everything sold, everything it cost, everything spent on running the place, the tax taken, and at the end one number — the profit. For Ilanga Limited that video ends at R394 200 after tax.

Question two: what do we HAVE now? That is the statement of financial position — a single photograph taken at midnight on the last day. On one side, everything owned: the building and equipment, the stock on the shelves, the money owed by customers, the cash. On the other, who has a claim on it all: the owners' slice — share capital plus every profit ever kept back — and the debts, long ones like the bank loan, short ones like suppliers, the final dividend promised to shareholders, and the last bit of tax still owed to SARS. The two sides both come to R3 750 000, because everything owned is claimed by somebody.

Question three — and this is the grade 12 question: where is the CASH? Because here is the strange thing the family notices: the video says we made almost four hundred thousand, but the tin has LESS money in it than last year. Both true. Profit counts sales the moment they happen, even if the customer pays next month; it spreads the cost of the fridge over years; it counts the promised dividend nowhere near the tin. Cash counts only notes and balances. The cash flow statement is the bridge between the two — and learning to walk that bridge is most of this lesson.

Questions on this piece are with you now: the video, the photograph and the bridge, and why a profitable year can still shrink the tin.

## Subtopic: Where Did the Cash Go?

Walk the bridge for Ilanga, in three strides.

Stride one: the everyday trading. Start from the profit before tax — R540 000 — and un-count the things that never touched the tin. Depreciation took R180 000 off the profit, but not one rand left the bank for it — add it back. Then look at the shelves and the notebooks: stock went UP by R40 000 — that is cash turned into boxes; customers owe R25 000 MORE than before — sales counted but not yet collected; suppliers are owed R32 000 more — things received but not yet paid for, which SAVES cash for now. After those, the trading of the year actually generated R762 000 of cash. Then the unavoidable outflows: interest to the bank, tax to SARS, and the dividends actually PAID this year — which, listen carefully, are LAST year's final dividend plus THIS year's interim. The final dividend declared last week is a promise, not a payment. After all that, trading put R297 000 into the tin.

Stride two: the future. The company bought R450 000 of new equipment and tucked another R20 000 into a fixed deposit. That is R470 000 out — not lost, invested.

Stride three: the funders. New shares brought in R200 000; the loan was paid down by R60 000. Net R140 000 in.

Now count the tin. In 297, out 470, in 140 — the tin is down R33 000, from R163 000 to R130 000. And that R130 000 is exactly the cash figure in the photograph. When your cash flow statement lands on the balance sheet's cash figure, you know every stride was true.

Read the story, not just the numbers: this company's trading is healthy, and the cash dip happened because it invested nearly half a million in its own future, funded partly by new shareholders. A cash dip caused by weak trading would be a very different story with the same bottom line — and examiners ask which story it is.

Your questions for this part are up now: depreciation added back, the shelves and notebooks read for cash, the dividends actually paid, and the tin counted to R130 000.

## Subtopic: The Auditor's Three Verdicts

Last piece: who says any of this is true? The directors wrote these statements about their own performance — like a learner marking their own examination. So the shareholders send in an outside examiner: the independent auditor.

The auditor checks the records, tests the figures, and writes a report with one of three verdicts. Think of a roadworthy certificate for a taxi. Verdict one — UNQUALIFIED: the taxi is roadworthy; the statements fairly present the company's position. The word sounds backwards, but unqualified means clean — no reservations attached. Verdict two — QUALIFIED: roadworthy, EXCEPT the brakes need attention; the statements are fair except for specific matters the auditor lists. Read the list before you ride. Verdict three — the serious ones: a DISCLAIMER, where the auditor could not get enough evidence to say anything, or an ADVERSE opinion, where the statements do not fairly present the position at all. Either way, do not board that taxi.

Three details carry marks. The report is addressed to the SHAREHOLDERS — the auditor works for the owners, checking the managers, never the other way round. An audit gives REASONABLE assurance, not a guarantee — auditors test samples, not every slip of paper. And FAIRLY PRESENT does not mean correct to the last cent; it means a reader can rely on the picture.

And the ethics thread that runs through every company question: an auditor must be INDEPENDENT. An auditor who golfs with the directors, holds shares in the company, or earns most of their income from this one client has a reason to soften the verdict — and a softened verdict makes the whole reporting system worthless, because every user downstream, from the bank to the pensioner buying shares, trusted that signature.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: the three verdicts matched to the taxi, who the report is addressed to and why, and what independence protects. Carry this whole company — every balanced figure — into the analysis lesson, where these statements get interrogated.
