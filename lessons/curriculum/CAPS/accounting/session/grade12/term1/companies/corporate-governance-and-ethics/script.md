# Part 1 — Expert

A company runs on other people's money. Thousands of shareholders hand their savings to a handful of directors they may never meet, and then trust those directors to spend it wisely, report on it honestly, and hand back a share of the profit. Everything in this lesson — corporate governance, the King Code, professional ethics, internal and independent audit — exists to make that trust reasonable instead of reckless. The syllabus tests it two ways: theory questions on who does what and answers to whom, and scenario questions where something has gone wrong and you must name the principle broken and recommend the remedy. This lesson builds both skills on one company, Vusani Limited.

## Subtopic: Owners, Directors and the Board

Start with the split that creates the whole subject: in a company, the people who OWN it are not the people who RUN it.

The SHAREHOLDERS own Vusani Limited. They provided the capital by buying shares, and their liability is limited to what they invested — the company's debts are not their debts. But ownership does not mean management. A shareholder's power is exercised at the ANNUAL GENERAL MEETING: attend and vote, one vote per share; ELECT the directors — and vote out those who disappoint; APPOINT the independent auditor and approve the audit fee; receive the annual report and interrogate it; question the directors face to face; and approve major decisions the law reserves for owners. Between meetings, shareholders receive dividends when the directors declare them — declared, never guaranteed.

The DIRECTORS run the company on the shareholders' behalf, and that word — behalf — carries legal weight. Directors owe a FIDUCIARY DUTY: act in good faith, in the best interests of the company, never for personal gain, and declare any personal interest in a company decision. Their responsibilities are examinable as a list: manage the company's operations, safeguard its assets through proper internal control, prepare financial statements that fairly present the results — the directors' report in the annual statements says exactly this — declare dividends the company can afford, and account to shareholders at the annual general meeting.

The BOARD itself has structure. EXECUTIVE directors work in the company daily — the chief executive, the financial director. NON-EXECUTIVE directors come from outside and bring oversight; INDEPENDENT non-executive directors have no other financial ties to the company at all, which is what makes their judgement trustworthy. Good governance separates the roles at the top: the chairperson, ideally an independent non-executive, runs the BOARD; the chief executive runs the COMPANY. One person holding both jobs concentrates power that governance exists to divide.

The questions for this section are with you now: shareholders' rights against directors' duties, the fiduciary idea, and the kinds of directors on a board.

## Subtopic: The King Code and Its Four Demands

Directors spending money that is not theirs need rules, and South Africa's rulebook for boards is the KING CODE — currently in its fourth edition, King IV. It is not an Act of Parliament; it is a code of good practice drawn up for South African boards, and the Johannesburg Stock Exchange requires listed companies to apply it and explain in their annual reports how they have done so. Its heart is ethical and effective leadership, and at examination depth it makes four demands of a board.

RESPONSIBILITY: the board must take charge of the company's assets and actions — set strategy, manage risk, and correct what goes wrong instead of hiding it. ACCOUNTABILITY: the board must be answerable — to shareholders above all — for every decision; in a well-governed company nobody spends unexplained money, because everybody answers to somebody. FAIRNESS: the board must weigh the legitimate interests of ALL stakeholders — minority shareholders alongside the majority, employees, creditors, customers, the community — not only the loudest voice. TRANSPARENCY: the board must report openly and honestly, good news and bad, so that outsiders can see the true position.

The Code turns those words into structures. The AUDIT COMMITTEE — independent non-executive directors — oversees the financial reporting, receives the internal auditors' findings, and protects the independence of the external auditor. The REMUNERATION COMMITTEE sets directors' pay, so that directors do not set their own — and TRANSPARENCY then requires that pay to be disclosed, director by director, in the annual report, where shareholders can weigh it against performance.

The examination skill is diagnosis. A scenario tells you the board hid a loss from shareholders — name transparency, and accountability with it. Directors paid themselves bonuses while the company failed to pay creditors — fairness, and responsibility. Learn the four demands as instruments, not vocabulary.

Pause for this section's questions, with you now: the Code's status, the four demands, and the two committees matched to their jobs.

## Subtopic: Professional Ethics and the Code of Conduct

Governance disciplines the board; ETHICS disciplines the individual. Professional accountants in South Africa belong to professional bodies — the chartered accountants' and professional accountants' institutes — and membership binds them to a CODE OF PROFESSIONAL CONDUCT built on five principles. INTEGRITY: be straightforward and honest — no massaged figures, however senior the person asking. OBJECTIVITY: let no bias, conflict of interest or pressure override judgement. PROFESSIONAL COMPETENCE AND DUE CARE: keep skills current and do the work properly. CONFIDENTIALITY: never use or leak information gained through work — though confidentiality never shields illegality. PROFESSIONAL BEHAVIOUR: obey the law and avoid conduct that discredits the profession.

Now run Vusani's scenarios against those principles. The financial director is instructed to record January sales in December to lift the year's profit — integrity refuses, because the statements would no longer fairly present. A director's spouse owns a cleaning company bidding for Vusani's contract — a CONFLICT OF INTEREST; the ethical route is to declare the interest and withdraw from the decision, letting the remaining directors decide on merit. Withholding the declaration and steering the contract is a governance breach even if the spouse's quote was competitive, because objectivity was never protected.

The sharpest ethics content is share dealing. A director learns, before publication, that Vusani's profit has collapsed, and quietly sells shares while the market still believes the old story — INSIDER TRADING: trading on information the market lacks, illegal under South African financial markets law. Its twin is SHARE-PRICE MANIPULATION: spreading false information, or arranging trades, to push the price up or down artificially. Both cheat the outsiders the published statements exist to protect, and both appear in examinations as scenarios: name the offence, state why it is wrong, and state the consequence — dismissal, prosecution, and for a professional, expulsion by their body.

This section's questions are ready for you now: the five principles, the conflict-of-interest procedure, and insider trading split from manipulation.

## Subtopic: Internal Control, Internal Audit and the Independent Audit

Trust needs checking, and a company checks itself in three layers.

Layer one: INTERNAL CONTROL — management's daily machinery for protecting assets and information. Division of duties, so the person who orders is not the person who pays and not the person who records; authorisation, so nothing moves without an approved signature; documentation, so every transaction leaves a trail; physical safeguards — safes, passwords, access control; and regular reconciliation of records against reality. Controls run every day, operated by management, and responsibility for them sits squarely with the directors.

Layer two: INTERNAL AUDIT — employees of the company who test those controls all year round. Their governance significance is the reporting line: internal audit reports its findings to the AUDIT COMMITTEE, not merely to the managers being tested, so that a finding which embarrasses a manager cannot be buried by that manager. Internal audit recommends; management remains accountable for acting.

Layer three: the INDEPENDENT AUDIT. The external auditor is a registered auditor in public practice, appointed by the SHAREHOLDERS at the annual general meeting, reporting to the shareholders, and expressing a published opinion on whether the annual financial statements fairly present the company's position. Everything of value in that opinion flows from INDEPENDENCE — which is why the auditor must hold no shares in the client, why lucrative extra consulting work for the same client threatens objectivity, and why the audit committee exists partly to guard the auditor's independence and recommend the appointment honestly.

Hold the chain as one picture: management operates controls and answers to the board; the board answers to shareholders; internal audit tests the controls for the audit committee; the independent auditor verifies the statements for the shareholders. Every arrow points toward the owners — that is what accountability means in practice.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the internal control measures, the reporting lines, and the three layers in order.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same machinery at street level — because a company's governance is just a soccer club's committee with more zeros on the bank statement.

## Subtopic: The Club and Its Committee

Phambili United is your neighbourhood's soccer club. Two hundred members each pay a joining fee — that money bought the kit, the nets, the clubhouse paint. The members OWN the club. But two hundred people cannot all run one club, so once a year they gather, and they ELECT a committee of five to run it for them. That yearly gathering is the club's annual general meeting, and the election is the members' real power: a committee that runs the club badly gets voted out at the next one.

Watch what the members keep and what they hand over. They keep the votes, the right to hear a full report-back once a year, the right to stand up at the meeting and ask the treasurer hard questions, and the right to choose an OUTSIDE person — someone on nobody's team — to check the books. They hand over the daily running: fixtures, kit orders, gate money, the bank card. That is exactly the shareholder-director split: owners hold the power to appoint, question and remove; the committee holds the power to manage.

And the committee members hold that power on the members' BEHALF. The treasurer's rand is not the treasurer's — every cent belongs to two hundred people who trusted him with it. The moment a committee member starts treating club money as personal money, or club decisions as personal favours, he has broken the deal the election created. Companies write that deal down and call it fiduciary duty; the club just calls it not stealing from your own people.

Questions on this piece are with you now: what members keep, what the committee gets, and whose money the treasurer is holding.

## Subtopic: House Rules That Keep the Money Clean

The four demands of the King Code are just Phambili United's house rules, written formally.

RESPONSIBILITY: the committee looks after what the club owns — kit counted in and out, gate money banked the same day, the leaking clubhouse roof fixed before it ruins the equipment. A committee that lets club property rot has failed before any money goes missing. ACCOUNTABILITY: at the yearly meeting the treasurer explains every rand — what came in, what went out, why. Not the highlights: everything. The test of accountability is simple — is there anyone in this club who spends money and answers to nobody? If yes, the rule is broken. FAIRNESS: the committee serves ALL two hundred members — the under-13s' kit matters as much as the first team's, and the quiet members' fees bought as much of that clubhouse as the chairperson's did. TRANSPARENCY: the books are open. Bad month? The members hear about the bad month. A committee that only reports good news is hiding, and hiding is where every scandal starts.

Now the scenario the examination loves. The treasurer, Sello, orders the new kit from his brother's shop — no other quotes, price never compared. Run the diagnosis: Sello had a personal interest and never declared it; objectivity and fairness are gone, and even a decent price cannot repair that, because nobody protected the decision. The remedy, step by step: declare the family connection, step out of the kit decision entirely, let the rest of the committee gather three quotes, and record the choice and reasons in the minutes. Same conflict, handled cleanly — the brother's shop may even win, honestly.

Your questions for this part are up now: the four house rules with a club example each, and the Sello scenario diagnosed and repaired.

## Subtopic: Who Checks the Checkers?

Trusting the committee does not mean nobody counts.

First, everyday habits — internal control in club clothing. Two signatures on the club account, so no one person can move money alone. Gate money counted by one member, recorded by another, banked by a third — the counter is never the recorder. A receipt book for every cent in, a file of slips for every cent out. These habits run every match day, and they protect honest people too: when nothing is missing, the treasurer can prove it.

Second, the club appoints Naledi — a sharp member, not on the committee — to check things through the year: count the kit against the register, trace a Saturday's gate money from the notebook to the bank slip. When she finds a gap, she reports it to the club's oversight group, not privately to the treasurer she is checking — a report handed only to the person it embarrasses tends to disappear. Naledi is internal audit: inside, year-round, testing the habits.

Third, once a year, the members — never the committee — choose an outside accountant with no ties to anyone in the club, who examines the year's books and tells the MEMBERS whether the treasurer's report fairly shows the truth. That is the independent audit, and the whole point is who appoints her and who she answers to: the owners, not the people being checked.

Now the examination method for every scenario question in this topic, three moves. One, STATE the problem from the facts: the treasurer's brother got the contract without quotes. Two, NAME the principle or duty broken: undeclared conflict of interest; fairness and transparency violated. Three, RECOMMEND the remedy that closes it: declared interests, competing quotes, decisions minuted, duties divided. Problem, principle, remedy — every time, and the marks follow. And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: the club's controls, Naledi against the outside accountant, and the three-move method itself.
