# Part 1 — Expert

The statements are built; now they get interrogated. Analysis and interpretation is where accounting stops being recording and becomes judgement — and it is worth a large slice of the final examination precisely because it cannot be memorised, only practised. Grade 11's indicators return in company clothing, and grade 12 adds the shareholder's own toolkit: return on shareholders' equity, return on total capital employed, earnings per share, dividends per share, net asset value and the pay-out rate. One company carries the whole lesson — Khanya Limited, one million shares in issue — and every indicator will be computed, then INTERPRETED, because the calculation earns half the marks and the sentence after it earns the rest.

## Subtopic: The Grade 11 Toolkit Revisited

Four families of indicators return from grade 11; run them on Khanya's figures.

PROFITABILITY. Gross profit on sales, gross profit on cost of sales, operating profit on sales, net profit on sales — each a percentage that tracks margin discipline. Khanya's operating figures behave, so move to the ones examiners weight at grade 12.

LIQUIDITY — can the company pay its way this year? Current assets are R840 000: inventories R360 000, trade receivables R330 000, cash R150 000. Current liabilities are R400 000. CURRENT RATIO: 840 000 to 400 000 — 2,1 to 1. ACID-TEST: strip out the inventories, leaving 480 000 to 400 000 — 1,2 to 1. Both comfortable: even without selling a single item of stock, short-term debts are covered.

But ratios can flatter, so grade 12 adds the OPERATING CYCLE checks. STOCK TURNOVER: cost of sales R2 880 000 divided by average stock of R360 000 — 8 times a year, roughly forty-five days on the shelf. DEBTORS' COLLECTION PERIOD: average debtors R330 000 over credit sales of R3 960 000, times three hundred and sixty-five — 30,4 days: customers pay inside the thirty-day terms, barely. CREDITORS' PAYMENT PERIOD: average creditors R400 000 over credit purchases of R2 920 000, times three hundred and sixty-five — 50 days. Read the three TOGETHER, because that is the skill: Khanya collects in thirty days and pays in fifty, so suppliers are financing the debtors' book — favourable, provided the fifty days is within terms and not damaging the company's credit rating.

SOLVENCY — will the company survive the long run? Total assets R4 385 000 against total liabilities R1 340 000 — 3,3 to 1. Solvent several times over.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: the two liquidity ratios computed, the three cycle periods read together, and liquidity kept distinct from solvency.

## Subtopic: The New Grade 12 Indicators

Now the shareholder's toolkit — six indicators, each answering a question an owner actually asks.

RETURN ON SHAREHOLDERS' EQUITY — ROSHE. Question: what did MY money earn? Net profit after tax, R450 000, divided by AVERAGE shareholders' equity — opening R2 955 000, closing R3 045 000, average R3 000 000 — gives 15 percent. Compare it to the alternative: a fixed deposit pays around 8 percent with far less risk. Fifteen beats eight comfortably, so shareholders are being compensated for their risk.

RETURN ON TOTAL CAPITAL EMPLOYED — ROTCE. Question: what does the WHOLE pot earn, regardless of who funded it? Net profit BEFORE tax plus interest on loans — 620 000 plus 80 000, R700 000 — divided by average capital employed: equity plus non-current loans, average R4 000 000. Answer: 17,5 percent. Before-tax and before-interest, because this indicator measures the business engine before the funders divide the output.

EARNINGS PER SHARE — EPS. Net profit after tax divided by shares in issue: R450 000 over one million shares — 45 cents. Every share EARNED 45 cents this year, whether or not it was paid out.

DIVIDENDS PER SHARE — DPS. Total dividends divided by shares: R360 000 over one million — 36 cents: an interim of 15 cents paid mid-year plus a final of 21 cents declared at year-end.

DIVIDEND PAY-OUT RATE. DPS over EPS: 36 over 45 — 80 percent. Khanya paid out four-fifths of its earnings and retained one-fifth. High pay-out pleases income-seeking shareholders; low pay-out funds expansion without borrowing. Neither is right in itself — the interpretation depends on the company's plans, and saying so is what earns the mark.

NET ASSET VALUE PER SHARE — NAV. Total shareholders' equity divided by shares in issue: R3 045 000 over one million — 304,5 cents. This is what each share is WORTH according to the books: assets minus liabilities, per share.

Stop for this section's questions now — each of the six computed on Khanya's figures, and the question each one answers stated in words.

## Subtopic: Gearing, Returns and the Share Price

Calculation done; now the judgement questions the paper actually sets.

First: SHOULD KHANYA BORROW MORE? The DEBT-EQUITY ratio measures gearing: non-current loans of R940 000 against equity of R3 045 000 — 0,31 to 1, lightly geared. Now the decisive comparison: ROTCE is 17,5 percent, while the loans cost about 8 percent interest. Every borrowed rand earns seventeen and a half cents and costs eight — POSITIVE gearing, working for the shareholders. The gap is the argument: with returns comfortably above the cost of debt, Khanya can afford to gear up to fund expansion; if interest rates rose toward the ROTCE, that argument would die. State the comparison, then the risk: higher gearing means fixed interest must be paid even in a bad year.

Second: IS THE SHARE PRICE SENSIBLE? Khanya's shares trade on the JSE at 380 cents, while NAV is 304,5 cents. The market pays 75,5 cents per share MORE than the book value of the net assets. That premium is not an error — it is the market pricing what the balance sheet cannot show: expected future profits, brand, management quality, momentum. A share trading far BELOW NAV signals the opposite — a market expecting decline, or an opportunity, and a well-argued answer can defend either reading. Anchor the comparison: price against NAV, always in the same units, cents against cents.

Third: ARE THE SHAREHOLDERS BEING WELL SERVED? Line up the returns: ROSHE 15 percent against the 8 percent fixed-deposit alternative — yes, with a risk premium. EPS 45 cents against DPS 36 cents — earnings genuinely cover the dividend. And watch EPS over TIME: growing EPS with a steady share count means real improvement; growing EPS after a buy-back needs care, because fewer shares flatter the ratio even when total profit is flat. Examiners reward exactly that caution.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — gearing argued through the ROTCE-against-interest comparison, the premium to NAV explained, and the shareholder's scorecard read with its traps.

## Subtopic: Reading a Published Annual Report

The syllabus's final demand: lift your eyes from computed ratios to the PUBLISHED annual report of a real company, because that is what Task work and the examination's interpretation questions simulate.

An annual report carries far more than statements. The DIRECTORS' REPORT: the board's own account of the year — operations, dividends declared, directors in office, significant events after year-end. Read it critically: it is honest, but it is the players writing the match report. The INDEPENDENT AUDITOR'S REPORT: the referee's page — find the opinion paragraph first; unqualified, qualified, disclaimer or adverse decides how much of the rest to trust. The ABRIDGED or summarised statements: the full statements compressed for publication — the analysis skills transfer directly. And the GOVERNANCE information: directors' remuneration disclosed, board committees, and compliance with the King Code — South Africa's governance framework, requiring boards to act with responsibility, accountability, fairness and transparency.

Then apply the whole toolkit with the syllabus's own scenarios. AUDIT OPINION FIRST: a qualified opinion on stock valuation, say, undermines the very ratios built on inventory. DIRECTORS' PAY against performance: remuneration rising while EPS falls is a governance question shareholders should raise at the annual general meeting. SHARE PRICE against NAV over time: a widening discount while directors buy shares privately raises the syllabus's sharpest ethics topic — share-price manipulation and insider trading: spreading false information, or trading on knowledge the market lacks, both illegal and both tested as ethics questions.

Close with the discipline every interpretation answer needs: QUOTE the figure, COMPARE it to something — last year, the norm, another company, an interest rate — then JUDGE with a reason. Figure, comparison, judgement. Three sentences, full marks.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the four parts of an annual report and what each is for, the King Code's four demands, and the figure-comparison-judgement discipline.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same toolkit as a family deciding whether Uncle Bongani's shop is a good place for their savings — because every ratio in this lesson is a question a careful relative would ask before putting money in.

## Subtopic: The Company's Report Card

Uncle Bongani wants the family to invest in his shop, which is now a company. Aunt Nomsa, who trusts nobody's smile, asks for the books and starts making a report card.

First mark: CAN IT PAY ITS BILLS THIS MONTH? The shop has R840 000 of things that are cash or will soon be cash — stock, customers' debts, money in the bank — against R400 000 of bills due soon. More than two rand ready for every one rand owed. And even if the stock refuses to sell, there is still R1,20 for every rand owed. The shop will not be embarrassed at month-end.

Second mark: HOW FAST DOES THE MONEY GO ROUND? The stock sells out about eight times a year — nothing gathering dust. Customers pay in about thirty days, right on their terms. And the shop pays its own suppliers in about fifty days. Aunt Nomsa smiles at that one: the suppliers' patience is financing the customers' slowness. Clever — as long as the suppliers stay happy, because a shop that stretches its suppliers too far gets cash-on-delivery terms next time, and that can strangle it.

Third mark: WOULD IT SURVIVE A BAD YEAR? Everything the shop owns is worth about three times everything it owes. Even a terrible year does not sink a ship with that much freeboard.

Notice what Aunt Nomsa never once asked: is the profit big? A big profit number means nothing by itself — big compared to WHAT is the only question. That habit — compare, then judge — is the entire subject of analysis, and it is worth more than any single ratio.

Questions on this piece are with you now: the month-end test with and without stock, the three speeds read together, and why the suppliers' patience matters.

## Subtopic: Whose Money Works Harder?

Now the question the family really cares about: what will OUR money earn in this shop?

The shop's whole pot of long-term money is about four million rand — the family's equity plus the bank's loan. Last year the business engine produced R700 000 before the funders took their shares — seventeen and a half cents on every rand in the pot. That is the engine's honest output: ROTCE.

Now split the funders. The BANK put in about a million and charges 8 percent — it takes its eighty thousand no matter what kind of year the shop had. The FAMILY gets what remains, and after tax their return works out to 15 percent on their money: ROSHE.

Here is the thought Aunt Nomsa wants everyone to sit with. The shop borrows money at eight cents on the rand and puts it to work earning seventeen and a half. Every borrowed rand WORKS FOR THE FAMILY — the bank does the funding, the family keeps the difference. That is why a sensible amount of debt makes owners richer, and it has a name: gearing. But hear the other half: the bank's eighty thousand is owed in the good years AND the bad ones. A shop earning seventeen and a half can gear up happily; if the engine slowed to seven while the bank still wanted eight, every borrowed rand would start eating the family's dinner. Borrowed money is an amplifier — it makes good years better and bad years worse.

And the fixed-deposit test, which settles every investment argument at the kitchen table: the bank pays 8 percent for doing nothing. The shop pays 15 — but with risk attached. Seven extra cents per rand is the price the shop pays the family for their courage. If ROSHE ever slid toward eight, the honest advice would be: take the fixed deposit and sleep well.

Your questions for this part are up now: the engine's output against the bank's price, gearing as an amplifier, and the fixed-deposit test applied.

## Subtopic: The Price on the Board and the Value in the Books

Last piece: the shop's shares trade — cousins buy and sell slices among themselves — and two numbers about each slice keep getting confused. Aunt Nomsa separates them.

Number one: what the BOOKS say a slice is worth. Add up everything the shop owns, subtract everything it owes, divide by the million slices: 304,5 cents each. That is net asset value — the liquidation answer, what each slice would collect if the shop stopped today and sold everything at book value.

Number two: what a cousin will PAY for a slice. The going price is 380 cents. Seventy-five and a half cents MORE than the books say. Are the cousins fools? No — they are not buying the shelves and the fridges; they are buying the shop's FUTURE: next year's profits, the loyal customers, Uncle Bongani's eye for stock. None of that appears on a balance sheet, and all of it is worth paying for. A price above book value is the market's compliment. A price far BELOW book value is the market's warning — or, for a brave buyer, a bargain, and a good answer can argue either way as long as it argues.

Then the two per-share numbers that track each slice's year. Each slice EARNED 45 cents — that is EPS, the slice's share of the year's profit. Each slice was PAID 36 cents — that is DPS, the dividend that actually reached pockets. The gap of nine cents did not vanish: it stayed in the shop as retained income, quietly making every slice more valuable. Pay-out rate: thirty-six out of forty-five — 80 percent paid, 20 percent kept. Grandmother, who lives on her dividends, loves the 80. A cousin who wants the shop to open a second branch would rather see 40. Neither is wrong; they want different things from the same shop, and the directors' pay-out decision is exactly that balancing act.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: the books' answer against the board's price, earned against paid per slice, and who wins from a high pay-out. Interpretation is the skill the whole paper rewards — figure, comparison, judgement, every single time.
