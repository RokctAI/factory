# Part 1 — Expert

Grade 12 accounting leaves the partnership behind and takes on the COMPANY — a business that is a legal person in its own right, owned by shareholders who may never set foot in it and run by directors who may own almost none of it. That separation changes the bookkeeping: shares are issued and bought back, profits are taxed in the company's own name, and the owners are paid dividends that the company chooses to declare. Today: the company form and its vocabulary under the Companies Act 71 of 2008; the issue and buy-back of shares; income tax and dividends in the ledger; and loans, directors' fees and audit fees. Every company question in the final examination stands on these transactions.

## Subtopic: From Owner to Shareholder — the Company Form

A company is INCORPORATED under the Companies Act 71 of 2008 by registering its Memorandum of Incorporation — the MOI — with the Companies and Intellectual Property Commission. From that day it is a separate legal person: it owns its assets, owes its debts, sues and is sued in its own name. The syllabus draws two consequences and examiners ask for both. First, LIMITED LIABILITY: a shareholder can lose what was paid for the shares, and nothing more — the company's creditors cannot reach a shareholder's house or car. Second, CONTINUITY: shareholders come and go, and the company continues.

Two kinds of company matter here. A PUBLIC company — Limited, Ltd — may offer its shares to the public and may list on the JSE, the Johannesburg Stock Exchange, where shares trade between investors at whatever price supply and demand set. A PRIVATE company — Proprietary Limited, Pty Ltd — may not offer shares to the public and restricts their transfer.

Now the separation the whole topic turns on: OWNERSHIP and CONTROL are split. The SHAREHOLDERS own the company and vote at the annual general meeting. The DIRECTORS, elected by the shareholders, control and manage it. Because the people running the business are spending other people's money, the law adds a watchdog: a public company must appoint an independent AUDITOR to express an opinion on whether the financial statements fairly present the company's position.

Last, the share vocabulary. The MOI states the AUTHORISED share capital — the maximum number of shares the company may issue. The ISSUED share capital is the number actually sold to shareholders, and it can never exceed the authorised number. South African companies issue NO PAR VALUE shares: a share has no fixed face value, and each issue is recorded at whatever price it fetched.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: the two consequences of separate legal personality, public against private companies, ownership split from control, and authorised against issued capital.

## Subtopic: Issuing Shares and Buying Them Back

Work with Thabiso Limited throughout this lesson. Its MOI authorises one million ordinary shares. At the start of the year it has 500 000 shares in issue, which raised a total of R2 000 000 — an average of R4,00 per share.

A SHARE ISSUE is the plainest entry in the topic. On 1 June the directors issue 300 000 new shares at an issue price of R6,00 each. The company receives 300 000 times R6,00 — R1 800 000. Debit Bank, credit Ordinary Share Capital, R1 800 000. There is no separate premium account: with no par value shares, the full issue price goes to Ordinary Share Capital. After the issue Thabiso has 800 000 shares in issue and share capital of R3 800 000 — and note the new AVERAGE price per share: R3 800 000 divided by 800 000 is R4,75. That average is not decoration. It is the figure the buy-back needs.

A SHARE BUY-BACK — the company repurchasing its own shares — is the entry examiners set to separate candidates. On 31 December Thabiso buys back 50 000 shares from an estate at R7,00 per share, paying out R350 000. The rule: Ordinary Share Capital is reduced by the AVERAGE issue price of the shares bought back, and the EXCESS paid above that average comes out of RETAINED INCOME. So debit Ordinary Share Capital with 50 000 times R4,75 — R237 500; debit Retained Income with the excess, R350 000 less R237 500 — R112 500; credit Bank R350 000. The logic: the capital account only ever held R4,75 per share on average, so only that much can leave it; the extra R2,25 per share is a distribution of past profits to the departing shareholder.

After the buy-back: 750 000 shares in issue, and share capital of R3 800 000 less R237 500 — R3 562 500. Bought-back shares are cancelled; they do not sit in a drawer waiting to be resold, and they receive no future dividends.

Stop for this section's questions now — the issue recorded at issue price, the average price recalculated, and the buy-back split between share capital and retained income.

## Subtopic: Income Tax and Dividends in the Ledger

A company pays INCOME TAX on its own profits, in its own name — unlike a sole trader or partnership, where the owners are taxed personally. And it does not wait until year-end to pay: PROVISIONAL TAX is paid in advance, normally two payments during the year, each recorded as debit SARS — Income Tax, credit Bank. The SARS account acts as a running slate with the tax authority.

Thabiso's year: a first provisional payment of R170 000 in August, a second of R180 000 in February — R350 000 paid in total. At year-end the profit is finalised: net profit before tax is R1 400 000, and at the company rate of 27 percent the income tax for the year is R378 000. Record the charge: debit Income Tax — the expense that appropriation will meet — and credit SARS — Income Tax, R378 000. Now read the SARS account: debits of R350 000 paid against a credit of R378 000 owed — a credit balance of R28 000, shown as a CURRENT LIABILITY: SARS — income tax. Had the provisional payments exceeded the assessment, the balance would flip to a current ASSET — a refund due. That flip is a favourite examination twist.

DIVIDENDS are the shareholders' reward, and the crucial word is DECLARED: no shareholder can demand a dividend; the directors decide. Dividends are stated in CENTS PER SHARE and paid on shares in issue. Thabiso's INTERIM dividend, declared and paid in August while 800 000 shares were in issue, was 25 cents per share — 800 000 times 25 cents is R200 000: debit Dividends on Ordinary Shares, credit Bank. The FINAL dividend is declared on the last day of the year, after the buy-back, so it belongs to only 750 000 shares: 40 cents per share gives R300 000 — declared but not yet paid, so credit SHAREHOLDERS FOR DIVIDENDS, a current liability that will be settled early in the new year. Total dividends for the year: R500 000. Count the shares at each date — paying the final dividend on 800 000 shares is the classic error, and it costs the marks for both the dividend and the balance sheet.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — provisional tax against the final charge, the R28 000 still owed read from the SARS account, and each dividend computed on the shares in issue on its own date.

## Subtopic: Loans, Directors' Fees and Audit Fees

Three more transactions complete the set.

A LOAN WITH CAPITALISED INTEREST. Thabiso borrowed R500 000 from Vuka Bank at 12 percent per annum, and the agreement capitalises interest — the interest is ADDED TO THE LOAN rather than paid separately. The year's interest is 12 percent of R500 000 — R60 000: debit Interest on Loan, credit the Loan account. During the year Thabiso paid instalments totalling R110 000: debit Loan, credit Bank. Trace the balance: R500 000 plus R60 000 less R110 000 — R450 000 owing at year-end. The trap is direction: candidates subtract the interest or ignore it. Capitalised interest makes the loan GROW before payments shrink it, and the interest is still an expense on the income statement even though no separate interest payment left the bank.

DIRECTORS' FEES are what directors earn for managing the company. They are an OPERATING EXPENSE — a cost of running the business, deducted before net profit — and they must never be confused with dividends. A director is paid fees for work; a shareholder is paid dividends on shares; a person who is both receives both, through two different doors. Examiners probe exactly this: fees reduce profit, dividends are an appropriation of profit after tax.

AUDIT FEES are the price of the watchdog — the independent auditor's charge for examining the records and reporting to the shareholders. Also an operating expense. And note the governance point the syllabus attaches: the auditor is appointed by and reports to the SHAREHOLDERS, not the directors, precisely because the audit exists to check the directors' stewardship. An auditor whose independence is compromised — large gifts, family ties, fees dependent on a friendly opinion — defeats the purpose of the appointment.

Close the loop on retained income. RETAINED INCOME is the profit kept in the company after tax and dividends, accumulated year after year — the account the buy-back dipped into, and the link between this lesson and the financial statements ahead.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the capitalised loan traced to R450 000, fees separated from dividends, and the auditor's appointment explained through independence.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same ground from a township supermarket that grew too big for one owner's pocket — because a company is just what happens when a business needs more money than its founders have.

## Subtopic: The Spaza That Became a Company

Thabiso started with a spaza shop. It grew into a supermarket, and now he wants five more branches — but the banks will only lend so much, and his own savings are spent. So he does what growing businesses do: he turns the business into a company and sells SLICES of it.

Each slice is a share. Whoever buys one owns a piece of the business — entitled to a piece of the profits and a vote at the yearly meeting — without working a single shift. The law now sees the supermarket as its own person: the COMPANY owns the tills and the stock, the company owes the suppliers. If it fails, the shareholders lose what they paid for their slices, and that is where it stops. Nobody comes for their houses. That promise — limited liability — is the whole reason strangers are willing to put money into a business they will never manage.

But strangers with money want rules. So the law makes the company write its rulebook — the Memorandum of Incorporation — and register it. The rulebook states the biggest number of slices the company may EVER sell: that is the authorised share capital. The slices actually sold so far are the issued share capital. Authorised is the size of the cake tin; issued is how much cake has been cut.

And who runs the shop day to day? Not the shareholders — most have never seen it. They elect DIRECTORS to manage it for them. Which creates the topic's central tension: the people spending the money are not the people who own it. Keep that sentence; it explains almost every rule that follows, including why an outside auditor is sent in to check the books once a year.

Questions on this piece are with you now: what a share is, what limited liability protects, the cake tin against the cake, and why owners and managers are different people.

## Subtopic: Selling Slices of the Business

Follow the money through Thabiso Limited's year, in shop language.

The company already sold 500 000 slices over the years for R2 000 000 all together — R4,00 a slice on average. This year it needs money for the new branches, so it sells 300 000 fresh slices at R6,00 each. Why more than R4,00? Because the business is worth more now — a slice of a bigger cake costs more. In comes R1 800 000, and the share capital account now shows R3 800 000 for 800 000 slices. New average: R4,75 a slice.

Then a shareholder dies, and the family wants money, not shares. The company agrees to buy 50 000 slices back at R7,00 each — R350 000 out. Here is the part that trips everyone, so take it slowly. The capital account never received R7,00 for those shares; on average it received R4,75. So the capital account gives back exactly what it held — 50 000 times R4,75, which is R237 500 — and the extra R112 500 comes out of the company's saved-up profits, the retained income. Think of it as two payments in one: a refund of what the shares put in, plus a parting bonus paid from profits.

And the bought-back slices? Torn up. Cancelled. The company is now cut into 750 000 slices, not 800 000 — which quietly makes every remaining slice a slightly bigger fraction of the cake. That is one honest reason companies buy back shares at all.

Your questions for this part are up now: why new slices sell above the old average, where the R350 000 came from in the books, and what happens to slices the company buys back.

## Subtopic: The Company's Own Bills

The company is a person in the law's eyes, so it gets its own bills.

First bill: TAX. Thabiso the man used to pay tax on the shop's profits himself; Thabiso Limited pays its own, at 27 percent of profit. And SARS does not wait for year-end — the company pays twice during the year, in advance, guessing its profit. Those guesses came to R350 000. When the real profit was counted, the tax came to R378 000. So the year ends with the company still owing SARS R28 000 — a debt on the balance sheet. Guess too much instead, and SARS owes YOU — the debt flips sides. One account, read like a slate at the counter: what you paid on one side, what you owe on the other, the difference is the balance.

Second bill — except it is not really a bill: DIVIDENDS. Shareholders are not employees; nobody owes them a wage. When the year goes well, the directors may DECLARE a dividend — so many cents per slice. Thabiso Limited paid 25 cents a slice mid-year, when there were 800 000 slices — R200 000. At year-end it declared 40 cents more, but by then the buy-back had shrunk the company to 750 000 slices — R300 000, promised but not yet paid, so it sits as a debt to shareholders until the money goes out. Count the slices on the day of each dividend. Always.

Third: the people who serve the company get paid for WORK, not ownership. Directors earn directors' fees for managing; the auditor earns an audit fee for checking the books on the shareholders' behalf. Both are ordinary expenses of running the business. A director who also owns shares is paid twice, through two different doors — fees for the work, dividends for the slices — and the exam loves asking you to keep those doors apart.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: the tax slate read both ways, the two dividends counted on the right number of slices, and fees kept separate from dividends. Master these transactions — the financial statements ahead are built directly on top of them.
