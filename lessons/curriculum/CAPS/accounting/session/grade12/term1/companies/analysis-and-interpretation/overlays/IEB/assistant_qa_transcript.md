**Mandy:** The class questions are in — first one: ROSHE used profit AFTER tax but ROTCE used profit BEFORE tax plus the interest. Why do the two returns get different profit figures? It feels like the lesson switched rules halfway.

**Tutor:** The rule never switched — each return uses the profit that belongs to the audience it serves, and once you see the audiences the choice becomes obvious. ROSHE answers the shareholders, and the only profit that is truly theirs is what remains after every other claimant has been satisfied: after the bank took its interest and after SARS took its tax. Feeding a before-tax figure into ROSHE would promise shareholders money that was never going to reach them. ROTCE answers a different question entirely: how good is this business at using long-term money, full stop? The pot it measures was filled by two funders — owners and lenders — so the output must be counted before either funder's reward is subtracted. That is why the interest of R120 000 is added back: it is not a cost of running the butchery's engine, it is one funder's share of the output, and stripping it out first would count the lenders' money in the bottom of the fraction while excluding their earnings from the top. Tax is excluded for a similar reason — it is calculated after interest, so it is entangled with the financing decision. Here is the test to remember: match the top of the fraction to the bottom. Equity at the bottom, owners' profit at the top. All capital at the bottom, all-funders' profit at the top. Mismatch the two and the ratio answers no one's question.

***

**Mandy:** Next: the lesson said average equity — opening plus closing divided by two. Why the average? The closing equity is right there on the balance sheet; using it would be quicker.

**Tutor:** Quicker, and quietly unfair to the ratio. Think about what the fraction claims to measure: the profit a pool of money generated over a YEAR. The profit accumulated across all twelve months — but the pool itself was not the same size all year. Bokamoso's equity started at R2 840 000 and ended at R3 000 000, partly because retained profit was flowing in along the way. If you divide the year's profit by the closing figure, you are pretending the company had the full R3 000 000 working from the first of March, which it did not — and the pretence always flatters or punishes in a predictable direction. A company that raised fresh capital in the last month would see its return unfairly crushed by a big closing denominator; one that bought back shares late in the year would look artificially brilliant. The average of opening and closing is a rough-but-honest estimate of the money actually at work across the year, and the same logic drives average stock in the turnover rate and average debtors in the collection period. In an answer, the safest habit is to name your denominator — say "on average equity of R2 920 000" — so your reasoning is visible. And if a question gives you only the closing figure, use it and move on; the principle matters, but so does working with the information actually supplied.

**Mandy:** Third question, and it caused a proper debate: the pay-out rate is nearly eighty percent, and someone said that proves the directors have run out of ideas — a growing company would keep the money. Is that fair?

**Tutor:** It is a sharp observation, and half right, which makes it the perfect interpretation question. The reasoning goes: money retained can be reinvested at ROTCE, eighteen percent, so a board with profitable projects should keep more of the seventy-three cents and pay out less. A pay-out near eighty percent can indeed signal a business that has more cash than opportunities — mature, steady, short of new frontiers. But hold on before writing the directors off. First, look at who owns the company: if its shareholder base bought precisely because they want income — pensioners, trusts living on dividends — a high, reliable pay-out IS the strategy, and cutting the dividend would drive those owners away and dent the share price. Second, check whether the retained fifteen cents per share is actually enough: on six hundred thousand shares that is R90 000 a year staying in, which alongside borrowing capacity — remember, gearing is modest at 0,35 to 1 and returns beat the cost of debt — may fund all the expansion the company can sensibly manage. Third, watch the pattern over years, not one snapshot: a pay-out that leaps around signals a board with no policy at all, which worries analysts more than any particular level. So the fair answer runs: the eighty percent is defensible for an income-focused shareholder base with limited expansion needs, and questionable for a company claiming aggressive growth plans — then pick the reading the scenario supports. That IS the skill: not the number, the argument.

**Mandy:** Last one: if the market price of 620 cents already tells us what the share is worth, why compute NAV at all? Nobody selling their shares would accept 500 when the market pays 620.

**Tutor:** Correct — nobody should sell at NAV while the market pays more, and yet NAV remains one of the most useful numbers on the page, because its job is not to set the price. Its job is to be the anchor the price is judged AGAINST. Think of the two numbers as answering different questions. NAV answers: what would each share collect if the company stopped, sold its recorded assets and settled its debts — the floor built from verifiable figures. The market price answers: what will a stranger pay today for this share's future — a number built from expectation, mood and momentum. The GAP between them is where the information lives. A price twenty-four percent above NAV, like Bokamoso's, tells you the market believes the company will create value beyond its recorded assets — reasonable, while returns hold above the cost of capital. A price BELOW NAV is a market saying the assets will shrink in these directors' hands, or a market that has not looked properly — and value-hunting investors screen for exactly that discount. NAV also earns its keep in the ugly scenarios: in a liquidation it approximates what shareholders might salvage, and in a takeover bid it is the seller's first bargaining line. And notice its discipline for your own answers: NAV moves slowly, built from audited figures, while price moves daily on sentiment — so when a question shows the two diverging sharply over time, the honest analyst asks which one has lost touch with reality. Keep both numbers, in cents, side by side; the comparison is the analysis.
