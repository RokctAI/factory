**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First: why does the buy-back use the AVERAGE issue price? The shares the company bought back were probably the R6,00 ones from the new issue — why not use what those specific shares were sold for?

**Tutor:** Because once shares are issued, they lose their individual history. Every ordinary share is identical to every other ordinary share — same rights, same vote, same dividend — and the share capital account holds one pooled amount for all of them, not a filing cabinet of separate prices. When Thabiso Limited's account shows R3 800 000 for 800 000 shares, there is no meaningful sense in which any particular share "is" a R4,00 share or a R6,00 share; each one simply represents one eight-hundred-thousandth of the pool. So when 50 000 shares are cancelled, the only defensible amount to remove from the pool is 50 000 times the pooled average of R4,75 — that is R237 500, the portion of contributed capital those shares represent. Anything paid beyond that is not a return of capital at all; it is value the company built up through trading, which lives in retained income, and that is why the excess of R112 500 is debited there. There is also a practical impossibility behind the principle: shares trade between investors on the JSE without the company even knowing, so the person selling back to the company may never have paid the company anything — they bought from another shareholder. The company cannot trace what it originally received for those particular certificates, and the average price is the honest, auditable substitute.

***

**Mandy:** Next: the interim dividend was PAID but the final dividend was only DECLARED. Why does the company even split its dividend into two like that, and why does the final one sit as a liability?

**Tutor:** Take the timing question first. Directors declare an interim dividend part-way through the year as a signal of confidence and a way of giving shareholders income during the year — but they keep it modest, because the year is not finished and prudence says do not distribute profits you have not yet confirmed. The final dividend is declared at year-end, once the full profit is known and the directors can judge what the company can afford after tax and after its expansion plans. That is why the final dividend is usually the larger one. Now the liability. The moment the directors formally declare a dividend, the company is legally bound to pay it — declaration converts a discretionary hope into an enforceable debt. But declaration happens on the last day of the financial year, and the actual payment run — checking the share register, paying each shareholder — happens weeks into the new year. So on the balance sheet date the company owes 750 000 shareholders 40 cents a share, R300 000, and owing something you have not yet paid is the definition of a liability: Shareholders for Dividends, under current liabilities. The interim dividend never appears there, because it was declared and paid within the same year — the cash already left. In the appropriation section, though, BOTH dividends appear, because both come out of this year's profits. Paid or owing describes the balance sheet; declared describes the appropriation.

**Mandy:** This one caused an argument: if the company still owes SARS R28 000, does that mean somebody did the provisional tax wrong? Should the two provisional payments not add up to the final tax exactly?

**Tutor:** No error at all — a gap is the normal outcome, and understanding why teaches you what provisional tax actually is. The final income tax can only be computed once the year is over and the profit is known: 27 percent of R1 400 000 is R378 000, and nobody knows the R1 400 000 until the books close. Provisional tax is the law's answer to a cash-flow problem: SARS does not want to wait a year and more for its money, so it requires the company to pay during the year based on ESTIMATES of the profit — one payment mid-year, one at year-end, each a best guess made with the information available at the time. Thabiso's guesses totalled R350 000 against a final answer of R378 000, so the slate shows R28 000 still owing, a current liability, settled when the assessment is finalised. Had trading slowed after the estimates were made, the payments might have exceeded the final tax, and the same account would show a debit balance — SARS owing the company a refund, a current asset. Examiners flip that direction deliberately, so read the account rather than assuming. What WOULD suggest wrongdoing is a deliberate, gross underestimate to hold onto cash — SARS charges penalties and interest for that, which is why honest estimation is itself an ethics point in this topic.

**Mandy:** Last one: my cousin owns shares in a company and says the share price on the JSE went up, but the company's share capital account did not change. How can the price move without the books moving?

**Tutor:** Because after the issue, the company is no longer part of the transaction. Distinguish the two markets. When the company ISSUES shares, money flows from the buyer into the company's bank account, and share capital rises — that is the only moment the company's books record a share price. Afterwards, those shares trade between investors on the JSE: your cousin bought from some other shareholder, and if she sells at a higher price, the profit is hers and the money passes from the new buyer to her. The company receives nothing, pays nothing, and records nothing — only the name in its share register changes. The JSE price is simply the constantly renegotiated answer to the question, what will a stranger pay today for a slice of this company's future profits? It moves with results, dividends, news and mood, thousands of times a day, while the share capital account records a historical fact: what the company itself received when it created the shares. The two numbers touch only when the company returns to the market — a fresh issue at the current price, or a buy-back like Thabiso's, where the company had to pay the going R7,00 for shares whose pooled history was R4,75. That gap between market price and book records, by the way, is exactly where the net asset value comparison in the analysis lesson picks up the story.
