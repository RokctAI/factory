**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First: the dividends confusion. The final dividend appears in the appropriation account AND as a current liability, but NOT in the cash flow statement — while last year's final dividend appears in the cash flow statement but nowhere else. Can you untangle that once and for all?

**Tutor:** The untangling tool is one question asked three times: what is each statement trying to measure? The appropriation account measures what happened to THIS YEAR'S profit, so it shows every dividend declared out of this year's earnings — the interim of R120 000 and the final of R150 000, R270 000 in total — regardless of whether the cash has moved. The statement of financial position measures what is OWED on the last day, so it shows only the final dividend of R150 000, because that is the one declared but unpaid; the interim was settled in August and is nobody's liability anymore. The cash flow statement measures what the BANK experienced, so it shows only rands that actually left: last year's final dividend of R130 000, which was a liability at the previous year-end and was paid early in this year, plus this year's interim of R120 000 — R250 000 of real outflow. This year's final dividend has moved no cash and therefore cannot appear there; it will appear in NEXT year's cash flow statement, when it is paid. Notice the machinery: each year's final dividend is declared in one year's appropriation, sits as a liability at that year-end, and flows through the following year's cash flow statement. Once you see that conveyor belt, every dividend question becomes a matter of asking which statement you are standing in.

**Mandy:** Next: why does the cash flow statement start from net profit before tax and then ADD BACK depreciation and interest? Adding back interest especially feels wrong — the company really did pay that interest.

**Tutor:** Both add-backs exist for the same reason: the starting point is contaminated, and the statement cleans it in two steps rather than one. Net profit before tax is an accountant's number, built on matching, and it contains items that do not describe cash. Depreciation is the pure case: R180 000 was subtracted in computing profit, yet no rand left the bank — the cash left years ago when the asset was bought, and the cash flow statement recorded it under investing in THAT year. Counting it again now would double-count, so it is added back, permanently. Interest is subtler, and you are right that it was genuinely paid — which is exactly why it reappears three lines later as interest paid, R75 000. The reason for the add-back-then-deduct dance is presentation: the statement wants to show cash generated from OPERATIONS — the pure trading engine — before separately disclosing the cost of servicing debt. Burying interest inside the operating figure would hide a number that lenders and analysts specifically want to see on its own line. So interest is lifted out of the profit figure, the operating engine is measured clean, and then interest paid is shown explicitly as its own outflow. The net arithmetic is identical; the disclosure is far more informative. A useful self-check: depreciation is added back and never returns, because it is not cash; interest is added back and always returns as a separate payment line, because it is cash that deserves its own spotlight.

**Mandy:** This one caused an argument: how can tax PAID be R140 000 when the tax CHARGE for the year is R145 800? Someone insisted those must always be the same number.

**Tutor:** They are almost never the same number, and the gap between them is precisely what the SARS slate on the balance sheet records. The charge of R145 800 is an accrual figure: 27 percent of this year's profit, matched to this year regardless of payment dates. The paid figure of R140 000 is a bank figure: rands that actually flowed to SARS during these twelve months. Rebuild it. The company opened the year owing SARS R22 000 — last year's unsettled balance — and paid that early in the year. During the year it made provisional payments of R118 000 against the current year's liability. Total cash to SARS: R140 000. Meanwhile the year's charge came to R145 800, of which only R118 000 was covered by the provisional payments, leaving R27 800 owing at year-end. Confirm with the account's own arithmetic: opening balance R22 000, plus the charge of R145 800, less payments of R140 000, equals the closing balance of R27 800. It reconciles perfectly — the three figures are different because they answer different questions: what does this year owe in principle, what moved through the bank, and what remains on the slate. The examination's favourite trick is to give you any two of charge, paid and balances, and ask for the third, so practise walking that account in both directions.

**Mandy:** Last one: if the auditor only gives reasonable assurance and only tests samples, what is the point? Could a fraud not simply slip through the sample?

**Tutor:** It could, and auditors have missed frauds — the honest starting point is that an audit is not a guarantee and was never priced as one. Checking every transaction of a company with millions of them would cost more than the company earns, so the profession's answer is disciplined sampling: the auditor studies the company's internal controls first, identifies where misstatement is most LIKELY — high-value items, unusual entries, areas where controls are weak — and concentrates testing there, rather than sampling blindly. Reasonable assurance means the auditor has done enough targeted work that a MATERIAL misstatement — one big enough to change a reader's decision — would probably have been detected. Why is that still valuable? Three reasons. First, deterrence: managers who know an independent examiner will probe the records behave differently all year; the audit works even on the days the auditor is absent. Second, the signal: a qualified opinion, a disclaimer or an adverse opinion is a public alarm that no internal memo could sound, and banks, investors and SARS all read it. Third, accountability has an address: a registered auditor signs the report, and a negligent audit carries professional and legal consequences — that signature is skin in the game. What the audit cannot replace is the company's own internal controls and internal audit function; the external audit is the final check on a system, not a substitute for the system. That division of labour — internal controls daily, internal audit continuously, external audit annually — is exactly the framework the term 2 work builds on.
