**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First: the practice run kept saying carry your own figure forward without apology. But if my net profit after tax is wrong, surely everything built on it is wrong too — how do the markers actually handle that?

**Tutor:** Through the follow-on principle, and understanding it changes how you spend your examination. When your net profit after tax is wrong — say you calculated R500 000 instead of R525 600 — the marker checks your retained income note using YOUR R500 000. If your method is right — opening balance, plus your profit, minus the buy-back premium, minus your dividends — every method mark survives. The error is punished once, at its birthplace, not at every reappearance. That is deliberate marking policy: the paper tests whether you know how the statements connect, and punishing one slip five times would test luck instead. Now the practical consequences. First, never abandon a question because an early figure feels doubtful — the downstream marks are still fully available. Second, never leave a working invisible: a bare wrong answer earns nothing, but the same wrong answer with its method displayed earns almost everything. Third, when a total refuses to balance, resist the urge to force it — a fabricated figure with no working is the one thing follow-on cannot rescue. And one caution: follow-on protects method, not impossibility. If your carried figure produces something absurd — negative share capital, dividends exceeding equity — a marker expects you to flag it: one sentence, this figure appears unreasonable, continuing with method. That sentence signals command of the subject even in the middle of an error, and examiners reward command.

**Mandy:** Next: in the buy-back, the share capital note shows R212 500 but the cash flow shows R252 500, and the R40 000 premium hides in retained income. Three different numbers for one transaction — how do we keep them straight under pressure?

**Tutor:** By remembering that the three statements ask three different questions of the same event, and each number answers its own statement's question. The cash flow statement asks: how much money left the bank? R252 500 — fifty thousand shares at the full R5,05 paid. Cash is indifferent to our bookkeeping subdivisions; the financing outflow is the whole price. The share capital note asks: how much of the pot of contributed capital was returned? The average issue price measures what the pot holds per share — R4,25 — so fifty thousand shares take out R212 500. The note cannot show more, because the pot never contained the premium. The retained income note asks: what happened to accumulated profits? The 80 cents per share paid above the average is profit the company had retained on those shares, now finally distributed — R40 000 out. So the exam technique is a three-line check you can run in seconds: capital portion plus premium must equal cash paid. Two hundred and twelve and a half plus forty is two hundred and fifty-two and a half — locked. If your three numbers fail that addition, one of them is wrong, and you know before the marker does. The deeper insight, worth carrying into the analysis question: after any buy-back, equity falls, shares in issue fall, and earnings per share rises with no improvement in actual profit — which is exactly why examiners follow a buy-back question with an interpret-the-EPS-trend question. The transaction is one story; the statements are three witnesses telling it from three seats.

**Mandy:** Third: the analysis question compared two companies, and the answer said there is no wrong company to choose. That feels dangerously loose — how is such a question actually marked?

**Tutor:** With a rubric that pays for reasoning structure, not for the verdict — and once you see the rubric, the question becomes the most predictable on the paper. A typical six-mark investment-choice question wants: at least two relevant indicators QUOTED with figures, a genuine COMPARISON — against the other company, an interest rate, a norm or a trend — and a JUDGEMENT that follows logically, usually with one risk acknowledged. Choose Protea: return on equity 16,8 percent against Aloe's 9,2, positive gearing at 15,4 against an 11 percent loan rate, market confidence in the 230-cent premium to net asset value — but acknowledge the tighter liquidity at 1,4 and the higher risk in 0,7 gearing. Full marks. Choose Aloe: the argument must work harder, but it exists — a discount to net asset value means buying assets below book, room to gear up later if returns improve, fortress liquidity — while conceding the returns currently trail a simple fixed deposit. Also full marks, if argued. What earns zero: a verdict with no figures, figures with no comparison, or comparison with no conclusion — and the classic half-mark tragedy, listing every ratio the formula sheet offers without selecting the relevant ones. Selection is itself assessed: gearing questions want return on capital against the loan rate; shareholder questions want return on equity, earnings and dividends; liquidity questions want the ratios plus the cycle periods. Read the scenario, pick the indicators that speak to it, and argue like the money is yours.

**Mandy:** Last one: everyone treats the audit and governance question as leftover marks for waffle. What separates a full-mark governance answer from a padded one?

**Tutor:** Specificity — every sentence must name a mechanism, a party or a consequence, because the padding detector in every marking guideline is the answer that could be written without reading the scenario. Take the classic: the CEO chairs the audit committee. A padded answer says this is bad corporate governance and unfair — two sentences, zero marks, because nothing was explained. A full-mark answer names the mechanism and the failure: the audit committee exists to oversee the integrity of financial reporting on behalf of the BOARD and shareholders; the CEO is the senior person whose management the committee is checking; chairing it means overseeing scrutiny of himself, so independence collapses — and the fix is a non-executive, preferably independent, chair. Three sentences, every mark. The pattern generalises. Directors' pay rising while earnings fall: name the comparison — remuneration against earnings per share — name the forum, the annual general meeting, where shareholders vote and question; name the committee, remuneration, that should have prevented it. Insider trading: name the offence, trading on price-sensitive information the market lacks; name it as illegal, not merely unethical; name the damage — investors lose faith that the market is fair. And anchor everything in the King Code's four demands: responsibility, accountability, fairness, transparency — not recited as decoration, but attached: chairing your own oversight fails accountability; trading ahead of results fails fairness and transparency. Scenario, mechanism, consequence, remedy. Governance marks are not soft — they are the fastest marks on the paper for the candidate who answers the actual story.
