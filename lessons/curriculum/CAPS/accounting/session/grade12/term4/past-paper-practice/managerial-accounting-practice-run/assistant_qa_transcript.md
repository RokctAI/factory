**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First: the practice run said the unit cost fell from R64 to R60 and warned us not to just cheer. What are the possible readings, and how does a marker want them separated?

**Tutor:** The warning exists because a falling unit cost has three very different causes, and only some of them deserve applause. Split the R60 into its layers. The variable layer — materials and labour per unit — is the true efficiency signal, because it SHOULD stay constant per unit: if materials dropped from R26 to R24 a unit, someone negotiated better prices, cut waste, or reduced theft on the factory floor — genuine management achievement, quote it and praise it. The fixed layer is where the illusion lives: R348 000 of overheads spread over twenty-one thousand units adds about R16,57 each, but the same overheads over eighteen thousand units last year added over R19. If the entire improvement comes from producing MORE, efficiency never changed — the rent just found more shoulders. And the third reading is the dangerous one: unit cost can fall because quality was cut — cheaper thread, thinner soles — which buys a lovely statement this year and returns as complaints and lost sales next year. So the full-mark answer does the split: compare variable cost per unit across the two years for real efficiency; attribute the fixed-cost dilution to volume; and raise the quality question if the paper hints at returns or complaints. One number, three stories, and the marker's grid usually has a mark waiting for each story. That is also why the production cost statement asks for the notes separately — they are the evidence file for exactly this argument.

**Mandy:** Next: in the collection schedule, why do we build the whole table from the SALES months instead of just working out each receipt month directly? Half the class tangled August and October.

**Tutor:** Because every rand a debtor pays is named after the month it was SOLD, not the month it arrives — and anchoring on sale months is the only structure that survives pressure. Think of each month's credit sales as a small cohort that ages on a fixed schedule. August's credit sales of R108 000 are one cohort: half of it arrives in September, forty-five percent in October, five percent never comes. September's R120 000 is the next cohort on the same schedule, one month behind. When the paper asks for October's receipts, you do not reason forward from October — you ask which cohorts have an instalment falling due in October: September's cohort pays its fifty percent, R60 000; August's cohort pays its forty-five percent, R48 600; and October's own credit cohort pays nothing yet, because its first instalment only lands in November. Add October's cash sales of R88 000 and the month is complete at R196 600. The tangling happens when candidates apply percentages to the RECEIPT month's sales — fifty percent of October — which double-counts the young cohort and starves the old ones. On paper, draw the grid: sales months down the side, receipt months across the top, and each cohort's instalments march diagonally. The five percent bad debts simply never get a column — and check the discipline: fifty plus forty-five plus five is one hundred, so if your collection percentages total a hundred INSIDE the budget, you have banked money the question told you is dead. The grid is a small piece of drawing that protects ten marks of schedule.

**Mandy:** Third: in the reconciliation, the R300 overcharge got corrected on the statement side, but our own missing debit note on our side. The rule each side fixes its own errors — what is the reasoning underneath, and does it ever bend?

**Tutor:** The reasoning is about whose books misrepresent reality, because a reconciliation is two records of ONE relationship, and each correction must land where the record is false. The supplier overcharged R300 against the agreed price: reality says we owe the agreed amount, their statement says more, so their document is the false one — adjust the statement copy in our working and, in practice, request a credit note; our ledger was never wrong, so touching it would CREATE an error. The unrecorded debit note is the mirror image: we returned goods, reality shrank our debt, the supplier may even have processed it already — but OUR ledger still shows the old figure, so ours is the false record and ours gets the entry. Timing differences are the third category, and they are nobody's error: our last-day payment of R12 000 is real and recorded by us, just not yet by them — it sits as a reconciling item on the statement side of the working and dissolves next month on its own. Does the rule bend? Only in appearance. If the question says the invoice on the statement is for goods we will receive and keep, the debt is real and coming — still a statement-side timing item this month, a ledger entry next month when goods land. What never bends is this: a ledger entry is only ever made for something that genuinely changes OUR position — an omission, a duplication, a misposting in our books. The examiner's favourite trap is tempting you to fix their error in your ledger; the mark is for refusing. And the reason businesses do this monthly is internal control: the reconciliation is where duplicated invoices, missed returns and fraud surface while the trail is still warm.

**Mandy:** Last one: the ethics answer named SARS, the bank and profit-sharing as victims of the two-till scheme. Why does the marking want victims listed — is dishonest and illegal not enough?

**Tutor:** It is not enough, because dishonest and illegal is a verdict, and Grade 12 ethics questions assess whether you can trace HARM through the accounting system — that is what makes it an accounting question rather than a general knowledge one. Walk the second till. First victim: SARS, and through SARS the public. The fifteen percent VAT charged at that till was never the shop's money — the vendor is a collection agent, holding it in trust. Diverting it is not aggressive tax planning; it is spending the country's hospitals-and-schools money. Naming the TRUST relationship is usually a dedicated mark. Second circle of victims: everyone who reads the understated statements. The bank sees weaker sales and prices the loan for a riskier business, or declines it. A partner or minority shareholder paid a share of declared profit is directly robbed by the hidden till. SARS again on income tax, since understated sales understate profit at twenty-seven percent — the fraud is always double. Third: the business itself. The scheme requires a corrupted record, so the owner loses the one thing accounting exists to provide — a trustworthy mirror. Stock counts stop agreeing with sales, honest staff learn dishonesty is policy, and the auditor's trail is broken, which courts read as intent. Then the consequences: penalties and interest, criminal prosecution, deregistration. Structure every ethics answer that way — the rule broken, the trust relationship, the named victims, the consequence — four sentences, and each one is typically a mark. Moral outrage is free; traced harm is what the paper pays for.
