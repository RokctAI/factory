# Part 1 — Expert

This session is a full practice run of Paper 1, Financial Reporting and Evaluation — one hundred and fifty marks of company accounting, walked question by question the way a marker reads it. The questions are exam-style, set to the official guideline: statements, notes, cash flow, analysis, audit and governance. One company, Amanzi Limited, carries the recording questions; a two-company comparison carries the analysis. Every step is spoken with its marks attached, because pacing an examination is mark arithmetic: effort spent in proportion to the marks on offer, and the workings always shown, since the method mark survives even when the answer dies.

## Subtopic: Question One — Concepts and the Asset Disposal

Papers open gently: four concept marks, one each, matching statements to terms. Practise the pairs that recur. The going-concern assumption: statements are prepared expecting the business to continue — one mark. Prudence: never overstate income or assets — one mark. Materiality: information that would change a user's decision must be shown separately — one mark. IFRS: the international standards listed companies follow — one mark. Four marks in four sentences; bank them fast.

Then the warm-up working: an asset disposal, usually eight marks. A delivery vehicle, cost R180 000, accumulated depreciation of R100 800 at the start of the year, is sold three-quarters through the year. Step one, current depreciation: twenty percent on cost is R36 000 a year, but only nine months have run — R27 000. Two marks: one for the rate on cost, one for the time apportionment, and the time fraction is where the paper harvests wrong answers. Step two, total accumulated depreciation at sale: 100 800 plus 27 000 — R127 800, one mark. Step three, carrying value: 180 000 minus 127 800 — R52 200, one mark. The guideline fixes Grade 12 disposals in financial statements context at carrying value, so the selling price is R52 200, and the remaining marks sit in the ledger presentation: cost out of the asset account, accumulated depreciation closed off, bank in. Eight marks, no surprises, provided the nine-twelfths reflex fires.

## Subtopic: The Income Statement Question

The big recording question — call it thirty-five marks. Amanzi Limited, year-end results needing adjustments, and the marker follows your adjustments one by one.

Rent income first. The account shows R78 000 received, but the tenant has paid thirteen months. Divide by thirteen — R6 000 a month — so the year's true rent income is R72 000, and R6 000 is income received in advance, a current liability. Two marks: the thirteen-month insight, the R6 000 deferral.

Insurance: R14 400 covers twelve months, but three of those months belong to next year. Prepaid expense: R3 600, current asset; this year's expense drops to R10 800. Two marks, and the direction matters — students who ADD the prepayment lose both.

Audit fees: the auditors have completed the work, the fee of R30 000 is unpaid and unrecorded. Accrue it: expense up, accrued expenses up. One mark, pure vigilance.

Depreciation: vehicles R80 000, equipment R25 000 — R105 000 total, usually three or four marks of rate-times-base workings, each asset class its own mark.

Interest on loan: the loan of R600 000 at ten percent should cost R60 000, but only R55 000 was recorded. Accrue R5 000 more. Two marks: one for the ten-percent expectation, one for the shortfall.

Now the two lines every Paper 1 ends with. Net profit before tax: R720 000 after all adjustments. Income tax at twenty-seven percent: R194 400 — one mark, and it must be twenty-seven, the company rate. Net profit after tax: R525 600 — one mark, carried forward mercilessly into the retained income note, which is why an error here bleeds; but the marking principle of carrying forward means your FOLLOW-ON work still earns marks if the method stands. Show every working.

## Subtopic: The Share Capital and Retained Income Notes

Two notes, roughly eighteen marks, and they tell the year's ownership story.

Ordinary share capital. Opening: seven hundred thousand shares that raised R2 800 000 — average R4,00. During the year, one hundred thousand new shares at R6,00: R600 000 — one mark for the multiplication, one for adding it in. Subtotal: eight hundred thousand shares, R3 400 000, new average R4,25 — one mark, and say the average aloud because the buy-back depends on it. Then the directors repurchase fifty thousand shares at R5,05. The note shows the capital portion only: fifty thousand times the R4,25 average — R212 500 out. Two marks. Closing: seven hundred and fifty thousand shares, R3 187 500 — one mark for the balance.

Retained income, five lines. Opening balance R410 000 — given. Add net profit after tax: R525 600, carried from the income statement — one mark, follow-on protected. Subtract the buy-back premium: fifty thousand shares times the 80 cents paid above average — R40 000 — two marks, and this is the line that separates prepared candidates, because the premium lives HERE, not in share capital. Subtract dividends: interim of 30 cents on eight hundred thousand shares, R240 000; final of 40 cents on seven hundred and fifty thousand shares — after the buy-back — R300 000; total R540 000 — three marks, one per calculation, one for the share counts being different, which is precisely the trap. Closing balance: 410 000 plus 525 600 minus 40 000 minus 540 000 — R355 600, one mark. Shareholders' equity now reads R3 187 500 plus R355 600 — R3 543 100.

## Subtopic: The Balance Sheet Question

The statement of financial position, perhaps twenty-five marks, is an exercise in filing under pressure. Speak the skeleton: non-current assets, current assets; then equity, non-current liabilities, current liabilities — and the two totals must agree.

Amanzi's equity side is already built: R3 543 100. Non-current liabilities: the loan, R600 000 — but check the paper for the portion repayable within twelve months, which shifts to current. Current liabilities, and here the adjustments come home to roost: trade creditors R255 900; accrued expenses R35 000 — the audit fee and the loan interest from the income statement question; income received in advance R6 000 — the tenant's extra month; SARS for income tax R14 400 — the charge of R194 400 less provisional payments of R180 000; shareholders for dividends R300 000 — the final dividend, declared but unpaid. Total current liabilities: R611 300. Each correctly placed item is a mark, and each is a mark BECAUSE it was an adjustment — the paper tests whether your adjustments landed on both sides of the accounting equation.

Total equity and liabilities: 3 543 100 plus 600 000 plus 611 300 — R4 754 400. The asset side must meet it: fixed assets at carrying value R3 840 000; inventories R420 000; trade and other receivables R380 000, which includes the prepaid insurance of R3 600; cash R114 400. Total: R4 754 400. Balance. If it does not balance, do not go hunting — rule it off, move on, and let the follow-on marks protect you; the balancing figure itself is rarely worth more than one mark, and the hunt costs marks elsewhere.

## Subtopic: The Cash Flow Statement Question

Cash flow, around twenty marks, is three workings and a map. The marker awards the workings even when the final statement stumbles, so speak them as small ledgers.

Tax paid. Charge R194 400; owed to SARS at the start R9 600; owed at the end R14 400. Paid: 194 400 plus 9 600 minus 14 400 — R189 600. Three marks: opening, closing, direction.

Dividends paid. The interim of R240 000 left in cash this year; last year's final of R260 000, sitting in the opening shareholders for dividends, was paid this year too. Total: R500 000. This year's final of R300 000 stays behind as a liability. Three marks, all direction marks.

Fixed assets purchased. Opening carrying value R3 400 000, plus purchases, minus depreciation R105 000, minus the disposal at carrying value R95 000, equals closing R3 840 000. Solve: purchases R640 000. Four marks, and the equation form is the safest route — the marker can see every component.

Financing section: proceeds of the share issue, R600 000 in; buy-back of shares, R252 500 out — the FULL price paid, capital plus premium, because cash left in full. Two marks, and the buy-back at full price catches everyone who forgets that the R40 000 premium was a retained income story, not a cash story. Interpretation closes the question: this company raised equity, returned some to shareholders, and invested R640 000 in capacity — one sentence, one mark, easy money.

## Subtopic: The Analysis and Interpretation Question

Thirty-plus marks, and the formula sheet is provided — so the marks are for choosing indicators and arguing, not remembering. Two companies, same industry. Protea Limited: debt-equity 0,7 to 1; return on total capital employed 15,4 percent; return on equity 16,8 percent; current ratio 1,4 to 1; share price 850 cents against net asset value of 620 cents. Aloe Limited: debt-equity 0,1 to 1; return on total capital 9,6 percent; return on equity 9,2 percent; current ratio 3,8 to 1; price 510 cents against net asset value of 560 cents. Loans cost eleven percent.

Gearing, usually six marks. Protea borrows at eleven and earns 15,4 — positive gearing, each borrowed rand clears 4,4 cents, which is why its return on equity beats its return on capital. Quote both figures, compare to eleven, judge: Protea's gearing is justified though risk rises with the 0,7 ratio. Aloe earns 9,6 against the same eleven — borrowing would destroy value, so its timid 0,1 gearing is wise, not weak. Marks fall one per quoted figure, one per comparison, one per judgement.

Liquidity, four marks: Protea at 1,4 is tight but workable; Aloe at 3,8 is not safe, it is lazy — money sleeping in stock and debtors that could fund expansion or dividends. The counter-intuitive judgement is the mark.

The market's verdict, four marks: Protea trades 230 cents above its net asset value — confidence in its future; Aloe trades 50 cents BELOW — the market doubts its assets will earn their keep, though a bidder might smell a bargain. Same units, cents against cents, always.

Then the shareholder question: which company would you invest in? There is no wrong company — there are only argued and unargued answers. Two indicators quoted, one risk acknowledged, a conclusion: full marks.

## Subtopic: The Audit Report and Governance Question

The paper closes with the published-report question — twelve to fifteen marks of reading, not arithmetic.

The audit report first. Know the three examinable opinions as verdicts: unqualified — the statements fairly present, in all material respects; qualified — fairly present EXCEPT FOR stated matters; disclaimer or adverse — the auditor cannot, or will not, vouch for them. One mark each when defined, two when applied to a scenario. Know the addressee: the report speaks to the SHAREHOLDERS, because the auditors work for the owners, not the directors they are checking. Know what the audit fee in the income statement implies: an external, independent examination was bought — and if the same firm also runs the company's bookkeeping, independence is compromised, which is a classic two-mark scenario.

Governance scenarios: a CEO who chairs the audit committee — concentration of power, no independent oversight; directors' remuneration rising while earnings fall — a question for the annual general meeting; a director trading shares before results are released — insider trading, illegal, and the ethics marks want the word. The King Code's four demands — responsibility, accountability, fairness, transparency — map onto almost any scenario the paper invents.

Final discipline for the whole paper, spoken once: attempt every part, show every working, carry your own figures forward without apology, and spend marks' worth of effort, not affection's worth, on each question. One hundred and fifty marks, walked. That is the practice run.
