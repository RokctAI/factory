**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we finish! First: the factory rent is fixed, but it sits inside factory overheads in the production cost statement, which changes the unit cost when production changes. How can a FIXED cost make the UNIT cost move?

**Tutor:** Because the two documents ask different questions of the same rand. The production cost statement asks: what did it cost, in total, to make everything we made? Rent goes in whole — R300 000 of overheads whether Thola makes ten thousand overalls or fourteen thousand. But the unit cost divides that total by the units produced, and a fixed number divided by a changing number moves: at twelve thousand units the overheads add R25 to each overall; at fifteen thousand they add only R20. That is why unit cost FALLS as production rises — the same rent is spread across more shoulders — and it is one of the examiner's favourite interpretation questions: unit cost improved, was management efficient? Answer carefully: the variable cost per unit is the efficiency signal, because it should stay constant; if materials per unit dropped, someone negotiated or wasted less. The fixed cost per unit falling may just mean the factory ran busier, no skill involved. Break-even analysis avoids the whole confusion by refusing to spread fixed costs at all: it keeps them as one lump, works in contribution per unit, and that is why the two calculations use the same ingredients but must never be mixed. The trap in the paper is exactly that mixture — using the R95 full unit cost, fixed portion included, as the variable cost in a break-even formula. Contribution uses variable costs only, always.

**Mandy:** Next: in the collection schedule, September collects money from July and August. But the two percent bad debts confused everyone — where exactly do they go, and why do they never appear in the cash budget?

**Tutor:** Follow the money — or in this case, follow its absence. A cash budget is a forecast of the BANK ACCOUNT: every line must be a rand physically arriving or leaving in that month. Now ask what a bad debt does to the bank account: nothing. No rand arrives — that is the whole tragedy of it — and no rand leaves either; the business simply stops expecting the money. An event with no cash movement can never earn a line in a cash forecast. So where does the two percent live? Two places. First, invisibly, inside the collection schedule itself: the percentages collected add up to ninety-eight, not one hundred — thirty plus fifty plus eighteen — and the missing two percent IS the bad debt, already excluded from every month's receipts. Candidates who force collections to total one hundred percent have quietly resurrected debts the question killed. Second, visibly, in the projected income statement, where bad debts appear as an expense in the month the sales were made or written off, because that document matches losses to the trading that caused them, cash or no cash. The same logic sorts every item the examiner throws at the boundary: depreciation, income statement only, no cash moves; a loan received, cash only, no income earned; discount allowed, income statement — and note it also shrinks the cash actually collected. One question, always: did money move this month? Yes — budget. No — statement.

**Mandy:** Third: in the creditors' reconciliation we subtracted the R9 200 invoice from the STATEMENT because the goods arrive next month. But surely the supplier is right — they did send the invoice. Why do we get to remove it?

**Tutor:** We do not remove it because the supplier is wrong; we remove it because the two documents are photographs taken from different sides of a moving parcel. The supplier records a sale when goods LEAVE their store — their invoice is dated the twenty-ninth, honestly. We record a purchase when goods REACH ours — and on the thirty-first, nothing has arrived, so our ledger honestly shows no such debt. Both books are correct about their own side of the journey; the reconciliation's job is to prove the difference is explainable, not to declare a winner. So on the working, the invoice is deducted from the statement balance for THIS month's comparison, and next month, when the flour lands and we record it, the difference dissolves by itself — that is the definition of a timing difference. Contrast that with a genuine error, like our invoice posted twice at R4 800: that is not timing, it will never dissolve on its own, and it gets corrected in the ledger of the side that made it. That is the whole discipline in one sentence: timing differences wait, errors get fixed, and each side fixes only its own. Why do examiners love this topic? Internal control. A business that reconciles monthly catches duplicated invoices, missed discounts and even fraud within thirty days; a business that just pays whatever statement arrives is an open till. The reconciliation is not bookkeeping housekeeping — it is the audit you perform on your own suppliers.

**Mandy:** Last one: the drawings adjustment adds VAT owed, but the bad debts adjustment subtracts, and half the class just memorises the directions. Is there one piece of logic that gives both without memorising?

**Tutor:** There is, and it is the input-output logic itself: SARS only ever finances VAT on goods that end up SOLD to a customer, and only ever keeps output on sales that actually get PAID for. Run drawings through that sentence. When the business bought the stock, it claimed input VAT back from SARS — legitimately, because the stock was destined for shelves. The owner then takes R8 000 of it home for the family. That stock will never be sold; the reason for the input claim has collapsed, so the claim must be returned: fifteen percent of eight thousand, R1 200 back to SARS, amount owed rises. No new money changed hands — it is the reversal of a favour that no longer applies. Now bad debts. When the business sold on credit for R4 600 including VAT, it declared output of R600 immediately — VAT is triggered by the invoice, not the payment. The debtor vanishes. The business has now paid SARS R600 of tax on money it never received; fairness reverses the charge, so it claims the fifteen over one hundred and fifteen back, and the amount owed falls. Same principle, opposite directions, zero memorising: ask what the original VAT entry assumed, and whether that assumption survived. Stock assumed a future sale — it died, pay the input back. Output assumed the debtor would pay — the debtor died, take the output back. And notice the fraction discipline riding alongside: drawings figures are usually given excluding VAT, so multiply by fifteen percent; a debtor's balance is always inclusive, so fifteen over one-fifteen. Read the words excluding and including as instructions, not decoration.
