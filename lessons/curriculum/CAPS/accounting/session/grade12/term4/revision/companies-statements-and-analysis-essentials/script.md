# Part 1 — Expert

Term four is revision territory, and Paper 1 rewards the student who can rebuild the whole company story from memory: income statement, notes, balance sheet, cash flow, and then the analysis that interrogates all of it. One company carries this whole revision — Marula Limited, six hundred thousand ordinary shares in issue — and every figure will be computed out loud, because in the examination the marks sit on the workings, not on the memory of a format. The plan is the paper's own plan: profit first, then equity, then cash, then judgement.

## Subtopic: The Income Statement Chain Rebuilt

The income statement is a chain, and every link is a mark. Say the chain before touching any number: sales, minus cost of sales, gives gross profit; add other income, subtract operating expenses, gives operating profit; add interest income, subtract interest expense, gives net profit before tax; subtract income tax, and net profit after tax remains. Six links, learned as a spine.

Now Marula's year. Sales R3 600 000, cost of sales R2 400 000 — gross profit R1 200 000, and notice the mark-up check: profit of 1 200 000 on cost of 2 400 000 is exactly fifty percent on cost, so if the paper states a fifty percent mark-up, the figures agree. Other income of R40 000 lifts the total to R1 240 000. Operating expenses of R600 000 — this is where the adjustments live: depreciation, the audit fees still owed, directors' fees, trading stock deficit after the count — leaving operating profit of R640 000. Interest income R10 000, so profit before interest expense is R650 000. Interest expense R50 000 — a R500 000 loan at ten percent, and the examiner will make you find one of those three numbers from the other two. Net profit before tax: R600 000.

Then the line students lose softest marks on: income tax at twenty-seven percent of R600 000 is R162 000, leaving net profit after tax of R438 000. Twenty-seven percent is the company rate — not the VAT fifteen, not a personal rate — and the tax calculation is a gift mark that must not be donated back.

Hold R438 000 in mind. Divided by six hundred thousand shares it will become earnings per share of 73 cents, and half of Part 4 hangs on it. This section's questions are with you now: the chain in order, the mark-up check, and the tax line done at the correct rate.

## Subtopic: Share Capital, Buy-backs and Retained Income

Shares have no par value — one account, ordinary share capital, credited with the full proceeds of every issue. Marula began the year with five hundred thousand shares that had raised R1 500 000. On the first day of this financial year the directors issued one hundred thousand new shares at the market-related price of R4,50 — R450 000 straight into share capital. The note now reads, line by line: five hundred thousand shares at the beginning, R1 500 000; one hundred thousand shares issued at R4,50, R450 000; six hundred thousand shares at the end, R1 950 000. Average issue price: R1 950 000 divided by six hundred thousand — R3,25. That average is the key that unlocks every buy-back question.

The buy-back drill, exactly as the examination guideline prescribes it. Suppose a company with the same R3,25 average repurchases forty thousand shares at R4,00 — always above the average, by policy. Three lines, spoken in order. Ordinary share capital decreases by forty thousand times R3,25 — R130 000. Retained income decreases by forty thousand times the 75 cents difference — R30 000. Bank decreases by the full forty thousand times R4,00 — R160 000. Check it: 130 000 plus 30 000 equals 160 000. The logic earns the marks: the capital account gives back the average it holds, and retained income surrenders the premium, because that premium is income the company had retained on those very shares.

Now Marula's retained income note, the appropriation story in four lines. Opening balance R288 000. Add net profit after tax, R438 000 — the link from Part 1. Subtract dividends of R324 000: an interim of 24 cents on six hundred thousand shares, R144 000, already paid, plus a final of 30 cents, R180 000, declared but unpaid, so it doubles as a current liability. Closing balance: 288 000 plus 438 000 minus 324 000 — R402 000. Shareholders' equity therefore stands at R1 950 000 plus R402 000 — R2 352 000. Questions on this section are ready now: the share capital note built, the three buy-back lines with their check, and the retained income note closed at R402 000.

## Subtopic: Cash Flow Signposts and the Three Workings

The cash flow statement answers one question the income statement cannot: where did the money actually go? Three sections, spoken as a map. Operating activities: cash generated from operations, then interest paid, tax paid, dividends paid. Investing activities: fixed assets bought and sold — and the guideline fixes disposals at carrying value, no profit or loss complication. Financing activities: shares issued, shares repurchased, loans raised or repaid.

Examiners set the same three workings every year, so drill them as subtractions.

Tax PAID is not the tax charge. Marula's charge is R162 000, but SARS was owed R15 000 at the start of the year and R21 000 at the end. Paid equals charge plus opening owed minus closing owed: 162 000 plus 15 000 minus 21 000 — R156 000. Logic, not formula: the year began owing 15 000 and that was settled; of this year's charge, 21 000 remains unpaid.

Dividends PAID follows the same shape with the current liability called shareholders for dividends. Last year's final dividend of R150 000 sat in that account at the start and was paid during this year; this year's interim of R144 000 was paid in cash; this year's final of R180 000 is declared, unpaid, and waiting in the closing balance sheet. Paid: 150 000 plus 144 000 — R294 000.

Financing tells Marula's strategy in two lines: proceeds of shares issued, R450 000 in; the loan unchanged at R500 000. A reader sees the company funding itself with new equity rather than new debt — and writing one sentence of that kind, reading the statement rather than reciting it, is where the interpretation marks sit. The questions for this section are with you now: paid versus charged, the dividend timing, and disposals at carrying value.

## Subtopic: The Indicators That Decide the Marks

Analysis is quote, compare, judge — three sentences per indicator, every time. Run Marula through the four decisive comparisons.

Liquidity first. Current assets R690 000 — inventories R300 000, debtors R250 000, cash R140 000 — against current liabilities of R300 000: creditors R99 000, SARS R21 000, shareholders for dividends R180 000. Current ratio 2,3 to 1; acid-test, stripping the stock, 1,3 to 1. Both healthy, and the operating checks agree: stock turns eight times a year — cost of sales R2 400 000 over average stock R300 000 — and debtors on credit sales of R2 500 000 pay in about 36,5 days, just past thirty-day terms and worth one sentence of caution.

Returns second. Return on shareholders' equity: R438 000 over average equity of R2 070 000 — 21,2 percent, towering over the roughly eight percent a fixed deposit pays, so shareholders are richly paid for their risk. Return on total capital employed: profit before tax plus interest, R650 000, over average equity plus the loan, R2 570 000 — 25,3 percent.

Gearing third, and here is the argument the paper wants written out: the whole pot earns 25,3 percent while the loan costs ten. Every borrowed rand earns fifteen cents more than it costs, so gearing is positive and, with debt-equity at a light R500 000 against R2 352 000, Marula could afford to borrow for expansion — always adding the caution that interest is owed in bad years too.

Value last. Net asset value: equity of R2 352 000 over six hundred thousand shares — 392 cents. The shares trade at 460 cents, a premium of 68 cents, which is the market paying for a future the balance sheet cannot record. Earnings per share 73 cents; dividends per share 54 cents; pay-out about 74 percent — generous to income-seekers, leaving about a quarter of earnings to fund growth. Final questions of this part are up now: the two liquidity ratios, the gearing argument stated in full, and price against net asset value read in the same units.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Same company, new clothes: a family bakery that became a company, six hundred thousand slices of ownership spread across a big family — because every statement in Paper 1 is just an honest answer to a question a family member would ask at the kitchen table.

## Subtopic: The Bakery's Year on One Page

The bakery sold R3 600 000 of bread this year. The flour, yeast and electricity behind those loaves cost R2 400 000, so the baking itself made R1 200 000 — every rand of cost baked into a rand and a half of price. Renting out the spare room above the shop brought in R40 000 more. Then the running of the place — wages, insurance, the delivery van wearing out, the accountant who checks the books — took R600 000. A little interest arrived on savings, R10 000, and the bank took R50 000 for the loan it gave the bakery. What was left before the tax collector called: R600 000.

The tax collector takes a fixed slice of company profit — twenty-seven cents in every rand — R162 000. The family's kept profit for the year: R438 000. That single number now does everything. Spread across six hundred thousand slices, each slice earned 73 cents. Of that, 54 cents was actually paid out to the family during the year — some at mid-year, some promised at year-end — and the remaining 19 cents per slice stayed in the bakery, quietly making every slice worth more. Profit earned, profit paid, profit kept: one page, three fates. Questions on this piece are with you now: the cost of the loaves against their price, the tax collector's fixed slice, and what each slice earned against what it was paid.

## Subtopic: Following the Cash, Not the Profit

Grandfather asks the awkward question: if the bakery made R438 000, why did the bank balance not grow by R438 000? Because profit is a story about the YEAR, and cash is a story about the TILL — and the two run on different clocks.

Three clocks, in fact. The tax clock: the bakery owed the tax collector a bit from last year, R15 000, and settled it; of this year's R162 000 slice, R21 000 will only be paid next year. So the cash that actually left for tax was R156 000 — charge plus old debt minus new debt. The dividend clock: the R150 000 promised to the family at the end of LAST year was paid early THIS year, and this year's mid-year payout of R144 000 left in cash too — R294 000 out — while this year's year-end promise of R180 000 has not left the till yet. The growth clock: the family sold one hundred thousand new slices to cousins for R450 000 cash, money that came IN without a single loaf being baked.

So the bank balance moves for reasons profit never mentions, and profit moves for reasons the bank never sees — the van wearing out costs profit every year but takes no cash at all. A family that watches only profit gets surprised at the till; a family that watches only the till forgets the van is dying. Watch both. Your questions are ready now: why paid differs from charged, which promise is still in the till, and what wears out without spending a cent.

## Subtopic: Three Questions Before You Buy a Slice

A cousin from Durban wants to buy slices. Three questions settle it, and they are the whole analysis section in disguise.

One: what is a slice honestly worth in the books? Everything the bakery owns, minus everything it owes, comes to R2 352 000. Divide by six hundred thousand slices: 392 cents each. But slices actually trade between cousins at 460 cents. The 68 cents extra is not foolishness; it is the price of the bakery's future — the loyal customers, the corner location, next year's loaves — none of which the books are allowed to record.

Two: does the family's money work harder in the bakery than in the bank? The bakery earned 21,2 cents on every rand of family money; the bank pays about eight for doing nothing. Thirteen extra cents per rand is the reward for risk, and it is generous.

Three: is the borrowed money helping or hurting? The whole pot of long-term money — family plus bank loan — earned 25,3 cents per rand this year, while the loan costs ten. Every borrowed rand earns more than it eats, so the loan is working FOR the family; if the bakery's earning power ever slid below ten, that same loan would start working against them, because the bank collects in bad years too.

Worth in the books against price between cousins; the bakery against the bank; earnings against the cost of debt. Three comparisons, each ending in a judgement — and that is the exact three-sentence habit the examination pays for, on every indicator, every time. The last questions of the lesson are with you now.
