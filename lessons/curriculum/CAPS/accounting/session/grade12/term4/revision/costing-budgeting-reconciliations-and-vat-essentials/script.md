# Part 1 — Expert

Paper 2 is the internal paper: what it costs to make a unit, whether next month's cash will stretch, whether the books agree with the outside world, and how much of the till belongs to SARS. Four topics, and revision means running each one at examination speed with the traps named. One factory anchors the costing work — Thola Workwear, twelve thousand overalls a year — and smaller businesses carry the budgeting, reconciliation and VAT drills. Everything is spoken as workings, because Paper 2 marks are workings marks.

## Subtopic: The Production Cost Statement in One Sweep

The production cost statement is a funnel with five levels; say them before any number: direct material cost, direct labour cost, prime cost, factory overhead cost, total cost of production — then the work-in-progress adjustment gives the cost of production of finished goods.

Thola's year. Direct materials issued to the factory: R480 000. Direct labour — wages of the machinists plus the employer's contributions that ride on those wages: R360 000. Prime cost, the direct costs added: R840 000. Factory overheads — the indirect factory family: factory rent, indirect labour like the supervisor and the cleaner, depreciation on factory machinery, factory water and electricity — R300 000. Total cost of production: R1 140 000.

Then the level that catches sleepy candidates: work-in-progress. Add the opening balance of half-made overalls, subtract the closing balance still on the machines. Thola began and ended the year with R30 000 on the floor, so the adjustment cancels and the cost of production of finished goods is R1 140 000. Twelve thousand overalls finished: unit cost R95.

The classification traps, because they are worth more than the additions. Salesperson's commission: NOT factory — a selling and distribution cost, R240 000 of it at Thola, living in the income statement. Office salaries and stationery: administration, R60 000. Factory rent when the building is shared: split by floor area, and the split is where the mark hides. Carriage on raw materials purchased: follows the materials into direct material cost. Every misfiled cost corrupts prime cost, total cost AND unit cost — one error, three wounds. Questions on this section are ready now: the five levels in order, the work-in-progress adjustment, and the classification of the sly costs.

## Subtopic: Contribution and the Break-even Argument

Break-even runs on one idea: every unit sold contributes something toward the fixed costs, and once the fixed costs are covered, that same contribution becomes pure profit.

Thola's overall sells for R135. The variable costs per unit — materials R40, labour R30, selling and distribution R20 — total R90. Contribution per unit: R45. The fixed costs — factory overheads R300 000 plus administration R60 000 — total R360 000, unmoved whether the factory makes one overall or twenty thousand. Break-even: fixed costs over contribution — 360 000 over 45 — eight thousand units. At eight thousand exactly, no profit, no loss.

Thola produced and sold twelve thousand. Four thousand units beyond break-even, each dropping its full R45 to the bottom line: profit R180 000. Verify the long way, because the examiner loves a proof: sales twelve thousand at R135 is R1 620 000; variable costs twelve thousand at R90 is R1 080 000; subtract fixed costs of R360 000 — R180 000. The two roads agree.

Now the interpretation marks, which outweigh the arithmetic. Compare break-even to PRODUCTION, this year and last: if last year break-even was eight thousand five hundred against eight thousand two hundred produced, the factory made a loss; this year's gap of four thousand units is the safety margin, and naming the improvement earns the mark. If the paper changes a variable — cotton price up, so contribution narrows and break-even climbs; selling price up, contribution widens and break-even falls — reason it through contribution, never by re-tabling every cost. And target profit: to add R90 000 of profit, divide by the R45 contribution — two thousand more units. Fixed costs stay out of it; they are already paid for. Pause for this section's questions: the contribution built, break-even proved both ways, and the safety margin argued.

## Subtopic: Cash Budgets and the Projected Income Statement

A cash budget is not an income statement, and the whole topic lives inside that difference. The budget records money the month it MOVES; the projected income statement records amounts the month they are EARNED or INCURRED. Two documents, two clocks.

The debtors' collection working, the heart of every budget question. A trader collects credit sales thirty percent in the month of sale, fifty percent the next month, eighteen percent in the second month, and writes two percent off. Credit sales: July R200 000, August R240 000, September R300 000. September's collections, three slices spoken slowly: thirty percent of September's own R300 000 — R90 000; fifty percent of August — R120 000; eighteen percent of July — R36 000. Total collected: R246 000. The two percent never appears in a cash budget — bad debts move no cash; they belong to the projected income statement.

The sorting drill that follows: depreciation — projected income statement only, never cash. A new loan received, or fixed deposit maturing — cash only, never income. And because companies now sit in this topic: provisional tax payments, interim dividends paid, the proceeds of a share issue, even a buy-back — all cash budget lines when the money moves.

Then the comment marks, usually the cheapest on the paper. Budgeted advertising R10 000, actual R4 000: not a saving to praise — possibly sales sabotaged to flatter one line. Wages jumping eight percent against a budgeted five: query it — overtime, a new appointment, or leakage. A projected overdraft in a month with a big equipment purchase: recommend moving the purchase, arranging the loan in advance, or delaying — resourceful, specific, sensible. The examiner is hiring a manager, not a calculator. This section's questions are with you now: the three-slice collection, the sorting of cash from non-cash, and one budget-versus-actual judgement.

## Subtopic: Reconciliations, Age Analysis and the VAT Settlement

Three housekeeping topics, each a guaranteed question somewhere in Paper 2.

Creditors' reconciliation: our ledger account for a supplier against the statement the supplier posts us. The month-end truth is usually that BOTH are right about different days. Our ledger shows R44 000 owing; the statement demands R52 400. Work the statement side first: our payment of R4 000 made on the thirtieth has not reached their books — subtract; an invoice of R9 200 on the statement is for goods that only arrived after month-end — subtract, we record goods when received. Statement now: R39 200. Our side: one invoice was posted twice, R4 800 — subtract the duplicate. Ledger now: R39 200. Agreement, and the rule that produced it: fix each side only for ITS OWN errors and omissions; timing differences sit on the statement side until the post catches up.

Age analysis: the debtors book of R240 000 sliced by age — current R96 000, forty percent; thirty days R72 000, thirty percent; sixty days R48 000, twenty percent; ninety days and older R24 000, ten percent. Terms are thirty days, so thirty percent of the book is overdue beyond terms and a tenth is drifting toward bad debt. The recommendations that earn marks are operational: interest on overdue accounts, suspend further credit to the ninety-day names, credit checks before opening accounts, discounts for prompt payment.

VAT closes the paper: one subtraction with nudges. Output tax collected on sales, R69 000, minus input tax paid on purchases and expenses, R48 000 — R21 000 owed. The nudges: the owner took stock costing R8 000 excluding VAT — input claimed on goods never sold must come back, add fifteen percent, R1 200. A debtor of R4 600 written off — that figure is VAT-INCLUSIVE, so reclaim fifteen over one hundred and fifteen, R600, subtract. Amount owed to SARS: 21 000 plus 1 200 minus 600 — R21 600. And the sentence worth two marks whenever ethics is asked: VAT was never the business's money; it is held in trust for SARS, and hiding cash sales is theft from the public purse. Final questions of this part are up: both reconciliation sides fixed, the age analysis judged, and the settlement built with its two nudges.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the whole paper again at a vetkoek stall — because a one-woman stall meets every Paper 2 idea before breakfast: what one vetkoek costs, whether Friday's cash will stretch, whether her book agrees with the wholesaler's slip, and which coins in the tin belong to SARS.

## Subtopic: The Price of One Vetkoek

Ma Khoza fries vetkoek at the taxi rank. One vetkoek eats R6 before it earns anything: flour, oil and filling about R5, plus gas and the paper packet about R1. Those costs ride on EVERY single vetkoek — make ten, spend sixty; make a hundred, spend six hundred. Costs that travel with each unit: variable.

But some costs do not care how many she fries. The stall spot costs R2 000 a month and the gas bottle deposit and burner add R400 — R2 400 owed whether it rains all month or the queue never ends. Costs that sit still: fixed.

She sells at R10. So each vetkoek, after feeding itself its R6, drops R4 into the tin toward that stubborn R2 400. That R4 is the contribution — each vetkoek's little payment toward the rent. How many little payments cover the rent? 2 400 divided by 4: six hundred vetkoek. Number six hundred in the month is the break-even vetkoek — the moment the stall stops owing and starts earning. From six hundred and one onward, the full R4 is hers.

She usually sells nine hundred: three hundred past the line, times R4 — R1 200 profit. And if flour spikes so each vetkoek eats R7? Contribution thins to R3, and the rent now needs eight hundred sales, not six hundred. She feels her break-even move the week prices move — and that instinct is exactly what the factory question tests, with bigger numbers and the same one idea. Questions on this piece are with you now: costs that travel, costs that sit, and the six-hundredth vetkoek.

## Subtopic: Planning Next Month's Money

Ma Khoza sells to the taxi drivers on account — they settle when they get paid. So she keeps two lists that refuse to match. What next month should EARN: school orders, rank sales, the drivers' vetkoek they will eat that month. And what next month will PUT IN THE TIN: this month's drivers finally paying, plus next month's cash customers — but not next month's account sales, because that money only walks in later.

The tin list is the cash budget; the earning list is the projected income statement — and the gap between them is where stalls die. A brilliant month on paper can still leave the tin empty if the drivers pay late and the flour supplier wants cash now. So she plans like the examiners want managers to plan: the gas bottle refill lands the same week as the school's big order ingredients — move the refill a week earlier, or ask the wholesaler for a few days' grace, arranged in advance, not begged on the day.

And when she compares plan to reality at month-end, she reads like a detective, not an accountant. She budgeted R300 for the boy who shouts her prices at the rank, and spent nothing — saved money, or the reason sales dipped? Oil spend jumped fifteen percent — price rise, or a leaking drum? A number off its budget is not an answer; it is a question with a mark attached. Your questions are ready now: the tin against the earning list, the payment that arrives a month late, and one budget line interrogated.

## Subtopic: Two Sets of Books and the Taxman's Slice

Month-end, and the wholesaler's statement says Ma Khoza owes R5 240. Her little book says R4 400. Nobody panics; they find the days that wrote the difference. Her Friday payment of R500 is not on the statement — it crossed in the post, so the statement's true figure is R4 740. The statement charges R340 for a flour delivery arriving only next Tuesday — goods she has not received, so not hers yet: R4 400. And her own book once entered the same delivery twice... this month it did not, but she checks, because HER errors get fixed in HER book, and THEIR timing sits on THEIR paper. Two honest books, brought to one truth: that is a reconciliation, whether the numbers are hundreds or millions.

Her account customers get the same month-end stare. R2 400 owed to her: half of it fresh, but R240 of it three months old and answering no calls. She stops the oldest debtor's account, offers the slow ones a small discount for paying this week, and thinks twice before opening a new account — the age analysis question, played out at a taxi rank.

Last: the coins that were never hers. If Ma Khoza registers as a vendor, her R10 vetkoek becomes R11,50 — R10 plus fifteen percent VAT — and that R1,50 sleeps in the tin belonging to SARS. But she also PAID VAT on flour and oil, and SARS gives that back. So settlement day is one subtraction: collected on sales, minus paid on supplies, send the difference. If a registered spaza takes stock home for supper, the taxman wants his input claim back; if it writes off a customer's inclusive debt, it claims fifteen over one-fifteen back. Collected minus paid, nudged honestly — and hiding Friday cash sales is not cleverness; it is spending money held in trust for the country. The last questions of the lesson are with you now.
