**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First: the buy-back takes the difference to retained income, not to some loss account. Why is the extra 75 cents per share treated as retained income and not as an expense?

**Tutor:** Because nothing was lost — the company simply returned to those shareholders what it had been keeping for them. Walk the logic. A share's claim on the company has two layers: the capital originally paid in, represented by the average issue price of R3,25, and the profits earned since then that the company chose not to pay out — retained income. When the company repurchases a share for R4,00, it is settling both layers at once: R3,25 of capital goes back through the share capital account, and the 75 cents premium is the retained profit attached to that share finally being paid out. An expense is a cost of operating — wages, rates, depreciation — something consumed to earn income. A buy-back consumes nothing; it is a distribution to owners, exactly like a dividend in economic character, which is why it never touches the income statement. That placement also protects the numbers the examiner will test next: net profit after tax is undisturbed, so earnings per share is undisturbed, while equity shrinks — and that is why a company can look MORE profitable per share after a buy-back without earning a cent more. The three-line working carries the discipline: share capital gives back the average it holds, retained income surrenders the premium, bank pays the total, and 130 000 plus 30 000 must equal 160 000 or a mark has leaked.

**Mandy:** Next one: the cash flow statement seems to repeat the income statement with different numbers — tax appears in both, dividends in both. What is it actually adding that the other statements cannot show?

**Tutor:** It adds the one dimension accrual accounting deliberately blurs: timing. The income statement matches income and expenses to the YEAR they belong to, whether or not money moved — that is its virtue, and its blindness. The cash flow statement reports only rands that crossed the bank account, whenever their story began. So tax appears twice with different numbers because they answer different questions: R162 000 is what this year's profit OWES, R156 000 is what the bank actually surrendered, and the R6 000 gap is timing — old debt settled, new debt still waiting. Same with dividends: R324 000 declared for this year, R294 000 physically paid during it. Why does an examiner care? Because companies rarely die of unprofitability alone; they die of running out of cash while looking profitable. A company can report R438 000 profit while its cash shrinks — stock piling up, debtors slow, a dividend habit too generous — and only the cash flow statement exposes that. It also reveals strategy in a way no ratio does: Marula's financing section shows R450 000 of new share money and no new debt — a company funding growth with equity. Read the three sections as a biography: operations generate, investing consumes, financing explains who funds the difference. One interpretive sentence of that kind is routinely a full-mark answer.

**Mandy:** Third: for return on equity we divided by AVERAGE equity, R2 070 000, not closing equity. Half the class used closing and got a different answer. Who is right?

**Tutor:** The average is right, and the reason is fairness in time. The R438 000 of profit was earned across the whole year — January's sales as much as December's. But the equity working to produce it was not constant: the year opened at R1 788 000 and closed at R2 352 000, because new share capital arrived and profits accumulated. Dividing a full year's profit by only the closing figure punishes the company for money that arrived late and did little of the earning; dividing by the opening figure flatters it for the same reason. The average — opening plus closing over two — is the honest estimate of the capital that was actually at work across the period, and the formula sheet says average for exactly this reason. The same logic drives average debtors in the collection period and average stock in the turnover rate: any ratio that compares a FLOW over a year to a STOCK at a moment should use the average of that stock. Now, the examiner's mercy: if a paper gives you only one equity figure, use it and state what you used — the marking guideline follows the available information. But when both balances sit in the question, choosing closing over average is a signalled trap, and the difference is visible: 438 000 over 2 352 000 gives 18,6 percent, not 21,2 — a different judgement about a company from the same year.

**Mandy:** Last one: if the market pays 460 cents for a share whose net asset value is 392 cents, why would anyone ever sell below net asset value — and what would a price below it even mean?

**Tutor:** Start with what net asset value is: the balance sheet's liquidation answer — assets at book value, minus every liability, per share. It is backward-looking by construction, because the books record what was paid, less depreciation, not what the assets will earn. The market price is a forward-looking auction: buyers pricing every profit the company will ever make. When buyers pay 68 cents above book, they are saying the assets in combination — the location, the customer loyalty, the management — will earn more than the assets alone suggest. So when would a share trade BELOW net asset value? When the market believes the opposite: that the assets will not earn their keep, that the book values are optimistic — stock that will not sell at its recorded cost, debtors who will not pay — or that management will waste what the balance sheet shows. A discount is the market's vote of no confidence, and a seller accepts it because the alternative is holding a claim on a future they doubt. But here is the examiner's twist, worth its own sentence in any answer: a deep discount can also be an opportunity — a takeover bidder can buy the whole company for less than its net assets and unlock the difference, which is why discounts attract predators. So never write that a discount is simply bad: quote the two numbers in the same units, cents against cents, name the gap, and then argue what the market must be believing for that gap to exist. That argued sentence, not the subtraction, is where the marks live.
