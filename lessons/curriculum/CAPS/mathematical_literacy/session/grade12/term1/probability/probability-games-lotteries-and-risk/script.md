# Part 1 — Expert

Every Saturday night, millions of South Africans watch six numbered balls decide whether their R5 ticket becomes a fortune. Every day, insurers price policies, weather services forecast rain, and taxi drivers judge whether to risk the shortcut. All of it is PROBABILITY — the mathematics of uncertainty — and matric examines it from the dice on a board game to the fine print of a lottery. This session builds the tools: the probability scale, the two ways of measuring chance, the honest arithmetic of gambling, and the diagrams that tame events happening one after another.

## Subtopic: The Language of Chance

An EXPERIMENT is any action with an uncertain result: rolling a die, tossing a coin, drawing a card. An OUTCOME is one possible result; the SAMPLE SPACE is the list of all possible outcomes — for a die, the six faces 1 to 6. An EVENT is the outcome or set of outcomes you care about: rolling an even number is the event containing 2, 4 and 6.

PROBABILITY measures how likely an event is, on a scale from 0 to 1. Zero means impossible: rolling a 7 on one die. One means certain: rolling a number between 1 and 6. Everything uncertain lives in between, and the same probability wears three outfits — fraction, decimal, percentage. A fair coin shows heads with probability one half, 0,5, or 50%.

When all outcomes are equally likely, probability is a counting exercise: the number of outcomes in the event, divided by the total number of outcomes. Rolling a six: one favourable outcome out of six, so one sixth, which is 0,17 rounded to two decimal places. Rolling an even number: three out of six, one half. Drawing a red card from a full pack: 26 out of 52, one half again.

Two guard rails. First, the phrase equally likely is doing real work — a loaded die or a bent coin breaks the counting method. Second, probabilities of all outcomes in a sample space add to 1, so the probability that an event does NOT happen is 1 minus the probability that it does: the chance of not rolling a six is 1 minus one sixth, five sixths. The questions for this section are with you now; write the sample space before any fraction, and place every answer on the 0-to-1 scale.

## Subtopic: Theoretical Probability and Relative Frequency

There are two honest ways to attach a number to chance, and the curriculum tests the difference sharply.

THEORETICAL PROBABILITY comes from reasoning about the experiment before it happens: a fair coin has two equally likely faces, so heads has probability 0,5. No coin need ever be tossed.

RELATIVE FREQUENCY comes from actually doing the experiment: toss the coin 100 times, count 58 heads, and the relative frequency of heads is 58 out of 100 — 0,58. It is calculated as the number of times the event occurred, divided by the number of trials.

Why do the two disagree — 0,58 against 0,5? Because chance is lumpy in the short run. Toss the coin only ten times and seven heads is unremarkable. But as the number of trials grows, relative frequency drifts toward the theoretical value: after ten thousand tosses of a fair coin, the proportion of heads will sit very close to 0,5. That long-run settling is why casinos and insurers, who play thousands of times, can rely on probabilities that any single gambler cannot.

Relative frequency also earns its keep where theory cannot reach. What is the probability that a taxi on a given route arrives late? No symmetry argument exists — you must observe: out of 40 recorded trips, 12 were late, giving 12 over 40, which is 0,3. Insurers price young drivers the same way: claim records, not coin logic.

And the FAIRNESS test: a game is fair when each player has an equal probability of winning. If observed frequencies drift far from the theoretical claim over many trials — a die showing six 30 times in 60 rolls — suspect the die, not the mathematics. The questions for this section are ready; label each probability as theoretical or observed, and quote the number of trials whenever frequency speaks.

## Subtopic: The Lottery and Other Long Shots

The national lottery asks players to match six numbers drawn from 52. The probability of a single board matching all six is one in 20 358 520 — roughly one in twenty million. Numbers that size defeat intuition, so translate: a player buying one board every week can expect a jackpot about once every 390 000 years. Meanwhile the probability of SOME ticket winning somewhere is decent, because millions of boards are sold — which is precisely how the advertising works: the winner is real, visible and photographed; the twenty million losers are not.

Examiners require the vocabulary of gambling read honestly. Every game of chance has a price, a prize and a probability, and the three combine into an expected long-run result that always favours the operator — lotteries, casinos and betting shops are businesses, and the mathematics of their profit is printed in their own rules. A R5 ticket with a one-in-twenty-million chance at a typical jackpot returns, on average, a fraction of its price; the rest funds the operator and its causes. Playing for entertainment is a choice; playing as a financial plan is arithmetic error.

INSURANCE is the same mathematics with the roles reversed. The insurer collects a small premium from many clients, and pays large amounts to the few who suffer the insured event. Premiums are set by RISK ASSESSMENT — relative frequencies harvested from data: young drivers claim more often, so their premiums are higher; a car parked behind a locked gate claims less often, so its premium drops. Where the gambler pays small for a tiny chance of gaining big, the insured pays small to cancel a small chance of LOSING big. Same probabilities, opposite purpose.

The examinable core: state the probability, interpret its size in words, and follow the money honestly. The questions for this section follow now; treat every game as price, prize and probability, and say who profits in the long run.

## Subtopic: Compound Events: Tree Diagrams and Two-Way Tables

A COMPOUND EVENT combines two or more simple events: rain AND a late taxi; two coin tosses in a row. Two tools organise the counting.

The TREE DIAGRAM handles events in sequence. In Durban's rainy season, the probability of morning rain is 0,3. When it rains, the probability that Sipho's taxi runs late is 0,6; on dry mornings it is 0,2. Draw two branches for rain, 0,3, and no rain, 0,7 — they must sum to 1. From each, branch again for late and on time. The rule: MULTIPLY along branches, ADD between paths. Probability of rain and late: 0,3 × 0,6 = 0,18. Probability of no rain and late: 0,7 × 0,2 = 0,14. Probability Sipho is late at all — either path: 0,18 + 0,14 = 0,32, just under a one-in-three chance. Check the machine: the four end-of-path probabilities, 0,18, 0,12, 0,14 and 0,56, sum to exactly 1.

The TWO-WAY TABLE handles two characteristics recorded together. A roadworthy centre tests 100 vehicles: 40 minibuses, of which 24 pass and 16 fail; 60 cars, of which 48 pass and 12 fail. The table's rows are vehicle type, its columns pass or fail, and the totals row confirms 72 passes, 28 failures, 100 vehicles.

Read probabilities straight off the counts. Probability a randomly chosen vehicle failed: 28 over 100, 0,28. Probability a randomly chosen MINIBUS failed: now the pool shrinks to the 40 minibuses, and 16 over 40 gives 0,4. That shift of pool — from all vehicles to minibuses only — is the heart of two-way table questions: the words given that, or of the minibuses, change the denominator, and the mark scheme watches exactly there.

The questions for this section are with you now; multiply along, add across, and read every denominator out of the question's own words.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same chances at street level: a board game on the stoep, a lotto kiosk, and a rainy Monday taxi.

## Subtopic: A Scale from Impossible to Certain

Chance is a ruler, and every event in the world sits somewhere on it. At the left end, 0: impossible — your die shows a seven. At the right end, 1: certain — the sun sets this evening. Halfway, 0,5: the fair coin, could go either way. Everything uncertain lives strictly between the ends.

To place an event on the ruler when every outcome has an equal shot, count two things — that is the entire method. Count the ways your event can happen; count all the ways anything can happen; divide. A die has six faces: rolling a six is 1 way out of 6, about 0,17 — close to the impossible end. Rolling an even number is 3 ways out of 6 — dead centre, 0,5. Rolling less than seven is 6 out of 6 — certain, probability 1.

Now the trick that solves half the exam's probability one-liners: the NOT trick. The whole ruler holds exactly 1 of probability, so the chance an event does NOT happen is 1 minus the chance it does. Chance of no six: 1 minus one sixth — five sixths. If a weather service says 0,3 for rain, dry sits at 0,7, no further thought required.

One warning before the questions: equal shots is a real condition, not decoration. A cooldrink-cap competition where one cap in ten thousand wins is not a coin; counting faces only works when the faces are fair. Some questions on this section are with you right now; place each event on the ruler, and let the NOT trick do the heavy lifting.

## Subtopic: Why the Lotto Is Not a Retirement Plan

Here is the number the adverts never say slowly: one in twenty million, three hundred and fifty-eight thousand, five hundred and twenty. That is the chance that one lotto board matches all six balls. Try to feel its size: fill Soccer City to the roof — about ninety thousand people. Now fill it more than two hundred and twenty times over, give every single person a ticket, and ONE of them wins. You are one face in those two hundred and twenty stadiums.

But someone wins almost every few weeks — is that not proof it works? It is proof that MILLIONS of boards are sold. When twenty million tickets chase a one-in-twenty-million chance, a winner somewhere is unsurprising; a winner being YOU stays as close to impossible as everyday life gets. The camera interviews the one; it cannot interview the twenty million.

Follow the R5. The game keeps roughly half of all money staked for prizes; the rest runs the operator and its funded causes. So the average ticket buys back less than half its price in prize money, meaning every rand staked is, on average, a donation with a lottery attached. Fine as entertainment, honestly priced as a dream — fatal as a financial plan, because the long run is arithmetic, not luck.

And notice insurance is the same machine turned to protect you. With insurance you pay a small premium so that a rare disaster — the taxi crash, the shack fire — cannot destroy you; the many premiums of the lucky pay the claims of the unlucky. Gambler and insured hold the same tiny probability, but one buys risk and the other buys rescue. Questions on this section are coming your way now; check every game by its price, its prize, and its honest chance.

## Subtopic: Two Steps of Chance

Some chances come in sequences: first the sky decides, then the taxi decides. A tree diagram is nothing more than drawing the story before living it.

Monday morning in Durban. First fork: rain, 0,3, or dry, 0,7. Notice the two branches share the whole ruler — they add to 1. Now each branch forks again. On the rain branch: taxi late, 0,6, or on time, 0,4. On the dry branch: late drops to 0,2, on time 0,8.

Two rules run every tree ever drawn. Rule one: multiplying along a path answers AND. Rain and late: 0,3 times 0,6 — 0,18. Why multiply? Because the second chance happens inside the first: late-when-raining only matters on the 3 mornings in 10 that rain at all — 6 tenths of those 3 tenths is 18 hundredths. Rule two: adding across paths answers OR. Late can arrive by two roads — rainy-and-late, 0,18, or dry-and-late, 0,14 — and since the roads cannot both happen on one morning, add: 0,32. Sipho should set the alarm earlier about one Monday in three.

The self-check: all the endpoints of the tree — 0,18, 0,12, 0,14, 0,56 — must add to exactly 1, because the tree contains every possible morning.

Two-way tables are the same idea for counted things, and the only trap is the shrinking pool. Of 100 tested vehicles, 28 failed: 0,28. But of the 40 minibuses, 16 failed: 16 over 40, 0,4. The words of the minibuses shrank the pool from 100 to 40 — always ask, out of WHOM, before writing the fraction. The final questions of the lesson are with you now; multiply along the path, add between paths, and check the endpoints swallow the whole 1.
