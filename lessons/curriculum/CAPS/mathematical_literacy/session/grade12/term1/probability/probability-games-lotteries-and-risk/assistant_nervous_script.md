Hey, it's Mandy — probability feels like the topic where anything can happen, but the exam questions are the most predictable in the whole paper. Four moves cover nearly everything.

Move one: the counting fraction. When outcomes are equally likely — dice, coins, cards — probability is just ways-it-can-happen over ways-anything-can-happen. Write the sample space first, count, divide. And keep every answer between 0 and 1; a probability of 1,2 is the marker's favourite red pen moment.

Move two: the NOT trick. The whole scale adds to 1, so not-the-event is 1 minus the event. Rain at 0,3 means dry at 0,7. Half the one-mark questions in this topic are exactly that subtraction.

Move three: know your two kinds of probability. Fair object described? Theory — count the faces. Trial data given — 58 heads in 100 tosses, 12 late taxis in 40 trips? Relative frequency — occurrences over trials. Label which one you used and quote the number of trials.

Move four: trees and tables. On a tree, multiply along the path for AND, add between paths for OR, and check the endpoints add to exactly 1 — that check catches almost every slip. On a two-way table, the whole game is the denominator: ask out of whom? The words of the minibuses shrink the pool from 100 to 40, and the fraction follows the words.

And when the lotto question arrives, keep your head: one in twenty million is the honest number, winners on television only prove millions of tickets were sold, and the operator profits in the long run — say it plainly and collect the marks.

Count, subtract from 1, label your method, follow the denominator. You've got this!
