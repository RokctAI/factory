**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First, the one that comes up every year: a coin landed heads six times in a row, and half the class swears tails is now overdue. Is the coin owing us a tails?

**Tutor:** The coin owes nothing, remembers nothing, and that intuition has a name — the gambler's fallacy — precisely because so many people trust it. Each toss of a fair coin is an independent event: the probability of tails is 0,5 on the first toss, 0,5 after six heads, 0,5 after sixty heads. The metal has no memory and no sense of justice. What confuses people is a true fact wearing a false disguise: in the LONG run, the relative frequency of tails does drift toward 0,5 — but it gets there by drowning the streak in thousands of new tosses, not by producing extra tails to repay old heads. After six heads and then a thousand fair tosses, you expect around five hundred more heads and five hundred more tails; the six-head surplus just becomes invisible inside the crowd. Where a streak SHOULD change your mind is about the coin itself, not the future: if a die shows six thirty times in sixty rolls, the theoretical claim of one sixth deserves suspicion, and the observed relative frequency of 0,5 becomes your better estimate. So the exam-safe summary is this: for a fair object, past results never alter the next probability; but persistent disagreement between observed frequency and claimed theory is evidence the object is not fair.

**Mandy:** Next: theoretical probability and relative frequency gave different answers for the same coin — 0,5 against 0,58. In an exam, which number are we supposed to use?

**Tutor:** Use the one the question's information supports, and the question always tips its hand. If it describes a fair object with equally likely outcomes — a fair coin, an unbiased die, a shuffled pack — it wants theory: count the favourable outcomes, divide by the total, no experiment needed. If it gives you trial data — 100 tosses, 58 heads; 40 taxi trips, 12 late — it wants relative frequency: occurrences divided by trials, so 0,58 or 0,3. And notice that for many real situations, relative frequency is not second best; it is the only tool that exists. There is no symmetry argument for a taxi being late, a learner passing matric, or a driver claiming from an insurer — nothing about those situations comes in equally likely faces. That is why insurers are, at heart, professional relative-frequency collectors: thousands of claim records stand in for the sample space that theory cannot supply. One refinement earns the top marks: quote the number of trials with any relative frequency, because 3 late taxis out of 10 trips and 300 out of 1 000 both give 0,3, yet the second deserves far more trust — larger samples settle closer to the truth. So label your method, show the fraction, and state the trials.

**Mandy:** This one caused an argument: on the tree diagram, why do we multiply along the branches? Someone multiplied when they should have added and got a probability bigger than 1.

**Tutor:** Let's fix both directions at once, because the tree only has two moves and each answers a different word. Multiplying answers AND — both things happening, one after the other. Why multiplication? Take the rainy Monday: rain has probability 0,3, and late-given-rain is 0,6. The late taxi only gets its chance on rainy mornings — 3 mornings in every 10 — and on those mornings it takes 6 out of 10. So rain-and-late happens on 6 tenths OF 3 tenths of all mornings, and OF is multiplication: 0,18. A fraction of a fraction must shrink, which gives you a built-in alarm: an AND probability can never exceed either of the branches it came from. Adding answers OR — the event arriving by different, mutually exclusive paths. Late happens via rainy-and-late, 0,18, or via dry-and-late, 0,14; one morning cannot be both rainy and dry, so the paths never overlap and their probabilities stack: 0,32. Your classmate's answer above 1 means paths were added that overlap, or branches were added when the story said AND. And always run the final audit: the tree's endpoints — 0,18, 0,12, 0,14, 0,56 — must sum to exactly 1, because the tree must contain every possible morning. If the endpoints miss 1, a branch pair somewhere fails to add to 1, and the error is upstream, not in the arithmetic.

**Mandy:** Last one: in the two-way table, the same event — a minibus failing — gave two different answers, 0,16 and 0,4. How can one event have two probabilities?

**Tutor:** Because those are two different questions wearing similar clothes, and the difference lives entirely in the denominator — in the pool you are choosing from. Question one: a vehicle is chosen at random from ALL 100 tested vehicles; what is the probability it is a minibus that failed? The pool is 100, the favourable count is the 16 failed minibuses, and the answer is 16 over 100 — 0,16. Question two: a vehicle is chosen at random FROM THE MINIBUSES; what is the probability it failed? The words of the minibuses shrink the pool to 40, the favourable count is still 16, and the answer is 16 over 40 — 0,4. Same cell of the table on top; different totals underneath. The signal words for the shrunken pool are given that, of the, and among the — the moment one appears, your denominator is no longer the grand total but the row or column total the phrase names. A reliable exam habit: before writing any fraction from a two-way table, say the sentence out of whom? aloud in your head, circle that group's total in the margin, and use it as the denominator. And check direction, too: the probability that a minibus failed, 16 over 40, is not the probability that a failed vehicle was a minibus, 16 over 28 — about 0,57. Three different fractions from one little cell, and the words of the question choose between them.
