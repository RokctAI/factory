# Part 1 — Expert

Every month, in every municipality from Tshwane to Buffalo City, a meter turns and a bill is born. The price of that electricity and water is not one number — it is a TARIFF SYSTEM, a set of rules that decides what each unit costs depending on how many units you use. Matric examiners love tariff systems because they test reading, calculating and deciding all at once. This session works through the tariffs of one household in Soshanguve: a prepaid electricity meter, a water account, and a decision about which electricity option is cheaper.

## Subtopic: What a Tariff Is and Who Charges One

A TARIFF is a rate: a price charged per unit of something used. Electricity is charged per kilowatt-hour, written kWh. Water is charged per kilolitre, written kl, and one kilolitre is one thousand litres. Telephone networks charge per minute or per megabyte, banks charge per transaction, and taxis and buses charge per trip or per kilometre. Wherever a service is used in measurable units, a tariff attaches a price to each unit.

Municipal tariffs come in two broad designs. A FLAT TARIFF charges the same price for every unit, no matter how many you use: 100 units or 600 units, each one costs the same. A RISING BLOCK TARIFF divides consumption into blocks and charges a higher price for each successive block — the first units are the cheapest, and the price climbs as usage climbs. South African municipalities use rising blocks deliberately: basic usage stays affordable for low-income households, while heavy usage pays a premium.

On top of the per-unit charges, many accounts carry a FIXED CHARGE, sometimes called a basic or service charge: an amount payable every month simply for being connected, even if consumption is zero. A complete municipal charge is therefore fixed charge plus consumption charges, and then VAT at 15% on the total.

The examinable skills are three: reading the tariff table correctly, calculating a bill from a given consumption, and comparing two tariff systems to advise which is cheaper. Every one of those skills starts with the same discipline — identify the block boundaries and the price in each block before touching the calculator. The questions for this section are with you now; name the tariff type before you calculate anything.

## Subtopic: Rising Block Electricity: Filling the Blocks in Order

The household's prepaid electricity uses this rising block structure. Block one: the first 100 kWh at R2,20 per kWh. Block two: from 101 to 400 kWh at R2,60 per kWh. Block three: from 401 to 650 kWh at R2,95 per kWh. Above 650 kWh, R3,20 per kWh.

The golden rule: consumption fills the blocks IN ORDER, like water filling a stack of containers from the bottom. You never pay one price for everything — you pay each block's price for the units inside that block only.

The household used 380 kWh this month. Block one takes the first 100 units: 100 × 2,20 = R220,00. That leaves 380 − 100 = 280 units, which all fit inside block two because 280 is less than the 300 units that block two can hold: 280 × 2,60 = R728,00. Block three is never reached. The total: 220 + 728 = R948,00.

The classic error is to see 380 kWh, notice it lands in block two, and charge all 380 units at R2,60: that gives 380 × 2,60 = R988,00, which is R40 too much and earns nothing. The block rate applies only to the units within that block.

One more reading: how many units does each block hold? Block two runs from 101 to 400, which is 400 − 100 = 300 units, not 400. Block three runs from 401 to 650, which is 250 units. Examiners set the trap of subtracting wrongly at the boundaries, so write down each block's capacity before multiplying. The questions for this section are ready; fill the blocks from the bottom, one at a time, and show the units in each block in your working.

## Subtopic: Water Tariffs and the Free Basic Allowance

Water tariffs follow the same rising block logic, with one humane difference: most municipalities supply the first block free. This is FREE BASIC WATER, typically the first 6 kl per month, priced at R0 because the Constitution regards basic water as a right.

The household's water tariff: 0 to 6 kl free; above 6 up to 15 kl at R18,50 per kl; above 15 up to 30 kl at R26,40 per kl. The household used 22 kl.

Fill the blocks in order. The first 6 kl cost nothing. The second block runs from 6 to 15 kl, a capacity of 9 kl, and the household passes right through it: 9 × 18,50 = R166,50. That accounts for 15 kl, leaving 22 − 15 = 7 kl in the third block: 7 × 26,40 = R184,80. Consumption total: 166,50 + 184,80 = R351,30.

Water is a standard-rated supply, so VAT at 15% applies: 351,30 × 0,15 = R52,70 to the nearest cent. The amount payable is 351,30 + 52,70 = R404,00.

Notice what the rising blocks do to behaviour. The first kilolitres are free or cheap; the 22nd kilolitre costs R26,40 plus VAT. A household that cuts consumption from 22 kl to 15 kl saves the entire third block — R184,80 before VAT — which is the municipality's way of pricing conservation into the bill. Examiners ask exactly that: how much would be saved by reducing consumption to a given level, and the answer is always found by recalculating the bill at the new consumption and subtracting. The questions for this section follow now; start at the free block and account for every kilolitre.

## Subtopic: Comparing Two Tariff Systems

The curriculum requires comparison of two or more tariff systems using tables, graphs and calculations, ending in advice. The household can stay on the prepaid rising block system, or switch to a conventional metered option: a fixed monthly charge of R220,00 plus a flat rate of R2,05 per kWh for every unit.

Compare at the household's usual consumption of 380 kWh. Prepaid: R948,00, as calculated. Conventional: 380 × 2,05 = R779,00 plus the fixed charge of R220,00, giving R999,00. At 380 kWh the prepaid option is cheaper by 999 − 948 = R51,00.

Now compare at a high-consumption month of 600 kWh. Prepaid: block one gives R220,00; block two is filled completely, 300 × 2,60 = R780,00; block three takes the remaining 200 units, 200 × 2,95 = R590,00; total R1 590,00. Conventional: 600 × 2,05 = R1 230,00 plus R220,00 = R1 450,00. At 600 kWh the conventional option is cheaper by R140,00.

The advice therefore depends on consumption: low users belong on prepaid, heavy users on conventional. Somewhere between 380 and 600 kWh the two systems cost the same — the BREAK-EVEN point. On a graph with consumption on the horizontal axis and cost on the vertical axis, each tariff draws its own line, and break-even is where the lines cross: below the crossing one option lies lower, above it the other does. An exam question shows the graph and asks you to read off the crossing point, or gives the table and asks you to advise a specific household in a sentence.

Answer comparison questions the full-marks way: calculate both totals, state which is cheaper, give the saving in rands, and tie the advice to the household's consumption. The questions for this section are with you now; two calculations, one comparison, one sentence of advice.

## Subtopic: The Staircase of Prices

Buying electricity on a rising block tariff is like buying at a staircase. The first units you buy each month stand on the bottom step — the cheap step, R2,20 each. Once you have bought 100 of them, the bottom step is full, and the next units stand on the second step at R2,60 each. Use even more and you are paying R2,95, then R3,20. The higher you climb in a month, the more each new unit costs you.

Here is the part that saves you from the classic mistake: climbing to a higher step never changes the price of the steps below. Our household bought 380 units. The first 100 cost R2,20 each — R220. The next 280 cost R2,60 each — R728. Altogether R948. Nobody goes back and re-prices the first 100 at the higher rate. If you charged all 380 units at R2,60 you would get R988, and you would be R40 wrong.

Why do municipalities build the staircase this way? So that a gogo running a fridge and a few lights buys everything on the cheap step, while a house running a pool pump and two geysers climbs to the expensive steps. The staircase protects small users and charges big users more per unit.

And notice something about prepaid vouchers: buy your units early in the month or late, the meter still counts your monthly total up the same staircase. The trick to a smaller bill is not when you buy — it is using fewer units, staying on the low steps. Some questions on this section are with you right now; take the units step by step from the bottom, and write each step's cost before adding.

## Subtopic: The First Six Kilolitres

Water works on the same staircase, except the bottom step is free. The first 6 kilolitres every month — six thousand litres — cost the household nothing. That is the free basic water allowance, and it exists because nobody should be priced out of drinking and washing.

Our household used 22 kilolitres. Walk it through the steps. Steps one to six: free, R0. From 6 up to 15 is nine kilolitres at R18,50 each: R166,50. We have now paid for 15; the last 7 kilolitres sit on the expensive step at R26,40 each: R184,80. Add them: R351,30. Then the taxman takes his slice — VAT at fifteen percent, R52,70 — and the bill says R404,00.

Feel what the staircase is telling you. The water you drink and cook with is basically free. The water that fills a leaking toilet or runs while someone brushes teeth is the most expensive water in the house, because every wasted litre lands on the top step. Fix a leak that wastes 7 kilolitres a month and the saving is not average-priced water — it is top-step water, R184,80 plus VAT, more than R210 a month.

That is why the first question to ask about any water bill is not the total; it is which step did we climb to, and what pushed us there. Questions on this section are coming your way now; start counting at the free step and account for every kilolitre before you touch the VAT.

## Subtopic: Which Deal Is Cheaper for You

Two electricity deals are on the table. Deal one, prepaid: no monthly fee, but the staircase — cheap first units, expensive later ones. Deal two, conventional: pay R220 every month just to be connected, and then every unit costs the same flat R2,05.

There is no such thing as the cheaper deal for everyone. There is only the cheaper deal for YOUR usage. So you test both deals with your own numbers, like trying on shoes.

An ordinary month, 380 units. Prepaid: R948. Conventional: 380 units at R2,05 is R779, plus the R220 fee, R999. Prepaid wins by R51. A heavy month, 600 units. Prepaid climbs the staircase hard: R220 plus R780 plus R590 — R1 590. Conventional: R1 230 plus R220 — R1 450. Now conventional wins by R140.

Same two deals, opposite answers — because the fixed fee is a bad deal for small users and a bargain for big ones. The R220 fee spread over 380 units adds about 58 cents to every unit; spread over 600 units it adds only about 37 cents, while the flat R2,05 dodges the expensive top steps entirely.

Somewhere between 380 and 600 units the two deals cost exactly the same — the break-even point. On a graph the two cost lines cross there. Below the crossing, take prepaid; above it, take conventional. In the exam, do both calculations, name the winner, say by how much, and say for whom. The final questions of the lesson are with you now; try both deals on your own numbers and give the advice in one honest sentence.
