**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! The one that comes up a lot first: for small amounts and short times, simple and compound interest look almost the same — the gap was only R138,38 after three years. Does the difference ever really matter?

**Tutor:** It matters more than almost anything else in personal finance, and the reason is time. In year one the two methods are identical twins: both deliver 7,5% of R8 000, which is R600. The divergence begins in year two, when compound interest works on R8 600 while simple interest still stares at the original R8 000 — a difference of only R45 that year. But the gap does not stay polite. It compounds too. After 3 years compound leads by R138,38; after 5 years by R485,03; run the table out to 20 years and the compound investment is thousands of rands ahead of the straight line, on the very same rate and the very same deposit. On a graph the two are easy to tell apart: simple interest is a ruler-straight line, compound interest is a curve that bends away from it upward, and the space between them widens forever. That is why the sensible reading of the lesson is directional: for savings you want the curve, because time is on your side; for debt you fear the curve, because unpaid compound interest snowballs against you exactly as efficiently as it snowballs for a saver. Short race, small gap — but almost nothing in real finance is a short race.

**Mandy:** Next one, straight off a homework statement: the balance went down twice in one month with no shopping at all. Where does money go when nobody spends it?

**Tutor:** It goes to the bank's tariff table, and a statement shows exactly where if you read the narration column and not just the amounts. Two kinds of quiet lines take money without a purchase. The first is fees: a monthly admin or account fee — the R6,50 on our statement — is charged for the account existing at all, and transaction fees follow specific actions, like the R9,00 for drawing cash at another bank's ATM, where your own bank's machine would have been cheaper. Declined debit orders, paper statements and immediate payments carry fees too, each listed in the account's tariff brochure. The second kind is debit orders: payments the account owner authorised once, months ago, that then run automatically — a store account, a policy, a gym. Nothing was bought this month, yet the money flows out on schedule. The defence is a monthly five-minute audit: read every line, circle every fee, confirm every debit order is one you still want, and then rebuild the closing balance yourself — opening balance plus credits minus debits, to the cent. On our statement that check lands exactly on R4 125,25, which proves the bank's arithmetic and, more importantly, proves you understand your own money. Fees of R15,50 a month look tiny; R186 a year is real money, and choosing the right account type is how you shrink it.

**Mandy:** This one caused a debate: if an investment earns 7,5% but inflation is 6%, one learner says you gained 7,5%, another says you gained almost nothing. Who is right?

**Tutor:** The second learner is closer to the truth, and the distinction between the two answers is the entire point of the inflation section. The account balance genuinely grew by 7,5% — nominally, in rands, the first learner is correct. But rands are not the true measure of wealth; what the rands can buy is. While the money grew 7,5%, the prices of bread, taxi fares and electricity grew about 6%, so most of the growth was merely keeping pace with the shrinking value of each rand. The real gain — the growth in actual buying power — is roughly the difference, about 1,5%. That is a genuine gain: the investment beat inflation, and the saver can buy more next year than this year, just not 7,5% more. Now run the same logic on the sadder cases. Money in a low-interest account earning 3% during 6% inflation is losing about 3% of its buying power a year while its number grows — a shrinking loaf wrapped in a growing price tag. And cash under the mattress earns 0%, so it loses the full 6% every year, guaranteed; at that rate a R100 note keeps only about R55 of its power after ten years. So the exam-ready habit is to compare every growth rate to the inflation rate before pronouncing a verdict: above inflation is a real gain, equal to inflation is standing still, and below inflation — including every mattress in the country — is a real loss.

**Mandy:** Last one: a learner's gogo says banks just eat your money with fees, so cash at home is safer. What does the arithmetic of this lesson say to her?

**Tutor:** It says the mattress is the most expensive bank in South Africa, with respect to every gogo who trusts it. Put the fear into numbers. Suppose the account charges R15,50 a month in fees — R186 a year — and pays 7,5% interest on her R8 000. The interest in the first year is R600, so even after every fee the account leaves her about R414 ahead, and compounding widens that lead each year. The mattress charges no fees, but inflation charges it 6% of buying power annually — R480 of value gone from R8 000 in the first year alone, and again the next year, relentlessly. So the account gains ground while the mattress bleeds it, before even mentioning fire, theft or the temptation of money within arm's reach. The honest concession is that fees are real and worth fighting: accounts differ enormously, low-fee accounts exist precisely for savers like her, drawing cash at your own bank's ATM costs less, and a fixed deposit — where the money is locked away for an agreed term — typically pays a higher rate while charging nothing monthly. That is the fair comparison the exam loves: not bank versus mattress on emotion, but two full calculations, interest earned minus fees paid on one side, buying power lost on the other, and a one-sentence verdict for that household. On these numbers the verdict is not close.
