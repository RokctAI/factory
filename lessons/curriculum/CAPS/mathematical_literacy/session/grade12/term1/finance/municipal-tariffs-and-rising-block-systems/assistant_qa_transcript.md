**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! The one that comes up every single year: for 380 units, why can't I just multiply all 380 by R2,60, since 380 falls inside block two?

**Tutor:** Because the block a consumption FINISHES in is not the block it was all charged in. Think of the blocks as containers that fill from the bottom. The first 100 units always land in block one and are charged at block one's price, R2,20 each — that is R220. Only the units that spill over into block two get block two's price: 380 minus 100 leaves 280 units at R2,60, which is R728. The correct total is R948. Multiplying all 380 by R2,60 gives R988, and the R40 difference is exactly the discount the tariff gives you on your first 100 units — a discount the wrong method throws away. The tariff table is written as ranges, 0 to 100, 101 to 400, and the range tells you which units it prices, not which customers it applies to. A reliable habit for the exam: before multiplying anything, write down how many units sit in each block — 100 in the first, 280 in the second, zero in the rest — and check that those numbers add back to the total consumption. If your block units do not sum to 380, the bill will be wrong no matter how careful the multiplication is.

**Mandy:** Next one, from the water bill: the household used 22 kilolitres, so why is the middle block charged for 9 kilolitres and not 15?

**Tutor:** Because 15 is where the block ENDS, not how many units it holds. The middle block runs from 6 kl to 15 kl, and its capacity is the difference between those boundaries: 15 minus 6, which is 9 kilolitres. The first 6 kl were already accounted for in the free block, so by the time the household's consumption reaches the middle block, only kilolitres number seven through fifteen pass through it — nine of them, at R18,50 each, R166,50. Then the remaining 22 minus 15, which is 7 kilolitres, land in the top block at R26,40. This boundary subtraction is where most marks are lost in tariff questions, because the table shows the numbers 6, 15 and 30 so prominently that learners multiply by them directly. Train yourself to convert every range into a capacity first: 0 to 6 holds 6; 6 to 15 holds 9; 15 to 30 holds 15. Then check the used amounts against those capacities — free block full at 6, middle block full at 9, top block holding the last 7 — and confirm that 6 plus 9 plus 7 gives back the 22 kilolitres on the meter. That check takes ten seconds and catches nearly every boundary error.

**Mandy:** This one caused an argument: someone said prepaid electricity is always cheaper because there is no fixed monthly charge. Is that true?

**Tutor:** It is true only for some households, and the whole point of the comparison skill is to find out which. The fixed charge is a bad deal for small users and a good deal for big ones, because it is the same R220 regardless of usage. A household using 380 units spreads that R220 over 380 units — about 58 cents extra per unit — and for them prepaid wins, R948 against R999. But a household using 600 units spreads the same R220 much thinner, about 37 cents per unit, and meanwhile the prepaid staircase is punishing them: their last 200 units cost R2,95 each, while the conventional deal keeps charging a flat R2,05. At 600 units the conventional option costs R1 450 against prepaid's R1 590, and prepaid loses by R140. So the honest answer is: prepaid is cheaper below the break-even consumption and more expensive above it, and the break-even sits where the two cost lines cross on the graph, somewhere between those two usage levels. In an exam, never answer a comparison question with a general rule. Calculate both options at the consumption the question gives, name the cheaper one, state the saving in rands, and tie your advice to that specific household's usage. The examiner is testing whether you compare, not whether you memorised a slogan.

**Mandy:** Last one: when does the VAT go on? Someone added 15% to each block separately and someone else added it right at the end, and they got confused about whether it matters.

**Tutor:** Mathematically it gives the same answer either way, because 15% of a sum equals the sum of 15% of the parts — but one of the two methods is far safer, and the mark scheme is built around it. Add all the consumption charges and any fixed charge first, write that line down as the subtotal, and apply VAT once, to the whole subtotal. On the water bill: R166,50 plus R184,80 gives R351,30, VAT is 351,30 times 0,15, which is R52,70 to the nearest cent, and the amount payable is R404,00. Doing VAT block by block multiplies your rounding opportunities — each block's VAT gets rounded to a cent, and three small roundings can drift the total by a cent or two away from the mark scheme's answer. Doing it once at the end matches the way the real bill is printed: consumption lines, subtotal, VAT line, total due. Two cautions while we are here. First, if a block is free, its VAT is zero — you never add VAT to the free basic water. Second, if a question hands you a total that already includes VAT and asks for the consumption charges, do not subtract 15%: divide by 1,15, because the inclusive total is 115% of the subtotal. The bill reads downward with VAT last; your working should read the same way.
