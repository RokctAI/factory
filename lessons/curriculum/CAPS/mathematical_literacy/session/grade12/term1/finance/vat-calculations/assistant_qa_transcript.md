**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! The big one first, because it comes up every year: why is taking 15% off an inclusive price wrong when adding 15% to an exclusive price is right? It feels like the same move in reverse.

**Tutor:** It feels symmetrical, but percentages are not symmetrical, because the two moves take their 15% from different bases. Adding VAT takes 15% of the SMALLER number, the exclusive price: 15% of R260 is R39, and R260 grows to R299. Now try to reverse it by taking 15% of R299 — but R299 is the bigger number, so 15% of it is R44,85, a thicker slice than the R39 that was ever added. Subtract it and you land at R254,15, below the true R260. The genuine reverse of multiplying by 1,15 is dividing by 1,15, the same way the reverse of doubling is halving, not subtracting the same amount you added. The inclusive price is 115% of the original, so dividing by 1,15 recovers the original 100% exactly: 299 divided by 1,15 is 260. And you can always certify your answer in five seconds by running it forwards — 260 times 1,15 must give back the 299 on the shelf. If a backwards answer cannot reproduce the price you started from, it is wrong, no matter how reasonable it looked on the way down.

**Mandy:** Next: a shop is advertising a fridge at R6 899 and the poster says price includes VAT. The question asked for the VAT amount, and half the class multiplied by 0,15. What is the fastest correct route?

**Tutor:** Two correct routes exist, and both start from the same fact: R6 899 is 115%, not 100%. Route one is the two-step: divide by 1,15 to find the exclusive price — 6 899 divided by 1,15 gives R5 999,13 to the nearest cent — then subtract from the inclusive price: 6 899 minus 5 999,13 gives VAT of R899,87. Route two is the direct fraction: VAT is fifteen parts of every 115 parts of an inclusive price, so multiply 6 899 by 15 and divide by 115, which gives the same R899,87 in one move. What you may never do is multiply the inclusive price by 0,15, because that computes 15% of a number that already contains the tax — it treats R6 899 as if it were the pre-VAT price, and it inflates the VAT to R1 034,85. The quick mental test that guards you: the VAT amount must always be smaller than 15% of the sticker price, never equal to it, precisely because the sticker is the swollen 115% figure. In the exam, show the fraction 15 over 115 or the divide-by-1,15 step explicitly — that visible step is usually where the method mark lives.

**Mandy:** This one caused an argument: someone said their whole till slip should have VAT taken out with the 15 over 115 fraction, and someone else said you cannot, because of the bread. Who is right?

**Tutor:** The one who mentioned the bread. The fraction 15 over 115 is perfectly correct — but only on money that actually contains VAT, and a South African trolley is nearly always a mixture. Brown bread, maize meal, rice, milk, eggs, fresh fruit and vegetables, dried beans, tinned pilchards, cooking oil — these are zero-rated, taxed at 0% by deliberate policy so that basic food stays affordable, and their prices contain not one cent of VAT. Apply 15 over 115 to a total that includes them and you invent tax that was never charged. On our Saturday slip the zero-rated pile came to R187,40 and the starred, standard-rated pile to R412,85; the honest VAT is 15 over 115 of the R412,85 only, which is R53,85, not 15 over 115 of the R600,25 total, which would claim R78,29. So the order of operations for any till-slip question is fixed: sort first, calculate second. And learn the trap pairs the examiners love — brown bread zero-rated but white bread taxed, fresh vegetables zero-rated but frozen chips taxed, raw eggs zero-rated but a takeaway omelette taxed, because preparation moves food off the protected list. When a question mentions a till slip, your first pen stroke should be splitting it into the two piles.

**Mandy:** Last one: a builder's quote said R14 800 excluding VAT, and someone answered the customer pays R14 800, arguing VAT is the builder's problem. How should we think about who actually pays?

**Tutor:** Follow the money one step at a time and the roles become clear. The builder is a registered vendor, which makes him a tax COLLECTOR, not the tax payer. When the job is invoiced, the law obliges him to add 15% — R2 220 on that quote — so the customer's bill reads R17 020. The customer pays the full R17 020; the builder keeps R14 800 for the work and holds the R2 220 in trust for SARS. When he does his VAT return, he pays that R2 220 over, minus any VAT he himself paid on materials — which is why the system is called value-added: each link in the chain pays tax only on the value it adds. But trace the whole chain to its end and every cent of VAT was funded by one person: the final consumer, the one with nobody further down the chain to charge. That is why exam questions phrase it as the amount payable by the customer — and why answering R14 800 loses the marks, because an excluding-VAT price is by definition not what a consumer pays. Practical reading habit: on any quote or invoice, find the VAT line before the total. If the words excl. VAT appear anywhere, the real cost to you is the printed figure times 1,15, and it is worth doing that multiplication before you sign anything.
