# Part 1 — Expert

Almost nothing big in South Africa is bought with cash on the counter. The lounge suite goes on hire purchase, the car on finance with a balloon at the end, the emergency on a personal loan, and the risks of life — the funeral, the crash — are carried by insurance premiums. Every one of these is a deal with a price hiding inside it, and matric papers test whether you can find that price. This session follows one street in Polokwane: a lounge suite bought on instalments, a first car financed with a balloon payment, a R25 000 personal loan, and the policies that pay out on the worst day.

## Subtopic: Hire Purchase: The True Price of Pay-Later

HIRE PURCHASE, written HP, is buying on instalments: pay a DEPOSIT now, take the goods home today, and pay the balance in equal monthly INSTALMENTS — with interest and charges loaded inside them. Until the final instalment, the goods legally still belong to the store.

Mrs Mokoena wants a lounge suite with a CASH PRICE of R11 999. The HP deal: a 10% deposit and 24 monthly instalments of R589,50. First the deposit: 10% of 11 999 is R1 199,90. Then the instalments: 589,50 × 24 = R14 148,00. The TOTAL HP PRICE is deposit plus all instalments: 1 199,90 + 14 148,00 = R15 347,90.

Now the number the exam always wants: the EXTRA paid for the convenience of paying later. 15 347,90 − 11 999 = R3 348,90 — nearly 28% more than the cash price. That extra is the interest and fees of the agreement, and the comparison sentence is compulsory: on HP the suite costs R3 348,90 more than paying cash.

The gentler cousin is LAY-BY: pay the store in instalments with no interest, but the goods stay in the store until the last payment. The same suite on a six-month lay-by costs exactly R11 999 — about R2 000 a month — but Mrs Mokoena sits on her old couch until it is paid. HP sells time; lay-by sells patience. The questions for this section are with you now; calculate deposit, total instalments and total HP price separately, because each carries its own mark.

## Subtopic: Car Finance: Deposits, Instalments and the Balloon

A car is usually the biggest HP-style deal a household ever signs, and it comes with a special trap: the BALLOON PAYMENT, also called a RESIDUAL — a large lump sum left over at the END of the agreement, after all the monthly instalments are done.

Thabo finances a car priced at R189 900. The agreement: a 10% deposit of R18 990, then 60 monthly repayments of R3 499, and a balloon payment of 30% of the price — R56 970 — due in month sixty. The balloon's job is seduction: because R56 970 of the debt is pushed to the end, the monthly instalment looks affordable. Without the balloon, the same loan would need a much higher monthly payment.

Total the deal honestly. Repayments: 3 499 × 60 = R209 940. Add the deposit and the balloon: 18 990 + 209 940 + 56 970 = R285 900. The car costs R285 900 in total — R96 000 more than the price on the windscreen. And the balloon is not optional: in month sixty Thabo must pay R56 970 in one blow, refinance it into a new loan with new interest, or sell the car to settle it.

The exam skills: compute each component, total the true cost, compare against the cash price, and answer the interpretation question — why do balloon deals advertise such low instalments, and what waits at the end? The questions for this section follow now; list deposit, instalments and balloon as three separate lines before adding.

## Subtopic: Personal Loans and the Total Cost of Credit

A PERSONAL LOAN is borrowed cash: the bank pays out a lump sum, and the borrower repays monthly over a fixed TERM. The advertised monthly repayment is never the whole story, because loans carry FEES: a once-off INITIATION FEE when the loan starts, and a monthly SERVICE FEE for administering it.

Lerato borrows R25 000 over 36 months. The repayment is R1 095 per month, the initiation fee is R1 207,50, and the service fee is R69 per month. Her true monthly outflow is 1 095 + 69 = R1 164. The TOTAL COST OF CREDIT question asks: how much does the loan cost in total, and how much of that is the price of borrowing?

Total paid: the initiation fee plus 36 months of repayment-plus-service-fee: 1 207,50 + 36 × 1 164 = 1 207,50 + 41 904 = R43 111,50. The cost of credit is everything paid minus the cash received: 43 111,50 − 25 000 = R18 111,50. Borrowing R25 000 cost more than R18 000 — over 72% of the loan itself.

Affordability is the other examinable angle, and it is an income-and-expenditure calculation. Lerato's monthly income is R9 800; her expenses — rent, transport, food, electricity, airtime — total R8 400, leaving a SURPLUS of R1 400. The instalment of R1 164 fits, but only just: R236 of breathing room, one taxi-fare crisis away from a missed payment. Lenders must run this check by law, and exam questions ask you to run it too. The questions for this section are ready; separate the once-off fee from the monthly ones, and let total paid minus amount borrowed give the cost of credit.

## Subtopic: Insurance: Premiums, Cover and the Excess

INSURANCE is a deal about risk: you pay a small, certain amount every month — the PREMIUM — and the insurer pays a large amount — the COVER — if the insured disaster strikes. The household on our street carries two policies.

The first is FUNERAL COVER: R120 per month for R30 000 of cover. Funeral insurance is the most widely held policy in South Africa because funerals are certain, sudden and expensive. The arithmetic of the deal: R120 × 12 = R1 440 per year in premiums. To pay in R30 000 at that rate would take 250 months — over twenty years — yet the full R30 000 pays out even if the policy is three months old. That is the point of insurance: the many who do not claim this year carry the few who must.

The second is CAR INSURANCE at R850 per month — R10 200 a year. Car policies carry an EXCESS: the first part of any claim that the owner must pay personally. Thabo's excess is R4 500. When a crash causes R28 600 of damage, the insurer pays 28 600 − 4 500 = R24 100, and Thabo pays the R4 500. The excess exists to keep small claims out of the system and premiums lower.

The examinable comparisons: premium versus cover, total premiums over a period versus a claim, and the effect of the excess on a payout. One more reading skill — a car financed with a balloon almost always REQUIRES comprehensive insurance in the contract, because the bank wants its R285 900 protected. The questions for this section are with you now; name the premium, the cover and the excess before calculating anything.

## Subtopic: The Lounge Suite That Cost a Third More

The price tag in the shop window says R11 999. Mrs Mokoena walks out having agreed to pay R15 347,90. Nobody robbed her — she signed for it, and the signing is called hire purchase.

Here is the deal in plain words: pay a slice now — the deposit, R1 199,90 — take the couch home today, and pay R589,50 a month for two years. Each payment feels small. That is the design. But do the multiplication the shop hopes you skip: 589,50 times 24 is R14 148. Add the deposit back: R15 347,90. The couch costs R3 348,90 more than the window price — nearly a third extra — and until the last payment, the couch still belongs to the store. Miss payments, and it can go back on the truck.

The alternative in the same shop is lay-by: pay about R2 000 a month for six months, pay exactly R11 999 in total, not one cent of interest — but the couch waits in the storeroom until the money is done. So the real choice is honest: HP buys the couch today and charges R3 348,90 for the favour; lay-by costs nothing extra but tests your patience for half a year.

The exam move, every single time: total everything you would pay — deposit plus every instalment — and stand it next to the cash price. The gap is the price of impatience, and naming that gap in rands is where the marks live. Some questions on this section are with you right now; multiply, add the deposit, compare, and say the difference out loud.

## Subtopic: The Bubble at the End of the Car Loan

Thabo's eyes are on the monthly number: R3 499. Tight, but doable. The salesperson smiles, because the number was built to be looked at. The trick is floating sixty months away: a balloon payment of R56 970.

A balloon deal is simple once you see the shape. Take the car's price, R189 900. Slice off a deposit, R18 990. Then, instead of spreading ALL the remaining debt over the sixty months, push a fat bubble of it — 30% of the price — to the very end. The monthly instalments only carry the middle part, so they look light. The bubble waits.

Add the whole thing up like an accountant, not like a dreamer: deposit R18 990, plus sixty payments of R3 499 — that is R209 940 — plus the balloon, R56 970. Total: R285 900, for a car that was priced at R189 900. R96 000 extra. And in month sixty, the bubble bursts into a bill: pay R56 970 cash, or borrow AGAIN to cover it — new loan, new interest, the debt getting a second life — or sell the car to settle it and start from nothing.

None of this makes balloon deals evil; it makes them expensive, and the law says the dealer must show you the total. The skill is refusing to judge a deal by its monthly payment. Judge it by one number: everything you will ever pay, added up. Questions on this section are coming your way now; find the three pieces — deposit, instalments, balloon — and total them before any opinion.

## Subtopic: Paying for the Worst Day Before It Comes

Every month, R120 leaves the account for funeral cover, and every month nothing happens. Gogo asks the fair question: is this not money thrown into the river?

Look at what the R120 actually buys: a promise of R30 000 on the family's hardest day. Save R120 a month in a tin instead, and reaching R30 000 takes 250 months — more than twenty years. The policy pays the full amount even if the worst day comes in month three. That is the machine of insurance: thousands of families each put in a little, and the money flows to the few whose worst day arrived this month. You are not buying a payout; you are buying certainty that the funeral will not bury the family in debt as well as grief.

The car policy runs the same logic with one extra lever: the excess. Thabo pays R850 a month, and when the crash comes — R28 600 of damage — the insurer pays R24 100 and he covers the first R4 500 himself. The excess is the deal's way of saying: bring us the disasters, not the scratches. Without it, every tiny claim would push everyone's premiums up.

And the two halves of this lesson lock together: the bank financing Thabo's R285 900 car insists on insurance, because until the balloon is paid, the car is the bank's security. A financed car that burns uninsured leaves the debt alive with nothing to show for it — the worst outcome in this whole topic. So the closing habit: premium, cover, excess — name all three on any policy, and weigh the certain small cost against the possible huge one. The final questions of the lesson are with you now; calculate what the insurer pays after the excess, and say in a sentence what the premium is really buying.
