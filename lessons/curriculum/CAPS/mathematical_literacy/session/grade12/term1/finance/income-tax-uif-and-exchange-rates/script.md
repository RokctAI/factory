# Part 1 — Expert

Two forces reach into a South African payslip and a South African wallet from opposite directions: the taxman, who takes a share of what you earn, and the exchange rate, which decides what your remaining rands can buy from the rest of the world. Matric Paper 1 tests both. This session follows Zanele, a radiographer at a Polokwane hospital earning R28 500 a month, from her gross salary through the tax table to her net pay — and then follows her rands abroad, where they are measured against the American dollar.

## Subtopic: From Gross Salary to Taxable Income

GROSS INCOME is everything earned before anything is subtracted: basic salary, overtime, bonuses, allowances. But personal income tax is not charged on gross income — it is charged on TAXABLE INCOME, which is what remains after removing income the law does not tax and deductions the law allows.

Some money is NON-TAXABLE: inheritances, most bursaries and scholarships, donations between spouses, and the portion of interest income below the annual exemption. Other amounts are DEDUCTIONS that shrink taxable income before the tax table is applied — the most important being contributions to a pension or retirement annuity fund, within limits set by SARS.

Zanele's numbers. Her gross salary is R28 500 per month, which is 28 500 × 12 = R342 000 per year. For this lesson she has no deductions, so her taxable income is R342 000. Her employer reports all of this to SARS on a certificate called an IRP5, which records the year's income and every cent of tax already deducted — the document every employee should check against their own payslips.

Vocabulary that carries marks: the TAX YEAR for individuals runs from 1 March to the last day of February. PAYE, pay-as-you-earn, is the monthly deduction the employer sends to SARS in advance, so that the year's tax is collected gradually rather than in one impossible lump. The TAX THRESHOLD is the taxable income below which no tax is payable at all — for a person under 65, R95 750 in the 2023/24 year. Earn less than that and the tax table never touches you. The questions for this section are with you now; separate gross, non-taxable and taxable before any table is opened.

## Subtopic: Reading the Tax Table and the Rebate

The income tax table is a STAIRCASE OF RATES: each band of income is taxed at its own percentage, higher bands at higher rates. In the 2023/24 table, the first band runs to R237 100 and is taxed at 18%. The second band, from R237 101 to R370 500, is taxed at 26% — but only the income INSIDE that band. The table prints this as: R42 678 plus 26% of taxable income above R237 100. The R42 678 is the tax on the whole first band, already worked out: 18% of 237 100.

Apply it to Zanele's taxable income of R342 000. She is in the second band, because 342 000 lies between 237 101 and 370 500. Tax before rebates: 42 678 + 26% of the amount above 237 100. The amount above: 342 000 − 237 100 = R104 900. Then 104 900 × 0,26 = R27 274,00. Add the first-band tax: 42 678 + 27 274 = R69 952,00.

Now the REBATE — a discount every taxpayer receives, subtracted from the calculated tax. The primary rebate for under-65s is R17 235. Zanele's annual tax: 69 952 − 17 235 = R52 717,00. Monthly PAYE: 52 717 ÷ 12 = R4 393,08 to the nearest cent.

The classic error is to charge 26% on ALL R342 000, which gives R88 920 — massively wrong, because only the slice above R237 100 is taxed at 26%; everything below kept its 18% rate inside the R42 678. The rebate is also where the threshold comes from: tax only becomes payable once the calculated tax exceeds the rebate. The questions for this section are ready; find the band, tax only the slice above the band's floor, then subtract the rebate.

## Subtopic: UIF and What the Payslip Shows

Beyond PAYE, one more statutory deduction appears on nearly every payslip: UIF, the Unemployment Insurance Fund. The employee contributes 1% of their salary, the employer adds another 1%, and the fund pays benefits during unemployment, maternity leave or illness.

The 1% has a ceiling. Contributions are calculated only on salary up to a maximum — R17 712 per month in this period — so the employee contribution can never exceed 1% of that maximum: R177,12. Zanele earns R28 500, which is above the ceiling, so her UIF is not 1% of 28 500; it is the capped R177,12. A colleague earning R12 000, below the ceiling, would pay the straightforward 1%: R120,00.

Now assemble Zanele's payslip. Gross salary: R28 500,00. Deduction one, PAYE: R4 393,08. Deduction two, UIF: R177,12. NET PAY, the amount actually landing in her bank account: 28 500 − 4 393,08 − 177,12 = R23 929,80.

Read the proportions the way an examiner asks. Total deductions: 4 393,08 + 177,12 = R4 570,20. As a percentage of gross: 4 570,20 ÷ 28 500 × 100 = 16,04%, to two decimal places. So roughly one rand in six leaves before Zanele sees her salary — a sentence worth writing, because interpretation marks sit at the end of every payslip question.

Note what UIF is not: it is not a savings account, and the employer's matching 1% never appears as a deduction on the employee's slip — it is paid on top. The questions for this section follow now; apply the ceiling before the percentage, and end at net pay in a sentence.

## Subtopic: Exchange Rates and Buying Power

An EXCHANGE RATE is the price of one currency measured in another. The quotation R18,50 to the dollar means one American dollar costs eighteen rand fifty. Written as a rate: R18,50 per dollar.

Two directions, one decision. Converting DOLLARS TO RANDS multiplies, because each dollar becomes R18,50 of ours: an American tourist exchanging 450 dollars receives 450 × 18,50 = R8 325,00. Converting RANDS TO DOLLARS divides, because every R18,50 buys just one dollar: Zanele saving R5 000 for a trip receives 5 000 ÷ 18,50 = 270,27 dollars, to two decimal places.

The reliable test: a dollar is worth many rands, so a number in dollars must GROW when it becomes rands and SHRINK when rands become dollars. If your rand answer is smaller than the dollar amount you started with, the operation went the wrong way.

Now the language of movement. If the rate shifts from R18,50 to R19,20 per dollar, the dollar now costs more rand — the rand has WEAKENED, or depreciated. Imported goods, fuel and overseas travel become more expensive for South Africans: a laptop priced at 899 dollars rises from 899 × 18,50 = R16 631,50 to 899 × 19,20 = R17 260,80. If the rate falls to R17,80, the rand has STRENGTHENED: imports cheapen, and exporters earn fewer rands for the same dollar sales.

That is BUYING POWER: the weaker the rand, the less of the world each rand can buy. Examiners ask for the calculation and then the consequence — who wins, who loses, in a sentence. The questions for this section are with you now; write the rate as rand per dollar, choose multiply or divide by the growth test, and finish with the effect on buying power.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same payslip and the same rands, at the kitchen table with a calculator and a cup of tea.

## Subtopic: The Ladder of Tax Brackets

Here is the fear that lives in every workplace: do not take the increase, it will push you into a higher bracket and you will take home less. That fear rests on a misunderstanding, and one picture cures it.

Income tax is a ladder. The first rung of your income — everything up to R237 100 a year — is taxed at 18 cents in the rand. Only the money ABOVE that line climbs to the next rung, where it is taxed at 26 cents in the rand. Moving into a higher bracket never re-taxes the money on the lower rungs; it only means your newest rands, the ones above the line, pay the higher rate.

Watch it work for Zanele, who earns R342 000 a year. The tax table has already done the first rung for us: tax on the full R237 100 at 18% comes to R42 678 — the table prints that number so nobody recalculates it. Her income above the line is 342 000 minus 237 100: R104 900. That slice, and only that slice, pays 26%: R27 274. Add the two rungs: R69 952.

Then comes the good news line: the REBATE. The government gives every taxpayer a discount of R17 235 off the calculated tax. So Zanele truly owes 69 952 minus 17 235: R52 717 for the year, about R4 393 a month, which her employer quietly sends to SARS as PAYE before her salary lands.

And the wrong way? Charging 26% on the whole R342 000 gives R88 920 — nearly R19 000 too much tax. The ladder never does that. Some questions on this section are with you right now; tax the slice above the line, never the whole salary, and remember the discount at the end.

## Subtopic: One Percent for the Rainy Day

There is a small deduction on the payslip that most people never read: UIF. One percent of your salary, matched by another one percent from your employer, into a national fund that pays you when the work stops — retrenchment, maternity leave, long illness. It is insurance, priced at one cent in every rand.

The one wrinkle worth knowing is the ceiling. The fund only counts salary up to R17 712 a month. Earn less than that, and your UIF is a clean one percent — on R12 000 you pay R120. Earn more, like Zanele on R28 500, and the counting stops at the ceiling: her UIF is one percent of 17 712, which is R177,12, and not one percent of 28 500, which would be R285. The high earner and the very high earner pay the same capped amount.

Now put the whole payslip together like a shopping receipt in reverse. Zanele's gross: R28 500. The taxman takes R4 393,08. The rainy-day fund takes R177,12. What actually arrives in the bank — the net pay — is 28 500 minus those two: R23 929,80.

Step back and read it like a person, not a calculator: out of every hundred rand Zanele earns, about sixteen leave before she wakes up on payday. That sentence — deductions are about 16% of gross — is exactly the kind of plain-language conclusion the exam pays marks for. Questions on this section are coming your way now; check the ceiling first, then subtract to net pay, then say what the payslip means in one sentence.

## Subtopic: Rands per Dollar: Which Way to Divide

Exchange rates confuse people for one reason only: they cannot decide whether to multiply or divide. Fix the rate in your head as a price tag and the decision makes itself.

R18,50 per dollar means exactly what a price tag means: one dollar COSTS R18,50, the same grammar as bananas at R18,50 a kilogram. Buying 450 dollars is like buying 450 kilograms — you multiply: 450 times 18,50 is R8 325. Turning R5 000 into dollars asks how many R18,50 price tags fit into R5 000 — you divide: 5 000 divided by 18,50 is about 270 dollars.

Then run the sanity check: rands are the small unit, dollars the big one. Rand amounts should always be the bigger number, dollar amounts the smaller. If your R5 000 turned into 92 500 dollars, the machine ran the wrong way — nobody gets rich at a bureau de change.

Last piece: what it means when the rate moves. From R18,50 to R19,20 per dollar, the price tag on a dollar went UP — the rand weakened. Everything imported quietly follows: the 899-dollar laptop climbs from about R16 630 to about R17 260, petrol follows the same arithmetic, and your rand buys a little less of the world. When the rate drops to R17,80, the rand strengthened, and imports breathe out. Weaker rand, expensive imports; stronger rand, cheaper imports — say it with the numbers and the interpretation mark is yours.

The final questions of the lesson are with you now; treat the rate as a price tag, multiply when buying dollars-worth of rands, divide when buying dollars, and finish with what the movement does to your buying power.
