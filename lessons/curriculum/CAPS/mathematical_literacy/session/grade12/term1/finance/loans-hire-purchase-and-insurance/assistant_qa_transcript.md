**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! The one that comes up a lot: if hire purchase makes a R11 999 couch cost R15 347,90, why would anyone ever choose it over lay-by?

**Tutor:** Because the two products sell different things, and sometimes the expensive one is genuinely the rational choice. Lay-by sells a price: pay about R2 000 a month for six months, pay exactly R11 999, not a cent of interest — but the couch lives in the storeroom until the last payment. Hire purchase sells time: the couch is in your lounge tonight, and you pay R3 348,90 for that privilege over two years. If the item is a luxury, lay-by wins on pure arithmetic almost every time. But picture the item being a fridge in January, or a bed, or the stove a family cooks on — six months of waiting has a real cost too, just not one printed on a receipt. The honest exam answer computes both totals, names the difference in rands, and then matches the deal to the household's situation: HP when the goods are needed now and the budget can carry the instalment; lay-by when patience is affordable and the extra R3 348,90 is better kept. What is never rational is signing an HP agreement without doing the total — deposit plus every instalment — because the monthly figure of R589,50 is designed to feel small, and only the total reveals that the couch costs nearly a third more. And remember the ownership clause: on HP the store owns the couch until the final payment, so missed instalments can take it back off your floor after months of paying.

**Mandy:** Next: the car adverts with tiny instalments and a balloon payment — my cousin says the balloon is just a formality the dealer sorts out later. True or dangerous?

**Tutor:** Dangerous, and the arithmetic shows exactly how dangerous. The balloon is not a formality; it is real debt — 30% of the car's price, R56 970 on our deal — that the agreement simply postpones to month sixty. Nothing about postponement makes it smaller; in fact interest arrangements around a residual typically make the whole deal dearer, which is why Thabo's R189 900 car totals R285 900 once the deposit, the sixty instalments of R3 499 and the balloon are all added. When month sixty arrives, exactly three doors exist. Door one: pay R56 970 in cash, which very few households have waiting after five years of instalments. Door two: refinance — borrow again to pay the balloon, meaning new interest on old debt, and the car keeps costing money into its eighth or ninth year. Door three: sell the car to settle the balloon, and start again with no car and no savings, hoping the car is still worth more than R56 970 by then. The reason dealers love the structure is the reason buyers should be wary of it: the advertised instalment answers the question can I afford this month, while the balloon quietly changes the answer to can I afford month sixty. The law obliges the credit agreement to state the total cost, so the defence is simply to read it: one line, everything you will ever pay, and that line — not the instalment — is the price of the car.

**Mandy:** This one came from a homework argument: the loan advert said R1 095 per month, but the memo's answer for total cost was thousands higher. Which fees count?

**Tutor:** All of them — that is the whole principle of the total cost of credit, and it is why the advertised instalment is never the finish line. The National Credit Act allows a lender three main charges: interest, which is baked inside the R1 095 repayment; a once-off initiation fee for setting up the loan, R1 207,50 on Lerato's agreement; and a monthly service fee for running the account, R69 here. The method is one honest sum: everything paid out, minus everything received. Lerato pays the initiation fee, plus thirty-six months of repayment and service fee together — 1 095 plus 69 is R1 164 a month, times 36 is R41 904 — so R43 111,50 leaves her pocket over the term. She received R25 000. The credit cost her 43 111,50 minus 25 000, which is R18 111,50 — more than 72% of what she borrowed. Memos award marks for each component, so list them separately before totalling: repayments, service fees, initiation fee. And the companion skill is affordability, which is an income-and-expenditure statement in miniature: income R9 800, expenses R8 400, surplus R1 400, instalment R1 164 — it fits, with only R236 to spare, and that thin margin is exactly the sort of interpretive point an exam asks you to comment on. A loan that fits on paper with no room for a single emergency is a loan that fits badly.

**Mandy:** Last one, from Gogo herself: years of premiums, never a single claim. Would the family not be richer just saving the money?

**Tutor:** Run both plans forward and let the worst day arrive in each one — that is the only fair test of insurance, because insurance is bought for the worst day, not the average one. Plan one, the tin: R120 a month saved faithfully. After two years the tin holds R2 880. If the funeral comes then, the family is R27 120 short, and shortfalls like that are exactly how funeral debt starts — often on a personal loan whose cost of credit we have just seen. Plan two, the policy: the same R120 a month, and if the funeral comes in month three or month three hundred, the full R30 000 pays out. The premium does not buy a payout; it buys the removal of a risk the family cannot absorb. The tin would need 250 months — nearly twenty-one years of perfect, untouched saving — just to match what the policy promises from its first month. Where Gogo's instinct deserves respect is on the details: cover should match real funeral costs, premiums must fit the budget because a lapsed policy can pay nothing, and waiting periods in the fine print matter. The same weighing applies to the car: R850 a month feels heavy until the R28 600 crash, when the insurer's R24 100 — the damage minus the R4 500 excess — is the difference between an inconvenience and a catastrophe, especially on a financed car the bank still effectively owns. Premiums are not wasted money; they are the price of the family never facing its biggest bills alone.
