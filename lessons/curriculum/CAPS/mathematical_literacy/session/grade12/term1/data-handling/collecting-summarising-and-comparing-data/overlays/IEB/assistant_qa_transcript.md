**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! The one that returns every year: with eleven values the median was easy — the sixth patient on the bench. But what happens with an even number, say twelve values? People got stuck between two patients.

**Tutor:** With an even count there is no single middle patient, so the median becomes the halfway point between the two middle values. Order the twelve values, count to positions six and seven, and take the mean of just those two: add them and divide by two. If patient six waited 40 minutes and patient seven waited 50, the median is 45 minutes — a value nobody actually recorded, and that is perfectly fine, because the median is a position marker, not necessarily a member of the data. A quick way to find the middle position for any count: add one to the number of values and divide by two. Eleven values gives position six exactly; twelve values gives position six and a half, which tells you to average the sixth and seventh. The same principle carries into quartiles: when a half of the data has an even count, its quartile is the average of the two values straddling the middle of that half. What never changes, odd or even, is the first commandment: order the data before counting anything. Most lost marks in this topic come from counting to the middle of an unordered list, which produces a confident, well-presented, wrong answer.

**Mandy:** Next: someone asked why we bother with quartiles and the IQR when we already have the range. The range felt easier and seemed to use all the data.

**Tutor:** The range feels like it uses all the data, but it actually uses only two values — the biggest and the smallest — and those are precisely the two least trustworthy numbers in any data set, because that is where the freak values live. Ntabeni's range is 120 minus 20, which is 100 minutes, and nearly a third of that comes from one patient whose file went missing for an hour. If her wait had been an ordinary 90 minutes, the range would collapse from 100 to 70 — a huge change in our description of the clinic, caused by one person. The interquartile range tells a steadier story. It measures the width of the middle half only, from Q1 at 30 to Q3 at 90, giving 60 minutes, and it would not move at all if that patient's wait doubled to four hours, because she sits outside the quartiles either way. That robustness is why the IQR pairs with the median: both are found by counting positions, both ignore the tails, and together they describe the typical morning without being hijacked by the exceptional one. Use the range when you genuinely care about the full stretch — say, planning staff shifts around the worst case — and the IQR when you care about what a clinic visit is like for most patients. Naming which measure you chose, and why, is exactly the kind of justification final exams reward.

**Mandy:** This one caused an argument: a survey asked, do you agree that our caring new mobile clinic has cut your waiting time? Half the class said the data from it would be fine, half said it was poisoned. Who wins?

**Tutor:** The poisoned camp wins, and the word for the poison is bias — specifically a leading question. The phrase our caring new mobile clinic tells the reader how to feel before they answer, and agreement questions carry their own gravity: people tend to agree with a friendly questioner. Data collected through that question will overstate satisfaction no matter how carefully you calculate afterwards — and this is the deep point — no statistic can rescue biased data. The mean of a poisoned data set is a precise summary of poison. A clean version asks neutrally: has your waiting time changed since the mobile clinic began visiting — shorter, longer, or the same? — with the options balanced on both sides. Bias also sneaks in through WHO gets asked, not just how. Handing the form out only in the mobile clinic's own queue, and only on pension payout day, over-represents its current users and one slice of the community, and misses the people who gave up on the service entirely — arguably the most important voices in the data. A fair sample gives everyone in the population a chance of being selected; that is what random selection buys you. In a critique question, identify the flaw AND its direction: say who gets over-represented or which way answers get pushed, not merely that the method is biased.

**Mandy:** Last one: looking at the two box plots, someone concluded that Ntabeni serves more patients because its plot is longer. That sounds convincing — why is it wrong?

**Tutor:** Because a box plot shows position and spread, never quantity. Both of our plots were built from exactly eleven patients; the Ntabeni plot stretches further only because Ntabeni WAITS are more spread out — the same eleven people, sitting further apart on the time line. Length in a box plot is minutes, not headcount. In fact the plot deliberately forgets the sample size: from the drawing alone you cannot tell whether it summarises eleven patients or eleven thousand. What you CAN read is proportion, using the quarters rule: every whisker and every half of the box holds one quarter of whatever the sample was. So the honest comparisons run like this: the Ntabeni median at 60 sits twenty-five minutes beyond the Umbilo median at 35, meaning the typical rural patient waits longer. The Ntabeni box spans 30 to 90 against the Umbilo 20 to 45, meaning rural waits vary far more. And the Ntabeni upper whisker reaching 120 says a quarter of its patients wait 90 minutes or more, while no Umbilo patient waited past 55. Notice every sentence names the context — patients, minutes, mornings. A conclusion without context is only half an answer; the point of the picture is to read it back into the real lives it describes.
