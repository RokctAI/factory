# Part 1 — Expert

South Africa argues about numbers every day: unemployment figures, matric pass rates, taxi fares, load-shedding hours. Data handling is the discipline of settling those arguments honestly — asking a clear question, collecting data properly, summarising it fairly and displaying it so the truth is visible. This session investigates a question close to home: how long do learners travel to school each morning? Two schools collect the data — one deep rural in the Eastern Cape, one urban in Gqeberha — and the numbers tell a story about inequality that a single average would hide.

## Subtopic: Asking the Question and Collecting the Data

Every investigation begins with a QUESTION, and the curriculum expects questions about real national and global issues: service delivery, road safety, unemployment, education access. Our question: how many minutes do grade 12 learners spend travelling to school in the morning, and does it differ between a rural and an urban school?

Next, decide WHO to ask. The POPULATION is everyone the question is about — all grade 12 learners at each school. When the population is too large to measure completely, a SAMPLE is selected, and it must be chosen fairly: a random selection across all classes, not just the first learners at the gate, because those early arrivals probably live close by — a built-in BIAS that would drag the answer downward.

Then choose the COLLECTION INSTRUMENT. A QUESTIONNAIRE puts written questions to many people cheaply; its questions must be short, unambiguous and unleading. An INTERVIEW asks in person, allowing follow-up but taking time and possibly influencing answers. A RECORDING SHEET logs observations directly — a teacher noting arrival times at the gate. For travel time, a one-page questionnaire works: how do you travel, and how many minutes does the trip take on a normal morning?

Finally, classify what comes back. Travel minutes are NUMERICAL data — numbers you can calculate with. Transport mode — walk, taxi, bus, bicycle — is CATEGORICAL data: labels that can be counted but not averaged. The distinction decides every later step, because means and quartiles exist only for numerical data. The questions for this section are with you now; name the population, the instrument and the data type before anything is counted.

## Subtopic: Organising: Tallies, Frequency Tables and Class Intervals

Eleven questionnaires come back from the rural school, travel times in minutes: 5, 10, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30, 35, 40, 45 and 60. Raw data is honest but unreadable at scale, so it is organised.

The first tool is the TALLY: a stroke per response, bundled in fives, turned into a FREQUENCY TABLE — each value alongside how many times it occurs. The value 10 has frequency 2; every other value has frequency 1.

With many different values, single-value rows waste effort, so the data is grouped into CLASS INTERVALS of equal width. Using intervals of fifteen minutes: 0 up to 14 minutes holds 5, 10 and 10 — frequency 3. The interval 15 to 29 holds 15, 20 and 25 — frequency 3. The interval 30 to 44 holds 30, 35 and 40 — frequency 3. And 45 to 59 holds just 45 — frequency 1 — while 60 to 74 holds the single learner at 60 minutes. The frequencies total 3 + 3 + 3 + 1 + 1 = 11, and that check matters: frequencies must always sum to the number of responses, or data has been dropped or double-counted.

Two rules protect class intervals. Intervals may not overlap — if one interval ends at 14, the next begins at 15, so no value can land in two rows. And intervals should have equal width, because unequal widths distort every graph built from the table.

Grouping trades detail for clarity: the table no longer remembers whether a learner said 35 or 40, only that two learners sit in the thirties. That trade is worth making for large data sets, and exam questions test whether you can place a borderline value in the correct interval. The questions for this section are ready; bundle the tallies, check the total, and respect the interval boundaries.

## Subtopic: Summarising: Mean, Median, Quartiles and Percentiles

Summary statistics compress a data set into a few honest numbers. Order the rural times first — summarising unordered data is the cardinal sin: 5, 10, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30, 35, 40, 45, 60.

The MEAN adds and divides: the sum is 295, and 295 ÷ 11 = 26,8 minutes to one decimal place. The MEDIAN is the middle value of the ordered list: with 11 values the middle is the sixth, which is 25 minutes — five values below it, five above. The MODE is the most frequent value: 10 minutes, appearing twice. The RANGE is largest minus smallest: 60 − 5 = 55 minutes.

QUARTILES cut the ordered data into four equal parts. The LOWER QUARTILE Q1 is the median of the bottom half — of 5, 10, 10, 15, 20 the middle is 10. The UPPER QUARTILE Q3 is the median of the top half — of 30, 35, 40, 45, 60 the middle is 40. The INTERQUARTILE RANGE, IQR, is Q3 minus Q1: 40 − 10 = 30 minutes, the width of the middle half of the data. Unlike the range, the IQR ignores the extremes, so one learner with an extraordinary journey cannot inflate it.

PERCENTILES generalise the idea: the 25th percentile is Q1, the 50th is the median, the 75th is Q3. A learner at the 90th percentile travels longer than 90% of learners. Doctors track babies on weight percentiles; examiners report marks the same way.

Note the gap between mean and median — 26,8 against 25. The single 60-minute traveller pulls the mean upward while the median stands firm; the mean chases extremes, the median resists them. The questions for this section follow now; order first, then locate the middles, and say each statistic in a sentence with its unit.

## Subtopic: The Box-and-Whisker Plot: Comparing Two Data Sets

The FIVE-NUMBER SUMMARY — minimum, Q1, median, Q3, maximum — draws as a BOX-AND-WHISKER PLOT: a box from Q1 to Q3 with a line at the median, and whiskers stretching to the minimum and maximum. Rural school: 5, 10, 25, 40, 60.

The urban school's eleven times, already ordered: 5, 5, 10, 10, 15, 15, 20, 20, 25, 30, 35. Median: sixth value, 15 minutes. Q1: median of 5, 5, 10, 10, 15, which is 10. Q3: median of 20, 20, 25, 30, 35, which is 25. Five-number summary: 5, 10, 15, 25, 35. IQR: 25 − 10 = 15 minutes.

Now draw both plots on the same scale and read them together — this is the comparison skill the paper rewards. The medians: rural 25, urban 15 — the typical rural learner travels ten minutes longer every morning. The boxes: rural IQR 30 against urban 15 — rural journeys are far more spread out, some learners close to school and others very far. The right whiskers: rural reaches 60, urban stops at 35 — the longest rural journey is an hour.

Interpretation must be written in context: rural learners typically travel longer AND less predictably than urban learners, and a quarter of rural learners travel 40 minutes or more, because a quarter of the data always lies above Q3. That last move — a quarter lies beyond the quartile — is the most examinable sentence in this topic.

A box plot never shows the mean, the mode or the number of learners; it shows position and spread only. Choosing statistics that resist outliers, comparing like with like on one scale, and writing conclusions in context — that is data handling at matric level. The questions for this section are with you now; five numbers per school, one shared scale, and conclusions in full sentences.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same numbers with learners lined up in the schoolyard instead of listed on paper.

## Subtopic: Lining Up the Class

Forget formulas for a moment. Take the eleven rural learners and line them up in the yard, ordered by travel time, shortest journey on the left: 5, 10, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30, 35, 40, 45, 60 minutes.

The MEDIAN is just the learner standing dead centre. Eleven learners means the sixth one — count them off — and the sixth says 25 minutes. Five people travel less than her, five travel more. She is the typical learner, and no showoff at the far end of the line can move her, because she is found by counting, not adding.

Quartiles? Cut the line the same way twice more. Take the five learners to her left and find THEIR middle: the third one, 10 minutes. That is the lower quartile — a quarter of the class travels 10 minutes or less. Take the five to her right; their middle says 40. That is the upper quartile — a quarter of the class travels 40 minutes or more. The distance between those two markers, 40 minus 10, is the interquartile range: 30 minutes. It measures how stretched out the MIDDLE HALF of the class is, ignoring the girl at 60 completely.

And percentiles are only a finer version of the same lining-up: at the 90th percentile you stand further along the line than 90 out of every 100 learners. Baby clinics use it, exam boards use it — same queue, more cutting points.

Order first, count to the middles. That is the whole machine. Some questions on this section are with you right now; put the numbers in a line before you touch them, then count, do not calculate.

## Subtopic: The Box Tells the Story

A box-and-whisker plot is just the lined-up class photographed from above. Five numbers pose for the photo: the shortest journey, the lower quartile, the median, the upper quartile and the longest journey. For the rural school: 5, 10, 25, 40, 60.

Read the picture like this. The box, drawn from 10 to 40, is where the middle half of the class lives — half of all the learners are inside that box. The line inside the box at 25 is the median, the typical learner. The whiskers reaching out to 5 and 60 are the extremes, the learner who lives next door and the learner who crosses a mountain.

Now the magic of the picture: put the urban school's box — 5, 10, 15, 25, 35 — directly underneath on the same scale, and the story leaps out with no calculation at all. The urban median line stands at 15, the rural one at 25: typical urban journeys are ten minutes shorter. The urban box is narrow, 10 to 25; the rural box is wide, 10 to 40: rural journeys are all over the place, urban ones are predictable. And the rural whisker stretches all the way to 60 while the urban one stops at 35.

One rule unlocks most exam questions: every section of the plot — each whisker, each half of the box — holds a QUARTER of the data. So a quarter of rural learners travel 40 minutes or more; half travel between 10 and 40. The box never tells you how MANY learners there were, or the mean — only where they stand and how spread they are. Questions on this section are coming your way now; find the five numbers, then read quarters off the picture like slices of a loaf.

## Subtopic: Mean or Median: Which One to Trust

The rural class has two honest answers to what is the typical travel time. The mean adds all eleven times and divides: 295 over 11 gives 26,8 minutes. The median counts to the middle of the line: 25 minutes. Which one is telling the truth?

Both — but they have different weaknesses, and the exam wants you to know them. Watch what one extreme value does. The learner with the 60-minute mountain journey is one person, yet she pushes the mean up above everyone in the bottom half of the class. Replace her 60 with 25 and the mean drops to 260 over 11 — about 23,6 — while the median stays exactly where it was, at 25. One number moved the mean by more than three minutes and could not budge the median at all.

That is the rule of thumb: the mean is democratic with rands and minutes — every value votes with its full size, so a millionaire walking into a shebeen sends the average income through the roof. The median only asks who stands in the middle, so extremes shout into the wind. When data has outliers — salaries, house prices, travel times with one mountain crosser — the median is the safer typical value, and saying so, with the reason, is a standard three-mark question.

The same suspicion applies to spread. The range, 60 minus 5, listens to the two most extreme people only. The interquartile range, 30, listens to the solid middle half. Big range with a modest IQR means outliers at the edges, not chaos in the middle.

So when a politician or an advert quotes an average, ask the data handler's question: mean or median, and who is hiding in the tail? The final questions of the lesson are with you now; test every claim by asking what one extreme value would do to it.
