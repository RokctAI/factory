**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! The one that comes up every year: with eleven values the median was easy — the sixth person. But what happens with an even number, say twelve values? People got stuck between two learners.

**Tutor:** With an even count there is no single middle person, so the median becomes the halfway point between the two middle values. Order the twelve values, count to positions six and seven, and take the mean of just those two: add them and divide by two. If learner six says 20 minutes and learner seven says 25, the median is 22,5 minutes — a value nobody actually reported, and that is perfectly fine, because the median is a position marker, not necessarily a member of the data. A quick way to find the middle positions for any count: add one to the number of values and divide by two. Eleven values gives position six exactly; twelve values gives position six and a half, which tells you to average the sixth and seventh. The same principle carries into quartiles: when a half of the data has an even count, its quartile is the average of the two values straddling the middle of that half. What never changes, odd or even, is the first commandment: order the data before counting anything. Most lost marks in this topic come from counting to the middle of an unordered list, which produces a confident, well-presented, wrong answer.

**Mandy:** Next: someone asked why we even bother with quartiles and the IQR when we already have the range. The range felt easier and used all the data.

**Tutor:** The range feels like it uses all the data, but it actually uses only two values — the biggest and the smallest — and those are precisely the two least trustworthy numbers in any data set, because that is where the freak values live. Our rural range is 60 minus 5, which is 55 minutes, and more than half of that comes from one learner with a mountain to cross. If she moved houses tomorrow, the range would collapse from 55 to 40 — a massive change in our description of the school, caused by one person. The interquartile range tells a steadier story. It measures the width of the middle half only, from Q1 at 10 to Q3 at 40, giving 30 minutes, and it would not move at all if the mountain learner's journey doubled to two hours, because she sits outside the quartiles either way. That robustness is why the IQR pairs with the median: both are found by counting positions, both ignore the tails, and together they describe the typical situation without being hijacked by the exceptional one. Use the range when you genuinely care about the full stretch — say, planning the earliest taxi departure — and the IQR when you care about what school mornings are like for most learners. Naming which measure you chose and why is exactly the kind of justification the paper rewards.

**Mandy:** This one caused an argument: a questionnaire asked, do you agree that our wonderful new taxi rank has improved travel times? Half the class said the data collected from it would be fine, half said it was poisoned. Who wins?

**Tutor:** The poisoned camp wins, and the word for the poison is bias — specifically a leading question. The phrase our wonderful new taxi rank instructs the reader how to feel before they answer, and agreement questions carry their own gravity: people tend to agree with a friendly questioner. Data collected through that question will overstate satisfaction no matter how carefully you calculate afterwards — and this is the deep point — no statistic can rescue biased data. The mean of a poisoned data set is a precise summary of poison. A clean version asks neutrally: has your travel time changed since the new rank opened — shorter, longer, or the same? — with the options balanced on both sides. Bias also sneaks in through WHO gets asked, not just how. Sampling only morning commuters misses shift workers; sampling at the rank itself misses the people who gave up on taxis entirely, arguably the most important voices in the data. A fair sample gives everyone in the population a chance of being selected — that is what random selection buys you. In the exam, critique questions ask you to identify the flaw AND its direction: say who gets over-represented or which way answers get pushed, not merely that the method is biased.

**Mandy:** Last one: on the two box plots, someone concluded that the rural school has more learners because its plot is longer. That sounds convincing — why is it wrong?

**Tutor:** Because a box plot shows position and spread, never quantity. Both of our plots were built from exactly eleven learners; the rural plot stretches further only because rural TIMES are more spread out — the same eleven people, standing further apart. Length in a box plot is minutes, not headcount. In fact the plot deliberately forgets the sample size: from the drawing alone you cannot tell whether it summarises eleven learners or eleven thousand. What you CAN read is proportion, using the quarters rule: every whisker and every half of the box holds one quarter of whatever the sample was. So the honest comparisons run like this: the rural median at 25 sits ten minutes beyond the urban median at 15, meaning the typical rural learner travels longer. The rural box spans 10 to 40 against the urban 10 to 25, meaning rural journeys vary far more. And the rural upper whisker reaching 60 says a quarter of rural learners travel 40 minutes or more, while no urban learner exceeds 35. Notice every sentence names the context — learners, minutes, mornings. A conclusion without context earns half marks in this paper; the marker wants evidence that you can read the picture back into the real lives it describes.
