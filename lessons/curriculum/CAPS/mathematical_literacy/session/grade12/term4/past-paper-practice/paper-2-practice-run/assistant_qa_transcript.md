**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First: the painting question has six stages, and learners lose the thread halfway. Is there a way to hold the whole chain in your head?

**Tutor:** Hold it as a story instead of a list, because the stages are not arbitrary — each one answers the next natural question a painter would ask. Start at the wall: how much wall is there? Perimeter times height — 28 times 3, which is 84 square metres. But a painter doesn't paint glass and doors: subtract the openings — 1,68 for the door, 7,2 for the four windows — leaving 75,12. How many times will the roller cross it? Two coats doubles the work to 150,24. What does the tin promise? Nine square metres per litre, so divide: 16,69 litres. What does the shop actually sell? Five-litre tins, so 3,34 becomes FOUR, rounded up, and money closes the story: four times R649,00 is R2 596,00, compared against the budget in a full sentence. Six stages, but really six questions any human would ask in order: how much wall, minus what, how many coats, how many litres, how many tins, how much money. If you blank in the exam, re-enter the story at the painter's next question rather than trying to recall a memorised list. And two guard rails as you go: units written on every line — square metres until the division, litres after — and the round-up at tins stated with its reason, because the memo's mark sits on the word up, not just on the four.

***

**Mandy:** Next: why does the memo put its first tank mark on halving the diameter? It feels like a trivial step compared to the formula.

**Tutor:** Because that trivial step is where the answer lives or dies, and examiners weight marks by consequence, not by effort. The formula squares the radius. Feed it the 1,1 metre diameter instead of the 0,55 radius and the error doesn't just double your answer — it QUADRUPLES it, because the doubling gets squared along with the number: 1,1 squared is four times 0,55 squared. A tank that truly holds about 1 901 litres suddenly claims nearly 7 604, and every subsequent line, however beautifully calculated, describes a tank that doesn't exist. The memo pays the halving first because it is the gate through which a correct answer must pass. There is a deeper exam-craft lesson here: whenever a formula names its ingredient — radius, not diameter; perpendicular height, not slant; exclusive price, not inclusive — the question will happily supply the wrong-named twin and watch who notices. So build the checking reflex: before substituting, read the formula's ingredient list aloud against what the question gave you, and write the conversion as its own line — diameter 1,1 metres, so radius 0,55 metres. That line costs seconds and banks a mark. Then the sanity net catches whatever slips through: a household rain tank holds a couple of thousand litres; a claim of seven and a half thousand from a metre-wide tank should feel wrong before any re-checking begins.

**Mandy:** Then the elevation matching: learners can see the answer but can't say WHY, and the reason mark disappears. What sentence does the memo want?

**Tutor:** The memo wants a counting sentence anchored to a compass, and it has a fixed shape: name the face, list what the plan puts there, and point to the drawing that shows the same pattern. For the tuck shop: the floor plan places the serving hatch and one window on the north wall and leaves the south wall solid; elevation drawing two shows two openings while drawing one shows none; therefore drawing two is the north elevation. Three clauses — plan evidence, drawing evidence, conclusion — and the reason mark is banked. What the memo will NOT pay is geography by vibes: it looks like the front, or the shop usually faces the road. And there is one trap the sentence must dodge: never anchor to left and right, because they flip. Reading the floor plan, you hover above the building sharing its sense of direction; standing outside the north wall looking back, everything mirrors — what the plan drew toward the west end now sits on the other side of your view, exactly like facing a friend whose raised right hand meets your left. Compass directions survive the flip; left and right do not. So if the question pushes further — which SIDE of the hatch is the window — convert to compass first: the window sits west of the hatch on the plan, and facing the north wall from outside, west lies to your right. Count the openings, speak in compass, conclude in one sentence: that is the full reason, every time this question appears.

**Mandy:** Last one: Paper 2 keeps sneaking Finance in — fuel costs, tile budgets. How should learners handle money questions inside a measurement paper?

**Tutor:** Welcome them, because they are the cheapest marks on the page — the guideline explicitly allows Finance in Questions 4 and 5 wherever it links directly to Measurement or Maps and Plans, and the link is always the same shape: your measurement answer becomes a quantity, and the quantity meets a price. Fuel: the map gave 568 kilometres, consumption converts it to 44,304 litres, and the pump price of R23,50 converts litres to R1 041,14. Tiles: the plan gave a 6,2 by 4,5 floor, area converts it to 27,9 square metres, coverage converts that to twelve whole boxes, and R189,00 a box converts boxes to R2 268,00. Notice the chain is measurement, then quantity, then money — three currencies, two exchanges — and the memo places a mark on each exchange, not just the final rand figure. Two disciplines keep the money marks safe. First, the rounding rule changes at the shop door: measurements round as instructed, but PURCHASES round up to whole sellable units before the price multiplies — twelve boxes, not 11,16 of them, four tins, not 3,34 — and multiplying the unrounded quantity by the price is the error the memo watches for. Second, money answers keep Finance form even inside Paper 2: rand sign, two decimals, decimal comma. And when the question ends is the budget enough, finish with the verdict sentence containing both numbers — the cost and the budget — because Paper 2 pays for decisions exactly the way Paper 1 does: a number, a comparison, and the word because.
