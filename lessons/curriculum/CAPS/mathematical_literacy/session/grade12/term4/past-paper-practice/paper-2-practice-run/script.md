# Part 1 — Expert

This session is a full practice run of a National Senior Certificate Paper 2 — Measurement with Maps and Plans, 150 marks — walked question by question at examination pace, mark allocations named at every turn. Paper 2 is the practical paper: it hands you tariff-free, real-world objects — a road, a classroom, a tank, a plan — and pays for measured, converted, correctly-united arithmetic. The architecture is fixed, the mark allocations are instructions, and the walk-through below treats them exactly as a marker would.

## Subtopic: How Paper 2 Is Built and How Marks Are Won

Paper 2 carries 150 marks in four or five questions. Measurement holds about 55 percent of the paper, Maps and Plans about 40, Probability its 5 — and small Finance calculations may appear in Questions 4 and 5 where they connect directly to building, buying or travelling. Question 1 is worth about 30 marks at Level 1 only — short knowing-type items from Measurement and Maps. Question 2 is Maps and Plans; Question 3 is Measurement; Questions 4 and 5 integrate them, and only there may unfamiliar contexts arrive. The taxonomy split matches Paper 1: roughly 30 percent knowing, 30 routine, 20 multi-step, 20 reasoning.

The paper's special demand is UNITS. Nearly every chain crosses at least one conversion — millimetres to metres, cubic metres to litres, decimal hours to minutes — and the memo places a mark directly on the crossing. Read each allocation as a recipe: two marks, one operation plus answer; four marks, a conversion lives inside; six or more, a staged build where every stage pays, and a wrong stage carried forward keeps paying under consistent accuracy.

## Subtopic: Question 1 Walk-through: Level 1 Warm-ups

Thirty marks of single-step credit, taken at pace but never unwritten. Specimens.

For two marks: convert 3 500 metres to kilometres — divide by 1 000, which is 3,5 kilometres. For two marks: a tank holds 0,75 cubic metres of water; how many litres is that? One cubic metre is 1 000 litres, so 750 litres. For two marks: the scale on a floor plan reads 1 to 100 — explain its meaning. One unit on the plan represents 100 of the same units on the real building; the explanation mark wants SAME UNITS said aloud.

From the maps side, for two marks: name the national route joining Johannesburg and Durban shown on the given map — the N3, read straight off the labelling. For two marks: on the plan, the symbol of a gap in the wall with a quarter-circle arc — a door, the arc showing its swing. And a Level 1 measurement read, for two: the dimension line on the plan marks the kitchen wall as 4 200 millimetres; write it in metres — 4,2.

Question 1's discipline is the same as Paper 1's: one visible line each, correct units on every answer, no chain left implied.

## Subtopic: Question 2: The Road Trip on the N3

Question 2 belongs to Maps and Plans, and this specimen — worth eleven marks across its parts — plans a Johannesburg to Durban trip of 568 kilometres on the N3.

Part one, for three marks: at an average speed of 100 kilometres per hour, calculate the travelling time. Time is distance over speed: 568 over 100 is 5,68 hours. Then the conversion mark: 0,68 hours times 60 is 40,8, so the time is about 5 hours 41 minutes. Writing 5 hours 68 minutes — reading the decimal as minutes — is the single most marked-against error in this question family.

Part two, for four marks, the fuel: the car uses 7,8 litres per 100 kilometres. Litres needed: 568 over 100 times 7,8, which is 44,304 litres. At R23,50 per litre, cost is 44,304 times 23,50 — R1 041,14 to the cent. Two conversions of thinking, each carrying a mark: kilometres into hundred-kilometre blocks, litres into rands — the Finance link Paper 2 explicitly allows.

Part three, for four marks, the map work: the final approach is measured on a 1 to 50 000 strip map as 9 centimetres. Real distance: 9 times 50 000 is 450 000 centimetres — 4,5 kilometres after the unit ladder. Then the reasoning close: the family wants to arrive before 14:00 and plans a 45 minute lunch stop — working backwards from 14:00 through the stop and the 5 hours 41 minutes of driving, departure must be no later than 07:34. Reverse timelines pay a mark per correct subtraction.

## Subtopic: Question 3.1: Painting the Classroom

Question 3 is Measurement's centrepiece, and painting is its classic staged build — this specimen worth eight marks.

A classroom is 8 metres long, 6 metres wide, 3 metres high. All four walls get two coats. Subtract one door of 0,8 metres by 2,1 metres and four windows of 1,2 metres by 1,5 metres. Paint covers 9 square metres per litre and is sold only in 5 litre tins. How many tins, and is the given budget enough?

Stage one: wall area. Perimeter is 2 times the sum of 8 and 6, which is 28 metres; times the height 3 gives 84 square metres. Stage two: openings. The door is 0,8 times 2,1 — 1,68 square metres; the windows are 4 times 1,2 times 1,5 — 7,2 square metres. Paintable area: 84 minus 1,68 minus 7,2 is 75,12 square metres. Stage three: coats. Two coats double the work: 150,24 square metres. Stage four: litres. 150,24 over 9 is 16,69 litres to two decimals. Stage five: tins. 16,69 over 5 is 3,34 — and tins are bought whole, so FOUR tins, rounded UP, never to the nearest. Stage six, the Finance link: at R649,00 per tin, the paint costs 4 times 649 — R2 596,00 — and the verdict compares that against the stated budget in a sentence containing both numbers.

Every stage is a mark or two, and the round-up at tins is the one the memo watches: 3 tins leaves wall unpainted; 3,34 tins cannot be bought.

## Subtopic: Question 3.2: The Water Tank and the Boxes

Measurement's second movement pairs volume with packing — nine marks across the parts.

The tank, for five marks: a cylindrical rainwater tank has radius 0,55 metres and height 2 metres; using 3,142 for pi, calculate its capacity in litres. Substitution first — volume is 3,142 times 0,55 squared times 2. In stages: 0,55 squared is 0,3025; times 3,142 is 0,950 46 to five decimals; times 2 gives 1,901 cubic metres rounded to three. The conversion mark: times 1 000 is about 1 901 litres. The radius-diameter trap guards the door: a question giving the DIAMETER as 1,1 metres expects you to halve it first, and the memo's first mark sits exactly there.

The boxes, for four marks: cartons of exam paper measuring 15 centimetres by 10 centimetres by 20 centimetres are packed into a crate of 60 by 40 by 60 centimetres. How many fit? Axis by axis: 60 over 15 is 4 along the length; 40 over 10 is 4 across; 60 over 20 is 3 layers high. Total: 4 times 4 times 3 — 48 boxes. The volume check — crate 144 000 cubic centimetres over box 3 000 — agrees at 48, worth stating as confirmation, but the counting method is the answer: only WHOLE boxes ship, and volume division alone is merely a ceiling.

## Subtopic: Question 4: The Plan, the Budget and the Builder

The integrated back end, twelve marks, where plans meet money.

A builder holds a 1 to 100 floor plan of a new tuck shop for a school in Polokwane. Part one, for three marks: the longest wall measures 62 millimetres on the plan; calculate its real length. 62 times 100 is 6 200 millimetres — 6,2 metres after the ladder — and the sanity check earns its keep: rooms live between about 2 and 8 metres, so 6,2 rings true where 62 would ring alarm.

Part two, for three marks: the floor of the shop is 6,2 metres by 4,5 metres; calculate the area to be tiled — 27,9 square metres. Each box of tiles covers 2,5 square metres: 27,9 over 2,5 is 11,16, so TWELVE boxes, bought whole, rounded up.

Part three, for four marks, the elevation match: the plan places the serving hatch and one window on the north wall, a solid wall on the south. Of the two elevation drawings supplied, the one showing two openings is the north elevation — the answer earns full marks only with the counting reason attached, anchored to compass directions, never to left and right, which flip when the viewer steps outside.

Part four, for two marks, the Finance cameo: tiles cost R189,00 per box; the tiling budget line is 12 times 189 — R2 268,00. Paper 2 pays for exactly this kind of costing whenever it touches a plan.

## Subtopic: Probability Moments and Memo Habits

Probability's marks arrive inside other contexts. The school holds a raffle at the tuck shop opening: 250 tickets sold, and the builder bought 5. For two marks: the probability the builder wins — 5 over 250, which is 1 over 50, or 0,02, or 2 percent, any dialect accepted unless one is demanded. For two more, the complement: probability he does NOT win is 49 over 50 — 0,98 — because the two outcomes share the whole. And the reasoning mark the paper loves: buying 5 tickets in THIS raffle genuinely multiplies his chance by five, but last month's loss changes nothing about this month's draw — each raffle starts fresh.

Then the closing habits, Paper 2 edition. Habit one: UNITS ON EVERY LINE — square metres for area, cubic metres for volume, litres after the times-1 000 crossing; a correct number in a wrong unit is a lost answer mark. Habit two: conversions are mark-bearing steps — write them, never absorb them silently. Habit three: rounding obeys the context — tins, boxes and tiles round UP because shops sell whole things; measurements round as instructed, and never mid-chain. Habit four: substitution before evaluation — 3,142 times 0,55 squared times 2 on paper banks the method mark whatever the calculator then does. Habit five: verdict questions end in a sentence with a number inside it. A Paper 2 script running these habits typically recovers the same ten quiet marks as its Paper 1 twin — and the practice run is how they become reflex.
