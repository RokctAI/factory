**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First: the backwards VAT keeps eating marks. Why does dividing by 1,15 work, and why does taking 15 percent off the total fail?

**Tutor:** Build the total from the ground up once and the mystery dissolves. Every inclusive price is the exclusive price times 1,15 — one whole of the original plus fifteen hundredths of the original, glued into a single multiplier. So R368,00 was BORN as 320 times 1,15. To un-multiply, you divide by the same 1,15, and you land exactly back on R320,00. Now watch the wrong route: 15 percent OF R368,00 is R55,20, and subtracting gives R312,80 — too low, and here is the reason in one sentence: the VAT the shop charged was 15 percent of the SMALLER exclusive price, R48,00, not 15 percent of the bigger inclusive one, so the wrong route removes too much. The percentages are not symmetric — adding 15 percent and then removing 15 percent of the new total takes you below where you started, always. The exam habit that follows: before touching any VAT number, label it aloud as inclusive or exclusive. Exclusive travels forward by multiplying; inclusive travels backward by dividing; and the words on the slip — total, incl, excl — are the question's way of telling you which door you are standing at. One labelled line of working, one multiplier used in the correct direction, two marks banked.

**Mandy:** Next: the tax question has a base amount, a percentage of an excess, and then a rebate. Learners lose track of the order. Is there a fixed route through it?

**Tutor:** There is, and it never varies: bracket, excess, base plus percentage, rebate, then months. Step one, find the bracket — run the ANNUAL taxable income down the table the question prints until it fits between a bracket's limits; R285 000 lands in the row that reads R38 916 plus 26 percent of the amount above R226 000. Step two, the excess: 285 000 minus 226 000 is R59 000 — this subtraction is what the bracket's percentage applies to, never the whole income, and that is the single most common error in the question. The table has already taxed the first R226 000 inside its base figure of R38 916; only the overflow gets the 26 percent. Step three: 26 percent of 59 000 is R15 340,00, added to the base for R54 256,00. Step four, the rebate — subtract the R16 425 the question supplies, because the table grants that discount to every taxpayer in her age group, and forgetting it hands back a full mark on a silver tray. Annual tax R37 831,00. Step five, only if asked: divide by twelve for the monthly R3 152,58. Five steps, in that order, every time the question is set. Write each line separately — the marker cannot give you the excess mark if the excess never appears on your page.

**Mandy:** From the data side: when the paper says comment on the averages, learners freeze. What does a full-marks comment actually contain?

**Tutor:** A full-marks comment is a calculation wearing a sentence, and it has three required parts: a number, a comparison, and a consequence. Take the homework-book marks — mean 60, median 68, one blackout-week disaster of 22 in the set. A freeze answer says the marks are okay, and earns nothing, because it contains no mathematics. The full answer runs: the median of 68 sits well above the mean of 60 — that is the number and the comparison — and the gap exists because the single outlier of 22 drags the mean down while leaving the median's position untouched, so the median better represents typical performance — that is the consequence, with the mechanism named. Notice what made it work: every claim leans on a value you computed, and the direction of the pull is stated, not implied. The same recipe covers box plots — same median of 63, but Class A's interquartile range is half of Class B's, so A is more consistent — and misleading graphs, where the comment names the flaw, the false impression it creates, and what an honest version would show. If your comment could be written without doing any arithmetic, it is not yet an answer; go back and put a computed number inside the sentence. That single habit converts almost every hesitant reasoning attempt into marks.

**Mandy:** Last one: with the whole year to revise, why do these two lessons drill Finance and Data so much harder than everything else?

**Tutor:** Because concentration wins revision. Look at where the marks actually live in your own practice runs: almost every money context of the year — tariffs, VAT, tax, interest, exchange rates — is powered by the same four Finance engines, and almost every number-summary question is powered by the same short data toolkit. Master the four engines — tariffs split across blocks, VAT run both directions, the five-step tax route, and the compound-versus-simple distinction — and you are fluent in the majority of the money questions any assessment can ask. The same concentration argument runs inside Data: mean, median, quartiles and the interquartile range, plus one practised comment sentence, cover nearly every data question you have met all year, at every difficulty level. There is also a structural reason from our own practice paper: its opening question is thirty marks of warm-up items drawn from these same two topics — the definitions and single-step calculations we drilled are literally that paper's opening third, and warm-up marks are the cheapest to secure. Revision is triage: the skills that repeat across the most questions deserve the most practice. Measurement and Maps get the same treatment in their own session, because they power the practical contexts the way Finance powers the money ones. Practise where the marks live — that is the whole strategy.
