# Part 1 — Expert

The final Paper 1 is built from two big families — Finance at roughly 60 percent of the 150 marks, Data Handling at about 35 — with Probability threaded through for its 5. Term four is not new work; it is the year's work put under exam pressure. This session runs the Finance and Data essentials in exam order: tariffs, interest and inflation first, then VAT, income tax and exchange rates, then the data toolkit of averages and quartiles, and finally graphs — reading them, choosing them, and catching the ones designed to mislead. Every worked number here is the kind the memo rewards line by line.

## Subtopic: Tariff Systems, Interest and Inflation

Rising block tariffs first, because a tariff question opens almost every Finance section. The design principle: the more you consume, the more each unit costs. A municipal electricity tariff might charge R2,00 per kilowatt hour for the first 350 units and R2,50 for every unit above that. A household using 420 units does NOT pay 420 times one rate. Split the consumption across the blocks: the first 350 units cost 350 times R2,00, which is R700,00; the remaining 70 units sit in block two at R2,50, giving R175,00. Subtotal R875,00. Then the almost-always-present final step: add VAT at 15 percent — R875,00 times 1,15 equals R1 006,25. The classic lost mark is charging ALL units at the higher rate; the block boundary protects the first 350.

Interest next, and the split that organises everything: SIMPLE interest is calculated on the original amount only, every year the same; COMPOUND interest is calculated on the growing balance, so interest earns interest. Take R5 000 invested at 6 percent for three years. Simple: 5 000 times 0,06 is R300 per year, times three years is R900, final value R5 900,00. Compound: 5 000 times 1,06 to the power three — year by year if the formula feels slippery — gives R5 955,08. The gap of R55,08 is interest earned on interest, and a reasoning question loves asking you to explain exactly that sentence.

Inflation is compound growth wearing grocery prices. A loaf costing R120,00 — think of a family food basket item — rising at 5 percent per year for two years reaches 120 times 1,05 squared, which is R132,30. Not R132,00: adding 5 percent twice to the ORIGINAL is the simple-interest error in disguise. Each year's increase works on the new, higher price.

Questions on this section reach you now: the block split on 420 units, simple against compound on the same deposit, and the two-year inflation chain.

## Subtopic: VAT, Income Tax and Exchange Rates

VAT is 15 percent, and the paper tests both directions. Forward: a printer priced at R250,00 excluding VAT carries VAT of 250 times 0,15, which is R37,50, for a total of R287,50. Backward — the direction that costs marks — a till slip total of R287,50 INCLUDES the VAT, so the exclusive price is 287,50 DIVIDED by 1,15, which lands back on R250,00. Taking 15 percent of the inclusive price and subtracting is wrong, because the 15 percent was charged on the smaller, exclusive amount.

Income tax runs on a bracket table, and the method is pure substitution. A payslip first: gross salary is everything earned; deductions — PAYE, UIF at 1 percent, pension, medical aid — come off; net pay is what reaches the bank. The tax table works on ANNUAL taxable income. Say Thabo's taxable income is R310 000 per year, and his bracket reads: R42 678 plus 26 percent of the amount above R237 100. The amount above is 310 000 minus 237 100, which is R72 900; 26 percent of that is R18 954,00; add the bracket's base R42 678 for R61 632,00. Then subtract the primary rebate of R17 235 — the discount every taxpayer under 65 receives — leaving annual tax of R44 397,00, which is R3 699,75 per month. Four operations, four marks, and the rebate step is the one most often forgotten.

Exchange rates close the set. At R18,50 to one US dollar, a 120 dollar pair of imported boots costs 120 times 18,50, which is R2 220,00. Banks charge for the service: a 2 percent transaction fee lifts the cost to 2 220 times 1,02, which is R2 264,40. Direction check before any conversion: rands per dollar means MULTIPLY when going from dollars to rands, divide coming back.

This section's questions are with you now: VAT run both directions, the bracket-plus-rebate chain, and the fee-loaded conversion.

## Subtopic: Summarising Data: Mean, Median and Quartiles

The data toolkit compresses a list of numbers into a handful of honest summaries. Here is a marked set — eleven learners' test percentages, already arranged: 34, 41, 45, 52, 55, 58, 61, 66, 72, 78, 85.

MEAN: add and divide. The sum is 647, divided by 11 gives 58,8 — the balancing point of the data. MEDIAN: the middle value of ARRANGED data. With eleven values the middle position is position six — add one to the count and halve it — so the median is 58. RANGE: maximum minus minimum, 85 minus 34, which is 51 — one number describing the spread's full width.

Quartiles cut the arranged data into quarters. The lower quartile is the middle of the bottom half: positions one to five have middle position three, so Q1 is 45. The upper quartile mirrors it: positions seven to eleven have middle position nine, so Q3 is 72. The INTERQUARTILE RANGE is Q3 minus Q1, which is 27 — the width of the middle half of the class, immune to the extremes at either end.

The reasoning layer, where Level 4 marks live: which average represents the data best? The mean uses every value but is dragged by outliers; the median ignores how extreme the extremes are. If one learner had scored zero, the mean would drop sharply while the median barely moved — so for data with outliers, the median is the honest spokesperson, and full marks require saying WHY, not just naming it. Growth charts belong here too: a baby on the 75th percentile for mass is heavier than 75 percent of babies that age — a percentile is a position in a population, not a score.

Questions for this section arrive now: mean, median, both quartiles and the interquartile range on the eleven marks, and one justified choice of average.

## Subtopic: Representing Data and Spotting Misleading Graphs

Choosing the graph is itself examinable. Categories compare on a BAR graph; shares of a whole belong on a PIE chart; change over time runs on a LINE graph; continuous grouped data fills a HISTOGRAM, bars touching; and the five-number summary — minimum, Q1, median, Q3, maximum — draws a BOX-AND-WHISKER plot. Two box plots on the same scale are the exam's favourite comparison tool: if Class A and Class B share a median of 58 but A's box is narrow and B's is wide, the classes are equally strong at the centre while A is more CONSISTENT — same middle, smaller interquartile range. Write both halves of that sentence; the comparison mark needs both.

Then the misuse questions, worth easy marks to a suspicious reader. Trap one: the axis that starts at 45 instead of zero, making a rise from 50 to 55 look like a doubling — the picture exaggerates because the bars show only their tips. Trap two: a three-dimensional pie chart tilted so the front slice looks fatter than its percentage. Trap three: the biased sample — a survey about taxi fares conducted only among car owners cannot speak for commuters, whatever its graph claims. The exam verb is CRITIQUE: name the flaw, explain what impression it creates, and state what an honest version would show. Three sentences, three marks, no calculation required.

The final questions of this part reach you now: matching data types to graphs, the two-box-plot comparison in full, and one misleading graph dismantled properly.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same machinery on one Saturday in Tembisa — a tuck shop table at the taxi rank, a gogo's stokvel decision, and a report card on the fridge door.

## Subtopic: The Tuck Shop Till

Naledi runs a Saturday tuck shop table, and her pricing IS the Finance section. She buys a box of chips for R6,50 and sells each for R10,00. Profit per packet: R3,50. But the exam word is MARK-UP, and mark-up is measured against what SHE paid: 3,50 divided by 6,50, times 100, gives 53,8 percent. Not 35 percent — the comparison base is the cost price, the R6,50, because mark-up answers the question how much did the price grow from what it cost her.

Then the supplier invoice arrives reading R250,00 excluding VAT. The rule of the till: excluding means the tax is still coming. VAT is 15 percent of 250 — R37,50 — so R287,50 leaves her account. Going backwards works too, and this is the one that catches people: her till slip from the wholesaler shows a TOTAL of R287,50, VAT already inside. To find the price before tax, divide by 1,15 — back to R250,00 exactly. Never take 15 percent OF the total and subtract: the tax was charged on the smaller number, so only division unwinds it.

Why it matters beyond marks: a vendor who mistakes 53,8 percent for 35, or forgets which prices contain VAT, is quietly mispricing every packet on the table.

Quick questions on the tuck shop reach you now: the mark-up measured off the right base, and VAT walked in both directions.

## Subtopic: Stokvel Rands and Bank Interest

Naledi's gogo saves R200,00 every month in the street's stokvel. Twelve months, twelve payments: R2 400,00 saved by December — safe, social, but the money does not GROW; a stokvel payout returns what the members put in.

The bank across the road offers growth. Gogo has R5 000,00 from a pension payout and two choices for two years. Choice one, a simple-interest account at 6 percent: interest is 5 000 times 0,06, which is R300 every year — the same R300, because simple interest only ever looks at the original deposit. Two years, R600,00, final balance R5 600,00. Choice two, a fixed deposit at 6 percent COMPOUND: year one ends at 5 000 times 1,06, which is R5 300,00; year two charges interest on the NEW balance — 5 300 times 1,06 is R5 618,00. The extra R18,00 over simple interest is year two's interest earned on year one's interest. Small now; the gap widens every added year, which is the whole argument for starting early.

Inflation is the same engine running against her: if prices rise 5 percent a year, the R120,00 food basket item costs 120 times 1,05 twice over — R132,30 — in two years. Money standing still in a tin loses the race that compound interest is trying to win.

Questions on gogo's choices arrive now: the two-year balance under each option, and the one-sentence reason compound wins.

## Subtopic: Marks on the Fridge Door

Naledi's five term-test percentages hang on the fridge: 62, 58, 71, 65 — and one disaster, 24, from the week she had flu.

Her mean: the sum is 280, divided by 5 gives 56,0. Her median: arrange first — 24, 58, 62, 65, 71 — and the middle of five values is the third, so 62. Now the family argument the exam loves to stage: her uncle says she is a 56 learner; the report card median says 62. Who is honest? Remove the flu mark and the mean of the remaining four is 64,0 — the 24 alone dragged the mean down eight points, while the median never flinched, because the median only cares about position, not how BAD the bad mark was. For data carrying an outlier, the median represents the typical performance; the mean must be reported with its story attached. That is the full Level 4 answer: name the average, name the outlier, say which way it pulls.

One graph habit closes the Saturday: a phone-shop poster shows sales bars climbing steeply — but the money axis starts at R45 000, not zero, so a small rise poses as a boom. Cover the axis numbers with a thumb and the picture tells no story at all; that thumb test is the fastest lie detector in the subject.

The last questions of the lesson reach you now: mean against median on the fridge marks, the outlier's pull explained in one sentence, and the poster caught by its axis.
