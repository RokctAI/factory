**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First: the decimal-hours thing. Why is 3,3 hours not 3 hours 30 minutes, and how do we make the conversion automatic?

**Tutor:** Because the clock does not run on tens — it runs on sixties, and the decimal point speaks tens. When the division 264 over 80 produces 3,3 hours, that ,3 means three TENTHS of an hour, and a tenth of an hour is six minutes, not ten. So the honest conversion is always the same single move: take the decimal part alone and multiply it by 60. Three tenths times 60 is 18 — the trip is 3 hours 18 minutes. Make it automatic with a checkpoint habit: every time your calculator shows hours with a decimal, say the words times sixty before you write anything. Then test yourself both ways, because the questions reverse it too: 3,1 hours is 3 hours plus 0,1 times 60, which is 6 minutes; and going backwards, 2 hours 45 minutes is 2 plus 45 over 60, which is 2,75 hours — minutes become a fraction OF sixty, never a decimal tail glued on. Markers see this error so often that the conversion line usually carries its own mark, which is good news: one written line, times sixty shown explicitly, and the mark is yours even before the final answer. And run the sanity check as a net: 0,3 of an hour must be LESS than half an hour — 30 minutes would claim the decimal three is half of ten, which it is not, and the false 3 hours 30 fails that little test immediately.

**Mandy:** Next: for the drum question, learners substitute correctly and still lose marks. Where exactly do the marks leak?

**Tutor:** Three leaks, all pluggable. Leak one is the radius. The formula wants radius squared, and questions love handing you a diameter — a drum 1,2 metres across has a radius of 0,6, and the very first mark sits on that halving. Substituting the diameter doesn't just lose that mark; it quadruples the answer, because the error gets squared with the number. Leak two is squaring order: 3,142 times 0,6 squared means square the 0,6 FIRST — 0,36 — then multiply; learners who type left to right square the product instead and get a different wrong answer. Write the substitution line in full — 3,142 times 0,36 times 1,2 — because that visible line is the method mark, and it also forces the correct order through your own hands. Leak three is the unit landing. The formula delivers cubic metres — 1,357 of them — but a household question asks in litres, and the times-1 000 crossing carries its own mark: about 1 357 litres. Leaving the answer in cubic metres strands you one step short; leaving off the unit entirely surrenders the answer mark even with perfect digits. So the full-marks shape is four written lines: radius stated, substitution shown, cubic-metre result rounded as instructed, litre conversion with its unit. Practise the shape until the four lines write themselves — the arithmetic was never the hard part.

**Mandy:** Then the map question: learners measure correctly but the scale answer comes out a thousand times wrong. What's the fail-safe routine?

**Tutor:** The fail-safe is to refuse all shortcuts and walk the unit ladder every single time. A scale of 1 to 25 000 promises that one unit of map is 25 000 of the SAME unit of world — same unit is the whole contract. So the routine has three fixed lines. Line one: multiply in the unit you measured. Nine centimetres times 25 000 is 225 000 centimetres — still centimetres, and writing that unit down is what protects you. Line two: climb to metres by dividing by 100 — 2 250 metres. Line three: climb to kilometres by dividing by 1 000 — 2,25 kilometres. Two divisions, never one guessed division by some remembered number like ten thousand — the guess is exactly where the factor-of-ten errors breed. Then deploy the sanity net: picnic spots on a hiking map sit a couple of kilometres apart, not hundreds of kilometres and not hundreds of metres; a map answer of 225 kilometres from a hand-span measurement should feel wrong before any checking, the same way a 44-metre bedroom does on a floor plan. The reverse direction uses the same ladder downward: a real 7 kilometres at 1 to 100 000 is 700 000 centimetres divided by 100 000, which is 7 centimetres on paper. Measure, multiply, ladder, sanity check — four beats, and the scale question becomes the most reliable four marks on the page.

**Mandy:** Last one: probability is only worth a small handful of marks. Why give it a whole revision subtopic instead of letting it slide?

**Tutor:** Because those few marks are the cheapest in the whole subject, and they hide in every context, not in one place you could skip. Probability weaves through the money questions AND the practical ones — a raffle inside a fundraiser question, a rain chance inside a reunion budget, a table of learners inside a data question — so you cannot dodge it; you can only meet it prepared or unprepared. And prepared is genuinely small: four tools cover essentially everything examinable. Tool one, the three dialects — fraction, decimal, percentage — and the discipline of obeying whichever one the question names. Tool two, the complement: rain and no-rain share the whole, so 40 percent of one means 60 of the other, one subtraction, one mark. Tool three, denominator discipline on tables: out of the whole 60 unless the question narrows the group — the probability a chosen BOY walks divides by 24, not 60, and reading which group the question chose is the entire skill. Tool four, the reasoning classics, which repeat almost verbatim across practice runs: a 40 percent rain chance is not 40 percent of the day; last week's lottery loss changes nothing about this week's draw; six tickets in one raffle do multiply your chance, but across raffles each draw starts fresh. A handful of marks, maybe an afternoon of practice, and unlike the big topics there is nothing deeper waiting underneath. That ratio — marks per minute of revision — is the best in the subject, which is exactly why it earns its own subtopic.
