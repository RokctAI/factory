**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First: the decimal-hours thing. Why is 4,2 hours not 4 hours 20 minutes, and how do we make the conversion automatic?

**Tutor:** Because the clock does not run on tens — it runs on sixties, and the decimal point speaks tens. When the division 420 over 100 produces 4,2 hours, that ,2 means two TENTHS of an hour, and a tenth of an hour is six minutes, not ten. So the honest conversion is always the same single move: take the decimal part alone and multiply it by 60. Two tenths times 60 is 12 — the trip is 4 hours 12 minutes. Make it automatic with a checkpoint habit: every time your calculator shows hours with a decimal, say the words times sixty before you write anything. Then test yourself both ways, because the paper reverses it too: 5,68 hours is 5 hours plus 0,68 times 60, which is about 41 minutes; and going backwards, 4 hours 45 minutes is 4 plus 45 over 60, which is 4,75 hours — minutes become a fraction OF sixty, never a decimal tail glued on. The marker sees this error so often that the conversion line usually carries its own mark, which is good news: one written line, times sixty shown explicitly, and the mark is yours even before the final answer. And run the sanity check as a net: 0,68 of an hour must be MORE than half an hour — 68 minutes would make the decimal bigger than the whole hour it lives in, which is impossible.

***

**Mandy:** Next: for the tank question, learners substitute correctly and still lose marks. Where exactly do the marks leak?

**Tutor:** Three leaks, all pluggable. Leak one is the radius. The formula wants radius squared, and questions love handing you a diameter — a tank 1,4 metres across has a radius of 0,7, and the memo's very first mark sits on that halving. Substituting the diameter doesn't just lose that mark; it quadruples the answer, because the error gets squared with the number. Leak two is squaring order: 3,142 times 0,7 squared means square the 0,7 FIRST — 0,49 — then multiply; learners who type left to right square the product instead and get a different wrong answer. Write the substitution line in full — 3,142 times 0,49 times 1,5 — because that visible line is the method mark, and it also forces the correct order through your own hands. Leak three is the unit landing. The formula delivers cubic metres — 2,309 of them — but a household question asks in litres, and the times-1 000 crossing carries its own mark: about 2 309 litres. Leaving the answer in cubic metres strands you one step short; leaving off the unit entirely surrenders the answer mark even with perfect digits. So the full-marks shape is four written lines: radius stated, substitution shown, cubic-metre result rounded as instructed, litre conversion with its unit. Practise the shape until the four lines write themselves — the arithmetic was never the hard part.

***

**Mandy:** Then the map question: learners measure correctly but the scale answer comes out a thousand times wrong. What's the fail-safe routine?

**Tutor:** The fail-safe is to refuse all shortcuts and walk the unit ladder every single time. A scale of 1 to 50 000 promises that one unit of map is 50 000 of the SAME unit of world — same unit is the whole contract. So the routine has three fixed lines. Line one: multiply in the unit you measured. Seven centimetres times 50 000 is 350 000 centimetres — still centimetres, and writing that unit down is what protects you. Line two: climb to metres by dividing by 100 — 3 500 metres. Line three: climb to kilometres by dividing by 1 000 — 3,5 kilometres. Two divisions, never one guessed division by some remembered number like ten thousand — the guess is exactly where the factor-of-ten errors breed. Then deploy the sanity net: towns sit kilometres apart, not hundreds of kilometres and not hundreds of metres; a map answer of 350 kilometres from a hand-span measurement should feel wrong before any checking, the same way a 62-metre bedroom does on a floor plan. The reverse direction uses the same ladder downward: a real 6 kilometres at 1 to 50 000 is 600 000 centimetres divided by 50 000, which is 12 centimetres on paper. Measure, multiply, ladder, sanity check — four beats, and the scale question becomes the most reliable four marks on the page.

**Mandy:** Last one: probability is only worth about seven marks. Why give it a whole revision subtopic instead of letting it slide?

**Tutor:** Because those seven marks are the cheapest in either paper, and they hide in every question, not in one place you could skip. The guideline weaves probability through Papers 1 AND 2 — a raffle inside a tuck-shop question, a rain chance inside a wedding budget, a table of learners inside a data question — so you cannot dodge it; you can only meet it prepared or unprepared. And prepared is genuinely small: four tools cover essentially everything examinable. Tool one, the three dialects — fraction, decimal, percentage — and the discipline of obeying whichever one the question names. Tool two, the complement: rain and no-rain share the whole, so 30 percent of one means 70 of the other, one subtraction, one mark. Tool three, denominator discipline on tables: out of the whole 120 unless the question narrows the group — the probability a chosen BOY walks divides by 50, not 120, and reading which group the question chose is the entire skill. Tool four, the reasoning classics, which repeat almost verbatim across years: a 30 percent rain chance is not 30 percent of the day; last week's lottery loss changes nothing about this week's draw; five tickets in one raffle do multiply your chance, but across raffles each draw starts fresh. Seven marks, maybe an afternoon of practice, and unlike the big topics there is nothing deeper waiting underneath. That ratio — marks per minute of revision — is the best in the subject, which is exactly why it earns its own subtopic.
