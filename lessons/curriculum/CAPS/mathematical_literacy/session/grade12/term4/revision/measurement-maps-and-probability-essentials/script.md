# Part 1 — Expert

Paper 2 lives in the physical world: Measurement carries roughly 55 percent of its 150 marks, Maps and Plans about 40, and Probability threads through for its 5 — with small Finance calculations allowed wherever they connect to building, buying or travelling. This revision block runs the four essentials in exam order: conversions with perimeter, area and volume; scale and travel planning on maps; plans, elevations and models; and probability in the language the memo rewards. The numbers are chosen the way examiners choose them — realistic, South African, and mark-bearing at every line.

## Subtopic: Conversions, Perimeter, Area and Volume

Conversions are the toll gates of Paper 2 — nearly every question passes through one. The metric ladders: 10 millimetres to a centimetre, 100 centimetres to a metre, 1 000 metres to a kilometre; 1 000 grams to a kilogram; 1 000 millilitres to a litre. So 2,5 kilograms of flour is 2 500 grams, and a 750 millilitre bottle holds 0,75 litres. The heavyweight conversion, printed on the formula sheet but tested for understanding: one cubic metre is 1 000 litres.

Perimeter, area, volume — three different questions about the same object. A vegetable garden 8 metres by 5 metres: PERIMETER is the walk around the edge, 2 times the sum of 8 and 5, which is 26 metres of fencing. AREA is the surface covered, 8 times 5, which is 40 square metres of ground. The units police themselves: metres for distance around, square metres for surface, cubic metres for space inside.

Volume earns its marks on the cylinder. A water tank with radius 0,7 metres and height 1,5 metres, using pi as 3,142: volume is 3,142 times 0,7 squared times 1,5. Work it in steps for the substitution marks — 0,7 squared is 0,49; times 3,142 is 1,539 58; times 1,5 gives 2,309 cubic metres, rounded to three decimals. Then the conversion finishes the job: 2,309 cubic metres times 1 000 is about 2 309 litres of water. Two classic errors guard this question: using the diameter where the radius belongs, and forgetting that the answer in cubic metres is useless to a household that thinks in litres.

Questions on this section reach you now: the ladder conversions, fencing against ground cover on the garden, and the tank walked from formula to litres.

## Subtopic: Scale, Maps and Travel Planning

A scale of 1 to 50 000 means one unit on the map is 50 000 of the same units in the world. Measure 7 centimetres between two towns on such a map: real distance is 7 times 50 000, which is 350 000 centimetres — divide by 100 for metres, 3 500, then by 1 000 for kilometres: 3,5 kilometres. Always run the unit chain to kilometres; an answer left in centimetres is a method mark stranded short of its answer mark.

The distance, speed and time triangle carries the travel questions: distance equals speed times time, so time is distance divided by speed. A 420 kilometre trip at an average of 100 kilometres per hour takes 420 over 100, which is 4,2 hours. The decimal trap sits right here: 4,2 hours is NOT 4 hours 20 minutes. The 0,2 is two tenths of an hour — 0,2 times 60 is 12 — so the trip takes 4 hours 12 minutes. Converting decimal hours through 60 is one of the most reliably examined single steps in the paper.

Map-reading itself is mark-bearing: national routes carry N labels — the N1, the N3 — and questions ask which route joins two cities, which towns line the way, and what the distance chart says. A distance chart is read like a multiplication table: run along the row of one town to the column of the other; where they cross sits the kilometres. Elevation charts for a marathon route add a vertical story: climbs where the line rises, and reasoning marks for statements like the steepest section is where the graph is steepest, not highest.

This section's questions arrive now: the scale chain to kilometres, decimal hours converted honestly, and the chart-reading habits.

## Subtopic: Plans, Elevations and Models

A floor plan is the roofless view from above, drawn to scale — 1 to 100 for a house, where every millimetre of paper is 100 millimetres of building, so every centimetre is a metre. A wall measuring 62 millimetres on such a plan is really 62 times 100, which is 6 200 millimetres — 6,2 metres. The sanity check never retires: rooms live between about 2 and 8 metres; an answer of 62 metres announces a dropped conversion.

The plan's symbol language is directly examinable: paired thick lines are walls, a gap with a quarter-circle arc is a door and its swing, thin double lines are windows, WC marks the toilet. What the floor plan cannot show is UP — heights belong to the ELEVATIONS, the views a neighbour gets of each outside wall, named for the compass direction the wall faces. The matching question — which drawing is the north elevation — is solved by counting: list the doors and windows the plan puts on the north face, then find the elevation showing that pattern. Anchor to compass directions, never to left and right, because left and right flip when you step outside the wall.

Models shrink buildings to desks. A school stage of 10 metres by 6 metres modelled at 1 to 50 becomes 20 centimetres by 12 centimetres — divide each dimension by the scale factor. The reasoning trap: AREAS do not shrink by 50 but by 50 squared, so the model's top is one two-thousand-five-hundredth of the real stage. Packaging closes the family: count whole boxes along each axis of a carton and multiply — volume division alone is only a ceiling, because part of a box fits nothing.

Questions for this section reach you now: the plan measurement converted, the elevation matched by counting, and the model with its squared-area rule.

## Subtopic: Probability in the Paper

Probability is worth about 7 marks, woven into other questions, and it speaks three dialects: fraction, decimal, percentage. One die: the probability of rolling a six is 1 over 6, which is 0,17 rounded to two decimals, or about 17 percent. The memo accepts any dialect unless the question names one — then it must be obeyed.

Two coins tossed together produce four equally likely outcomes — head-head, head-tail, tail-head, tail-tail — so the probability of two heads is 1 over 4, which is 0,25. Listing ALL outcomes first is the method mark; the answer mark follows for free.

Contingency tables carry the data-flavoured marks. A school surveys 120 learners: 70 girls and 50 boys; 42 girls and 30 boys walk to school. Probability that a randomly chosen learner walks: 42 plus 30 is 72, over 120, which is 0,6. Probability of choosing a boy who walks: 30 over 120, which is 0,25. The denominator discipline decides everything — out of the WHOLE 120 unless the question narrows the group: the probability that a chosen BOY walks is 30 over 50, which is 0,6 as well, but by a different road.

The complement closes the toolkit: if the weather service says 30 percent chance of rain, the chance of no rain is 70 percent — the two must sum to 1. And the reasoning classics: a 30 percent chance does not mean rain for 30 percent of the day, and lottery numbers played every week do not grow more likely — each draw starts fresh.

The final questions of this part arrive now: three dialects of one die, the coin outcomes listed, and the table read with denominator discipline.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same skills carry one family event — a cousin's wedding at a community hall outside Mthatha, with a hall to set up, a gogo to transport, and a sky to worry about.

## Subtopic: Setting Up the Hall

The hall floor measures 15 metres by 9 metres — an area of 15 times 9, which is 135 square metres. The plan is round tables, each needing a square of floor 3 metres by 3 metres so chairs can slide and aunties can pass: 9 square metres per table. Divide the floor: 135 over 9 gives 15 tables. Eight guests per table: 15 times 8 seats 120 people — and suddenly the guest list argument has a number to respect.

The fairy lights run the edge of the hall: perimeter is 2 times the sum of 15 and 9, which is 48 metres — a different question from area, answered in plain metres, because edging and covering are different jobs.

Tea after the ceremony: the urn holds 20 litres and a cup takes 250 millilitres. Convert first — 20 litres is 20 000 millilitres — then divide: 20 000 over 250 gives 80 cups per urn. With 120 guests, one urn falls short; two urns pour 160 cups and the kitchen breathes. That last step is the exam's favourite shape: calculate, then DECIDE — is it enough, and by how much. The answer states the number and the verdict: 80 cups is less than 120 guests, so no; two urns give 40 spare.

Questions on the hall setup reach you now: tables from floor area, lights from perimeter, and the urn verdict with its reason.

## Subtopic: Getting Gogo to the Venue

Gogo lives 180 kilometres from the hall, and the ceremony starts at 10:00. Her son drives at an average of 90 kilometres per hour: time is distance over speed, 180 over 90, which is exactly 2 hours. But averages hide stops — a petrol stop and a stretch for her knees add half an hour of not-driving. Working backwards from a 09:30 arrival, comfortably before the 10:00 start: subtract the 2 hours of driving to 07:30, then the half hour of stops to 07:00. Departure 07:00, stated as the answer with its chain of reasons — that reverse timeline is a full question in the paper, marks at every subtraction.

The last stretch to the hall is on a tourist map with scale 1 to 50 000. The road from the tar turnoff measures 12 centimetres: real distance 12 times 50 000, which is 600 000 centimetres — 6 kilometres of gravel. At a careful 60 kilometres per hour, that is 6 over 60, which is 0,1 hours — times 60 gives 6 minutes. Small number, same discipline: through the units, through the 60, never guessing.

And the petrol money is a Finance cameo Paper 2 allows: the bakkie drinks 8 litres per 100 kilometres, so 180 kilometres uses 180 over 100 times 8, which is 14,4 litres; at R23,50 per litre the one-way trip costs 14,4 times 23,50 — R338,40. The wedding budget line just wrote itself.

Questions on the trip arrive now: the reverse timeline to 07:00, the gravel road off the map, and the petrol cost to the cent.

## Subtopic: Will It Rain on Saturday?

The weather app says 30 percent chance of rain on the wedding day. Three honest readings of one number: as a fraction it is 30 over 100, which simplifies to 3 over 10; as a decimal, 0,3; and its COMPLEMENT — the chance of staying dry — is 70 percent, because rain and no-rain must share the whole 100.

What 30 percent does NOT mean: it will rain for 30 percent of the day, or over 30 percent of the district. It means that on days like this one, rain has historically arrived about 3 times in 10. So the family checks history themselves: of the last 20 years, this weekend saw rain 4 times. Experimental probability: 4 over 20, which is 0,2 — 20 percent, close to the forecast and calculated from lived data. The exam names both kinds: the app's figure is a modelled prediction; the family's figure is experimental, and more years of data would make it steadier.

The decision layer carries the reasoning marks: with a 30 percent rain chance, does the family hire the gazebo for R850? There is no single right answer — the marks sit in the argument. Seventy percent says the money is wasted; 30 percent says 120 guests could stand soaked. A justified answer weighs the cost of the gazebo against the cost of being wrong, and states the choice with its reason — exactly the shape of every probability reasoning question this paper asks.

The last questions of the lesson reach you now: the three dialects and the complement, the 20-year check against the forecast, and the gazebo argued properly.
