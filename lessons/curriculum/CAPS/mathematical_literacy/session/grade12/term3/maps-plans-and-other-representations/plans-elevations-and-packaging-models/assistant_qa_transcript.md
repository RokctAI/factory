**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First: the mirror thing with elevations refuses to sit still in anyone's head. Standing outside the north wall, why does the plan's left become my right — and how do we stop losing that mark?

**Tutor:** Fix it with one physical rehearsal and one paper habit. The rehearsal: stand facing a friend and both raise your right hands — the two hands are on opposite sides of the space between you. That is exactly your situation with the house. When you read the floor plan, you hover above the rooms looking down, sharing the building's own sense of left and right. When you stand OUTSIDE the north wall looking back at the house, you have turned to face it, the way your friend faces you — so the lounge window the plan placed toward the western end now appears on the opposite side of your view. Nothing moved; your viewpoint reversed. Now the paper habit that makes the mark safe: never match elevations by left and right at all — match by compass and by neighbour. Say: the plan puts the front door in the middle of the north wall with the big window on the WESTERN side of it; then look at the candidate elevation and ask which side of the drawn door the big window sits when I am facing the wall from the north — the west is now on my right, so the window should be right of the door. Anchoring to west and east, which never flip, instead of left and right, which flip with every turn, removes the trap entirely. Examiners set the mirror question every year precisely because it separates learners who matched patterns from learners who genuinely stood themselves in the right place.

**Mandy:** Next: for the packing questions, if the volume method can lie, why does the memo still want the volume calculation at all? Why not just count along the sides and be done?

**Tutor:** Because the two calculations answer two different questions, and a full answer uses each for its own job. The axis-by-axis count answers the real question — how many whole boxes actually fit — and it must always be your headline method: 30 ÷ 6 gives five along the length, 24 ÷ 4 gives six across, 20 ÷ 10 gives two layers, sixty boxes. The volume division answers a different question: what is the absolute ceiling — the number that could fit if boxes could be sliced and poured like water. That ceiling has two honest uses. First, as a check: in our carton the count says sixty and the volume says 14 400 ÷ 240 = 60, and when the two agree exactly you have proof there is no wasted air — a perfect fit, worth stating. Second, as a measure of waste: if the boxes were 7 cm wide, the count would give 4 × 6 × 2 = 48, while the volume ceiling suggests more — and the gap between 48 and the ceiling QUANTIFIES the air you are shipping, which is exactly what a packaging question about wasted space wants calculated. What the memo penalises is using the ceiling as the answer: writing sixty-something boxes because the volumes divide, without checking that whole boxes fit along each side. So structure every packing answer the same way: count whole items axis by axis, try a second orientation if the remainders look big, then bring in the volume line labelled as a check or a waste measure. Two calculations, two labelled jobs — that is the full-marks shape.

**Mandy:** This one came from actual homework: the tin said one litre covers 8 square metres, but the hardware shop's poster said 10, and the two answers need different numbers of tins. Which coverage do we use in the exam, and what about real life?

**Tutor:** In the exam, the rule is absolute: use the number printed in the question, even if your family has painted six houses and knows better. Mathematical Literacy tests whether you can run the chain — area, minus openings, times coats, divided by the GIVEN coverage, rounded up to whole containers — on the data supplied; importing outside knowledge breaks the chain the marker is following, and your answer stops matching the memo through no arithmetic fault. If two rates ever appear in one question — a tin label and a poster — that is not carelessness, it is the question: expect to be asked to calculate under both and compare, or to explain why a painter plans with the lower coverage. Which brings us to real life, and it is worth a minute because the reasoning is examinable. Coverage per litre is a manufacturer's figure from smooth, sealed, ideal surfaces. Fresh plaster drinks paint; textured and patched walls hold more per square metre; rollers and brushes waste differently; and the first coat always spreads shorter than the second. A professional therefore plans on the conservative side of the label and adds a margin, exactly as the tiling rule adds 10 percent for cutting and breakage. Notice the shared principle, because it earns explanation marks in both contexts: estimates for buying always round pessimistically — up in quantity, down in coverage — because the cost of a small surplus is a bit of leftover paint, while the cost of a shortfall is a second trip, a colour-batch mismatch, and an unfinished wall. Optimism is free on paper and expensive in the aisle.

**Mandy:** Last one: the assembly questions feel like they belong in Life Orientation, not maths. Where are the actual marks hiding in a bunk-bed diagram?

**Tutor:** The marks are pure Mathematical Literacy, just wearing overalls — and knowing the four places they hide turns the whole question into collection work. Hiding place one: COUNTING with structure. The parts list says each of the two bed ends takes four bolts and the rails take another eight — questions ask for totals across sub-assemblies, which is multiplication and addition with the diagram as your data table. Read the list like a table and show the sum. Hiding place two: the PACK arithmetic, which is the same rounding law as tiles and paint: sixteen bolts needed, packs of six, 16 ÷ 6 = 2,67, buy three packs — and often a follow-up asks the cost, or how many bolts remain, which is the remainder: eighteen bought minus sixteen used leaves two. Whole-pack logic plus remainders is a guaranteed mark pair. Hiding place three: SEQUENCE reasoning. Why must the drawer runners go on while the side panel lies flat? Because once the box is assembled, the screwdriver cannot reach — the mark is for naming the specific constraint, access or alignment, not for saying order matters. Treat it as cause and effect stated in one precise sentence. Hiding place four: ORIENTATION and mirrored parts — the L and R panels, which way the arrow says the bolt travels along its dotted line, which face carries the pre-drilled holes. Questions ask what goes wrong if L and R swap: the holes face outward, and the mark is again in the named consequence. So approach every manual question as four small harvests: totals from the list, packs and remainders, one named reason for the order, one named consequence for the orientation. None of it is craft skill; all of it is reading structured information and calculating on it — which is this subject's whole job description.
