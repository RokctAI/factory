Hey, it's Mandy — plans look like an architect's secret code, and the exam knows most people freeze at the first double line. Good news: the code has ten words, and the maths underneath is the same scale arithmetic you already passed in term two.

Start with the friendliest conversion in the whole subject: at 1 : 100, every centimetre on the plan is a metre of real house. Ruler says the bedroom is 45 by 30 millimetres? That's 4,5 by 3 metres. Then run the sanity check: rooms are people-sized, two to eight metres. If your bathroom comes out at 40 metres, a zero slipped — catch it yourself before the marker does.

The symbols: fat double lines are walls, the quarter-moon is the door and its swing, thin lines in the wall are windows, WC is the toilet. Ten words, learned in a tea break.

Elevations are just the house seen by a neighbour, one wall at a time, named for the direction the wall FACES. For the matching question, don't trust left and right — they flip when you walk outside, like facing a friend whose right hand meets your left. Anchor to the compass instead: the window is WEST of the door on the plan, so find where west sits when you face that wall. West never flips.

Assembly and shopping questions are all one law: shops sell whole things. Eight bolts, packs of six — two packs. One hundred and sixty-five tiles, boxes of twelve — fourteen boxes. Always divide, always round UP. And packing: count whole boxes along each side — five across, six deep, two layers is sixty — and use volume only as a check, because the leftover slice ships nothing but air.

Centimetre-is-a-metre, ten symbols, compass not left-right, round up, count whole boxes. Five habits and the hard hat fits. You've got this!
