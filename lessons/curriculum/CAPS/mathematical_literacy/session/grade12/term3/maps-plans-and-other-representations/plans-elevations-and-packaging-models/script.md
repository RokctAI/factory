# Part 1 — Expert

Term two read the country off a page; this block reads a BUILDING off a page — and then packs its boxes. The Grade 12 paper hands you an architect's floor plan of an RDP house or a school block and expects four things: speak the plan's symbol language, turn plan millimetres into real metres, match the flat drawings to the building they describe from every side, and then step into three dimensions — assembly instructions, packaging arrangements, and the estimate that tells a builder how much paint and how many tiles to buy. Every skill here is the map skill wearing a hard hat: same scale arithmetic, new territory.

## Subtopic: Plan Symbols, Scale and Actual Dimensions

A building plan is a bird's-eye view of one storey, drawn as if the roof were lifted off, to a stated scale — most commonly 1 : 100 for a house, where every millimetre on paper is 100 mm of real building, so one centimetre is one metre. Larger detail drawings use 1 : 50; site plans shrink further to 1 : 200 or 1 : 500. Anchor the 1 : 100 conversion pair — millimetres times 100, then divide by 1 000 for metres — and the arithmetic of this whole topic is in hand.

The symbol language first, because the paper asks it directly. WALLS are pairs of thick parallel lines — the gap between them is the wall's real thickness. A DOOR is a gap in the wall with a quarter-circle arc showing which way it swings — the arc is the door's sweep, and questions ask what the arc means and whether the door opens into the bedroom or the passage. A WINDOW is a break in the wall drawn with thin double or triple lines. The plan also carries standard abbreviations and fittings: WC for the toilet, WHB for the wash-hand basin, a crossed rectangle for the bath, circles on the stove symbol for plates, plus the north arrow and a title block stating the scale.

Now dimensions. A bedroom wall measures 45 mm on the 1 : 100 plan: real length 45 × 100 = 4 500 mm = 4,5 m. The room's other wall measures 30 mm: 3,0 m. Floor area: 4,5 × 3,0 = 13,5 m². The reverse question supplies the real object and asks for its plan size — a 2 m wide built-in cupboard appears as 2 000 ÷ 100 = 20 mm. And the sanity check never retires: rooms come out between about 2 and 8 metres; a bedroom of 45 metres or 0,45 metres announces a conversion slip.

Note what plans measure BETWEEN: dimensions may run wall-to-wall inside a room, or over the walls for the whole building, and the paper will say which — read the dimension lines' arrows before assuming.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: the symbol language, 1 : 100 both directions, floor area from plan measurements, and the sanity check on room sizes.

## Subtopic: Floor Plans and Elevation Plans

One drawing cannot describe a whole building, so a plan set speaks in VIEWS. The FLOOR PLAN looks straight down — it fixes room layout, wall lengths and thicknesses, door swings and window positions, but says nothing about height. The ELEVATIONS look horizontally at each outside face — the NORTH ELEVATION shows the wall that faces north, and likewise south, east and west. Elevations reveal what the floor plan cannot: wall heights, roof shape and pitch, the height of windows and doors, chimneys and gutters.

The classic exam task is MATCHING: here is the floor plan, here are two or three elevations — which is the north elevation? Work it like a detective with the north arrow. From the floor plan, list what the north-facing wall contains — say, the front door plus two windows. Now interrogate each elevation: the drawing showing one door and two windows in that arrangement is the north elevation; the blank wall with a small bathroom window belongs to the side the plan shows it on. Count openings, match positions, and check left-right order — an elevation seen from outside runs mirror-wise to the way you read the room inside, and that mirror is precisely where markers set their trap.

Second task: reading COMBINED information. The floor plan gives the lounge window's width — 1 600 mm; the elevation gives its height — 1 500 mm; only together do they size the glass: 1,6 × 1,5 = 2,4 m². Height questions — will a 2,2 m wardrobe pass under a 2,4 m ceiling shown on the section — always come from the vertical drawings, never the floor plan.

Third: DRAWING to scale. Instructed to draw a 1 : 100 floor plan of a 4 m by 3 m storeroom with a 900 mm door on the long wall: the outline is 40 mm by 30 mm, the door gap 9 mm with its quarter-circle swing, window lines on the specified wall, dimensions labelled, scale stated. The marks sit in the ratios being right, the symbols standard, and the labelling complete — artistic shading earns nothing.

Stop for this section's questions now — what each view can and cannot show, the matching method with its mirror trap, window area from two drawings, and the storeroom drawn to scale.

## Subtopic: Assembly Diagrams and Instruction Manuals

From buildings to boxes that become furniture. An ASSEMBLY DIAGRAM is the wordless plan inside every flat-pack: an EXPLODED VIEW showing the parts floated apart along the lines where they join, so the eye can see what touches what and in which order. The syllabus wants you to read one methodically, and the method transfers to any manual — a bunk bed, a braai stand, a cellphone's SIM tray.

Step one: the PARTS LIST — every component drawn, named or lettered, and counted: four legs, two long rails, eight bolts marked B, sixteen washers, one Allen key. The professional habit is the INVENTORY CHECK: count the physical parts against the list before assembly starts, because discovering a missing bolt at step nine costs an undoing of steps eight through one. Exam questions mirror this: how many of part B are needed in total, and if bolts come in packs of six, how many packs must be bought — eight bolts needs two packs, with four spare, because packs are bought whole and answers round UP.

Step two: SEQUENCE. Instructions are numbered because order carries constraints — the shelf slides in before the back panel closes the box; the drawer runners screw on while the side still lies flat. The diagram encodes order in its geometry: a bolt drawn floating on a dotted line passes through the washer, then the rail, then into the leg, in exactly that order along the line. Questions probe consequence: what goes wrong if step 3 precedes step 2 — and full marks name the specific failure, the misaligned holes or trapped panel, not a vague "it will not work".

Step three: ORIENTATION cues — arrows for direction, L and R for mirrored parts, THIS SIDE UP, the wide edge to the wall. Mirrored parts are the classic assembly failure: a left and right side panel look near-identical until the drilled holes disagree.

Why examiners love this material: it tests whether you can extract an exact procedure from a technical picture under real-world constraints — counting, ordering, orienting, and buying whole packs. Read list, check count, follow numbers, respect arrows.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — the exploded view read along its dotted lines, the inventory habit, whole-pack purchases rounded up, and one out-of-order consequence named precisely.

## Subtopic: Packaging, Models and Material Estimates

The final skill family: fitting things into things, and estimating what a job consumes.

PACKAGING: how many juice boxes, 6 cm by 4 cm by 10 cm tall, fit a carton 30 cm by 24 cm by 20 cm? Method: match dimensions axis by axis, standing the boxes upright. Along the carton's 30 cm run 30 ÷ 6 = 5 boxes; along its 24 cm run 24 ÷ 4 = 6; so one layer holds 5 × 6 = 30. Height: 20 ÷ 10 = 2 layers. Total 30 × 2 = 60 boxes. Cross-check with volume: carton 30 × 24 × 20 = 14 400 cm³, box 6 × 4 × 10 = 240 cm³, and 14 400 ÷ 240 = 60 — the fit is perfect, no wasted air. The caution the paper tests: volume division alone can overpromise. It gives the ceiling, not always the fit — if the boxes were 7 cm wide, 30 ÷ 7 = 4 whole boxes per row with 2 cm left over, and the leftover slice fits nothing; only the axis-by-axis count tells the truth about whole items, and part-items do not ship. Try more than one orientation when the first leaves big remainders.

SCALE MODELS run the plan arithmetic into 3D: a school stage 10 m by 6 m modelled at 1 : 50 becomes 20 cm by 12 cm — divide each real dimension by the scale, and remember from measurement work that AREAS scale by the square of the factor, so the model's top is not one-fiftieth of the stage's area but one two-thousand-five-hundredth.

MATERIAL ESTIMATES, the money end. Tiles: the 4,5 m by 3 m bedroom floor is 13,5 m²; one 300 mm by 300 mm tile covers 0,09 m²; 13,5 ÷ 0,09 = 150 tiles. Add the standard 10 percent for cutting and breakage: 165. Sold in boxes of 12: 165 ÷ 12 = 13,75, so buy 14 boxes — rounding UP again, because a floor cannot be finished with three-quarters of a box. Paint: the room's walls run 2 × (4,5 + 3) = 15 m around, 2,4 m high: 36 m². Subtract openings — the 0,8 m by 2 m door is 1,6 m², the 1,6 m by 1,5 m window 2,4 m²: paintable area 32 m². Two coats doubles it to 64 m²; at a coverage of 8 m² per litre, 64 ÷ 8 = 8 litres exactly. State the chain — area, minus openings, times coats, divided by coverage, rounded up to buyable containers — and every link earns its mark.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the axis-by-axis fit against the volume ceiling, the model's dimensions and the squared area rule, and both estimates run to buyable quantities.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same skills on one Saturday — a cousin's RDP house plan on the kitchen table, a flat-pack bed in a box, and a trip to the hardware store.

## Subtopic: The Drawing That Speaks Builder

Your cousin unrolls the plan of her new house, and it looks like secret writing. It is — but the language has ten words and you can learn them before the tea cools.

The fat double lines are WALLS — the gap between them is how thick the real wall will be. A gap in the wall with a little quarter-moon is a DOOR, and the moon shows which way it swings — into the bedroom, so no bed may stand behind it. Thin double lines in a wall are a WINDOW. WC is the toilet, the crossed box is the bath, the circle-topped square is the stove. Roof off, looking straight down: that is all a floor plan is.

The magic is the scale: 1 : 100 — every millimetre of paper is 100 mm of house, which means EVERY CENTIMETRE IS A METRE. Ruler on the main bedroom: 45 mm by 30 mm. Real room: 4,5 m by 3 m. Can the 2 m wide wardrobe fit that 3 m wall and still leave the door's quarter-moon clear? Two centimetres of paper — 20 mm — parks the wardrobe on the plan and answers before the truck arrives. That is the whole point of a plan: rearrange furniture with a pencil, at no cost to your back.

And run the sense check like a habit: rooms are people-sized — two to eight metres. If your conversion says the bathroom is 40 m long, the house did not grow; a zero slipped.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the unrolled plan are coming to you right now: the ten-word language, centimetres to metres at 1 : 100, and the wardrobe parked with a pencil.

## Subtopic: Walking Around the Paper House

The floor plan sees the house as a hawk sees it. But a hawk cannot tell you how tall the windows are — for that, the plan set walks around the outside and stares at each wall like a neighbour: four ELEVATIONS, north, south, east and west, each named for the direction the wall FACES.

The exam's favourite game: here are the floor plan and two elevations — which is which? Play detective with the north arrow. The plan shows the front door and the two big lounge windows on the north side. So pick up each elevation and count: this one has a door with a window either side — north elevation, found. That one is nearly blank with one tiny window — that is the bathroom's wall; check the plan for where the bathroom hides — south. Count the openings, match the pattern — and mind the mirror: standing OUTSIDE looking at the north wall, what the plan showed on the left now appears on the right, the way a friend's raised right hand faces your left. That mirror flip is exactly where the trick answer waits.

The views also share the work. Plan says the lounge window is 1,6 m wide; elevation says it is 1,5 m tall; only together do they price the glass — 2,4 square metres. Will the 2,2 m wardrobe clear the ceiling? Only a vertical drawing knows; the floor plan is silent about UP.

And when the paper says DRAW — a 1 : 100 plan of a 4 by 3 storeroom — it is asking for the ratios, not for art: a 40 mm by 30 mm rectangle, a 9 mm door gap with its quarter-moon, the window's thin lines, sizes labelled, scale written. Neat counts; shading does not.

Your questions for this part are up now: what only elevations know, the detective method with its mirror, the window priced by two drawings, and what DRAW actually marks.

## Subtopic: Boxes, Beds and the Hardware List

Saturday afternoon: the flat-pack bed and the hardware run.

The bed's box holds a picture where the bed has EXPLODED — every part floating apart, dotted lines showing the flight path home: this bolt travels through this washer, through this rail, into this leg, in that exact order. Before anything else, do what the pros do: tip out and COUNT against the parts list. Eight bolts promised; if seven arrived, better to know now than at step nine with the frame half-standing. And if the shop sells bolts in packs of six, eight needed means TWO packs — you cannot buy a third of a pack, so buying always rounds up. Then obey the numbers: instructions are numbered because the shelf must slide in BEFORE the back panel closes the box — skip ahead and step nine includes undoing steps one to eight. Watch the L and the R: the two side panels are twins until the holes disagree.

Then the hardware store, with the bedroom's numbers in your pocket. Tiles: floor 4,5 by 3 is 13,5 square metres; each 30 cm tile covers 0,09; that is 150 tiles — but tiles crack and edges need cutting, so add 10 percent: 165. Boxes of twelve: 165 ÷ 12 = 13,75 — buy 14, because the shop does not split boxes and a floor does not stop at three-quarters. Paint: walls 15 m around, 2,4 m up — 36 square metres; take away the door's 1,6 and the window's 2,4 — 32 to paint; two coats — 64; the tin says 8 square metres a litre — exactly 8 litres. Say the chain out loud in the aisle: area, minus holes, times coats, divide by coverage, round UP to tins.

One last box trick for the taxi ride home: 60 juice boxes fit that carton — five across, six deep, two layers — and the volume division agrees, 14 400 over 240. But if the boxes were a centimetre wider, whole rows would vanish while the volume sum barely blinked: real packing counts WHOLE boxes along each side, and the leftover slice ships nothing but air. Divide axis by axis first; use volume only to check.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: the exploded view read along its dotted line, both hardware estimates rounded to what shops actually sell, and the wider-box trap explained in one sentence. Plans, elevations, manuals, cartons — one Saturday, one skill: scale with a purpose.
