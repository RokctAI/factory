Hey, it's Mandy — measurement is the friendliest section of Paper 2, because every formula you need is printed on the question paper. Your job is never to remember formulas; it is to feed them correctly and read the answer back into real life.

Conversions first. Inside the metric family, only the comma moves — ask whether the number should grow or shrink, and the direction sorts itself. Between metric and imperial, the exam gives you the factor like a little dictionary: bigger unit means divide, smaller unit means multiply, and check the answer's size against common sense. Temperature alone gets a formula, because the two scales start at different zeros: subtract 32, then divide by 1,8, and check against the anchors — 350 °F is a normal 180 °C oven, not a furnace.

Volume and surface area: one object, two questions. Filling, holding, litres — that is volume, cubic units. Painting, tiling, wrapping — that is surface area, square units. Before any formula, do the two safety moves: halve the diameter to get the radius, and get every measurement into the same unit. Then cubic metres become litres by the thousand.

Rates are just divisions the unit spells out for you: rands per kilogram is rands divided by kilograms, litres per minute fills the tank, distance over time gives speed. Do the division, then turn the number back into a decision — which bag is cheaper, when the tank is full.

And the golden shopping rule: materials round UP. 1,41 litres of paint means buy 2. Doses from a bottle round DOWN. Say the sentence with the rounding and the mark is yours.

Formulas are given, units are the referee, and the answer is always a sentence. You've got this!
