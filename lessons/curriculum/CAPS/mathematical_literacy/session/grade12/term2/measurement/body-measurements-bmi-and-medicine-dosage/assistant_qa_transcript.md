**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! The one that comes up every year: the BMI question gave height as 168 cm, and half the class got 0,003 and just wrote it down. How do we stop ourselves submitting an impossible answer?

**Tutor:** By keeping a mental picture of what human numbers look like, and treating any answer outside that picture as an alarm, not a result. Adult BMI values live roughly between 15 and 45 — a marathon runner might dip toward 17, a very large person might pass 40, but nobody on Earth is 0,003, and nobody is 2 900 either, which is what appears if the units are mangled the other way. The 0,003 has one cause: height fed into the formula in centimetres. The formula divides by height squared, and squaring amplifies the unit error ten-thousand-fold — 168 squared is 28 224, while 1,68 squared is 2,8224. So the working habit is mechanical: the moment a height arrives in centimetres, convert it before it touches the formula — slide the comma two places, 168 becomes 1,68, and write the converted value into your substitution line so the marker sees it. Then, after every calculation, spend three seconds asking whether the answer could belong to a person. That reasonableness check is not just self-protection; interpreting the sensibleness of results is itself an assessment skill in this subject, and the learner who writes 0,003 with a note saying this is impossible, the units must be rechecked, shows more mathematical literacy than the one who writes it in silence.

**Mandy:** Next: if the healthy BMI range tops out at 24,9, why did the lesson say a rugby player with a BMI of 28 might be perfectly healthy? It sounds like the categories contradict themselves.

**Tutor:** The categories are honest about the two numbers they see — mass and height — and blind to everything else, and that blindness is the whole point of the limitation question. BMI cannot tell what the mass is MADE of. Muscle is denser than fat, so a tight-head prop stacked with muscle registers a high mass, and the formula reads that as overweight even when a doctor examining him would find little body fat. The same blindness runs in other directions: an elderly patient who has lost muscle can show a comfortable BMI while being under-nourished; a pregnant woman's BMI means something different again; and the adult categories must never be applied to children, whose healthy values change with every year of age — that is exactly why the clinic uses percentile growth charts for the little ones instead. So the sentence the exam wants is precise: BMI is a screening tool, computed from mass and height alone, that flags who deserves a closer look; it is not a diagnosis, because it cannot distinguish muscle from fat or account for age and body type. When a question asks you to interpret a sportsman's high BMI, give both halves — the number falls in the overweight category, AND the category may misread an athlete because of muscle mass. The calculation mark and the insight mark are separate, and the strongest answers collect both.

**Mandy:** This one caused an argument: a mother sees her baby is on the 15th percentile and panics that the baby is underweight. Someone else's baby fell from the 75th to the 40th and the parents were relaxed. Who should actually worry?

**Tutor:** The relaxed parents are the ones who should be asking questions, and the panicking mother can probably breathe out — the chart is about the journey, not the address. Line up one hundred healthy six-month-olds from lightest to heaviest, and a healthy baby stands at EVERY position in that line; someone must be fifteenth from the light end, and standing there is not a disease. A baby who was born small, tracks steadily along the 15th curve visit after visit, feeds well and meets her milestones is simply a small, thriving baby. What the chart is designed to catch is CROSSING lanes downward: the baby who stood at the 75th percentile at three months and stands at the 40th at seven months has drifted backwards past a third of her peers, and even though the 40th percentile is a perfectly respectable address on its own, the direction of travel says her growth has slowed, and a nurse should look for the reason — feeding trouble, illness, or something else. One dot is a photograph; the sequence of dots is the film, and medicine watches the film. There is also an upper-side version: a rapid climb across lanes invites its own questions. For the exam, plot the point accurately — age across, mass up — name the percentile band, and then interpret the TREND across visits in a sentence with context. The trend sentence is where the interpretation marks sit.

**Mandy:** Last one: in the dosage question, someone got 8,88 doses from the bottle and rounded to 9, arguing we always round up so the medicine does not run out. Why is that dangerous here?

**Tutor:** Because the ninth dose does not exist — only 8,88 of a dose remains in the glass, and calling it 9 pretends the bottle contains medicine it does not hold. Work the numbers: eight full doses of 11,25 ml consume 90 ml, leaving 10 ml in a bottle whose proper dose is 11,25 ml. That remaining 10 ml is a SHORT dose — an under-dose that treats the fever inadequately while teaching the family the bottle is finished when it is not. So doses-per-container always rounds DOWN: the honest answer is 8 full doses, which at three a day is two full days and two doses of the third, and the practical conclusion — the family needs the next bottle or the follow-up visit before the third day ends — is the sentence the marker wants. Notice how this flips the paint-tin rule, and understand WHY it flips rather than memorising two rules blindly: rounding always bends toward safety, and safety points in opposite directions in the two contexts. Short of paint, the wall is ugly, so you buy more — round up. Short of medicine in a measured dose, the child is under-treated; and the mirror error, stretching or doubling doses to catch up, risks overdose, which for paracetamol means liver damage. So the dosage chapter of rounding reads: doses per bottle round down, a missed dose is never doubled, and totals are checked against the daily maximum. In this topic the arithmetic is easy precisely because the judgement is the examinable part.
