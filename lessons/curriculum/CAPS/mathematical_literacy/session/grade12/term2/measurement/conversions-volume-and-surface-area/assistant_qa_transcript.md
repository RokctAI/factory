**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! The one that comes up every year: how do I decide whether to multiply or divide by a conversion factor? Half the class turned 2 pounds into 4,41 kg.

**Tutor:** Let the size of the units make the decision, before any arithmetic. Every conversion factor is a sentence about size: 1 kg equals 2,205 pounds says the kilogram is the bigger unit — one of it contains just over two pounds. Moving INTO the bigger unit, your number must shrink, so you divide; moving into the smaller unit, your number must grow, so you multiply. The recipe's 2 pounds heading into kilograms is moving into the bigger unit: divide, 2 by 2,205, giving 0,907 kg. The classmates who multiplied got 4,41 kg, and the answer itself was shouting at them — two pounds of flour is a modest bag you can lift with one finger, while 4,41 kg is a sack, and no honest conversion doubles a quantity while switching to a LARGER unit. So build the habit in three beats: first say which unit is bigger, then say whether your number should grow or shrink, then pick the operation that obeys. And write the factor down as given in the exam's table, because the method mark attaches to the factor line. The check at the end costs five seconds: convert your answer back the other way, and 0,907 times 2,205 lands within rounding of 2 pounds, which certifies the direction was right.

**Mandy:** Next: why does temperature get a formula while every other conversion gets a factor? Someone tried 350 °F times a factor and reached oven temperatures that would melt the baking tray.

**Tutor:** Because Fahrenheit and Celsius disagree about something no other unit pair disagrees about: where zero sits. Kilograms and pounds both agree that zero mass is zero — nothing weighs nothing in every system — so the two scales are pure stretches of each other, and one multiplication converts them. Temperature scales were invented before anyone knew what zero heat meant, so each picked its own landmark: Celsius parks 0 at freezing water; Fahrenheit parks freezing water at 32. The scales are shifted, not just stretched, and a shift needs addition or subtraction as well as multiplication — hence the formula: Fahrenheit is 1,8 times Celsius, plus 32. Going from Fahrenheit back to Celsius, undo the steps in reverse order, exactly like unwrapping a parcel: first undo the plus 32, then undo the times 1,8. The recipe's 350 °F: subtract 32 to get 318, divide by 1,8 to get 176,7 °C, and set the oven to 180, the nearest dial marking. The learner who multiplied 350 by 1,8 was running the machine in the wrong direction on top of skipping the shift — the formula as written STARTS from Celsius. Read which unit you are holding before choosing the direction, and remember the sanity anchors: 0 °C is 32 °F, body heat is about 37 °C or roughly 98 °F, and a moderate oven is about 180 °C or 350 °F. Any conversion that violently disagrees with those landmarks went wrong somewhere.

**Mandy:** This one caused an argument: for the tank, one group calculated the volume and another calculated the surface area, and both insisted they had answered how big the tank is. Who was right?

**Tutor:** Both calculated correctly and only one answered the question asked — which is precisely why this pair of concepts is the most examined confusion in measurement. Volume measures the space INSIDE: how much water the tank swallows, in cubic metres or litres. Surface area measures the skin OUTSIDE: how much steel wraps the water, in square metres. The tank holds 4 581 litres but wears only about 12,73 square metres of paintable skin — two different questions about one object, with different units as the giveaway. So make the units your referee: any answer about filling, holding, capacity or litres is volume, in cubic units; any answer about painting, tiling, wrapping or covering is area, in square units. If your surface area came out in cubic metres, you answered the wrong question, however beautiful the arithmetic. The practical test that never fails: ask what the material in the question actually touches. Water fills the inside — volume. Paint lies on the skin — area. A question about a concrete floor under the tank is area again, the circle only. And when the exam asks how big is the tank with no material mentioned, the follow-up unit tells you what they meant: litres means volume, square metres means area. Read for the material, answer in its units, and the confusion never costs you again.

**Mandy:** Last one: the paint came to 1,41 litres and someone rounded to 1 litre because we are taught to round to the nearest whole number. When does normal rounding stop applying?

**Tutor:** The moment a number turns into something you must physically buy or provide, ordinary rounding retires and context rounding takes over — and this is a favourite mark in Paper 2 precisely because it tests judgement, not arithmetic. Nearest-whole rounding treats 1,41 as closer to 1, which is true on the number line and useless at the hardware store: one litre covers 9 square metres, the tank needs 12,73 covered, and the job dies a third short with the shop closed. Materials round UP, always — 1,41 litres means buy 2; 7,2 tiles means buy 8; 3,1 taxi loads means 4 trips. The leftover is the price of finishing the job. The same logic runs in reverse for questions asking how many fit: a 100 ml bottle of syrup providing 11,25 ml doses gives 8 full doses, not 9, because the ninth dose would be short — capacity questions round DOWN. So the rule pair is: buying rounds up, fitting rounds down, and the nearest-whole habit applies only to pure numbers nobody must purchase or pour. In the exam, write the decimal first — 12,73 over 9 gives 1,41 — then the context sentence: paint is sold in whole litres, so 2 litres must be bought. The decimal earns the calculation mark; the sentence earns the interpretation mark; both together are full marks.
