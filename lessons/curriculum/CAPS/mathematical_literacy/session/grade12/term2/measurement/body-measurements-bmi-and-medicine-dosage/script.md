# Part 1 — Expert

At the clinic in Umlazi, measurement becomes personal. A community health worker named Naledi weighs adults for their annual check, plots babies on growth charts, and counts out medicine doses measured against body weight. Get the mathematics wrong here and the consequences are not lost marks but real harm — which is exactly why Paper 2 tests it. This session covers the measuring itself, the BMI formula and its categories, the percentile curves in the Road to Health book, and the milligrams-per-kilogram arithmetic of safe dosage.

## Subtopic: Measuring the Body: Mass, Height and the Right Units

Body measurement runs on two instruments and two units. MASS is measured on a scale in kilograms; the clinic scale is zeroed before each patient, shoes off, heavy jackets off, because a measurement is only as honest as its setup. HEIGHT is measured in metres against a stadiometer — the sliding ruler on the clinic wall — heels together, back straight, eyes level.

The unit discipline matters more here than anywhere else in measurement, because the BMI formula ahead demands kilograms and METRES — not centimetres. A patient measured at 168 cm must be recorded as 1,68 m before any calculation: divide centimetres by 100, shift the comma two places. Naledi's first patient this morning, a taxi driver named Bongani, weighs 82 kg and stands 1,68 m tall.

Babies get their own instruments: a hanging or pan scale for mass in kilograms to the nearest 0,1 kg, a measuring mat for length in centimetres, and a tape for HEAD CIRCUMFERENCE — the distance around the skull, tracked because it follows brain growth in the first two years.

Precision is chosen to fit the purpose. Adult mass to the nearest 0,5 kg is fine for a check-up; a baby's mass needs 0,1 kg because a newborn's whole body is only about 3 kg, and a 0,5 kg error would be enormous in proportion. Reading scales also means reading BETWEEN the printed numbers: if the needle rests halfway between 82 and 83, the mass is 82,5 kg, and interpolating a scale correctly is itself an examinable skill. The questions for this section are with you now; record each measurement with its unit, and convert centimetres to metres before anything else.

## Subtopic: BMI: the Formula and What It Means

The BODY MASS INDEX compresses two measurements into one screening number: BMI equals mass in kilograms, divided by height in metres SQUARED. Its unit is kilograms per square metre, and the formula is always supplied — the marks are for substitution, calculation and interpretation.

Bongani: 82 kg and 1,68 m. Height squared first: 1,68 × 1,68 = 2,8224. Then divide: 82 ÷ 2,8224 = 29,1 to one decimal place.

Now interpretation, against the adult categories. Below 18,5: underweight. From 18,5 to 24,9: the healthy range. From 25 to 29,9: overweight. From 30 upward: obese. Bongani's 29,1 places him in the overweight category, near its upper edge — the full-marks sentence names the number, the category and the context: a BMI of 29,1 falls in the overweight range, so the nurse advises changes to diet and activity.

The catastrophic error is using centimetres. Divide 82 by 168 squared and the result is 0,003 — a number no human could have. Any BMI outside roughly 10 to 60 is the calculator telling you the units are wrong. Reasonableness-checking your own answer is a mark-earning habit: adult BMIs live between about 15 and 45.

Know also what BMI cannot see. It reads only mass and height — not muscle, not bone, not age, not pregnancy. A rugby prop dense with muscle can register overweight while carrying little fat, which is why BMI is a SCREENING tool that flags who needs a closer look, never a diagnosis by itself. Exam questions ask exactly that limitation in words. The questions for this section are ready; square the height, divide, name the category, and question the tool politely.

## Subtopic: Growth Charts and Percentiles

A baby cannot be judged by adult categories, so clinics track growth on PERCENTILE CURVES — the printed bands in the Road to Health book every South African child receives at birth. The chart's horizontal axis is age; the vertical axis is mass, or length, or head circumference; and the curves fanning across it are labelled 3rd, 15th, 50th, 85th and 97th percentiles.

A PERCENTILE locates a child among a hundred peers of the same age and sex. On the 50th percentile, the child sits exactly at the middle of the crowd — half of peers weigh less. On the 85th, the child outweighs 85 of the hundred; on the 15th, only 15 weigh less. A baby girl of 6 months weighing 7,3 kg plots on the 50th curve: perfectly average, and average here is excellent news.

The reading skill is two-way. Given age and mass, find the point and name its percentile band. Given age and a percentile, read off the expected mass: the same chart says a 6-month girl on the 85th percentile weighs about 8,4 kg. Between labelled curves, interpolate: a point midway between the 15th and 50th bands is read as roughly the 30th percentile — approximation is expected and stated.

What matters medically is less the position than the JOURNEY. A healthy child tracks roughly along one curve as the months pass. A child who falls from the 50th percentile to the 15th across two visits is growing too slowly and is referred, even though the 15th percentile alone is a perfectly acceptable address; a sudden climb across bands raises the opposite question. One reading is a photograph; the chart is a film. Examiners ask for both: plot the point, and interpret the trend in a sentence. The questions for this section follow now; age across, measurement up, and read the story of the curves, not just the point.

## Subtopic: Medicine Dosage: Milligrams per Kilogram

Children's medicine is dosed by body weight, because a 12 kg toddler and an 82 kg adult cannot swallow the same amount. The prescription arrives as a RATE — so many milligrams of the active ingredient per kilogram of the child — and the label of the bottle supplies a CONCENTRATION: so many milligrams dissolved in so many millilitres of syrup.

Naledi's afternoon patient is a girl of 18 kg with a fever. The paracetamol syrup prescribes 15 mg per kilogram per dose. Step one, the child's dose in milligrams: 15 × 18 = 270 mg. Step two, translate milligrams into syrup. The label reads 120 mg per 5 ml. Each millilitre therefore carries 120 ÷ 5 = 24 mg, and the dose needs 270 ÷ 24 = 11,25 ml — measured with a marked syringe, because kitchen teaspoons are not instruments.

Step three, the day's total and the bottle's life. The dose repeats every six hours, at most three times in this schedule: 11,25 × 3 = 33,75 ml per day. A 100 ml bottle delivers 100 ÷ 11,25 = 8,88 doses — which the mathematics of medicine rounds DOWN: 8 full doses, because dose nine would be short. Eight doses at three per day is two full days and two doses of the third.

The safety arithmetic is not optional decoration; it is the topic. Overdosing paracetamol damages the liver, so the maximum daily limit is checked against the plan, doses are never doubled after a missed one, and rounding in dosage always errs toward LESS, the opposite of the paint tin. Exam questions replicate the pharmacist's checks exactly: dose from rate, volume from concentration, doses per bottle rounded down, and a sentence confirming the schedule is safe. The questions for this section are with you now; rate times mass, then millilitres from the label, and round every division the safe way.

## Subtopic: The Number on the Clinic Wall

Two measurements start everything: the scale under your feet and the ruler at your back. Kilograms and metres — and that second word is where the whole topic is won or lost. The wall chart says 168. That is centimetres, and the formula ahead eats METRES, so slide the comma two places: 1,68. Do that conversion the moment the number is written down, before it has a chance to sneak into a formula wearing the wrong clothes.

Why so strict? Watch what happens to Bongani, 82 kg, if the centimetres stay. The BMI recipe is mass divided by height squared. With metres: 1,68 squared is about 2,82, and 82 divided by 2,82 is 29,1 — a number that sounds like a body. With centimetres: 168 squared is over twenty-eight thousand, and 82 divided by that is 0,003 — a number that sounds like a paperclip. The calculator did not make a mistake; it obediently cooked the wrong ingredients. Your defence is knowing what human numbers look like: adult BMIs live roughly between 15 and 45. See anything outside that and you check the units before anything else.

Reading the scale itself is a skill too. The needle between 82 and 83, resting in the middle: 82,5. A baby's scale counts in tenths because a newborn is only about 3 kg — half a kilogram is a sixth of the whole child.

Zero the scale, shoes off, comma across, and know what a human answer sounds like. Some questions on this section are with you right now; convert first, calculate second, and reject any answer that could not belong to a person.

## Subtopic: Reading the Curves

Open a Road to Health book and the growth chart looks like a rainbow of curves. Here is the picture that makes it simple: imagine one hundred six-month-old babies lined up from lightest to heaviest. Your baby stands somewhere in that line, and the percentile is her position. On the 50th percentile she stands dead centre — half the babies are lighter. On the 85th, she is heavier than 85 of them. On the 15th, she is one of the smaller ones — and that can be completely fine, because somebody healthy stands everywhere in a line of healthy babies.

Using the chart is two moves: age along the bottom, weight up the side, and a dot where they meet. The curve the dot lands on is the position in the line. Baby girl, six months, 7,3 kg: the dot kisses the 50th curve, dead centre of the line. If the dot lands between two printed curves, say between the 15th and the 50th, estimate — around the 30th — and nobody expects more precision than the printed bands allow.

Now the part parents and examiners both need: one dot is a photograph, but the book collects dots every visit, and the STORY is in the sequence. A healthy baby walks along her own curve month after month — the 15th-percentile baby staying on the 15th is thriving. The alarm is crossing lanes downward: 50th at three months, 30th at six, 15th at nine — that baby is drifting backwards through the line, and the nurse refers her even though every single dot, alone, looks acceptable. The chart watches the journey, not the address.

Questions on this section are coming your way now; find the dot with two fingers, one from the age, one from the weight, then read the story across the visits.

## Subtopic: The Medicine Spoon

Children's medicine is a two-step recipe, and both steps are just the multiplication and division you have done all year — with a life attached.

Step one: how much MEDICINE does this child need? The doctor's rate says 15 milligrams for every kilogram of child. Our patient is 18 kg: fifteen times eighteen — 270 milligrams. A bigger child gets more, a smaller child less; the rate scales the medicine to the body.

Step two: how much SYRUP carries that medicine? The bottle's label says 120 mg in every 5 ml — that is the strength of the mixture, like the strength of cordial. So each single millilitre carries 24 mg. To gather 270 mg, divide: 270 over 24 — 11,25 ml, drawn up in the marked syringe from the clinic, never a kitchen spoon, because spoons at home hold anything from 3 to 7 ml.

Now the bottle question, and the rounding rule that flips. The bottle holds 100 ml; each dose takes 11,25. Divide: 8,88. With paint you rounded UP to finish the job — with medicine you round DOWN: 8 full doses, because squeezing out a ninth would give the child a short dose. Three doses a day means the bottle serves two full days and two doses of the third, and the family needs the follow-up visit planned BEFORE the syrup dies.

And the two safety rules the numbers must always respect: never exceed the daily maximum — three doses of 11,25 ml is 33,75 ml of syrup carrying 810 mg, inside the limit for an 18 kg child — and never double a dose to catch up a missed one. When in doubt, less and ask, never more and hope. The final questions of the lesson are with you now; rate times weight, label turns milligrams into millilitres, and every rounding bends toward safety.
