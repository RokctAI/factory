# Part 1 — Expert

Nomvula runs a catering business from her home in Gqeberha, and this month everything is measurement. An American recipe wants pounds and Fahrenheit; the new water tank needs its capacity checked against the supplier's promise; the tank stand must be painted; and the delivery bakkie's trip to Makhanda must be timed and costed. Paper 2 builds its measurement questions from exactly this kind of month — conversions, volume, surface area and rates, always in context, always with a decision at the end.

## Subtopic: Conversions: Metric, Imperial and the Oven Dial

The metric system moves in tens: 10 millimetres to a centimetre, 100 centimetres to a metre, 1 000 metres to a kilometre; 1 000 millilitres to a litre, 1 000 litres to a kilolitre; 1 000 grams to a kilogram, 1 000 kilograms to a tonne. Converting inside the metric family is only shifting the comma: 2 500 g is 2,5 kg; 0,75 m is 75 cm.

Between metric and IMPERIAL units — the older system American recipes and some tools still use — there is no comma-shifting, only CONVERSION FACTORS supplied in a table: 1 inch is 2,54 cm; 1 foot is 30,48 cm; 1 mile is 1,609 km; 1 pound is 0,454 kg, so 1 kg is 2,205 pounds; 1 gallon is 3,785 litres. The method is one honest multiplication or division, guided by direction: going FROM the named unit, multiply by its factor; going TO it, divide. The recipe's 2 pounds of flour: 2 ÷ 2,205 = 0,907 kg, so Nomvula weighs off about 907 g.

Temperature is the exception — Celsius and Fahrenheit are related by a formula, not a factor, because their zeros sit in different places. Fahrenheit equals 1,8 times Celsius, plus 32. The recipe says bake at 350 °F: work backwards, 350 − 32 = 318, then 318 ÷ 1,8 = 176,7 °C, so the oven dial goes to 180 °C, the nearest setting. Checking forwards: a 25 °C summer day is 1,8 × 25 + 32 = 77 °F.

The examinable habits: quote the conversion factor you used, keep units written at every line, and round only at the end — and sensibly, because an oven cannot be set to 176,7 degrees. The questions for this section are with you now; write the factor, state the direction, and carry the units.

## Subtopic: Volume: How Much the Tank Holds

VOLUME measures the space inside a three-dimensional object, and CAPACITY expresses that volume as liquid: one cubic metre holds exactly 1 000 litres; one cubic centimetre holds one millilitre.

Nomvula's new water tank is a cylinder: radius 0,9 m, height 1,8 m. The volume of a cylinder is pi times the radius squared times the height, with pi taken as 3,142. Substitute in order: radius squared is 0,9 × 0,9 = 0,81. Multiply by the height: 0,81 × 1,8 = 1,458. Multiply by pi: 3,142 × 1,458 = 4,581 m³ to three decimal places. Convert to capacity: 4,581 × 1 000 = 4 581 litres. The supplier's brochure promised roughly 4 500 litres — the promise checks out, and saying so IS the answer: a Mathematical Literacy calculation ends in a sentence about the context.

Her chest freezer is a rectangular prism: 1,2 m long, 0,8 m wide, 0,9 m deep. Volume of a prism is length times width times height: 1,2 × 0,8 × 0,9 = 0,864 m³, which is 864 litres.

Two disciplines carry the marks. First, the radius is HALF the diameter — a tank advertised as 1,8 m wide has a radius of 0,9 m, and substituting the diameter quadruples the answer, since the radius gets squared. Second, keep units consistent before multiplying: mixing metres and centimetres in one formula produces nonsense. If a measurement arrives in centimetres, convert it before substitution, not after. The questions for this section are ready; write the formula, substitute with units, convert to litres at the end.

## Subtopic: Surface Area: How Much Paint

Volume fills an object; SURFACE AREA wraps it. Painting, tiling, wrapping and rust-proofing are all surface area problems, and confusing the two is the classic measurement error.

The tank's cylinder must be painted on its curved wall and its flat top — not the base, which stands on concrete. The curved wall unrolls into a rectangle whose length is the circle's circumference, 2 × pi × radius, and whose width is the height: 2 × 3,142 × 0,9 × 1,8. Step by step: 2 × 3,142 = 6,284; times 0,9 gives 5,656 to three decimals; times 1,8 gives 10,180 m². The top is a circle: pi times radius squared, 3,142 × 0,81 = 2,545 m². Total to paint: 10,180 + 2,545 = 12,725 m², call it 12,73 m².

Now the buying decision, where CAPS rounding discipline decides real money. The paint tin says one litre covers 9 m². Litres needed: 12,73 ÷ 9 = 1,41 litres. Paint sells in whole litres, so Nomvula buys 2 litres — and here is the rule: quantities you BUY round UP, always, regardless of the decimal. Rounding 1,41 down to 1 litre leaves a third of the tank bare; the mathematics of the shop is the ceiling, not the nearest whole number.

Notice what was NOT painted: the base. Real surface area questions specify which faces count — an open box has no lid, a room's floor is tiled but its ceiling is not — and the marks follow the reading as much as the formula. Area is measured in square units, volume in cubic units; an answer in the wrong unit family signals the wrong concept entirely. The questions for this section follow now; list the faces first, add their areas, and round the shopping upward.

## Subtopic: Rates: Speed, Cost and Filling Time

A RATE joins two different units into one number: kilometres per hour, rands per kilogram, litres per minute. Every rate question is a division waiting to be read correctly — the unit itself says what to divide by what.

Speed is distance over time. The delivery bakkie covers the 420 km to Makhanda and back in 4,5 hours: 420 ÷ 4,5 = 93,33 km/h on average. Rearranged, time is distance over speed, and distance is speed times time — one triangle of relationships, three questions the exam can ask.

Filling time runs on the same logic with litres. The municipal pipe fills the tank at 12 litres per minute; the tank holds 4 581 litres: 4 581 ÷ 12 = 381,75 minutes. Translate for a human: 381,75 ÷ 60 is 6 hours and about 22 minutes, since 6 hours accounts for 360 minutes and 21,75 remain. Opening the tap at 08:00 means a full tank a little after 14:20 — the sentence, again, is the answer.

Cost rates decide value for money. Rice for the weekend function: a 5 kg bag at R89,99 or a 10 kg bag at R169,99. Convert both to rands per kilogram: 89,99 ÷ 5 = R18,00 per kg; 169,99 ÷ 10 = R17,00 per kg. The bigger bag is the better buy, saving one rand on every kilogram — but only if 10 kg will actually be used before it spoils; the paper awards the calculation AND the judgement.

The discipline: write every rate with its full unit, rands per kilogram, litres per minute, and the division writes itself — the unit's top goes on top, its bottom on the bottom. The questions for this section are with you now; divide as the unit instructs, then turn the number back into a time, a cost or a choice.

## Subtopic: Changing Units Without Fear

Metric units are a family staircase: millimetre, centimetre, metre, kilometre — each step a jump of ten or a hundred or a thousand. Walking down to smaller units, the number grows: 2,5 kg becomes 2 500 g. Walking up to bigger units, the number shrinks: 75 cm becomes 0,75 m. Ask yourself one question before every conversion: should my answer get bigger or smaller? Grams are smaller than kilograms, so the same flour needs MORE of them — if your converted number moved the wrong way, the comma jumped the wrong direction.

Imperial units — pounds, inches, gallons, miles — are the overseas cousins, and you are never expected to know them by heart. The exam gives a little dictionary: 1 kg is 2,205 pounds, 1 inch is 2,54 cm, 1 gallon is 3,785 litres. Using the dictionary is one multiplication or one division, and the bigger-or-smaller question picks between them. The recipe wants 2 pounds of flour. A pound is less than half a kilogram, so 2 pounds must be UNDER one kilogram: 2 divided by 2,205 gives 0,907 kg — about 907 g. Had you multiplied, 4,41 kg would be screaming that it moved the wrong way.

Ovens are the special case: 350 °F is not 350 × anything — Fahrenheit starts counting from a different zero, so there is a two-step recipe instead of a factor. Off with the 32, then divide by 1,8: 350 becomes 318 becomes about 177, so set the dial to 180 °C.

Estimate first, calculate second, and let the size of the answer confirm the direction. Some questions on this section are with you right now; before each one, say out loud whether the number should grow or shrink.

## Subtopic: Filling the Tank

How much water fits in the new tank? Before formulas, understand what volume is: counting how many litre-boxes stack inside a shape. A box one metre by one metre by one metre — a cubic metre — swallows exactly 1 000 litres. Every volume answer in cubic metres is secretly counting thousands of litres.

The tank is a cylinder — a circle wearing height. Its recipe: area of the circular floor, times how tall the water stands. The floor: pi — call it 3,142 — times the radius squared. The tank is 1,8 m across, so the radius, centre to edge, is HALF of that: 0,9. Radius squared: 0,81. Floor area: about 2,545 square metres. Now stack the water 1,8 m high: 2,545 × 1,8 gives about 4,581 cubic metres. Thousands of litres: 4 581 litres. The brochure said 4 500 — the supplier told the truth, more or less.

The freezer is easier — a straight box: 1,2 × 0,8 × 0,9 = 0,864 cubic metres, 864 litres of frozen chicken and ice cream.

Two potholes swallow most marks here. Pothole one: using the full width, 1,8, as the radius — squaring doubles the damage and the tank magically holds four times too much. Whenever a question says wide or diameter or across, halve it before anything else. Pothole two: mixing units — centimetres in one slot, metres in another. Convert everything to the same unit BEFORE the formula, and the formula behaves. Questions on this section are coming your way now; halve the diameter, match the units, and turn cubic metres into litres by the thousand.

## Subtopic: Covering the Outside

Volume is what a shape holds; surface area is what a shape WEARS. Paint, wrapping paper, tiles, plaster — whenever material lies on a surface, you are adding up faces, not filling space.

The tank needs painting: the curved side and the lid, not the bottom pressed against the concrete. The curved side is sneaky until you imagine peeling it: slice the label off a tin of beans and it flattens into a rectangle. One side of that rectangle is the tank's height, 1,8 m. The other is the distance AROUND the tank — the circumference, 2 × 3,142 × 0,9, about 5,66 m. So the peeled wall is 5,66 × 1,8 — about 10,18 square metres. The lid is a flat circle: 3,142 × 0,81 — about 2,55 m². Wall plus lid: roughly 12,73 square metres of steel wanting paint.

Now the shop maths, and this rule saves real money and real marks: the tin covers 9 m² per litre, and 12,73 ÷ 9 is 1,41 litres — but nobody sells 1,41 litres. Shopping ALWAYS rounds up: buy 2 litres. Round down and the job stops two-thirds done, with the hardware store closed on Sunday. The same ceiling rule runs through tiles, bricks, fence poles and paint: real objects come in whole units, and running short costs more than the leftover.

Last habit: read WHICH faces the job touches. An open tank has no lid; a room gets paint on walls and ceiling but tiles on the floor; a box wrapped as a gift needs all six faces. List the faces on paper, one line each, before any formula — the list is where the marks live. The final questions of the lesson are with you now; peel the label, list the faces, and round the shopping up.
