**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! The big one first: gradients broke everyone's instincts. How can 1 : 5 be steeper than 1 : 20 when twenty is obviously the bigger number?

**Tutor:** Because the two numbers in a gradient are doing different jobs, and the instinct fails when you forget which is which. Read the ratio as a sentence: 1 : 5 says you climb ONE metre for every FIVE metres you walk forward; 1 : 20 says you climb one metre for every twenty. So the second number is not the amount of climbing — it is the distance you are GIVEN to do the climbing in. Less distance for the same climb means harder work: a metre gained in five steps is a scramble, a metre spread over twenty steps is a stroll. Test it at the extremes, which is the quickest way to fix an instinct. A gradient of 1 : 1 means one metre up for one metre forward — a 45-degree face, mountaineering territory. A gradient of 1 : 1 000 means you would barely notice the rise across a kilometre. As the second number grows, the slope flattens, without exception. If you prefer one physical picture, use the ladders: the 1 : 5 ladder stands nearly upright against the wall, the 1 : 20 ladder lies far out and leans lazily. And in the exam, always finish a gradient comparison with the sentence in words — path A climbs a metre every five metres, so it is steeper than path B, which takes twenty — because that sentence proves understanding and usually carries its own mark.

**Mandy:** Next: on the trip calculation, why do we bother with the ruler and scale at all when the distance table already says 568? The table is quicker and more accurate.

**Tutor:** For planning the N3, use the table every time — that is exactly the right instinct, and knowing WHEN each tool wins is itself an exam skill. But notice what the table can and cannot do. A distance table stores one number per pair of listed towns, measured along the main road. The moment your question leaves that list, the table goes silent: the distance to a dam that is not in the index, the length of a hiking trail, the gap between two farms on a gravel road, a stretch of coastline — no table row exists, and only the ruler and scale can answer. The two tools also measure different things, and papers exploit that difference deliberately. The ruler on the map gives the straight-line distance — as the crow flies; the table gives the driving distance — as the wheels roll. The road is always the longer of the two, because roads bend around mountains, rivers and towns. A favourite question hands you both numbers and asks why they differ, or asks you to calculate the straight-line distance precisely so it can be COMPARED with the table's road distance. So carry both tools with their labels: table for listed towns along roads, ruler-and-scale for everything else and for straight lines — and when a question gives you a choice, say which you chose and why. The reason is worth a mark; the habit is worth more.

**Mandy:** This one caused a small war at the back: the question said the school is west of the taxi rank, but two people had the map turned sideways and swore it was north. Who is right when the map is turned?

**Tutor:** The map is right, and the person holding it sideways is reading their own hands, not the world — which is why orientation is the first habit of every map reader. Compass directions are anchored to the planet: west is where the sun sets, whichever way your paper points. The map records that truth with its north arrow — and on nearly every published map, north is the top of the sheet. Turn the map physically and the ink does not change: the school is still printed on the western side of the rank; what changed is where YOUR up now points. So the rule for answering is mechanical. First, find the north arrow. Second, place the map so its north arrow agrees with the page's up — or, out in the world, with real north. Third, only then translate: up is north, right is east, down is south, left is west, and the diagonals fill between. If the exam prints the map with north tilted — it happens, precisely to catch sideways readers — rotate the paper or rotate your imagination until the arrow points up, and answer from that position. The deeper lesson from the little war: left and right belong to bodies, north and west belong to the world, and an exam answer in compass language must come from the world's frame. The marker cannot know which way your desk was facing.

**Mandy:** Last one: when a question asks us to CHOOSE a scale for a drawing or model, how do we know our answer is the one the memo wants? It feels like guessing.

**Tutor:** It is not guessing — it is a short calculation followed by a justification, and the memo rewards the reasoning more than the exact number. Work the method. Step one: take the largest real dimension of the thing — the 30 m length of the hall, say — and the size of the space it must fit — the 80 cm board. Step two: divide to find the minimum shrinking needed: 3 000 cm ÷ 80 cm is 37,5, so any scale of 1 : 38 or smaller-looking would fit. Step three, and this is the marked step: choose a ROUND, workable number just beyond that minimum — 1 : 50 — and show the check: 3 000 ÷ 50 = 60 cm, which fits the 80 cm board with room to spare. Step four: justify against the two failures. Too small a divisor, like 1 : 20, gives 150 cm — it does not fit; too big, like 1 : 200, gives 15 cm — it fits but wastes the space and hides detail, and a model nobody can read serves nobody. The principle to write in words: a suitable scale makes the drawing as large as the space allows while staying easy to calculate with. Memos accept a small family of answers — 1 : 40 and 1 : 50 might both earn full marks for the hall — provided the fit is checked and the choice defended. What never earns marks is a bare number with no working: the examiner is testing whether you can size a drawing on purpose, and purpose is shown in the checking line. Choose, check, compare with a too-big and too-small rival — and the memo will meet you there.
