# Part 1 — Expert

December in South Africa means one great migration: Gauteng empties down the N3 toward the Durban beaches. This session plans that trip like a Grade 12 paper does — on a national road map where one centimetre swallows twenty kilometres — and then walks off the road entirely, into the Drakensberg, where the map's brown curves tell you how steep the path will be before your legs find out. Four skills: number scales at country size, building and choosing scales of your own, grids and compass directions, and the new Grade 12 skill — reading slope from a flat page.

## Subtopic: Number Scales on the Country's Road Map

A national road map carries a scale like 1 : 2 000 000 — one unit on paper is two million of the same unit on the ground. Convert once and carry the fact: 2 000 000 cm is 20 km, so on this map EVERY CENTIMETRE IS 20 KILOMETRES. Working that conversion out before touching the ruler is the professional habit; it turns every later question into one multiplication.

The route from Johannesburg to Durban along the N3 measures 28,5 cm on the map. Distance: 28,5 × 20 = 570 km. Check against sense: the N3 is signposted at about 568 km, so a map answer of 570 is exactly the accuracy a ruler deserves — map measurement is honest estimation, and the paper's memo allows a small range for it.

Now the reverse direction, which examiners love because it catches the divide-or-multiply confusion. Bloemfontein is about 300 km from Pretoria by road; how far apart on this map? Ground to paper means shrink, so divide: 300 ÷ 20 = 15 cm. Always run the sense test both ways — paper to ground must give a big number, ground to paper a small one. An answer of 6 000 cm for a map distance means the operations swapped.

One more habit for full marks: the units must be stated and consistent. The safest chain is centimetres times scale gives centimetres, then divide by 100 000 for kilometres — 28,5 × 2 000 000 = 57 000 000 cm; ÷ 100 000 = 570 km, the same answer with the conversion shown. Markers award the method line, so write it.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: the one-centimetre fact worked out first, the trip forwards, the trip backwards, and the sense test on both.

## Subtopic: Bar Scales and Making a Scale of Your Own

The road map's corner also carries a BAR SCALE — a printed bar in segments, each labelled with real distance. Reading it is a two-step: measure the bar, price the centimetre. If one segment is 2 cm long and labelled 50 km, then 1 cm is worth 25 km; a coastal road measuring 7 cm is 7 × 25 = 175 km. The bar's superpower, tested year after year: it survives resizing. Photocopy or zoom the map and the printed ratio becomes a lie, but the bar shrinks and stretches WITH the roads, so bar measurements stay true on any copy. Its weakness is your ruler and your eye — a number scale is exact at original size; a bar scale is only as good as your measuring.

Grade 12 adds the reverse skill: DETERMINING a scale when nobody printed one. The rule is one honest fraction: scale equals drawn length over real length, in the same units, simplified to 1 : something. A bakkie is really 4,5 m long; its drawing is 9 cm. Same units first: 4,5 m is 450 cm. Scale = 9 : 450 = 1 : 50. State it in words to bank the understanding: every centimetre of drawing is 50 cm — half a metre — of real bakkie.

Then CHOOSING a suitable scale, the design version of the same fraction. The school hall is 30 m long and the model must fit a display board 80 cm wide. Try 1 : 50 — the model comes out at 3 000 ÷ 50 = 60 cm: fits the board with room for a label. Try 1 : 20 and the model is 150 cm — off the board; try 1 : 200 and it is 15 cm — fits, but wastes the board and hides detail. A suitable scale makes the drawing AS LARGE AS THE SPACE ALLOWS: large enough to read, small enough to fit, and preferably a round number others can work with.

Stop for this section's questions now — pricing a bar's centimetre, resizing survival, building 1 : 50 from the bakkie, and defending a chosen scale.

## Subtopic: Grid References and Compass Directions

Distance answers how far; the map's grid and compass answer WHERE and WHICH WAY.

The GRID: letters run across the top of the map, numbers down the side, cutting it into blocks. The reference works column-first: Harrismith in D3 sits where column D crosses row 3. A grid reference names a BLOCK, not a pinpoint — the index gets you to the block and your eye does the rest. In the exam, both directions appear: given a town, state its block; given a block, find and name what is there.

The COMPASS: eight directions the paper expects — north, north-east, east, south-east, south, south-west, west, north-west. On most maps north is straight up the sheet. From Johannesburg, Durban lies to the SOUTH-EAST — down and right on the page; Bloemfontein lies to the SOUTH-WEST; Polokwane to the NORTH. The marker wants the compass word, and wants it from the right viewpoint: direction is always FROM somewhere TO somewhere, and swapping the towns reverses the answer — Johannesburg from Durban is north-west.

Compass words are fixed by the world; LEFT and RIGHT travel with the traveller — so route descriptions mix both. Driving south down the N3, the Midlands lie to your right, which is west; the same hills are to the LEFT of the driver heading home in January. Orient before answering: face the map's north away from you in imagination, or turn the map, and only then translate between compass and body.

Complete the toolkit with the DISTANCE TABLE, the triangle chart printed with road atlases: find the row for one town, the column for the other, and read the crossing cell — Johannesburg row, Durban column, 568. The table is measured along ROADS, which is why it beats ruler-and-scale for planning: your wheels cannot take the straight line the ruler takes.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — block references both directions, eight compass points from the correct viewpoint, left against west on the southbound road, and the crossing cell of a distance table.

## Subtopic: Slope on Maps: Contours and Gradient

Now the Grade 12 headline skill: a flat page telling you about steep ground.

Mountain maps draw CONTOUR LINES — brown curves joining all points at the same height above sea level. Walk along a contour and you neither climb nor drop; step across contours and you are climbing or descending. Each map states its CONTOUR INTERVAL, the height difference between neighbouring lines — say 20 m. Cross five lines and you have climbed 5 × 20 = 100 m, whatever the path looked like.

The reading rule is spacing: contours CLOSE TOGETHER mean the ground rises fast — steep; contours FAR APART mean gentle. A ring of contours closing on itself is a summit; lines bunched tight into a V pointing uphill trace a valley with its stream. Before any calculation, the exam asks exactly this comparison: which path is steeper? The one whose contours crowd.

Then the number, GRADIENT: vertical rise over horizontal distance, expressed as 1 : something. A Drakensberg path climbs from the 1 400 m contour to the 1 500 m contour — rise 100 m — while its map length is 4 cm on a 1 : 50 000 map: horizontal distance 4 × 50 000 = 200 000 cm = 2 000 m. Gradient = 100 : 2 000 = 1 : 20 — for every 20 m you walk forward, you climb 1 m. A second path gains the same 100 m in only 500 m of walking: gradient 1 : 5. Now the reading that catches half of every class: 1 : 5 is STEEPER than 1 : 20 — the smaller the second number, the steeper the slope, because the same climb is packed into less distance. Say it as a picture: 1 : 5 climbs a metre every five steps; 1 : 20 spreads that metre over twenty.

Finish with the planner's use of slope: time. Flat-ground walking manages about 4 km/h, so 6 km of contour path is 1,5 hours — 90 minutes; a steep gradient slows the party and adds rest stops, so a route card always allows extra for the crowded-contour sections. Distance, direction, slope, time — one map answered all four.

The final questions of this part are with you now: rise counted from contour crossings, gradient built as 1 : n, the steeper-is-smaller comparison, and minutes from kilometres at walking pace.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same four skills the way a family actually uses them in one December — a kitchen table, a road atlas, and an uncle who trusts his ruler.

## Subtopic: Twenty Kilometres in Every Centimetre

The atlas is open on the kitchen table, Johannesburg to Durban, and the scale in the corner says 1 : 2 000 000. Scary number, friendly meaning: everything real got shrunk two million times. Do the one clever move FIRST: two million centimetres is twenty kilometres, so on this page, every centimetre is 20 km. Write that on your hand and the whole map becomes a times table.

Uncle lays his ruler along the N3: 28,5 cm. Times 20: 570 km. The road signs say 568 — the ruler is telling the truth to within a suburb, which is all a ruler can promise, and all the exam expects.

Gogo asks how far Bloemfontein is from Pretoria on the page, knowing the road is about 300 km. Going from real world to paper, things SHRINK: 300 divided by 20 is 15 cm — about a hand-span. If you ever get a map answer of thousands of centimetres, you multiplied where you should have divided; the sense test catches it before the marker does.

And when little sister zooms the same map on a phone, the printed 1 : 2 000 000 stops being true — the picture grew, the promise did not. Only the little striped bar in the corner keeps telling the truth, because it grew along with the roads. Ruler on the bar, price one centimetre, and measure with that. On paper, trust the ratio; on any copy or screen, trust the bar.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the kitchen-table map are coming to you right now: the one clever move, forwards times, backwards divide, and which scale survives the zoom.

## Subtopic: Where It Is and Which Way It Lies

Finding a small town in a big atlas without help is like finding one bead on a beach — so the atlas cuts the beach into blocks. Letters across the top, numbers down the side. The index says Harrismith, D3: run your finger down column D, across row 3, and search only that block. The grid takes you to the block; your eyes finish the job.

Directions come in two languages, and the trick is knowing which one the question speaks. The compass language never moves: north is up the page, east right, south down, west left, and the four in-betweens. Durban from Johannesburg: down and right — SOUTH-EAST. Polokwane: straight up — NORTH. Always check which town the question stands in, because direction is a view from somewhere: Johannesburg seen FROM Durban is north-west, the exact reverse.

The body language — left, right, straight — travels with the traveller. Driving south to the coast, the Midlands are on your RIGHT; driving home in January, the same green hills are on your LEFT. Same hills, different answer, because YOU turned around. When a question mixes the languages, orient first: point the map's north where north really is, stand yourself on the road in imagination, and then translate.

And for planning, skip the ruler entirely: the atlas's triangle table of distances already drove every road for you. Johannesburg row, Durban column, 568 — road kilometres, not crow kilometres. The ruler's straight line is always shorter than the road; the table knows the bends.

Your questions for this part are up now: block first, compass words from the right town, left against west both ways down the N3, and the table that knows the bends.

## Subtopic: Reading the Hills Without Climbing Them

After the beach week, the family drives inland for a Drakensberg hike, and the trail map looks like someone dropped brown spaghetti on the page. Those curves are the mountain, flattened: each brown line joins ground at the SAME height, and the map says neighbouring lines are 20 m apart in height. Walk along a line, stay level. Cut across lines, climb — cross five and you have climbed 100 m, guaranteed, however the path winds.

Read steepness with your eyes only: where the lines crowd together, the ground rears up; where they spread, it strolls. Rings closing into a ring is a peak; tight lines folding into a V is a valley with a stream in its crease. Dad compares two paths to the waterfall: the short one crosses the same five lines in a thumb of map, the long one in a full hand. The short one is the steep one — same climb, squeezed into less walking.

The number version is gradient, and it is one fraction: climb over distance, tidied to 1 : something. Path one: 100 m of climb over 2 000 m of walking — 1 : 20, a metre up for every twenty along. Path two: 100 m over 500 — 1 : 5, a metre up every five steps. Here is the sneaky part, and say it aloud: ONE-TO-FIVE IS STEEPER THAN ONE-TO-TWENTY. Small second number, steep path — the whole climb crammed into a few strides. If you remember it as ladders against a wall, the 1 : 5 ladder stands nearly upright.

Last, turn map into morning: the gentle path is 6 km, and legs do about 4 km each hour, so 6 ÷ 4 = 1,5 hours — 90 minutes, plus juice stops, plus extra for any stretch where the spaghetti bunches. That is the whole art: one flat page told you how far, which way, how steep and how long — before a single boot touched the mountain. And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: contour crossings counted into metres, the crowding rule, both gradients built and compared the right way round, and the 90 honest minutes. Next block, the same scale skills walk indoors — onto floor plans and the boxes and buildings of the third term.
