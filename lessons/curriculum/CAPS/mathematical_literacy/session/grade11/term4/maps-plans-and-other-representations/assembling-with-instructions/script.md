# Part 1 — Expert

Flat-pack furniture, a new plug on a kettle cord, a plastic model aeroplane, a toy that arrives in ninety pieces on Christmas morning: modern life keeps handing over boxes of parts and a folded sheet of instructions. The curriculum's final maps-and-plans skill is exactly this — reading assembly instructions and diagrams well enough to build the object, in the right order, with nothing left over that should not be. The session builds two things from Kimberley kitchen-table reality: a flat-pack bookshelf from a catalogue store, and a three-pin plug wired onto an extension lead.

## Subtopic: The Language of Instruction Diagrams

Assembly instructions speak a language designed to cross language barriers: mostly pictures, few words. An EXPLODED DIAGRAM shows the object pulled apart along its joints, each part floating in the position it will occupy, with lines showing what slides into what. Reading one is a skill of direction: follow each dotted line from part to hole, and the drawing tells you both the what and the where.

The vocabulary is standardised. Numbered steps fix the ORDER. Circled close-ups zoom into a joint that needs care. An arrow shows movement — slide, turn, press. A hand with a crossed-out hammer warns against forcing a part. A screw drawn beside a screwdriver names the tool; a double-headed arrow with a measurement sets a required gap. Ticks and crosses show the correct and the wrong orientation side by side — the wrong one struck through.

Orientation symbols do heavy lifting. THIS SIDE UP arrows, a shaded face meaning "front", the letters L and R distinguishing mirrored parts — the bookshelf's two side panels look identical but are drilled differently, and swapping them puts shelf holes facing outward. The diagram distinguishes them; the impatient eye does not.

Before any building, the instructions ask for two readings: once through completely, to understand the destination and the order, and then step by step during the build. The examinable skills are matching a written step to its diagram, stating what a symbol demands, and predicting the consequence of ignoring it. The questions for this section are with you now; read each diagram as a sentence — part, position, movement, warning.

## Subtopic: The Parts List and the Count Before Building

Every kit opens with a PARTS LIST: each component drawn, named, coded and counted. The bookshelf's list reads: side panels, code A, 2; shelves, code B, 4; back panel, code C, 1; cam locks, 16; cam bolts, 16; wooden dowels, 8; screws, 12; Allen key, 1.

The first job is an audit: count every part against the list BEFORE building. The reason is arithmetic of frustration — discovering at step 9 that cam lock 15 is missing means dismantling nothing but losing the afternoon; discovering it before step 1 means phoning the store while the bakkie can still go back. Papers reproduce this as pure counting with a twist: the kit contains 14 cam locks, the list requires 16 — how many are missing, and can any subassembly proceed? Two missing; the shelves each need 4, so three shelves can be fully locked while the fourth waits.

The parts list also feeds proportion questions. Each shelf uses 4 cam locks and 4 cam bolts: four shelves consume all 16 of each — the kit carries no spares, and losing one on the floor stops the build. A question may scale the kit: a double bookshelf needs twice every quantity except the single Allen key; a school ordering kits for 30 learners at 12 screws each needs 360 screws, sold in boxes of 50 — 7,2 boxes, so 8 boxes bought.

Checking is itself specified: tick each part on the list as it is verified, keep small fittings in the packet until needed, and lay parts out in the order of the steps. The method marks are for the audit, the subtraction of missing parts, and the sentence saying what the shortage stops. Your questions for this section are ready; count first, build second.

## Subtopic: Order Matters: Following the Sequence

Instructions are numbered because assembly is a ONE-WAY street: many steps cannot be done after the steps that follow them. The bookshelf's sequence: fit dowels into the side panels; bolt cam bolts into the shelf edges; join shelves to one side panel and lock the cams; add the second side panel; square the frame; nail the back panel on; stand it upright.

Break the order and the build punishes you. Nail the back panel first and the shelves cannot slide in — the back blocks the only assembly direction. Lock the cams before the second panel is on, and the bolts cannot enter their holes. Stand it up before the back is nailed and the frame parallelograms — the back panel is what squares it. Each failure costs disassembly: the steps must be undone in reverse order to return to the mistake, which is why skipping ahead is the most expensive shortcut in the box.

Wiring the plug makes the same point with higher stakes. The sequence: unscrew the plug cover; loosen the cord grip; strip the outer sheath; strip each core; twist the strands; connect each core to its terminal; tighten the cord grip on the SHEATH, not the cores; refit the cover. South Africa's colour code is law, not preference: BROWN to the LIVE terminal on the right, BLUE to NEUTRAL on the left, GREEN-AND-YELLOW to EARTH at the top — the tallest pin. A plug wired with brown and blue swapped may still power the kettle, which is what makes the error vicious: the appliance's switch now interrupts the neutral, leaving the element live even when switched off.

The examinable forms: put shuffled steps into order, explain why step n must precede step n+1, and state the consequence of a named violation. Answer consequences concretely — not "it won't work" but "the back panel blocks the shelves from sliding in." The questions for this section follow now; sequence, reason, consequence.

## Subtopic: Time, Cost and the Do-It-Yourself Decision

Assembly is also a financial decision, and the paper prices it. The bookshelf costs R1 899 flat-packed. The store offers delivery at R250, and an assembly service at R320. A handyman charges R150 per half hour and quotes two half-hours. The household owns the only tool required — the Allen key comes in the box — so self-assembly costs R0 in money and an afternoon in time.

The comparison table writes itself. Option one: self-assembly — 1 899 + 250 = R2 149. Option two: store assembly — 1 899 + 250 + 320 = R2 469. Option three: handyman — 1 899 + 250 + 2 × 150 = R2 449. Self-assembly saves R320 against the store and R300 against the handyman, provided the build succeeds.

That proviso is where judgement questions live. The instructions rate the bookshelf's difficulty at two spanners out of five and specify two people for step 7 — the second side panel needs four hands. A single person attempting it risks a cracked cam housing, and a replacement side panel costs R380 plus a fortnight's wait: the R320 saving carries a risk priced at R380. A sensible answer weighs exactly that: who is available, what the failure costs, and whether the saving covers the risk.

Percentages finish the arithmetic. The assembly service of R320 on a R1 899 bookshelf is 320 ÷ 1 899 × 100 = 16,9% of the item's price. A store promotion of free assembly on purchases over R2 500 invites a classic question: adding a R650 lamp lifts the basket to R2 549, earns the R320 service free, and the lamp effectively costs 650 − 320 = R330. Whether that is a bargain depends on whether the lamp was wanted — and saying so is part of the answer. The last questions of this part are with you now: build the options table, then defend a choice in one sentence.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same boxes on the same kitchen table, opened the way a careful builder opens them.

## Subtopic: Pictures That Give Orders

The instruction sheet has no sentences in your language — and needs none, because every picture is an order. The big drawing of parts floating apart in mid-air is the exploded view: it shows the bookshelf pulled open along its joints, every piece hovering exactly where it belongs, dotted lines running from each screw to its hole. Follow a line, and the drawing has told you what goes where.

The small symbols are the grammar. Numbers are the order — step 3 before step 4, no negotiation. A circle floating beside the drawing is a magnifying glass: this joint deserves a closer look. Arrows are verbs: slide, turn, press. The hammer with a cross through it says do not force anything. And the tick-and-cross pairs show a part placed rightly and wrongly, the wrong way crossed out — the sheet answering your mistake before you make it.

The letters matter most on lookalike parts. The two side panels of the bookshelf are twins — but one is marked L and one R, and their drilled holes mirror each other. Swap them, and at step 6 the shelf holes stare outward at the room while the shelves have nothing to grip. The drawing knew; the hurry didn't.

So read the sheet twice: once end to end with your hands still, to learn where the build is going, and then step by step with the parts. Some questions on this section are with you right now — say what each symbol commands, and what disobeying it would break.

## Subtopic: Count Everything Before You Build Anything

Open the box, and before any building: roll call. The parts list is the register — every piece drawn, coded, counted. Two side panels. Four shelves. One back panel. Sixteen cam locks, sixteen cam bolts, eight dowels, twelve screws, one Allen key. Tick each one as you count it.

Why so strict? Because a missing part found NOW is a phone call, but a missing part found at step 9 is an afternoon in ruins. The count is ten minutes of insurance on the whole build.

The counting also does exam work. The kit holds 14 cam locks but the list says 16: two missing. Each shelf locks with 4, so three shelves can be locked completely and the fourth must wait — that sentence, missing count plus what it stops, is the full answer. There are no spares: sixteen locks, sixteen needed; one rolling under the fridge stops the fourth shelf just as surely as the factory shorting you.

And the list scales. Two bookshelves: double everything except the Allen key, which happily serves both. A woodwork class of 30 learners using 12 screws each needs 360 screws; the hardware sells boxes of 50, so 7,2 boxes — buy 8, because seven boxes strand ten learners short. Keep the small fittings in their packet until their step arrives, lay the big parts out in step order, and the build begins already half-organised. Questions on this section are coming your way now — audit, subtract, and say what the shortage stops.

## Subtopic: One Step at a Time, and What a Shortcut Costs

The numbers on the steps are not suggestions. The bookshelf goes: dowels into the side panels, cam bolts into the shelves, shelves onto ONE side, lock those cams, second side on, lock again, square the frame, nail on the back, stand it up. Each step leans on the one before.

Try the tempting shortcut and the box teaches you. Nail the neat back panel on early, and the shelves have no direction left to slide in from — the back seals the only door. Stand the shelf up before the back is on, and the frame leans into a parallelogram, because that thin back panel is what holds the corners square. Every wrong-order move must be UNDONE in reverse before the build can continue: the shortcut is the longest route in the box.

The plug on the extension lead raises the stakes from furniture to safety. Colours are law: brown to live on the right, blue to neutral on the left, green-and-yellow to earth at the top, the tallest pin. Strip, twist, connect, and clamp the cord grip onto the thick outer sheath — never onto the thin cores, which the grip would slice. Here is the frightening part: swap brown and blue and the kettle still boils. Everything looks fine. But the switch is now on the wrong wire, so the element stays live with the switch off — a fault you cannot see, waiting. That is why the colour code is checked, not assumed.

Last, the money. Bookshelf R1 899, delivery R250. Build it yourself: R2 149. Store assembles: add R320 — R2 469. Handyman: two half-hours at R150 — R2 449. Doing it yourself saves about R300 — IF step 7's four-handed lift has four hands available, because a cracked side panel costs R380 and the saving evaporates. Weigh the saving against the risk and say your choice out loud with its reason. The final questions of the lesson are with you now — steps in order, colours by law, and a decision defended in rands.
