Hey, it's Bianca — assembly questions look like they belong in a workshop, not an exam, and that is exactly why they are friendly: every answer is sitting in the diagram, waiting to be read out. No formulas. Just careful eyes.

The pictures are orders. The exploded view shows every part floating where it belongs — follow the dotted line from screw to hole and the drawing has answered the question for you. Numbers are the sequence, arrows are the movements, the crossed-out hammer says do not force, and tick-and-cross pairs show right and wrong side by side. When a question asks what a symbol means, describe the order it gives AND what ignoring it would break. Two halves, two marks.

Count before you build. The parts list is a register: tick every piece. The exam's favourite: fourteen cam locks where sixteen are needed — say TWO are missing and say what stops: three shelves lock, the fourth waits. Missing count plus consequence is the full answer. Scaling up? Multiply everything except the one tool, and round box purchases UP so nobody stands stranded.

Sequences sort themselves with one question per step: what does this step need already done, and what does it close off? Things that seal or square — the back panel, the plug cover — go late, because they shut the door on everything else. And the plug's colours are law, not taste: brown right to live, blue left to neutral, green-and-yellow up to earth. Swapped wires still boil the kettle — that is the trap — so the check is the answer, never the boiling.

Costs: build the little table — self, store, handyman — then recommend with a reason that uses the numbers. The defended choice is the right choice.

Read the picture, count the parts, respect the order, defend the decision. You've got this!
