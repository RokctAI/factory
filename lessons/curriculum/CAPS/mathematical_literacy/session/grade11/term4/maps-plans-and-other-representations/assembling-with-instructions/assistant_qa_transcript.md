**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! The first one comes up whenever this topic appears: it feels like common sense, not maths — what is the examiner actually testing when a question shows a flat-pack diagram?

**Tutor:** Four genuinely mathematical skills, dressed in cardboard. First, spatial interpretation: an exploded diagram is a three-dimensional object encoded on flat paper, and reading which part enters which hole from which direction is the same representational skill as reading a floor plan or an elevation — this topic sits in maps and plans for exactly that reason. Second, sequencing: the numbered steps form a strict order, and questions ask you to re-order shuffled steps, justify why one must precede another, or predict what a violation breaks — logical reasoning with a physical consequence. Third, quantities and proportion: parts lists are counting, scaling a kit is multiplication, missing components are subtraction, and screws-per-learner across a class is ratio work ending in a round-up to whole boxes. Fourth, cost analysis: self-assembly against paid assembly is a full comparison-of-options question with fixed amounts, rates per half hour and a percentage or two. So treat every assembly question as one of those four in disguise, name which one you are doing, and show the working the disguise was hiding. The everyday setting is the point — the curriculum wants proof you can take mathematics out of the classroom and into a box of parts on the kitchen floor.

***

**Bianca:** Next: the sequencing questions where steps are shuffled and we must re-order them. When the furniture is unfamiliar, how do we find the order without ever having built one?

**Tutor:** By logic rather than memory — every sequence question carries its own evidence, and three tests extract it. Test one: dependency. For each pair of steps, ask which one physically needs the other already done. Cam locks cannot be locked before the bolts they grip are seated; a cover cannot be screwed on before the wires beneath it are connected. Dependencies chain the steps for you. Test two: access. Ask what each step closes off. Nailing on the back panel seals the frame's only open face, so anything that must enter through that face — the shelves — must come earlier; in general, steps that close, cover or seal migrate toward the end. Test three: reversibility. Fragile, finishing or squaring operations — standing the unit upright, fitting doors, sticking on the felt pads — sit last, because doing them early forces them to be undone. Run the three tests and the shuffled list mostly sorts itself; where two steps remain interchangeable, say so, because instructions do sometimes allow parallel branches and examiners respect a justified 'either order works'. And write the reason next to each placement — 'back panel last of the frame steps, since it blocks the shelf opening' — because the sequencing mark and the reasoning mark are usually separate marks.

**Bianca:** This one always causes a stir: if a plug with brown and blue swapped still boils the kettle, how would anyone ever know — and why does the exam care so much about a mistake nobody notices?

**Tutor:** The exam cares precisely BECAUSE nobody notices — the question is testing whether you understand that correct function is not proof of correct assembly. Follow the electricity to see the danger. Wired correctly, the appliance's switch sits on the live wire: switch off, and the electricity is stopped at the door — the element beyond it is dead. With brown and blue swapped, the switch now sits on the neutral: switch off, and the flow stops, so the kettle behaves identically — but the element remains CONNECTED to the live side the whole time, energised and waiting. A person who trusts the off switch and reaches in with a fork, or a fault that lets that live path touch the casing, meets full mains voltage from an appliance that is 'off'. Discovery, in practice, comes only three ways: a careful visual check against the colour code before the cover closes, a tester screwdriver or multimeter, or the accident itself. That is why the code is law and why the checking habit — brown right to live, blue left to neutral, green-and-yellow to the tall earth pin, grip on the sheath — is examined as knowledge, not common sense. The transferable lesson runs through the whole topic: 'it works' and 'it is right' are different claims, and only the second one keeps people safe.

**Bianca:** Last one: in the cost comparison, some learners chose the store's R320 assembly and were nervous they had picked the 'wrong' answer, since self-assembly is cheapest. Is the cheapest option always the expected answer?

**Tutor:** No — the expected answer is the DEFENDED option, and the mark scheme is built to reward the defence rather than the choice. The table is compulsory: self-assembly R2 149, handyman R2 449, store R2 469, and the arithmetic marks live there. But the final mark asks for a recommendation with a reason, and several reasons are acceptable when they engage the numbers. Choosing self-assembly: saves R320, the kit includes the only tool, and two people are available for step 7 — a complete argument. Choosing the store: the R320 buys skilled hands for a step that risks a R380 side panel, and for a household with no second person, insurance costing less than the part it protects is rational — also complete. What earns nothing is a naked choice: 'the store, because it is easier' engages no number, and 'self, because it is cheapest' ignores the risk the question deliberately planted. Notice the structure the examiner built: the saving, R320, sits just below the failure cost, R380, precisely so that both directions are defensible and only reasoning separates candidates. When a promotion complicates it — the free-assembly threshold at R2 500 — the same rule holds: calculate the effective cost of the added lamp, R330, then say whether the household wanted a lamp. Numbers first, then judgement, then a sentence that contains both. That sentence is the actual answer; the table is its evidence.
