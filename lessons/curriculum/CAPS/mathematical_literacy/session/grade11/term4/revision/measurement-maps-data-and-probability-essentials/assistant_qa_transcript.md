**Bianca:** Class questions before the final paper! First: rounding. Sometimes the memo wants rounding up, sometimes to two decimals — how do I know which rounding a question expects?

**Tutor:** Let the object being rounded decide, because rounding is about what the number IS, not about arithmetic taste. Money rounds to two decimal places, since cents exist in hundredths — R2 268,00, R189,50. Physical purchasable units — boxes of tiles, tins of paint, taxis for an outing, bricks — round UP to whole units whenever a shortfall breaks the job: 11,2 boxes becomes 12, because 0,2 of a box is not for sale and 11 leaves bare floor. The mirror case rounds DOWN: how many complete 1,5-metre curtain lengths can be cut from 8 metres of fabric — 5,33 becomes 5, because the sixth length cannot be completed. People in an average round sensibly in context — a mean of 3,4 children per household stays 3,4 in the statistics but becomes about 3 in a sentence describing a family. So before rounding, ask three questions: can fractions of this thing exist, does too little break the job, does an incomplete unit count? Purchasables round up, cuttables round down, money takes two decimals — and stating your rounding reason in the answer is itself worth a mark in most memos.

**Bianca:** Next: the plan-to-price questions that chain scale work into costing. Where do learners usually lose the thread?

**Tutor:** At the unit handover between the two halves — the moment the scale answer must feed the price formula. The chain runs: read the plan, convert with the scale, convert the UNITS, calculate area, then price. Take the bedroom at 1 to 50: it reads 60 millimetres by 70. Un-fold both: 3 000 millimetres by 3 500. Here is the danger point — multiplying now gives an area in square millimetres, a huge meaningless number that the price per square metre cannot touch. Convert FIRST: 3 metres by 3,5 metres, and only then multiply — 10,5 square metres — and only then price at, say, R240 per square metre: R2 520. The discipline is one line per link, each with its unit written: plan reading in millimetres, real length in millimetres, real length in metres, area in square metres, cost in rand. Five labelled lines, five opportunities for method marks, and no place for the silent unit slip that turns a bedroom into a football field. Whenever two topics chain, the marks concentrate at the handover — slow down exactly there.

**Bianca:** Third: comparing two data sets. I calculate both means correctly, but the memo wants more than the winner. What is the full comparison?

**Tutor:** A full comparison speaks twice — once about centre, once about spread — and then lands in context. Suppose both suppliers' quotes average 21 hundred rand. Stopping at the means are equal earns the first mark only. Now compare spreads: supplier A's quotes range across 13 while B's huddle within 4. Sentence two: A is unpredictable — a quote might land anywhere across a R1 300 stretch — while B's price is consistent. Then the landing, which is where the final marks sit: in context, B is the safer choice for a family on a fixed budget, because the expected cost is the same but the risk is smaller. Centre, spread, decision — three sentences, each naming its numbers. Two refinements lift the answer further. If the sets differ in size, compare percentages or means rather than raw totals — a bigger group wins totals by mere headcount. And if an outlier lurks in one set, note that its mean is inflated and lean on the medians for the centre comparison. The examiners' pattern is constant: every comparison question is secretly asking for two statistics and one contextual verdict.

**Bianca:** Last one: compound probability. When do I reach for a two-way table and when for a tree diagram?

**Tutor:** Choose by the shape of the situation: one group sorted two ways wants a table; events unfolding in sequence want a tree. The class register is one group — 60 learners — sorted by transport and by gender, so a two-way table holds it perfectly: rows for taxi and walk, columns for girls and boys, margins totalling everything. AND questions become cell-reading: girl and taxi is the cell 20, over the grand total 60 — one third — no formula, just careful location. The weather story is a sequence — first tomorrow rains or not, THEN the taxi is late or not — so a tree draws it: branches 0,3 and 0,7 for rain, then late-branches hanging off each. Multiply along a path for a compound outcome: 0,3 times 0,6 gives 0,18 for rain-then-late. Add across paths when several routes satisfy the question. The tell in the wording: percentages or counts of one population signal the table; the words then, given that, or if it rains signal the tree. And both tools end the same way — translate the number into context, roughly 18 such mornings in a hundred — because the interpretation sentence is the mark the calculation was building toward.
