**Bianca:** Class questions before the final exams! First: rounding. Sometimes a question wants rounding up, sometimes two decimals — how do I know which rounding is expected?

**Tutor:** Let the object being rounded decide, because rounding is about what the number IS, not about arithmetic taste. Money rounds to two decimal places, since cents live in hundredths — R2 520,00, R210,50. Physical purchasable units — boxes of tiles, tins of paint, taxis for an outing, bags of cement — round UP to whole units whenever a shortfall breaks the job: 11,7 boxes becomes 12, because 0,7 of a box is not for sale and 11 leaves bare floor. The mirror case rounds DOWN: how many complete 1,2-metre curtain lengths can be cut from 7 metres of fabric — 5,83 becomes 5, because the sixth length cannot be completed. People in an average round sensibly in context — a mean of 2,6 children per household stays 2,6 in the statistics but becomes about 3 in a sentence describing a family. So before rounding, ask three questions: can fractions of this thing exist, does too little break the job, does an incomplete unit count? Purchasables round up, cuttables round down, money takes two decimals — and stating your rounding reason inside the answer is part of what a complete answer looks like.

**Bianca:** Next: the plan-to-price questions that chain scale work into costing. Where do people usually lose the thread?

**Tutor:** At the unit handover between the two halves — the moment the scale answer must feed the price formula. The chain runs: read the plan, convert with the scale, convert the UNITS, calculate area, then price. Take the bedroom at 1 to 50: it reads 50 millimetres by 64. Un-fold both: 2 500 millimetres by 3 200. Here is the danger point — multiplying now gives an area in square millimetres, a huge meaningless number that a price per square metre cannot touch. Convert FIRST: 2,5 metres by 3,2 metres, and only then multiply — 8 square metres — and only then price at, say, R265 per square metre: R2 120. The discipline is one line per link, each with its unit written: plan reading in millimetres, real length in millimetres, real length in metres, area in square metres, cost in rand. Five labelled lines, five chances to show visible method, and no room for the silent unit slip that turns a bedroom into a sports field. Whenever two topics chain, the difficulty concentrates at the handover — slow down exactly there.

**Bianca:** Third: comparing two data sets. I calculate both means correctly, but apparently the answer needs more than the winner. What is the full comparison?

**Tutor:** A full comparison speaks twice — once about centre, once about spread — and then lands in context. Suppose both contractors' quotes average 20 hundred rand. Stopping at the means are equal is only the opening sentence. Now compare spreads: contractor A's quotes range across 13 while B's huddle within 5. Sentence two: A is unpredictable — a quote might land anywhere across a R1 300 stretch — while B's price is consistent. Then the landing, which is where the judgement lives: in context, B is the safer choice for a family on a fixed budget, because the expected cost is the same but the risk is smaller. Centre, spread, decision — three sentences, each naming its numbers. Two refinements lift the answer further. If the sets differ in size, compare percentages or means rather than raw totals — a bigger group wins totals by mere headcount. And if an outlier lurks in one set, note that its mean is inflated and lean on the medians for the centre comparison. The pattern never changes: every comparison question is secretly asking for two statistics and one contextual verdict.

**Bianca:** Last one: compound probability. When do I reach for a two-way table and when for a tree diagram?

**Tutor:** Choose by the shape of the situation: one group sorted two ways wants a table; events unfolding in sequence want a tree. The class register is one group — 50 learners — sorted by transport and by gender, so a two-way table holds it perfectly: rows for minibus and walk, columns for girls and boys, margins totalling everything. AND questions become cell-reading: girl and minibus is the cell 15, over the grand total 50 — three tenths — no formula, just careful location. The load-shedding story is a sequence — first the power goes off or stays on, THEN supper is late or on time — so a tree draws it: branches 0,4 and 0,6 for the power, then late-branches hanging off each. Multiply along a path for a compound outcome: 0,4 times 0,5 gives 0,2 for off-then-late. Add across paths when several routes satisfy the question. The tell in the wording: percentages or counts of one population signal the table; the words then, given that, or if the power goes signal the tree. And both tools end the same way — translate the number into context, roughly 20 such evenings in a hundred — because the interpretation sentence is what the calculation was building toward.
