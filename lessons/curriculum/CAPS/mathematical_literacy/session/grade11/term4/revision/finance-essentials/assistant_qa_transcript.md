**Bianca:** Revision questions from the class! First: multi-step payslip questions — a raise changes the gross, and then I lose track of which numbers to recalculate. What is the safe order?

**Tutor:** Work the payslip top-down, exactly as it is printed, and let the gross drive everything below it. Step one: apply the change to the gross alone — a 6 percent raise turns R9 800 into 9 800 times 1,06, which is R10 388. Step two: rerun every PERCENTAGE deduction on the new gross, because percentages resize automatically — UIF becomes 1 percent of 10 388, R103,88, and pension becomes 7,5 percent of 10 388, R779,10. Step three: leave rand-amount deductions alone unless the question changes them — a fixed union fee stays fixed; tax usually changes, but only if the question gives you the new figure or a tax table. Step four: only now subtract for the new net. The classic error is subtracting the old deductions from the new gross — mixing two payslips into one. The defence is a two-column layout: old payslip on the left, new on the right, every line recalculated or consciously carried over. That layout IS the method marks, and it makes the final comparison sentence — how much better off she is — a one-line subtraction between the columns.

***

**Bianca:** Next: stepped tariffs. I understand the blocks, but the wording first 100 units, thereafter, confuses my subtraction. How do I set it up without slipping?

**Tutor:** Build the ladder as a small table before calculating anything, one row per block, with three columns: how many units the block holds, the rate, and the block cost. Read the tariff sentence and fill the capacity column first: the first block holds 100 units at R1,80; the thereafter block holds whatever remains. Then place the household's usage: 240 units fills the first block completely — write 100, not 240 — and spills 240 minus 100, so 140 units into the second row. Now multiply across each row: 100 times 1,80 gives R180; 140 times 2,60 gives R364. Total the last column: R544. The table defends you from both classic slips: charging all 240 units at one rate, and forgetting to subtract the first block before pricing the second. It also scales — a three-step water tariff just adds a row. And when the question flips direction, giving you the bill and asking for the usage, the same table runs backward: subtract the first block's R180 from the bill, divide the remainder by the second rate, and add the 100 back. The ladder is one structure serving both directions.

***

**Bianca:** Third: exchange rates. Multiply or divide — I second-guess myself every time. Is there a check that settles it?

**Tutor:** Treat the rate as a price tag and let size settle it. R18,50 per dollar means one dollar COSTS R18,50 — the dollar is the item, the rand is the money. Buying 120 dollars is buying 120 items: 120 times 18,50 gives R2 220 — multiplication, exactly like buying 120 loaves of bread. Asking how many dollars R925 buys is asking how many items your money affords: 925 divided by 18,50 gives 50 — division, like counting loaves. Then run the size check, which catches every inversion: while a dollar costs more than a rand, the rand number in any correct conversion must be the BIGGER number. If your conversion says 120 dollars becomes R6,49, the direction was wrong, and the check caught it. The interpretation half of the topic needs one more anchor: when the rate climbs from R18,50 to R19,20, each dollar now costs more rand, so the rand has weakened — and imported goods, priced in dollars, tend to become more expensive locally. Rate up means rand down; say it with the word because and the numbers, and the interpretation marks follow.

**Bianca:** Last one: the compound interest table. Why does the memo insist on year-by-year working when one calculation could jump straight to the answer?

**Tutor:** Because the table proves you understand the mechanism, and the mechanism is what Grade 11 assesses — compound growth as interest earning interest, not as a formula recalled. Watch what the table exposes: R5 000 at 10 percent ends year one at R5 500. Year two's interest is 10 percent of 5 500 — R550, already bigger than last year's R500 — closing at R6 050. Year three earns R605, closing at R6 655. Each row's interest grows because the base grew, and THAT visible growth is the understanding being marked. The table also carries three practical protections. Method marks survive arithmetic slips — a marker following your rows pays for correct structure even where a multiplication stumbled. Comparison questions become trivial — simple interest's flat R500 rows against compound's climbing rows, gap R155 after three years, read straight off the page. And interpretation questions — why does the gap widen every year — are answered by pointing at your own table: because each year's interest is calculated on a larger amount than the year before. One calculation gives an answer; the table gives an argument, and finals reward arguments.
