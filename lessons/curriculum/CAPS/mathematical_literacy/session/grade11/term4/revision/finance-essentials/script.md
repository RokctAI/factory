# Part 1 — Expert

This session is the finance revision sweep for the final examination — the whole year's money work, reorganised around two paper trails: Ayanda's payslip from the clothing factory in Durban, and the books of her cousin Lwazi's vetkoek stall. Documents and deductions first, then VAT and the taxes that ride on a payslip, then tariffs and exchange rates, then the business mathematics of profit, break-even, interest and inflation. Nothing here is new; everything here is the exam-day version — each calculation compressed to its one deciding move, each answer ending in the sentence the memo reserves a mark for.

## Subtopic: Financial Documents, Income and Deductions

Every finance question begins with a document, and the first skill is naming its parts before touching a calculator.

Ayanda's payslip. Gross salary: R9 800 — the earnings before anything leaves. Then the deductions. UIF — the Unemployment Insurance Fund — takes 1 percent: 0,01 times 9 800 gives R98, matched by her employer behind the scenes. Pension: 7,5 percent — 0,075 times 9 800 — R735. Income tax as deducted here: R1 176. Net salary is what survives: 9 800 minus 98, minus 735, minus 1 176 — R7 791. Gross minus deductions equals net — the single most-tested line in the section, and the memo wants the subtraction shown, not just the survivor quoted.

The vocabulary distinctions that carry marks: income against expenditure; fixed income like a salary against variable income like overtime; fixed expenses like rent against variable ones like electricity. A household statement question is a sorting exercise before it is a calculating one — put every line in its column, total the columns, compare.

Documents beyond the payslip run on the same reading discipline. A till slip: find the VAT line, check the addition. A municipal account: opening balance, current charges, payments received, closing balance — and the classic question, explain a line item, wants words, not numbers: the opening balance is what was still owed from last month.

The habits: read the document twice, label what each number IS before using it, and quote line names in answers. A finance answer that names its lines audits itself.

This section's questions are with you now — one payslip stripped to its net, one statement sorted and explained.

## Subtopic: VAT, Payslip Percentages and the Reverse Gear

VAT is 15 percent, and the exam tests it in both gears.

Forward gear: a jacket costs R1 300 before VAT. The VAT is 0,15 times 1 300 — R195 — so the till price is R1 495; or in one move, 1 300 times 1,15. Reverse gear, the mark-harvester: a tag of R1 495 includes VAT, and the question wants the price without it. The tag is 115 percent of the true price, so divide by 1,15: R1 300, carrying R195 of tax. Subtracting 15 percent of the tag takes 15 percent of the wrong base and lands on the wrong answer — division is the only way back. The check is free: 1 300 times 1,15 returns exactly 1 495.

Zero-rated items complicate the shopping basket, and the exam knows it: brown bread, maize meal, rice and other basics carry no VAT, so a till-slip question may hand you a basket where only SOME items attract the 15 percent. Sort before calculating: subtotal the standard-rated items, apply VAT to that subtotal only, then add everything.

Back to the payslip percentages, because they are the same machine. UIF at 1 percent is the gentlest calculation in the paper — slide the decimal: 1 percent of R9 800 is R98. The employer contributes the same again, a fact the interpretation questions love. Pension at 7,5 percent: 0,075 times gross. And any percentage increase question — a salary rising 6 percent — is one multiplication by 1,06, with the new gross feeding new deductions, which is exactly how the multi-step questions chain.

The gear check before any percentage move: is the given amount the base, or the base plus the percentage already? Words like including, after the increase and now costs all warn that the reverse gear — division — is the honest route.

Take this section's questions now — both gears run, one mixed basket sorted, one payslip percentage chained.

## Subtopic: Tariffs, Stepped Charges and Exchange Rates

A tariff is a price per unit of something metered, and the year's upgrade is the STEPPED tariff, where the price climbs as usage climbs.

The municipal electricity structure: the first 100 units cost R1,80 each; every unit after that costs R2,60. A household uses 240 units. The forbidden move: 240 times either single rate. The honest move walks the steps: the first 100 units cost 180 rand; the remaining 140 units — 240 minus 100 — cost 140 times 2,60, which is R364. Total: R544. Stepped-tariff questions are laddered subtractions and multiplications, and the memo awards each rung — first block, remaining units, block cost, total. Show the ladder.

Water tariffs use the same ladder with kilolitres; a phone contract with free minutes then a per-minute rate is the same ladder in disguise. Whenever a tariff table appears, ask: where do the steps change, and how many units fall in each step? The graph of a stepped tariff bends upward at each step — reading the bend is reading the table.

Exchange rates convert one currency's units into another's, and the rate is just a price: R18,50 per US dollar means each dollar costs eighteen rand fifty. Buying 120 dollars for a trip: 120 times 18,50 — R2 220. Converting back: how many dollars does R925 buy? Divide: 925 over 18,50 — 50 dollars. The direction rule: multiply by the rate to turn dollars into rand, divide to turn rand into dollars — and sanity-check with strength: the rand amount is always the bigger number when the rate is above one rand per unit.

Interpretation completes the skill: if the rate moves from R18,50 to R19,20 per dollar, the rand has weakened — each dollar costs more rand — and imported goods trend more expensive. One sentence, two marks, no calculator.

Work through this section's questions — one ladder climbed, both conversion directions run, one weakening explained.

## Subtopic: Profit, Break-even, Interest and Inflation

Lwazi's vetkoek stall carries the business half of the syllabus.

His numbers: rent for the stand and equipment, R1 500 a month — the fixed cost, paid even if he sells nothing. Ingredients per filled vetkoek: R14 — the variable cost, paid once per item. Selling price: R26. Each sale therefore contributes 26 minus 14 — R12 — toward the fixed cost. Break-even asks: how many sales until the stall stops losing? Divide the fixed cost by the contribution: 1 500 over 12 — 125 vetkoek. The check: 125 sales bring in 125 times 26 — R3 250 — and cost 1 500 plus 125 times 14 — also R3 250. Income equals total cost: the definition of break-even, and the sentence sales above 125 make profit; below 125 make a loss is the interpretation mark. On a graph, break-even is where the income line crosses the total-cost line.

Interest, the two species. Simple interest grows on the original amount only: R5 000 at 10 percent for 3 years earns 500 each year — R1 500 — closing on R6 500. Compound interest grows on the growing total: year one ends at 5 500; year two grows 10 percent of 5 500, ending at 6 050; year three ends at 6 655. Same rate, same years, R155 apart — compounding earns interest ON interest, and the gap widens every year. The exam wants the year-by-year table for compound growth, not a memorised formula, and wants the comparison sentence naming the winner and the gap.

Inflation is compound growth applied to prices: at 6 percent, a R250 school shoe costs 250 times 1,06 — R265 — next year. Inflation questions are percentage-increase questions wearing an economics jacket, and the interpretation twist — your money buys less as prices compound upward — earns the final mark.

The final questions of this part are with you now — one break-even found and checked, both interests grown, one price inflated.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same revision from behind the vetkoek stall and around the kitchen table — the arithmetic of an ordinary money month.

## Subtopic: The Payslip Is a Story of Subtraction

A payslip tells one story: money arrives big and leaves smaller, and every line between is a named subtraction.

Ayanda earns R9 800 gross. Before it reaches her, three hands dip in. UIF takes 1 percent — R98 — tiny, and it is insurance: lose the job, and the fund pays out BECAUSE these deductions happened. Pension takes 7,5 percent — R735 — that is her own future self, paid first. Tax takes R1 176 — the schools-roads-hospitals subscription. What lands in the account: R7 791. The story in one line: 9 800 in, 2 009 named and explained, 7 791 out.

Why the exam cares: every deduction question is one of three askables. Calculate a line — one percentage multiplication. Explain a line — one sentence saying what the money buys. Recalculate after a change — a 6 percent raise makes the gross 9 800 times 1,06, and every percentage deduction resizes with it, which is the chained question's whole trick: change the gross first, then rerun the lines.

The same subtraction story runs the household. Money in: salaries, the child grant, the room rented to a cousin. Money out: fixed things that ignore your choices — rent, the policy — and variable things that obey them — electricity, food, airtime. Surplus or deficit is one subtraction, and advice questions always point at the variable pile.

Read any money document as a story with named characters, and no line on it can ambush you.

Your questions for this section are ready — one line calculated, one line explained, one raise chained through.

## Subtopic: The Stall That Teaches Break-even

Stand behind Lwazi's stall for one month and break-even stops being a formula.

The stand costs R1 500 whether he fries or sleeps. That is the hole the month starts in. Each vetkoek sells for R26 but eats R14 of ingredients — so each sale throws R12 into the hole. The only question that matters in week one: how many R12 shovelfuls fill a R1 500 hole? 1 500 over 12 — 125. Vetkoek number 125 lands exactly at ground level: income R3 250, costs R3 250, nothing lost, nothing gained. Vetkoek 126 is the first one he actually profits from — R12 clean.

Hear the three numbers in every break-even question: the hole — fixed cost; the shovel — selling price minus cost per item; the count — hole over shovel. The exam dresses them as gyms, tuck shops, taxi routes and craft markets, but the three numbers never change costume convincingly. On the graph version, the income line starts at nought and climbs at R26 per sale; the cost line starts at R1 500 and climbs at R14; they cross at 125 — the crossing IS the filled hole.

And the what-if questions are just new shovels: ingredients rise to R16? The shovel shrinks to R10, and the hole needs 150 sales. Raise the price to R28? Shovel R14, count about 107 — but will customers still buy? That last sentence — the realism check — is a mark, because mathematics that ignores the customer is bookkeeping, not business.

Here are this section's questions — name the hole, size the shovel, count the shovelfuls, then question the price.

## Subtopic: Slow Money — Interest, Inflation and the Long Game

The last pieces of finance move slowly, which is exactly why they are misunderstood — and why the exam checks who truly gets them.

Two savings accounts, both starting with R5 000 at 10 percent. The simple account pays interest on the original only: R500 a year, like a salary that never gets a raise — after three years, R6 500. The compound account pays interest on everything so far: 5 500 after year one, then 10 percent of 5 500 — R550 — ending year two at 6 050, then R605 more, closing year three at 6 655. Same rate, same years — R155 extra, earned by interest working on interest. Stretch the years and the gap stops being polite: compounding is a snowball, and the hill is time. The exam's ask is the table, year by year, and the sentence naming compound as the better saver WITH the gap quoted.

Inflation is the same snowball rolling against you. At 6 percent, the R250 shoes cost R265 next year, and the year after that the 6 percent bites the NEW price. Your money stands still; prices compound past it. That is why a salary that never rises is quietly a salary that shrinks — and why the interpretation questions ask what inflation does to buying power. The answer: the same rand buys less each year, because prices grow compound.

The slow-money habits for the paper: build the year-by-year table — it earns method marks even when arithmetic slips; never apply a multi-year percentage in one lazy multiplication unless the question says simple; and end with the comparison sentence — which option, by how much, shown by the table.

Read the last questions of the lesson now — grow both accounts, inflate one price honestly, and let the tables do the talking.
