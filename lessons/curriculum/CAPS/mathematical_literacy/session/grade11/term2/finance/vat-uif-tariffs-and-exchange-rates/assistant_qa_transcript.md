**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! The one that comes up every single time: why does dividing by 1,15 remove VAT? Everyone can memorise it, but nobody trusts it, and under exam pressure people fall back on taking fifteen percent off.

**Tutor:** Trust comes from seeing what the inclusive price actually is. When the shop added VAT, it took the original price — call it one whole, one hundred percent — and stacked another fifteen percent of that same original on top. So the shelf price is one hundred and fifteen percent of the original: not the original plus fifteen percent of ITSELF, but of the original. R276 is therefore 115 hundredths of the true price, and recovering the true price means undoing a multiplication by 1,15 — which is division by 1,15, giving R240. Now watch the wrong method fail: fifteen percent OF R276 is R41,40, because R276 is a bigger number than R240, so subtracting it gives R234,60 — you removed too much tax, R5,40 too much. The deeper lesson is that percentage increases and decreases are not symmetrical: adding fifteen percent and then subtracting fifteen percent never returns you to the start, because the two fifteens are percentages of different numbers. If you remember only one sentence, remember this: the inclusive price is 115%, so divide by 1,15 to find the 100%.

**Bianca:** Next: the two R84s confused people. If the employer also pays R84 UIF, why doesn't Sipho's payslip show R168, and does the employer's share come out of his salary somehow?

**Tutor:** The employer's share never touches the salary — it is an additional cost of employing Sipho, paid out of the business's own money, over and above the R8 400. Think of it as the law charging both sides of the employment relationship one percent each for the insurance: the worker insures his income, the employer contributes to the pool that makes the insurance possible. The payslip records only what happens to Sipho's earnings, so it shows his gross of R8 400, his own deduction of R84, and never the employer's matching payment. That is why exam questions circle these numbers so carefully. "What is deducted from the worker's pay?" — R84. "What does the employer contribute?" — R84, separately. "What does the fund receive in total?" — R168. Three questions, three answers, all from one salary. One more refinement for full marks: the one percent is taken on gross earnings, before tax and other deductions, and if a question mentions the legislated earnings ceiling — a maximum salary on which UIF may be calculated — then a worker earning above that ceiling contributes one percent of the ceiling, not of the full salary. The question will always supply the ceiling figure if it wants you to use it.

**Bianca:** This one caused real trouble: multiply or divide with exchange rates. People know the rate is R18,50 per dollar, and then freeze — half the class multiplied when they should have divided.

**Tutor:** Freeze no more, because one sentence settles it every time: say the rate as a price. "One dollar COSTS eighteen rand fifty." A dollar is now just an expensive item on a shelf, like a loaf of artisan bread. If you are buying a known number of dollars — the $120 visa fee — you do what you would do with any priced item: quantity times price, 120 times 18,50, R2 220. Notice the answer must land in rands, and it must be BIGGER than the dollar figure, because each dollar costs many rands. If instead you hold rands and want to know how many dollars they buy — R5 000 in your pocket — you are asking how many R18,50 items fit into your money: 5 000 divided by 18,50, roughly 270 dollars. The answer lands in dollars and is SMALLER than the rand figure. That size check is your safety net: converting rands to a stronger currency must shrink the number; converting a stronger currency to rands must grow it. If your answer breaks that expectation — R5 000 becoming 92 500 of anything — you multiplied where you should have divided, and the check catches it before the marker does.

**Bianca:** Last one: in the phone-deal comparison, someone asked why we bother with the crossing point at all. If the question tells us the person talks 100 minutes a month, can't we just work out both totals and pick the smaller?

**Tutor:** For that person, absolutely — and that is exactly what you should do: A costs R174 at 100 minutes, B costs R145, so B is the advice, and the saving of R29 belongs in your sentence. The crossing point answers a different and bigger question: not "which is cheaper for THIS user" but "for WHOM is each option cheaper". Around 141 minutes the two deals cost the same; below it every user is better off on B, above it every user is better off on A. That single number turns you from a calculator into an adviser — you can now counsel any customer who walks in, without redoing the arithmetic for each one. Examiners test it both ways: a table or graph question will ask you to read off where the lines intersect, and an advice question will describe two or three users and expect you to place each one on the correct side of the crossing. There is also a marks reason to care: the equation 99 plus 0,75 m equals 1,45 m, solved to m equals 141,43, carries its own method marks even when a later step slips. So do both — answer the specific user with both totals, and know that the crossing point is what makes your advice general. And keep the interpretation honest: at exactly the crossing point the options tie, so neither is wrong, and a sensible answer says so.
