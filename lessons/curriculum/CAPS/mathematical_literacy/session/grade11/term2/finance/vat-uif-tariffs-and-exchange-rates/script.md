# Part 1 — Expert

Three sets of numbers pass through almost every South African wallet without being examined: the VAT hiding in every till slip, the UIF line on every payslip, and the tariff tables that decide what a call, a kilolitre or a bank transaction costs. Add the exchange rate — the price of the rand itself — and you have the whole of this session. The setting is one family in Mthatha: a till slip from the supermarket, a payslip from a warehouse, two cellphone contracts to choose between, and a cousin sending money home from London.

## Subtopic: VAT: the Fifteen Percent on Almost Everything

VALUE-ADDED TAX is a tax of 15% that the government adds to the price of most goods and services. A price EXCLUDING VAT is the amount before tax; a price INCLUDING VAT is what the customer actually pays. Moving between the two is the core skill, and each direction has exactly one correct move.

Adding VAT: multiply the excluding price by 0,15 for the tax, or by 1,15 for the total in one step. A microwave priced at R240 excluding VAT carries VAT of 240 × 0,15 = R36, so the shelf price is R276 — or directly, 240 × 1,15 = R276.

Removing VAT is where marks die. The R276 includes the tax, so it represents 115% of the original price. To get back, divide by 1,15: 276 ÷ 1,15 = R240. The tempting wrong move — taking 15% off the R276 — gives 276 × 0,85 = R234,60, which is wrong by R5,40, because it takes 15% of the bigger, already-taxed number.

Some goods carry no VAT at all. ZERO-RATED items are basic foods the law protects: brown bread, maize meal, rice, dried beans, lentils, fresh vegetables and fruit, eggs, milk. A till slip marks them separately, and a question may ask you to total the VAT on a slip where only some items are taxed — tax the taxable, leave the zero-rated alone. The questions for this section are with you now; before every calculation, write down whether the price you hold includes or excludes the tax.

## Subtopic: UIF: One Percent from Each Side

The UNEMPLOYMENT INSURANCE FUND is a national fund that pays workers a temporary income when they lose work, or during maternity leave or illness. It is financed by a contribution of 1% of the worker's earnings — and the law takes that 1% twice: once from the WORKER, deducted off the payslip, and once from the EMPLOYER, paid on top.

Sipho earns R8 400 a month at the warehouse. His UIF deduction is 8 400 × 0,01 = R84. His employer pays a further R84 to the fund on his behalf. So R168 reaches the fund each month, but only R84 of it comes off Sipho's pay. An exam question will probe exactly that distinction: what the worker pays, what the employer pays, and what the fund receives — three different questions on the same two numbers.

On the payslip, UIF sits with the other DEDUCTIONS — amounts subtracted between GROSS pay, the earnings before anything is taken, and NET pay, the amount that actually arrives. Sipho's gross is R8 400; after UIF of R84 and other deductions of R516, his net pay is 8 400 − 84 − 516 = R7 800.

Two details worth a mark each. UIF is calculated on gross earnings, before the other deductions, not on net pay. And the percentage never changes with the salary — it is 1% for the cleaner and 1% for the manager, though the law caps contributions above a stated earnings ceiling, and if the question supplies that ceiling you calculate on the ceiling, not the salary. Your questions for this section are ready; one percent, taken twice, on the gross.

## Subtopic: Exchange Rates: the Price of the Rand

An EXCHANGE RATE is the price of one currency in another. R18,50 to the US dollar means every dollar costs eighteen rand fifty. The two calculations run in opposite directions, and choosing the direction is the whole skill.

Buying foreign currency: multiply. A visa application fee of $120 costs 120 × 18,50 = R2 220. Converting rands into foreign currency: divide. A traveller with R5 000 gets 5 000 ÷ 18,50 = $270,27.

The direction test that never fails: dollars are the expensive item, priced at R18,50 each. Going from the expensive unit to rands, multiply — many rands per dollar. Going from rands to the expensive unit, divide — your rands buy fewer dollars than their number suggests.

STRONGER and WEAKER describe movement. If the rate moves from R18,50 to R19,20 per dollar, each dollar now costs more rand, so the rand has WEAKENED — imported goods, petrol and overseas travel all become more expensive. If the rate falls to R17,80, the rand has STRENGTHENED. The cousin in London sending £400 home cares about the pound's rate: at R23,40 per pound the family receives 400 × 23,40 = R9 360; if the rand weakens to R24,10, the same £400 becomes R9 640 — a weaker rand is bad news at the petrol pump but good news for a family receiving money from abroad, and examiners love asking learners to see both sides. The questions for this section follow now; write the rate as a sentence — one dollar costs R18,50 — before choosing to multiply or divide.

## Subtopic: Tariff Systems: Which Option Is Cheaper for You

A TARIFF SYSTEM is a rule for charging that depends on usage: municipal water and electricity, phone contracts, transport fares, bank fees. Grade 11 work compares TWO options and finds where each one wins.

The family is choosing a cellphone deal. Option A: a monthly charge of R99 plus R0,75 per minute of calls. Option B: no monthly charge, but R1,45 per minute. Write each as a formula. Cost of A in rand = 99 + 0,75 × minutes. Cost of B = 1,45 × minutes.

Test real usage. At 100 minutes: A costs 99 + 75 = R174; B costs R145 — B wins. At 200 minutes: A costs 99 + 150 = R249; B costs R290 — A wins. Somewhere between, the options cross. Set them equal: 99 + 0,75 × m = 1,45 × m, so 99 = 0,70 × m, and m = 141,43 minutes. Below about 141 minutes a month, the light user should take B; above it, the heavy caller should take A. That crossing point is the BREAK-EVEN point of the comparison, and on a graph it is literally where the two cost lines intersect — B starting at zero but climbing steeply, A starting at R99 but climbing gently.

The same machinery prices bank accounts: an account with a monthly fee and cheap transactions against a pay-as-you-use account, where the crossing point is a number of transactions. The method never changes: formula for each option, test values on both sides, find the crossing, and answer with advice in words — who should choose what, and why. The last questions of this part are with you now: build both formulae before comparing anything.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same numbers at the till, at the pay queue and in the phone shop, with no formula sheet in sight.

## Subtopic: The Tax Inside the Till Slip

Nearly everything you buy carries a passenger: fifteen percent for the government, riding inside the price. The skill is knowing whether the price you are looking at has the passenger already on board.

Price WITHOUT the tax, and you want the final price? Add the fifteen percent: R240 for the microwave, plus fifteen percent — which is R36 — makes R276. One shortcut does it in a single move: times 1,15.

Price WITH the tax, and you want the original? Here is the trap the marker sets every year. Do not take fifteen percent off. The R276 is the original PLUS its fifteen percent — it is 115 percent of something. So divide by 1,15 and the something comes back: R240 exactly. Take fifteen percent off instead and you get R234,60 — close enough to feel right, wrong enough to lose everything. Adding fifteen percent and then removing fifteen percent does not return you home, because the second fifteen percent is taken of a bigger number.

And remember the protected basket: brown bread, maize meal, rice, beans, vegetables, fruit, eggs, milk. Zero-rated — no passenger at all. On a till slip, tax only the taxable lines. Some questions on this section are with you right now — first ask every price: is the fifteen percent already inside you?

## Subtopic: The Two R84s on the Payslip

Look at Sipho's payslip. Gross pay at the top: R8 400 — what he earned. Net pay at the bottom: what actually reaches him. Between the two live the deductions, and one of them says UIF: R84.

That R84 is exactly one percent of his gross pay, and it is insurance — if the warehouse retrenches him, the fund pays him a temporary income while he looks for work. One percent is the easiest percentage in the book: slide the comma two places. R8 400 becomes R84. A gross of R12 650 would give R126,50. No calculator needed.

Here is the piece most people never see: the employer pays another R84 to the fund, on top of the salary, out of the business's own pocket. So the fund receives R168 a month for Sipho, but only R84 ever touches his payslip. When a question asks "how much does Sipho contribute?", the answer is R84. "How much does the fund receive?" — R168. Read which one is being asked; both numbers will be sitting in the options.

Then finish the payslip: gross R8 400, minus UIF R84, minus the other deductions R516, leaves R7 800 net. Gross minus deductions equals net, always — and UIF is always worked on the gross, before anything else is removed. Questions on this section are coming your way now — slide the comma, and read whether the question wants the worker's side, the employer's side, or both.

## Subtopic: Rands Abroad and the Cheaper Phone Deal

Two decisions finish the lesson: changing money, and choosing between two deals.

Changing money first. A dollar is just an item with a price: R18,50 each. Buying 120 of them for the visa fee? Like buying 120 loaves: 120 times 18,50, R2 220. Turning R5 000 INTO dollars? Now you are asking how many R18,50 items your money buys: 5 000 divided by 18,50, about 270 dollars. Multiply towards rands, divide towards the foreign money — and if the rate climbs from R18,50 to R19,20, dollars got more expensive, which means the rand got weaker. Weaker rand: petrol and imports hurt more, but the cousin's £400 from London turns into MORE rand at home. Same movement, two different faces.

Now the phone shop. Deal A: R99 every month, then 75 cents a minute. Deal B: nothing per month, but R1,45 a minute. Do not guess — test. Talk little, say 100 minutes: A costs 99 plus 75, R174; B costs R145. B wins. Talk a lot, say 200 minutes: A costs R249, B costs R290. A wins. The deals swap somewhere in the middle — around 141 minutes — because A charges you rent but cheap minutes, and B charges no rent but expensive minutes.

That is every tariff comparison in one sentence: the option with the fixed charge suits the heavy user; the pay-as-you-go option suits the light user; and the crossing point is where they trade places. Work out both totals at the usage the question gives, then answer like an adviser: "at 100 minutes a month, choose B and save R29." The final questions of the lesson are with you now — test both options honestly and let the numbers pick the deal.
