**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! The big one first: people measure the map correctly, multiply correctly, and then bleed the mark in the unit conversion — 200 000 cm became 20 km for half the class. What is the reliable way through?

**Tutor:** Convert in two small hops instead of one giant leap, and write each hop down. Centimetres to metres first: divide by 100, because a metre holds 100 centimetres — 200 000 becomes 2 000 m. Then metres to kilometres: divide by 1 000 — 2 000 becomes 2 km. Two divisions, each trivially checkable. The learners who wrote 20 km divided by 10 000 in one jump, half-remembering the combined factor. If you insist on the single jump, weld the true factor into memory: 100 times 1 000 is 100 000 centimetres in a kilometre, so divide centimetres by 100 000 — 200 000 over 100 000 is 2. Then run the sense test the lesson handed you: an 8 cm route across the street map of one town cannot be 20 km, because 20 km is a highway stretch. The arithmetic and the picture must agree; when they quarrel, recheck the conversion before anything else, because nine times out of ten the error lives there.

**Bianca:** Next: why do questions keep asking us to compare number scales and bar scales? It feels like the same information written twice, and people battle to find two full sentences about them.

**Tutor:** They are the same information with opposite survival skills, and the two sentences a marker wants are exactly about that. Sentence one, the number scale: it is perfectly exact — 1 : 25 000 is a precise mathematical statement requiring no measurement of the scale itself — but it is true only at the printed size. The moment anyone enlarges or reduces the map — a photocopier, a projector, a phone screen pinched smaller — every distance on the paper changes while the printed ratio keeps insisting 1 : 25 000, and it becomes a confident lie. Sentence two, the bar scale: it is a drawing, so whatever resizing happens to the roads happens identically to the bar, and the proportion between them survives — the bar tells the truth on any copy at any size. Its price is precision: you must physically measure the bar with your ruler, so your answer inherits every wobble of hand and eye, where the ratio was exact. When a question asks which scale a map SHOULD carry, the strong answer names the situation: a document destined to be copied, projected or viewed at unknown sizes needs the bar; careful work on the original sheet is best served by the number scale; and most good maps print both for precisely this reason.

**Bianca:** This one returns every year with the travel questions: 22 km at 55 km an hour, and half the class wrote 40 minutes. Where does the 24 come from, and how do we block the decimal-hours trap?

**Tutor:** The division gives 22 over 55, which is 0,4 — and 0,4 of an HOUR, not 40 minutes. The trap is that 0,4 whispers "forty" to an eye in a hurry. But a decimal of an hour is a fraction of 60 minutes, so the conversion is always 0,4 times 60, which is 24 minutes. The general protection is a rule applied without exception: whenever a time calculation produces a decimal, immediately multiply the decimal part by 60 before writing any minutes. 0,5 hours is 30 minutes, fine — but 0,25 hours is 15 minutes not 25, 0,75 is 45 not 75, and 1,3 hours is 1 hour 18 minutes, never 1 hour 30. Question setters choose speeds and distances so that the lazy reading and the correct reading disagree. Write the honest line — 0,4 h × 60 = 24 min — and the mark is safe. The discipline works in reverse too: 45 minutes entering a formula must enter as 0,75 hours, because a rate per hour demands time measured in hours.

**Bianca:** Last one: in the car-versus-minibus comparison, some people answered "the minibus is cheaper" and others "the car is cheaper", and both showed working. How can both be defensible, and what does a full answer look like?

**Tutor:** Both are defensible because the verdict changes with the number of travellers — which is exactly why the question is set that way. For ONE person making the round trip, the minibus costs R60 while the car burns R75,24 of petrol; the minibus wins by R15,24. For FOUR people travelling together, the minibus collects R60 from each — R240 in total — while the car still burns the same R75,24 no matter how many sit in it; the car wins by R164,76, and per person the car costs about R18,81 against the minibus's R60. The car's cost is fixed for the vehicle; the minibus's cost multiplies per passenger — that structural difference is the insight under examination. So a full answer does three things: states the scenario it is pricing, shows both totals for that scenario, and delivers the verdict with the saving in rands and a reason. Something like: "for a single traveller the minibus is cheaper, R60 against R75,24; for four together the car is cheaper, R75,24 against R240, saving R164,76, because the car's petrol cost does not grow with passengers." And keep the honesty clause loaded: the petrol figure prices only fuel — a complete comparison could mention wear and the driver's time, and a mark sometimes waits for the learner who notices what the calculation leaves out.
