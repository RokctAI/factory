Hey, it's Bianca — maps feel like a geography exam gatecrashing your maths paper, but every map question is the same three-step dance, and you already know all the steps.

Step one: measure. Ruler on the map, honest centimetres. Step two: multiply by the scale — 1 : 50 000 means times 50 000. Step three: tidy the units in two small hops, never one big leap. Divide by 100 for metres, then by 1 000 for kilometres. 7 cm becomes 350 000 cm becomes 3 500 m becomes 3,5 km. Write all three hops and the conversion can never betray you.

Then run the sense test, your free safety net: paper to world must grow HUGE, world to paper must shrink tiny. If a route across town comes out as 35 km, the conversion slipped — catch it yourself, smile, fix it.

Bar scales: measure the bar, price one centimetre, count. And keep the exam sentence loaded: the bar survives photocopying because it shrinks with the roads; the printed ratio is exact but only true at original size.

Directions: find the north arrow FIRST. North top, east right — but left and right belong to the traveller, so a journey heading down the page flips every turn. Turn the map in your head before you answer.

And the money questions are just a chain: kilometres, divided by speed — decimals of an hour times 60 for minutes, 0,3 hours is 18 minutes, never 30 — then litres per 100 km, then rands per litre. One link at a time, each link a mark.

Measure, multiply, tidy, sense-check. That is every map ever printed. You've got this!
