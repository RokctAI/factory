**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! The big one first: people measure the map correctly, multiply correctly, and then lose the mark in the unit conversion — 350 000 cm became 35 km for half the class. What is the reliable way through?

**Tutor:** Convert in two small steps instead of one big leap, and write each step down. Centimetres to metres first: divide by 100, because a metre holds 100 centimetres — 350 000 becomes 3 500 m. Then metres to kilometres: divide by 1 000 — 3 500 becomes 3,5 km. Two divisions, each one easy to check. The learners who got 35 km divided by 10 000 in one jump, half-remembering the combined factor. If you prefer the single jump, fix the true factor in your head permanently: 100 times 1 000 is 100 000 centimetres in a kilometre, so divide centimetres by 100 000 — 350 000 over 100 000 is 3,5. And then run the sense test the lesson gave you: a 7 cm route across a street map of one town cannot be 35 km, because 35 km is a serious highway drive. The arithmetic and the picture must agree; when they argue, recheck the conversion before anything else, because that is where the error lives nine times out of ten.

**Bianca:** Next: why does the exam keep asking us to compare number scales and bar scales? It feels like the same information written twice, and people struggle to find two full sentences to say about them.

**Tutor:** They are the same information with opposite survival skills, and the two sentences the marker wants are exactly about that. Sentence one, the number scale: it is perfectly exact — 1 : 50 000 is a precise mathematical statement needing no measurement of the scale itself — but it is only true at the size the map was printed. The moment anyone enlarges or reduces the map — a photocopier, a projector, a cellphone screen pinched smaller — every distance on the paper changes while the printed ratio stubbornly keeps saying 1 : 50 000, and it becomes a confident lie. Sentence two, the bar scale: it is a drawing, so whatever resizing happens to the roads happens equally to the bar, and the proportion between them survives — the bar tells the truth on any copy at any size. Its price is precision: you must physically measure the bar with your ruler, so your answer inherits every wobble of your hand and eye, where the ratio was exact. When the question asks which scale a map SHOULD carry, the strong answer names the situation: a document that will be copied, projected or viewed at unknown sizes needs the bar; careful work on the original sheet is best served by the number scale; and most good maps carry both for exactly this reason.

**Bianca:** This one comes up every year with the travel questions: 18 km at 60 km an hour, and half the class wrote 30 minutes. Where does the 18 come from, and how do we stop the decimal-hours trap?

**Tutor:** The division gives 18 over 60, which is 0,3 — and 0,3 of an HOUR, not 30 minutes. The trap is that 0,3 looks like "thirty" to an eye in a hurry. But a decimal of an hour is a fraction of 60 minutes, so the conversion is always 0,3 times 60, which is 18 minutes. It is a coincidence of these numbers that the minutes equal the kilometres — at 60 km/h every kilometre takes exactly one minute, which is itself a lovely check. The general protection is a rule you apply without exception: whenever a time calculation gives a decimal, immediately multiply the decimal part by 60 before writing any minutes. 0,5 hours is 30 minutes, fine — but 0,25 hours is 15 minutes not 25, 0,75 is 45 not 75, and 1,4 hours is 1 hour 24 minutes, never 1 hour 40. Examiners choose speeds and distances specifically so that the lazy reading and the correct reading differ. Write the honest line — 0,3 h × 60 = 18 min — and the mark is safe. The same discipline works in reverse: 45 minutes entering a formula must go in as 0,75 hours, because a rate per hour demands time in hours.

**Bianca:** Last one: in the car-versus-taxi comparison, some people answered "the taxi is cheaper" and others "the car is cheaper", and both showed working. How can both be defensible, and what does a full answer look like?

**Tutor:** Both are defensible because the comparison changes with the number of travellers — which is precisely why the examiner set it. For ONE person making the return trip, the taxi costs R52 while the car burns R67,68 of petrol; the taxi wins by R15,68. For FOUR people travelling together, the taxi collects R52 from each — R208 in total — while the car still burns the same R67,68 regardless of how many sit in it; the car wins by R140,32, and per person the car costs about R16,92 against the taxi's R52. The car's cost is fixed for the vehicle; the taxi's cost multiplies per passenger — that structural difference is the insight being tested. So a full answer does three things: states the scenario it is pricing, shows both totals for that scenario, and gives the verdict with the saving in rands and a reason. Something like: "for a single traveller the taxi is cheaper, R52 against R67,68; for a family of four the car is cheaper, R67,68 against R208, saving R140,32, because the car's petrol cost does not grow with passengers." And keep the honesty clause ready: the petrol figure prices only fuel — a full comparison could mention wear and the driver's time, and papers sometimes award a mark for noticing what the calculation leaves out.
