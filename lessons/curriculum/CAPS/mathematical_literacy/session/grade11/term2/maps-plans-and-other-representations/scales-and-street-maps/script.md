# Part 1 — Expert

A map is a promise: everything on this paper is smaller than reality by exactly the same amount. The scale states that amount, and everything the curriculum asks — distances, directions, locations, routes, travel times, fuel costs — flows from reading it correctly. This session works with two maps of the same journey: a street map of Polokwane for finding the taxi rank and the clinic, and a provincial road map for the trip to a cousin in Tzaneen.

## Subtopic: Number Scales: One Centimetre Standing for Thousands

A NUMBER SCALE is written as a ratio: 1 : 50 000. It means one unit on the map represents fifty thousand of the same unit on the ground — one centimetre on paper is 50 000 centimetres of real Polokwane. The ratio has no units of its own, which is its power: it works in centimetres, millimetres, anything, as long as both sides use the same unit.

The method has three steps, always in this order. Measure the map distance with a ruler. Multiply by the scale number. Convert to sensible units. The route from the taxi rank to the clinic measures 7 cm on the 1 : 50 000 street map: 7 × 50 000 = 350 000 cm. Convert: divide by 100 for metres, 3 500 m, and by 1 000 for kilometres, 3,5 km.

Conversion is where the marks leak, so anchor two facts: 100 cm in a metre, 1 000 m in a kilometre — so 100 000 cm in a kilometre. A shortcut worth checking rather than memorising: on a 1 : 50 000 map, every centimetre is half a kilometre.

The calculation reverses cleanly. How long on the map is a 9 km road? 9 km is 900 000 cm; divide by 50 000 to get 18 cm of paper. Dividing where you should multiply produces absurdities — a town crossing measured in millimetres — so always test the answer against sense: map to ground grows, ground to map shrinks. The questions for this section are with you now; measure, multiply, convert, and sense-check the size.

## Subtopic: Bar Scales and the Photocopier Problem

A BAR SCALE says the same thing graphically: a printed bar divided into segments, labelled with the real distance each segment represents. To use it, set your ruler against the bar and read off what one centimetre of map is worth. On the provincial map, the bar shows that 2 cm represents 10 km, so every centimetre is 5 km.

Using it is a proportion. The road from Polokwane to the Tzaneen turn-off measures 9 cm: at 5 km per centimetre, that is 9 × 5 = 45 km. No ruler at all? Mark the route on the edge of a piece of paper, then step that marked length along the bar scale, counting the segments — the method surveyors' assistants have used for a century.

Now the examinable insight: why maps carry a bar scale at all when the number scale is more precise. Photocopy a map at 80% and every distance on the paper shrinks — but the number scale printed on it still says 1 : 50 000, which is now a lie. The bar scale, being a drawing, shrinks along with the roads, so the proportion between bar and route survives enlargement and reduction. A bar scale stays true on any copy; a number scale is only true at the original size. Examiners ask for exactly that comparison, and the answer must mention resizing.

The weakness runs the other way too: reading a bar scale involves measuring the bar itself, so it inherits the accuracy of your ruler and your eye, where a number scale is exact. Precision against robustness — name the trade-off and the mark is yours. Your questions for this section are ready; read what one centimetre is worth before measuring anything.

## Subtopic: Directions, Grids and Finding the Place

Maps answer where as well as how far. A street map carries a GRID: letters across the top, numbers down the side, cutting the map into blocks. The clinic in block B3 sits where column B crosses row 3 — and a grid reference locates the block, not the exact doorstep, which is why an index says "Grobler Street, B3" and expects your eye to finish the search.

Directions come in two languages, and the paper uses both. COMPASS directions are fixed by the world: north, south, east, west, and the in-betweens like north-east. On most maps north is the top of the sheet, and every other direction follows: east right, south bottom, west left. Tzaneen lies north-east of Polokwane; the answer "up and to the right" earns nothing, because the marker needs the compass word.

RELATIVE directions — left, right, straight — depend on which way the traveller is facing, and they are the language of route descriptions: from the taxi rank, travel north along Market Street, turn right into Grobler Street, and the clinic is on your left after two blocks. Writing such a description is itself an exam task, and the marks sit in the details: street names, turn directions, and a landmark to confirm arrival.

One habit ties the skills together: orient the map first. Find the north arrow, turn the map in your mind so its north matches the world's, and only then translate between "right" and "east". A traveller heading south along Market Street turns LEFT, not right, to go west — the classic trap when the journey runs down the page. The questions for this section follow now; check the north arrow before answering anything about direction.

## Subtopic: From Map Measurement to Time, Fuel and Rands

The final skill chains everything to the questions a real trip asks: how long will it take, and what will it cost?

Distance first. On the 1 : 150 000 road map, the full route from Polokwane to the cousin's house measures 12 cm: 12 × 150 000 = 1 800 000 cm = 18 km.

Time next, from the relationship: time equals distance divided by speed. At an average of 60 km/h through town traffic, 18 ÷ 60 = 0,3 hours. Convert honestly: 0,3 × 60 = 18 minutes. The trap is reading 0,3 hours as 30 minutes — decimal hours are not minutes, and examiners set the numbers to catch exactly that.

Fuel is a proportion. The family car uses 8 ℓ of petrol per 100 km. Over 18 km: 18 ÷ 100 × 8 = 1,44 ℓ. At R23,50 per litre, the one-way fuel cost is 1,44 × 23,50 = R33,84 — and the question usually wants the return trip, R67,68, so read whether the journey comes home.

Compare with the taxi: R26 per person each way. One person returning pays R52; the car's return fuel is R67,68, but four people in the car still pay R67,68 together against R208 by taxi. Cost per person is the comparison that decides, and stating it in a sentence — "for four travellers the car is cheaper by R140,32" — is the expected finish.

Every link in this chain — measure, scale, convert, divide by speed, multiply by consumption and price — earns its own mark, so write every link. The last questions of this part are with you now: map to kilometres, kilometres to minutes and rands.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same two maps spread on the taxi rank wall, read the way a traveller actually reads them.

## Subtopic: The Map Shrinks the World Evenly

A map is the world through a shrinking machine, and the scale tells you the setting on the dial. 1 : 50 000 means everything got fifty thousand times smaller — every road, every block, all by the same amount. That evenness is the whole trick: measure anything on the paper, multiply back up, and you have the real thing.

So the routine is measure, multiply, tidy up. The clinic route is 7 cm on the map. Multiply by 50 000: 350 000 cm. Nobody speaks in centimetres about a trip across town, so tidy: 100 cm make a metre — 3 500 m; 1 000 m make a kilometre — 3,5 km. Half an hour's walk, one taxi ride.

Going the other way, shrink instead of grow: a 9 km stretch of road, written in centimetres — 900 000 — divided by 50 000, is 18 cm of paper.

And always run the sense test. Paper to world: the number must get much bigger. World to paper: much smaller. If your answer says the clinic is 70 km away or the road fits in 2 mm, you divided where you should have multiplied — no shame, just fix it before it fixes you. Some questions on this section are with you right now: ruler first, multiply second, tidy the units last.

## Subtopic: The Little Bar That Survives the Photocopier

Down in the corner of the map sits a striped bar, and it is the most honest thing on the sheet. The label says each stripe is 10 km. Put your ruler on it: the stripe is 2 cm long. So the map is speaking a simple language — every centimetre means 5 km.

Read distances with that one fact. The road to the turn-off is 9 cm: nine times five, 45 km. No ruler? Lay the straight edge of any paper along the route, tick the start and end, then hold the ticks against the bar and count stripes. The bar does not care what you measure with.

Here is why the bar earns its place. Take the map to the photocopier and shrink it to fit your pocket. Every road just got smaller — and the printed ratio 1 : 50 000 is now wrong, because one centimetre of the small copy no longer covers fifty thousand real ones. But the bar shrank WITH the roads. Nine of its centimetres still equal the same stretch of road, stripe for stripe. The bar keeps its promise on any copy; the ratio only tells the truth at original size.

The bar's weakness is your eyesight — you measure the bar itself, so a slightly crooked ruler gives a slightly wrong answer, where the printed ratio is exact. Exact but fragile, or rough but robust: that is the choice, and saying it in a sentence is worth a mark. Questions on this section are coming your way now — find the bar, price one centimetre, then count.

## Subtopic: Getting There: Blocks, Turns and Petrol Money

Finding the place, describing the way, and paying for the trip — the last three jobs of any map.

Finding: the grid. Letters run across the top, numbers down the side, and "clinic, B3" means the block where column B meets row 3. The grid hands you the block; your eyes do the last hundred metres. Directions come in two flavours: compass words — north at the top of the sheet, east to the right — and body words — left, right, straight. The compass never moves; left and right travel with you. Walking south down Market Street, west is on your RIGHT — turn the map around in your head before you answer, or the down-the-page journey will flip every turn.

Describing: name the streets, name the turns, end on a landmark. From the rank: north up Market, right into Grobler, clinic on the left after two blocks. That sentence is worth marks exactly as written.

Paying: chain the map to your pocket. The road map says the cousin is 12 cm away at 1 : 150 000 — 18 km. At 60 km/h that is 18 divided by 60, which is 0,3 of an hour — and 0,3 of 60 minutes is 18 minutes, not 30. The car drinks 8 litres every 100 km, so 18 km sips 1,44 litres; at R23,50 a litre, R33,84 there and R67,68 there-and-back. The taxi wants R52 for one person returning — cheaper alone. Fill the car with four people and the R67,68 splits to about R17 each against the taxi's R52 — the car wins by R140,32 altogether. The final questions of the lesson are with you now: block, turns, kilometres, minutes, rands — in that order, every time.
