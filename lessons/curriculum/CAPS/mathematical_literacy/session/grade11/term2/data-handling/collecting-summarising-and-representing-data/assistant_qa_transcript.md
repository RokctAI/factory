**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! First, the one that comes up every single time: when do we use the mean and when do we use the median? People can calculate both perfectly and then freeze when the paper asks which one 'best represents' the data.

**Tutor:** The deciding question is always: does this dataset have outliers — a few values far from the rest? If it does, the median represents the typical member better, because the median is chosen by position in the sorted queue and an extreme value cannot move it; the mean, by contrast, adds every rand into the pot, so one enormous value drags it toward itself. Our nine spenders show it exactly: mean R40, median R35, and the whole disagreement is manufactured by the single R90 spender. Remove that one learner and the mean crashes to R33,75 while the median barely moves — that stability is what "resistant to outliers" means. When the data is tight and symmetrical, with no extremes, the two averages nearly agree and the mean is fine — it uses every value, which is a virtue when every value is trustworthy. In the exam, never answer with just a name. The mark scheme wants the name PLUS the mechanism: "the median, because the outlier of R90 inflates the mean, and the median is unaffected by extreme values." Salaries, house prices and spending data almost always carry a long tail of big values, which is why real-world reports quote median income, not mean income.

**Bianca:** Next: bias keeps costing marks because people describe it vaguely — they write 'the sample is unfair' and get nothing. What does a full bias answer look like?

**Tutor:** A full bias answer names three things: WHO is over- or under-represented, WHY the collection method caused that, and WHICH DIRECTION the result tilts. "Unfair" earns nothing because it is a feeling, not a mechanism. Compare: "surveying grade 11 friends at break over-represents seniors, who carry more pocket money than grade 8s, so the survey will OVERSTATE the school's average weekly spending." Every clause is doing paid work: the group, the cause, the direction. Practise spotting the standard machines of bias. Convenience sampling — asking whoever is nearby — over-represents people like the asker. Voluntary response — put a box in the foyer — over-represents the passionate, because indifferent people do not walk to boxes. Timing bias — surveying at the tuck shop — catches spenders and misses savers. Leading questions — "how much do you WASTE on airtime?" — push answers downward because nobody admits waste. And the repair is always the same family: define the population, list it, and let chance choose — a random draw from the register, or every twentieth name. When a question asks you to improve a survey, name the specific bias first and then the specific repair; a repair without a diagnosis is half marks at best.

**Bianca:** This one caused an argument at the tally table: why do the intervals say 'R20 to under R40' instead of just 'R20 to R40'? It feels like fussy wording for the same thing.

**Tutor:** It is the difference between a table that works and a table that double-counts, and the test case is a single learner: the one who spends exactly R40. With sloppy intervals — "R20 to R40" followed by "R40 to R60" — that learner qualifies for both rows. Two markers tallying the same slips would produce different tables, and the frequencies might sum to 61 with someone counted twice, or the learner might be dropped entirely while each tallier assumes the other row took them. "R20 to under R40" closes the door cleanly: R39,99 lives here, R40,00 lives in the next row, and no value on earth has two homes or none. That is also why your first act after tallying is the total check — 8 plus 19 plus 21 plus 7 plus 5 must hit exactly 60, and if it hits 59 or 61, an overlap or a gap in your intervals is the first suspect. The principle has a name worth using in an answer: intervals must be mutually exclusive — no value fits two — and exhaustive — every value fits one. Say those two words with the R40 example attached, and the wording mark and the reasoning mark both land.

**Bianca:** Last one: the misleading-graph questions feel like a trick — the graph IS the data, so how can it lie? People are not sure what the examiner wants them to actually do.

**Tutor:** The numbers are true and the impression is false — that is the entire trick, and the examiner wants you to separate the two out loud. The commonest weapon is the truncated axis: start the vertical scale at R35 instead of zero, and a bar for R40 stands five units tall while a bar for R36 stands one unit tall. The eye reads "five times more"; the data says "R4 apart, about ten percent." Your job in the answer is that exact translation: read the true values off the axis, state the honest difference, and name the device — "the vertical axis begins at R35, which exaggerates a R4 difference into a fivefold visual difference." The other regulars are worth a mental checklist. Uneven scale jumps, where gridlines step 10, 20, 50. Picture graphs where the image is scaled in height AND width, so doubling the value quadruples the visual area. Line graphs with missing months, squeezing time so a slow change looks sudden. A two-part exam question usually follows: explain how the graph misleads, then redraw or restate it honestly — and the honest restatement is always the same move, an axis from zero with equal steps, or a plain sentence quoting the real numbers. Data cannot defend itself; your quoting of the axes is the defence.
