# Part 1 — Expert

Every data question in this subject follows one story: somebody wanted to know something, asked people, wrote down the answers, squeezed them into a few honest numbers, and drew a picture. The curriculum calls the stages developing questions, collecting, classifying, summarising and representing — and it examines every stage, including the ways each one can quietly go wrong. This session follows one investigation from start to finish: a grade 11 class in Mahikeng studying what learners at their school spend on airtime and data each week, and whether the tuck shop should start selling data vouchers.

## Subtopic: The Question, the Population and the Sample

Research begins with a QUESTION sharp enough to be answered by counting or measuring. "Do learners spend too much on airtime?" cannot be answered — "too much" is an opinion. "How many rand does a learner at our school spend on airtime and data in a week?" can. Writing the sharp version is an examinable skill: the question must name the group, the quantity, and the unit.

The POPULATION is the entire group the question is about — all 1 240 learners at the school. Asking every one of them is a CENSUS, and it is accurate but slow and expensive. The alternative is a SAMPLE: a smaller group chosen to stand for the whole. The class surveys 60 learners.

Everything depends on HOW the 60 are chosen. A sample must be REPRESENTATIVE — a miniature of the population. Choosing 60 friends from grade 11 fails: seniors carry more pocket money than grade 8s, so the sample is BIASED, tilted away from the truth in a known direction. Better: a RANDOM sample, where every learner has an equal chance — drawing 60 names from the full register, or every twentieth name on an alphabetical list.

Then the collection INSTRUMENT: a questionnaire handed out, an interview conducted, an observation recorded, or existing records consulted. Questionnaires reach many people cheaply but suffer unanswered forms; interviews probe deeper but cost time and can lead the witness. Papers ask you to choose an instrument AND defend the choice in a sentence. The questions for this section are with you now; name the population, judge the sample, and hunt for the bias.

## Subtopic: Tallies, Frequencies and Sensible Groups

Sixty raw answers are a mess: 25, 40, 15, 60, 35, 20 and so on, in no order. The first organising tool is the TALLY: read each value once, make a stroke next to its group, bundle every fifth stroke across the previous four. The count of strokes for a group is its FREQUENCY, and the finished table is a FREQUENCY TABLE.

With rand amounts spread from R0 to R120, tallying every separate value is useless — nearly every value would score one. So the data is grouped into CLASS INTERVALS: R0 to under R20, R20 to under R40, R40 to under R60, and so on. The intervals must not overlap — a learner spending exactly R40 must have exactly one home, which is why "R20 to R40" followed by "R40 to R60" is an error the marker hunts for. Intervals should also be equal in width, and few enough to reveal a shape: five to eight groups is the working range.

The class's table comes out as: R0 to under R20, frequency 8; R20 to under R40, frequency 19; R40 to under R60, frequency 21; R60 to under R80, frequency 7; R80 and more, frequency 5. Check the total: 8 + 19 + 21 + 7 + 5 = 60 — every learner counted once. That check is a mark in itself.

The table already tells a story the raw list hid: spending clusters between R20 and R60, with a small tail of heavy spenders. But grouping has a price — the exact values are gone, and the table cannot say what the biggest single amount was. Naming that trade-off, detail exchanged for shape, is a favourite two-mark question. Your questions for this section are ready; tally carefully, check the total, and mind the interval boundaries.

## Subtopic: Mean, Median, Mode and the Story of the Range

A SUMMARY STATISTIC squeezes the whole dataset into one number, and grade 11 carries four. Take a small dataset first — nine learners' weekly spending in rand, sorted: 20, 25, 25, 30, 35, 40, 45, 50, 90.

The MEAN is the share-out value: add everything, divide by the count. The total is 360, so the mean is 360 ÷ 9 = R40. The MEDIAN is the middle value of the SORTED list: nine values, so the fifth — R35 — with four below and four above. The MODE is the most frequent value: R25, appearing twice. The RANGE measures spread, not centre: biggest minus smallest, 90 − 20 = R70.

Now the examinable insight: the mean and median disagree, R40 against R35, and the reason is the learner spending R90 — an OUTLIER, far above the rest. The mean is dragged upward by extreme values because every rand enters the addition; the median only cares about position, so the outlier moves it not at all. Remove the R90 and the mean falls to 270 ÷ 8 = R33,75, while the median barely shifts. So when a dataset carries outliers — salaries, house prices, spending with a few big spenders — the median is the honest "typical" value, and saying WHY is where the marks live.

Papers also run the machine backwards: if the mean of nine values is R40, the total must be 9 × 40 = R360, and a missing value can be found by subtracting the known eight from that total. Practise both directions. The questions for this section follow now; sort before finding the median, and name the outlier when the averages argue.

## Subtopic: Graphs That Tell the Truth — and Graphs That Lie

Data ends as a picture, and grade 11 must both draw and distrust them. Three forms carry the term. A MULTIPLE BAR GRAPH puts two bars side by side for each category — boys' and girls' average spending per grade — letting two datasets be compared category by category; it needs a key, a labelled axis and bars of equal width. A LINE GRAPH joins points over time and shows trend — average data price per gig falling month by month; a BROKEN-LINE graph is the same picture where the values jump rather than flow. A SCATTER PLOT places a dot for each person — airtime spending against pocket money — and reveals whether two quantities move together: dots climbing to the right suggest more pocket money goes with more spending.

Reading a graph is a three-step discipline: read the title and axis labels first, read the scale second — what one gridline is worth — and only then read the values. Most reading errors are scale errors.

Distrust is a listed skill, because graphs can be made to lie without a single false number. The classic: a vertical axis that starts at R35 instead of R0 makes a bar of R40 tower over a bar of R36, turning a R4 difference into a visual cliff. Watch for axes that skip numbers unevenly, pictures whose AREA grows when only the value grew, and line graphs with missing months squeezed together. The defence is always the same: read the numbers off the axes and quote them against the picture — "the difference is R4, though the graph makes it look triple."

The tuck shop verdict, honestly graphed, writes itself: spending clusters at R20 to R60 weekly, so a R29 voucher stocks well. The last questions of this part are in front of you now: read the scale before the bars, and check where the axis starts.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same survey from a desk in the Mahikeng classroom, one honest step at a time.

## Subtopic: Asking the Right People the Right Question

Everything starts with a question a calculator could answer. "Do learners waste money on data?" — no. Waste, according to whom? "How many rand a week does a learner here spend on airtime and data?" — yes. Group, quantity, unit: all named.

Who do you ask? The dream is everyone, all 1 240 learners — that is a census, and it is perfect and impossible before Friday. So you ask a sample, a small group standing in for the school, and here is the entire game: the small group must look like the school in miniature. All grades, both sexes, hostel kids and day kids.

The easy mistake is asking whoever is nearby — your own grade 11 friends at break. Grade 11s carry more money than grade 8s, so your answers all lean rich. That lean has a name: bias — the sample whispering a different answer than the population would. The cure is randomness: pull 60 names out of the full register, or take every twentieth learner on the alphabetical list. Random means every learner had an equal chance of being picked, and no one's convenience chose for them.

Then decide how to ask. A questionnaire: cheap, reaches all 60, but some forms come back blank or joking. An interview: you can explain the question, but it eats time and your tone can nudge answers. Pick the tool AND say the reason — that sentence is the mark. Some questions on this section are with you right now: find who was asked, and ask who was left out.

## Subtopic: Sixty Answers into One Small Table

Sixty slips of paper come back and it is chaos — R25, R40, R15, R60, R35 — no order, no story. The tally table is how you eat the chaos one bite at a time.

Since the amounts run from nothing to R120, you do not tally each amount — you build bins. R0 to under R20. R20 to under R40. R40 to under R60. R60 to under R80. R80 and up. Then read each slip ONCE, stroke the right bin, and bundle every fifth stroke across the four before it, so the counting later is counting bundles of five.

Two rules keep the bins honest. Every amount must have exactly one home — that is why the bins say "to under R40" and the next one starts AT R40; write "R20 to R40" and "R40 to R60" and the R40 spender lives in two houses, which is the error markers love to find. And keep the bins the same width, like equal cups in a cupboard.

The class's counts came out 8, 19, 21, 7 and 5. First move, always: add them — 60. All sixty slips counted, none dropped, none counted twice. That little addition is a mark on the paper.

And now the table talks. Most of the school spends between R20 and R60 a week; a handful spend over R80. You could not see that in the pile of slips. The price you paid: the exact numbers are gone — the table knows five people spent R80 or more, but not that one of them spent R120. Shape gained, detail lost; say both halves when asked. Questions on this section are coming your way now — bins that do not overlap, and a total that hits 60.

## Subtopic: Three Middles, One Spread, and a Graph with a Trick

Nine friends, sorted spending: 20, 25, 25, 30, 35, 40, 45, 50, 90. Three different "typical" numbers hide in there.

The mean is the share-out: pool all the money — R360 — and share it equally: R40 each. The median is the queue: line everyone up in order and tap the middle shoulder — the fifth person, R35. The mode is the crowd's favourite: R25, the only amount appearing twice. And the range is not a middle at all — it is the gap from smallest to biggest, 90 minus 20, R70 wide.

Why do mean and median disagree? Look at the R90 friend. The mean shook hands with that R90 — every rand goes into the pot, so one big spender pulls the share-out up. The median never met the R90; it only counts positions in the queue, and the queue's middle does not care how rich the last person is. So with a big outlier, the median tells the truer "typical" story — and WRITING that reason, not just the numbers, is where the marks sit.

Last stop: the pictures. Bars side by side compare groups; a line shows a story over time; scattered dots show whether two things rise together. Before trusting any of them, do the axis check: where does the vertical axis START? A graph starting at R35 makes R40 tower over R36 like a skyscraper over a shack — a R4 difference dressed up as a landslide. Read the numbers off the axis and say them out loud; the picture cannot lie to someone who quotes it. The final questions of the lesson are with you now: sort, share, queue, and check the bottom of every axis before you believe the top.
