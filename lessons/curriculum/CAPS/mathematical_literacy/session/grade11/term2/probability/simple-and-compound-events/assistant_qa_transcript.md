**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! The first one comes up every year: if the taxi was full on 12 of 40 mornings, is 0,3 the actual probability or just a guess? People are unsure what to call it in an answer.

**Tutor:** Call it an estimate, and be proud of the word — that precision is exactly what the marker is listening for. The true probability of a full taxi is a number nobody knows; it lives in the tangle of timetables, weather and month-end pay days. What Nomsa holds after forty mornings is a measurement of it: a relative frequency of 12 over 40, which is 0,3, and the best available estimate of the truth. The distinction matters because the two behave differently. A theoretical probability is fixed — a fair coin's half never moves. A relative frequency wobbles from sample to sample: watch a different forty mornings and you might get 11 over 40 or 14 over 40 without anything about the taxi changing. The law that rescues us says the wobble shrinks as trials grow — over four hundred mornings the estimate would settle much closer to the truth than over forty. So a full sentence in an exam reads: "the relative frequency is 0,3; this estimates the probability of a full taxi, and the estimate becomes more reliable as more mornings are recorded." Estimate, not guess — a guess ignores data; an estimate is built from it.

**Bianca:** Next: in the two-coin game, several people argued that there are three outcomes — two heads, two tails, and one of each — so each should be a third. The tree says otherwise, but the three-outcome logic feels convincing. Where exactly does it break?

**Tutor:** It breaks at the assumption that the three descriptions are equally likely — they are not, because one of them is secretly two outcomes wearing one name. Run the tree honestly: the first toss branches heads or tails, the second branches again, and the four end-points — heads-heads, heads-tails, tails-heads, tails-tails — each carry a half times a half, a quarter. Now look at the description "one of each": it covers BOTH heads-tails AND tails-heads, two distinct routes through the game, so it collects two quarters and weighs a half, while two heads and two tails weigh a quarter each. The three-outcome argument flattens that hidden doubling, and it is persuasive precisely because the language of "one of each" hides the order. The order matters even when it does not seem to: the coin tossed first and the coin tossed second are different events, and heads-then-tails is as real and separate a history as tails-then-heads. This is the deepest service the tree diagram performs — it refuses to let outcomes merge just because our words merge them. Whenever a compound question tempts you to list outcomes in your head, draw the tree and count the end-points; if your mental list is shorter than the tree's, the tree is right.

**Bianca:** This one caused genuine confusion in the two-way table: the difference between "the probability that a visitor is a learner who came by taxi" and "the probability that a learner came by taxi". They sound almost identical, but the answers were 28 over 120 and 28 over 70. How do we hear the difference?

**Tutor:** Listen for the size of the world the question is drawing from. "A visitor who is a learner AND came by taxi" starts from the whole gate — all 120 visitors are candidates, and you want the ones landing in that single cell: 28 over 120, about 0,23. But "of the learners, how many came by taxi" — or "given that the visitor is a learner" — has already told you something before asking anything: the person in front of you IS a learner. The 50 adults have left the problem entirely; the world has shrunk to 70 people, and within that smaller world, 28 took taxis: 28 over 70, which is 0,4. The numerator is the same cell both times; only the denominator changed, because the starting information changed. The reliable technique is to underline the conditioning phrase — "given that", "of the", "among the" — and ask: who is still in the room after this phrase? Everyone still in the room forms the denominator. And when no phrase shrinks the room, the grand total stands. One more practical habit: always complete every row and column total before answering anything, because a conditional question needs the row total, and computing it mid-question under time pressure is where arithmetic slips happen.

**Bianca:** Last one: the complement rule — 1 minus the probability. People know it exists but never spot WHEN to use it, and end up adding three branches instead.

**Tutor:** The signal phrase is "at least one", and it should trigger the complement almost automatically. "At least one head in two tosses" means one head or two heads — three of the four routes, and adding them, a quarter plus a quarter plus a quarter, does work: three quarters. But watch how the complement does it in one line: the ONLY way to fail "at least one head" is no heads at all — tails-tails, one route, a quarter — so the answer is 1 minus a quarter, three quarters, no adding required. Two tosses barely rewards the shortcut; the exam question with a bigger tree, or the tuck-shop style with unequal probabilities, rewards it heavily. The chance of pies running out at least once in two days: direct addition needs three routes with different weights — 0,2 times 0,8, plus 0,8 times 0,2, plus 0,2 times 0,2 — while the complement takes the single all-clear route, 0,8 times 0,8 equals 0,64, and subtracts: 1 minus 0,64 is 0,36. Same answer, a third of the working, a third of the slip opportunities. The discipline is to define the complement in words first — "the opposite of at least one is NONE" — because the classic error is subtracting the wrong thing. And keep the sanity check running: your answer and your complement must add to exactly 1, or one of them is broken.
