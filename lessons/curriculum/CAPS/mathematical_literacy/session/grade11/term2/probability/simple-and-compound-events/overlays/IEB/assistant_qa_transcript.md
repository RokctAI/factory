**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! The first one comes up every year: if the minibus was full on 18 of 50 mornings, is 0,36 the actual probability or just a guess? People are unsure what to call it in an answer.

**Tutor:** Call it an estimate, and use the word with confidence — that precision is exactly what a marker listens for. The true probability of a full minibus is a number nobody knows; it hides in the tangle of timetables, weather and month-end pay days. What Karabo holds after fifty mornings is a measurement of it: a relative frequency of 18 over 50, which is 0,36, and the best available estimate of the truth. The distinction matters because the two behave differently. A theoretical probability is fixed — a fair coin's half never moves. A relative frequency wobbles from sample to sample: log a different fifty mornings and you might get 16 over 50 or 20 over 50 without anything about the minibus changing. The law that rescues us says the wobble shrinks as trials grow — over five hundred mornings the estimate would settle far closer to the truth than over fifty. So a full sentence in an assessment reads: "the relative frequency is 0,36; this estimates the probability of a full minibus, and the estimate becomes more reliable as more mornings are recorded." Estimate, not guess — a guess ignores data; an estimate is built from it.

**Bianca:** Next: in the two-coin game, a few people insisted there are three outcomes — two tails, two heads, and one of each — so each should be a third. The tree disagrees, but the three-outcome logic feels convincing. Where exactly does it break?

**Tutor:** It breaks at the assumption that the three descriptions are equally likely — they are not, because one of them is secretly two outcomes sharing a name. Run the tree honestly: the first toss branches heads or tails, the second branches again, and the four end-points — heads-heads, heads-tails, tails-heads, tails-tails — each carry a half times a half, a quarter. Now inspect the description "one of each": it covers BOTH heads-tails AND tails-heads, two distinct journeys through the game, so it collects two quarters and weighs a half, while two heads and two tails weigh a quarter apiece. The three-outcome argument flattens that hidden doubling, and it persuades precisely because the phrase "one of each" erases the order. Order matters even when it seems not to: the coin tossed first and the coin tossed second are separate events, and heads-then-tails is as real and distinct a history as tails-then-heads. This is the deepest service the tree performs — it refuses to let outcomes merge just because our words merge them. Whenever a compound question tempts you to list outcomes mentally, draw the tree and count the end-points; if your mental list is shorter than the tree's, the tree wins.

**Bianca:** This one caused real confusion in the two-way table: the difference between "the probability that a visitor is a learner who came by minibus" and "the probability that a learner came by minibus". They sound nearly identical, but the answers were 36 over 150 and 36 over 90. How do we hear the difference?

**Tutor:** Listen for the size of the world the question draws from. "A visitor who is a learner AND came by minibus" starts at the whole gate — all 150 visitors are candidates, and you want the ones inside that single cell: 36 over 150, which is 0,24. But "of the learners, how many came by minibus" — or "given that the visitor is a learner" — has already told you something before asking anything: the person in front of you IS a learner. The 60 adults have left the problem entirely; the world has shrunk to 90 people, and within that smaller world, 36 rode: 36 over 90, which is 0,4. The numerator is the same cell both times; only the denominator changed, because the starting information changed. The dependable technique is to underline the conditioning phrase — "given that", "of the", "among the" — and ask: who is still in the room after this phrase? Whoever remains forms the denominator. When no phrase shrinks the room, the grand total stands. One more practical habit: complete every row and column total before answering anything, because a conditional question needs the row total, and computing it mid-question under time pressure is where arithmetic slips breed.

**Bianca:** Last one: the complement rule — 1 minus the probability. People know it exists but never spot WHEN to reach for it, and end up adding three branches instead.

**Tutor:** The signal phrase is "at least one", and it should fire the complement almost by reflex. "At least one tail in two tosses" means one tail or two tails — three of the four journeys, and adding them, a quarter plus a quarter plus a quarter, does work: three quarters. But watch the complement do it in one line: the ONLY way to fail "at least one tail" is no tails at all — heads-heads, one journey, a quarter — so the answer is 1 minus a quarter, three quarters, no adding required. Two tosses barely rewards the shortcut; a bigger tree, or the food-stand style with unequal probabilities, rewards it enormously. The chance of the boerewors rolls selling out at least once in two days: direct addition needs three journeys with different weights — 0,1 times 0,9, plus 0,9 times 0,1, plus 0,1 times 0,1 — while the complement takes the single all-clear journey, 0,9 times 0,9 equals 0,81, and subtracts: 1 minus 0,81 is 0,19. Same answer, a third of the working, a third of the slip opportunities. The discipline is to define the complement in words first — "the opposite of at least one is NONE" — because the classic error is subtracting the wrong thing. And keep the sanity check alive: your answer and its complement must add to exactly 1, or one of them is broken.
