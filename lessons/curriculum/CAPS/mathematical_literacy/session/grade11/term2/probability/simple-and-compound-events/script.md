# Part 1 — Expert

Chance runs through ordinary South African life: whether the morning taxi is full, whether load shedding hits during study time, whether the lucky draw at the school fete pays for the ticket. Probability is the mathematics of that uncertainty, and grade 11 asks for four things: the scale that measures chance, the difference between watching outcomes and calculating them, and the two machines for handling double events — tree diagrams and two-way tables. The setting is one school fete in Kimberley, with a coin game, a lucky-draw spinner, and a survey of who comes to the fete by taxi.

## Subtopic: The Scale from Impossible to Certain

An OUTCOME is a single possible result of an activity: a coin landing heads, a die showing 4. An EVENT is the collection of outcomes you care about: "an even number" bundles the outcomes 2, 4 and 6. PROBABILITY measures how likely the event is, on a scale that runs from 0 to 1 and nowhere beyond.

Zero means IMPOSSIBLE — rolling a 7 on an ordinary die. One means CERTAIN — rolling a number between 1 and 6. Everything real lives between: 0,5 is an even chance, below 0,5 unlikely, above 0,5 likely. The same probability wears three outfits — fraction, decimal, percentage: a half, 0,5, 50% — and papers switch outfits mid-question, so converting must be automatic.

For equally likely outcomes, theoretical probability is a ratio: the number of outcomes in the event, divided by the total number of possible outcomes. A die has six faces, three of them even, so the probability of even is three sixths — a half. The fete's spinner has eight equal sectors, one marked WIN, so the chance of winning is one eighth: 0,125, or 12,5%.

Two disciplines anchor the topic. Probabilities above 1 or below 0 are impossible — an answer of 1,2 or a negative is always wrong, no matter the working. And the probability of an event NOT happening is 1 minus the probability that it does: the spinner loses with probability seven eighths, because one eighth and seven eighths must total exactly 1. The questions for this section are with you now; place every answer on the 0-to-1 scale and check it belongs there.

## Subtopic: Watching versus Calculating: Relative Frequency

Theoretical probability is calculated before anything happens, from the symmetry of the situation: a fair coin gives heads a half, because two equally likely faces share the certainty. But taxis, weather and load shedding have no symmetry to consult — for them, probability is estimated by WATCHING.

RELATIVE FREQUENCY is the fraction of trials in which the event actually occurred: the number of times it happened, divided by the number of trials. Nomsa records the 07:00 taxi for 40 school mornings; it arrives full on 12 of them. The relative frequency of a full taxi is 12 over 40 = 0,3, and 0,3 becomes her working estimate of tomorrow's risk.

Relative frequency wobbles. Ten tosses of a fair coin can give seven heads — relative frequency 0,7 — without the coin being crooked. The law behind the topic says: as the number of trials grows, relative frequency settles toward the true probability. A thousand tosses landing 700 heads is evidence of a crooked coin; ten tosses landing 7 is Tuesday. So more trials mean a better estimate, and an exam answer comparing the two must say so.

The two ideas divide the world. Symmetric hardware — coins, dice, spinners, cards — takes theoretical probability. Real-world processes — rain, breakdowns, borrowed library books returned late — take relative frequency from collected data. The classic exam question hands you a coin tossed 200 times with 92 heads and asks for both numbers: theoretical 0,5, relative frequency 92 over 200 = 0,46, and a sentence saying the coin looks fair because 0,46 sits close to 0,5 and 200 tosses is a decent test. Your questions for this section are ready; decide first whether you are calculating from symmetry or estimating from data.

## Subtopic: Tree Diagrams: Chance in Two Steps

A COMPOUND event is two simple events in sequence: two coin tosses, a spinner spun twice, a child's route chosen then the weather happening. The TREE DIAGRAM is the machine that lists every combined outcome without missing one.

Build the fete's coin game: toss a coin twice, win a sweet for two heads. From the start, two branches — heads and tails, each carrying probability a half. From EACH of those, two more branches for the second toss, again a half each. The tree now has four end-points: heads-heads, heads-tails, tails-heads, tails-tails — four equally likely routes through the game.

Read events off the ends. Two heads is one route of four: probability one quarter. Exactly one head is two routes — heads-tails and tails-heads — so two quarters, a half. At least one head is three routes, three quarters; and the shortcut runs through the complement, 1 minus the probability of no heads at all: 1 minus a quarter, three quarters again.

The multiplication rule powers unequal trees too: multiply along the branches. The tuck shop runs out of pies one day in five — probability 0,2 — and the days behave independently. Two days running without pies: 0,8 × 0,8 = 0,64. No pies both days: 0,2 × 0,2 = 0,04. Ruled out by neither: the remaining 0,32, which is one day yes, one day no — and 0,64 + 0,04 + 0,32 = 1, the check that the tree caught everything.

Draw the tree even when the answer seems obvious; the diagram earns method marks, and the missed outcome — tails-heads, forgotten by everyone counting in their head — is exactly what it exists to catch. The questions for this section follow now; label every branch with its probability and multiply along the route.

## Subtopic: Two-Way Tables: Chance in a Grid

When each person carries TWO facts at once — gender and transport, grade and cellphone brand — the TWO-WAY TABLE organises the crowd into a grid, and probability becomes reading the grid.

The fete survey asked 120 visitors two questions: learner or adult, and arrived by taxi or on foot. The table: learners who came by taxi, 28; learners on foot, 42; adults by taxi, 20; adults on foot, 30. Row totals: 70 learners, 50 adults. Column totals: 48 by taxi, 72 on foot. Grand total: 120 — and every row and column must add to its total, which is the built-in check the examiner expects you to run.

Now the grid answers questions. The probability that a random visitor is a learner: 70 over 120, which simplifies to seven twelfths, about 0,58. That a random visitor came by taxi AND is an adult: the single cell 20, over the grand total 120 — one sixth. Compound events read straight from cells; no tree needed.

The subtle question is the CONDITIONAL one: given that a visitor is a learner, what is the chance they came by taxi? The words "given that" shrink the world: only the 70 learners exist now, and 28 of them took taxis, so 28 over 70 = 0,4. Compare that with taxi use among adults: 20 over 50 = 0,4 as well — identical rates, which is itself an exam-worthy observation: transport habit is independent of being a learner or adult in this crowd.

The whole skill is choosing the right denominator: the grand total for "a random visitor", a row or column total when the question says "given" or "of the learners". Read the words, pick the world, then divide. The last questions of this part are with you now: totals first, cells second, denominator chosen by the wording.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same fete from ground level, where chance is a coin in your hand and a crowd walking through a gate.

## Subtopic: How Sure Is Sure

Every chance in the world fits on one short ruler: from 0 — never — to 1 — definitely. The sun rising sits at 1. Rolling a 7 on a normal die sits at 0. Everything else lives in between, and the middle, 0,5, is the coin toss: could go either way.

The same chance speaks three languages: one half, 0,5, and 50% are the same statement in fraction, decimal and percent. The fete spinner has eight equal slices with one WIN: the chance is one out of eight — one eighth, 0,125, 12,5%. All the same size of hope.

Counting chances, when everything is equally likely, is just counting twice: how many ways can you win, out of how many ways in total? Even number on a die: three faces out of six — a half. Picking a red sweet blind from a packet of 3 red and 9 green: 3 out of 12 — a quarter.

Two rules keep answers legal. Nothing is ever more certain than certain: a probability of 1,2 is not a big chance, it is a broken answer. And happening plus not-happening always fills the whole ruler: if WIN is one eighth, LOSE is seven eighths, because together they must make exactly 1. Some questions on this section are with you right now — count the ways, count the total, and make sure your answer fits on the ruler.

## Subtopic: The Taxi You Watched for Forty Mornings

A coin tells you its chances before you toss it: two faces, same weight, a half each. The 07:00 taxi tells you nothing — there is no rulebook for taxis. So you watch.

Nomsa watched for 40 school mornings and counted 12 mornings when the taxi arrived full. Her estimate of the chance: 12 out of 40, which is 0,3 — about a thirty percent risk of standing at a full taxi's door. Watching, counting, dividing: that is relative frequency, and it is how the real world's chances get measured — rain, breakdowns, late deliveries, all of it.

But hold the estimate loosely when the watching was short. Toss a fair coin ten times and seven heads is nothing strange — the coin is not crooked, the sample is just small. Toss it a thousand times and seven hundred heads means something is wrong with that coin. The rule: the longer you watch, the closer your count creeps to the truth. Forty mornings is a decent watch; four mornings would be a rumour.

So when a question hands you both worlds — a coin tossed 200 times showing 92 heads — give both numbers and let them talk: the theory says 0,5, the watching says 0,46, and the sentence that earns the mark says they sit close together, so the coin behaves fairly and 200 tosses is enough of a test to take seriously. Questions on this section are coming your way now — theory for coins and spinners, watching for taxis and weather, and always say which one you used.

## Subtopic: Branches and Grids: Handling Two Things at Once

Two events at once is where heads start spinning — so stop counting in your head and build one of two pictures.

Picture one: the tree, for things happening one after another. The coin game: first toss branches into heads and tails, a half each. Each branch splits again for the second toss. Four paths through: heads-heads, heads-tails, tails-heads, tails-tails, each a quarter — multiply along the path, a half times a half. Two heads wins the sweet: one path, one quarter. Exactly one head: TWO paths — and there is the trap. Counting in your head, you find heads-tails and forget tails-heads; the tree puts both in front of your eyes, and that forgotten branch is exactly the mark everyone else drops.

Picture two: the grid, for a crowd where everyone carries two labels. At the gate, 120 visitors: learner or adult, taxi or foot. Fill the four cells — 28 learner-taxi, 42 learner-foot, 20 adult-taxi, 30 adult-foot — and totals: 70 learners, 50 adults, 48 taxi, 72 foot, 120 all told. Rows and columns must add up; if they refuse, a cell is wrong, and finding it now is free.

Then every question is a division, and the wording picks the bottom number. "A random visitor is a learner": 70 out of 120. "A random visitor is an adult who came by taxi": that one cell, 20 out of 120. But "GIVEN that a visitor is a learner, did they taxi?" — the words shrink the world to the 70 learners only: 28 out of 70, which is 0,4. See "given that" or "of the learners", and the denominator stops being 120.

Paths for sequences, cells for crowds, and the denominator chosen by the words. The final questions of the lesson are with you now — draw the picture first, and let it do the counting for you.
