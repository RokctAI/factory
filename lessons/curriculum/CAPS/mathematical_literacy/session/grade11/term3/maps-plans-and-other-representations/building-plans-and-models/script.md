# Part 1 — Expert

A house begins as a sheet of paper: a floor plan drawn to scale, elevation drawings of each wall, and a builder who must turn millimetres on paper into bricks, flooring and skirting bought in rands. Grade 11 asks for the whole journey — reading the plan's symbols, converting plan measurements to real ones, costing the materials, and finally packing real objects into real boxes, which is where models come in. This session works through one RDP-style house extension in Kimberley: a new bedroom drawn at 1 : 100, its elevations, its finishes, and the spaza-shop packing problem that ends the term.

## Subtopic: Reading the Floor Plan

A FLOOR PLAN is the view from directly above, as if the roof were lifted off: walls appear as thick double lines, and the space between them is the room. The plan speaks in symbols, and the curriculum expects them read fluently. A door is a gap in the wall with a quarter-circle showing which way it swings — and the swing matters, because a door opening inward eats floor space where a bed cannot stand. A window is a thin triple line within the wall. Fittings — bath, toilet, basin, stove — appear as outline shapes seen from above.

The plan's scale is 1 : 100 — every millimetre on paper is 100 mm of built house, which makes the arithmetic kind: one millimetre becomes one hundred millimetres, so measurements effectively shift one unit — 35 mm on the plan is 3 500 mm, which is 3,5 m.

Measure the new bedroom on the plan: 35 mm by 30 mm. Real size: 3,5 m by 3,0 m. The door gap measures 9 mm — a 900 mm door, the standard. The window is 15 mm wide on the plan: 1,5 m.

Two disciplines protect plan marks. Measure between the correct lines — inside wall face to inside wall face for a room's usable size, outside to outside for the building's footprint; papers ask for both and the difference is the wall thickness. And always write the plan measurement AND the real measurement, joined by the scale, so every conversion shows its working: 35 mm × 100 = 3 500 mm = 3,5 m. The questions for this section are with you now; read the symbols first, then the scale, then measure.

## Subtopic: Elevations: the House from the Side

The floor plan is the view from above; an ELEVATION is the view from the side — what you would see standing in the street or the yard, one flat face of the building at a time. Elevations are named by compass direction: the NORTH elevation is the face that LOOKS toward the north — the face a person standing to the north of the house sees.

Elevations carry what the floor plan cannot: heights. Wall height to the eaves, the roof's pitch, the height of windows off the floor, the chimney. The bedroom extension's elevation shows walls 2,6 m high and a window sill at 900 mm. The floor plan, meanwhile, holds what elevations cannot: internal layout, room sizes, door swings. Matching the two is the examinable skill — the paper shows a floor plan and four elevations and asks which elevation is which.

The matching method is systematic. Take a direction, say north. On the floor plan, find which rooms and openings touch the north side. List them left to right AS SEEN FROM THE NORTH — and mind the mirror: looking at the north face from outside, the plan's west end appears on your RIGHT if north is up the page and you are facing south toward the house. Then find the elevation whose windows and doors sit in that order and spacing. Count openings first — a face with two windows and a door cannot match an elevation showing one window — then check widths against the plan's scale, then heights for reasonableness.

One habit settles most matching questions: the tallest features. A double-storey section, a chimney, a lean-to roof — locate them on the plan, and the elevation showing them on the correct side identifies itself. Your questions for this section are ready; name the direction, list the openings in order, and respect the mirror.

## Subtopic: From Plan to Quantities and Cost

The builder prices the bedroom from the plan, and every price begins with a converted measurement.

Flooring is area. The room is 3,5 m by 3,0 m: 10,5 m². Laminate flooring sells in boxes covering 2,2 m² at R379 a box. With 10% extra for cutting waste: 10,5 × 1,10 = 11,55 m², and 11,55 ÷ 2,2 = 5,25 boxes — round UP to 6 boxes, costing 6 × 379 = R2 274.

Skirting is perimeter. The room's perimeter is 2 × (3,5 + 3,0) = 13 m, but the 900 mm doorway needs no skirting: 13 − 0,9 = 12,1 m. Skirting comes in 3 m lengths at R89 each: 12,1 ÷ 3 = 4,03 lengths — 5 lengths must be bought, R445, and the offcuts are the price of reality.

Paint is wall area. Perimeter times height: 13 × 2,6 = 33,8 m². Subtract the door, 0,9 × 2,0 = 1,8 m², and the window, 1,5 × 1,2 = 1,8 m²: 33,8 − 3,6 = 30,2 m². Two coats doubles it to 60,4 m²; at 8 m² per litre, 7,55 ℓ, so two 5 ℓ tins at R465: R930.

The pattern is always the same three-step chain: plan measurement to real measurement via the scale; real measurement to quantity via area, perimeter or coverage; quantity to rands via pack size, always rounding up whatever is sold in wholes. Total the bedroom honestly: flooring 2 274, skirting 445, paint 930 — R3 649 before labour. And check each number against sense: a bedroom needing 60 boxes of flooring or 0,6 of a box means a scale conversion slipped by a factor of ten. The questions for this section follow now; scale, quantity, pack, in that order.

## Subtopic: Models and Packaging: Cans into Boxes

A MODEL is a real or imagined three-dimensional object standing for another — and the term's packaging work is modelling at its most practical: how many cans fit in a box, and how much space is wasted?

The spaza shop stocks cans 7 cm in diameter and 11 cm tall, packed into a carton measuring 42 cm by 28 cm by 22 cm. Cans cannot be squashed or tilted, so the packing is counting, not dividing volumes. Along the 42 cm length: 42 ÷ 7 = 6 cans. Across the 28 cm width: 28 ÷ 7 = 4 cans. So one layer holds 6 × 4 = 24 cans. Upward, each can needs 11 cm: 22 ÷ 11 = 2 layers. The carton holds 24 × 2 = 48 cans.

Now the honest measure of the packing: wasted space. The carton's volume is 42 × 28 × 22 = 25 872 cm³. Each can — a cylinder — has volume π × radius² × height = 3,14 × 3,5² × 11 = 423,12 cm³, so 48 cans occupy 48 × 423,12 = 20 309,76 cm³. The air between the cans is 25 872 − 20 309,76 = 5 562,24 cm³ — about 21,5% of the carton, the unavoidable cost of packing circles into rectangles.

Division must respect wholeness. A carton 24 cm tall would give 24 ÷ 11 = 2,18 — still 2 layers, because 0,18 of a can is spilled cooldrink. Always FLOOR each dimension's division — round down — then multiply the three counts. And test orientation: lying the cans on their sides changes every division, and papers ask whether standing or lying packs more.

The same reasoning prices packaging decisions: a box that fits the cans snugly — 42 by 28 by 22 exactly — uses less cardboard than a generous one, and cardboard is area: the surface area of the closed carton is 2 × (42×28 + 42×22 + 28×22) = 5 432 cm². Volume for what fits, surface area for what wraps. The last questions of this part are with you now: count per dimension, round down per dimension, and keep volume and surface area in their separate jobs.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same extension from the builder's bakkie, plan folded in a back pocket.

## Subtopic: The Roof Comes Off

A floor plan is the house with its roof lifted off, seen from a drone directly overhead. Thick double lines are walls. A gap in the wall with a little quarter-circle is a door — the circle is the swing, showing where the door sweeps and where the bed therefore cannot go. Thin triple lines inside a wall: a window. The little shapes — a bath from above, a stove from above — are exactly what they look like.

The magic number on this plan is 1 : 100. Every millimetre of paper is a hundred millimetres of house. And a hundred is the friendliest scale there is: measure in millimetres, add two zeros, and you have real millimetres — 35 mm on paper is 3 500 mm in brick, which is 3,5 m. Slide the units, done.

So walk the plan with a ruler. Bedroom: 35 mm by 30 mm — 3,5 m by 3 m. Door gap: 9 mm — 900 mm, the standard door. Window: 15 mm — 1,5 m.

One trap: which lines you measure between. Inside face to inside face gives the room you can actually use; outside to outside gives the building's footprint, walls included. The difference IS the walls, and the paper likes asking for both to see if you know they differ. Say which one you measured, every time. Some questions on this section are with you right now — symbols, scale, then the ruler.

## Subtopic: Four Faces of One House

The plan shows the house from above; the elevations show it from the sides — four portraits, one per compass direction. The north elevation is the face a person standing on the north side sees. Elevations answer what the floor plan cannot: how TALL everything is — walls of 2,6 m, a window sill at 900 mm, the slope of the roof.

The exam's favourite game: here are four elevation drawings, which is which? Play it like a detective. Pick a face, say the north one. On the floor plan, find everything touching the north wall: one window, no door, and the kitchen's small window near the east corner. Now look for the elevation with exactly those openings in exactly that order. Count first — two openings cannot match three. Then check sizes with the scale. Then heights for sense.

And respect the mirror. Standing north of the house looking at it, you are facing south — so what sits on the plan's west side appears on your right, not your left. It feels backwards because it is backwards: you are looking at the house's face, and faces mirror. Sketch a little arrow of yourself standing outside, and the left-right question answers itself.

Fastest clue on any matching question: the tall landmarks. Chimney, double-storey block, lean-to. Find which side of the plan they live on; the elevation wearing them on the correct end names itself. Questions on this section are coming your way now — count the openings, mind the mirror, follow the chimney.

## Subtopic: Buying the Room, Packing the Van

Everything the builder buys comes off the plan through the same three moves: scale it, measure it, shop it.

The floor: 3,5 by 3 is 10,5 square metres. Add 10% for the cuts at the walls — 11,55. Boxes cover 2,2 each: 5,25 boxes, so buy 6 — R2 274 at R379 a box. The skirting: walk the walls, 13 m around, minus the 900 mm doorway — 12,1 m, sold in 3 m lengths, so 5 lengths, R445. The paint: walls are the walk-around times the height, 13 times 2,6 — 33,8 square metres — minus the door and window, 3,6 — leaves 30,2, doubled for two coats, 60,4. At 8 per litre that is 7,55 litres: two 5-litre tins, R930. Bedroom total: R3 649 before anyone lifts a brush. Round UP whenever the shop sells wholes, and write the reason.

Then the van pulls up at the spaza with the cans, and packing is not maths of volumes — it is counting cans that refuse to squash. Along the box: 42 divided by 7 is 6 cans. Across: 28 divided by 7 is 4. One layer: 24. Height: 22 divided by 11 is 2 layers. Forty-eight cans. If a division comes out 2,18, it is 2 — the spare 0,18 of a can exists only as air. Round DOWN in packing, per direction, before multiplying.

Want to know how much of the box is air? Box volume: 42 times 28 times 22 — 25 872 cubic centimetres. Each can: 3,14 times 3,5 squared times 11 — about 423. Forty-eight of them: 20 310. The difference — about 5 562, a fifth of the box — is the space circles always waste in rectangles. And when the question turns to cardboard, switch tools: volume measures what FITS, surface area measures what WRAPS. The final questions of the lesson are with you now — scale, quantity, pack for the room; count, floor, multiply for the box.
