**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! The first one comes up constantly: why is percentage profit worked out on the cost price and not the selling price? Both feel legitimate, and people lose marks guessing.

**Tutor:** Both are legitimate measures in the business world — they even have names, markup on cost and margin on sales — but they answer different questions, and the default in this subject is profit on cost unless the question explicitly says otherwise. The reasoning: percentage profit asks how hard your invested money worked. You put R6,00 into each roll; it returned R4,00 on top of itself; so your money earned 4,00 over 6,00, which is 66,7% — a statement about the return on what you risked. Dividing by the R10 selling price instead gives 40%, which answers a different question: what slice of each sale is profit. Same stall, same rands, two honest percentages — which is exactly why questions probe it, because a candidate who cannot name their base cannot be trusted with either. The protection is mechanical: write the fraction with words before numbers — profit over cost price — then substitute. And if a question ever asks you to work backwards — a trader wants 40% profit on a cost of R6,00, find the selling price — the same base discipline carries you: 40% of cost is R2,40, so the price is R8,40. Base first, arithmetic second.

***

**Bianca:** Next: in the break-even calculation, someone divided the R290 site fee by the R10 selling price, got 29, and called it 29 rolls to break even. It looks so reasonable — where is the flaw?

**Tutor:** The flaw is that the R10 coming in is not free money — R6,00 of every sale immediately leaves again to pay for the bread, the boerewors and the gas that made that very roll. Only the remainder, 10 minus 6,00, which is R4,00, is available to chip away at the site fee. Dividing 290 by the full R10 pretends the ingredients cost nothing, and it fails the honesty test you can run yourself: at 29 rolls sold, income is 29 times 10, R290 — but total cost is 290 plus 29 times 6,00, which is R464. The stall is R174 underwater at the point that method calls break-even. Divide instead by the contribution of R4,00 and you get 72,5, rounded up to 73, and the check confirms it: at 73, income of R730 against costs of R728 — just across the line. So chant the structure: fixed cost in the hole, contribution per unit doing the filling, divide the one by the other. And keep the two cost types separated from the very first line of your answer, because almost every break-even error begins with a fixed cost and a variable cost shaken together.

**Bianca:** This one always surfaces with the interest table: why do we build compound interest year by year in a table instead of using the formula older siblings talk about? It feels like doing it the slow way.

**Tutor:** It is the slow way, deliberately — at this level you calculate without formulae, and the table IS the understanding the formula later abbreviates. Each row forces the one insight that matters: this year's interest is calculated on this year's balance, not the original deposit. Watch it happen — 8 000 earns 480; 8 480 earns 508,80; 8 988,80 earns 539,33. The interest column climbs, and WHY it climbs is visible in your own working: the base grew. A formula delivers 9 528,13 in one keystroke and hides exactly that. There are practical reasons too: questions ask for the interest earned IN year two — R508,80 — or the balance AFTER year one, and a table holds every intermediate answer ready, while a formula user must recompute. Rounding is safer row by row: keep cents at each step and the final figure lands correctly. And the table generalises to the questions formulas handle badly at this level: a deposit added mid-way, a rate that changes after two years, a loan with a repayment in year two — each is just an extra line item in the right row. Build the table until it bores you; boredom is what mastery of compounding feels like at first.

**Bianca:** Last one: inflation questions that stretch over two or three years keep catching people — adding 5% plus 5% and multiplying by 1,10. Why is that wrong, and how wrong is it really?

**Tutor:** It is wrong because each year's rise stands on the shoulders of the last, exactly like the bank's staircase. In year one, the R2 400 basket climbs 5% of 2 400 — R120 — to R2 520. In year two, prices rise 5% again, but 5% OF THE NEW LEVEL: 5% of 2 520 is R126, taking the basket to R2 646. Adding the percentages instead — 10% of the original, R240 — lands on R2 640, undercounting by R6 because it charged the second year's rise on year-zero prices that no shop was charging anymore. The correct multiplier is 1,05 times 1,05, which is 1,1025 — effectively 10,25% over two years, not 10%. Over two years the gap is small change; over ten years at 5%, the true multiplier is about 1,63 against the added-up guess of 1,5, and the error is enormous — which is precisely why long-term planning, pensions and school-fee projections live or die on compounding done properly. For the exam, the habit is: one multiplication per year, chained — or equivalently, build an inflation table exactly like the interest table. And when the question runs downhill — a price DECREASING 10% then 10% — the same rule holds with multipliers of 0,9: two years gives 0,81 of the original, not 0,8. Percentages stack by multiplying, never by adding: that single sentence, remembered, is worth marks across three different topics.
