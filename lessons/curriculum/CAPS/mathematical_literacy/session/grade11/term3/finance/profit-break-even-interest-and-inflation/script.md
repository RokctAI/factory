# Part 1 — Expert

A business, however small, answers four money questions or dies: what does each item cost to make, at what price does it sell, how many must be sold before the venture stops losing, and what happens to the money afterwards — in the bank, against a loan, and against rising prices. This session runs those questions through one venture the curriculum loves: a school fundraising stall in Bloemfontein selling vetkoek, with the takings banked, part invested, and next year's plans priced against inflation.

## Subtopic: Cost Price, Selling Price and Percentage Profit

The COST PRICE of an item is what it costs to produce: materials, plus a fair share of the other spending the production causes. The stall's batch of 100 vetkoek uses flour at R95, cooking oil at R120, curried-mince filling at R185 and gas at R60 — total production cost 95 + 120 + 185 + 60 = R460, so the cost price per vetkoek is 460 ÷ 100 = R4,60.

The SELLING PRICE is what the customer pays, and it is a decision, not a calculation — but a decision informed by three numbers: the cost price, the prices of competitors, and what customers will bear. The stall chooses R8. PROFIT per item is selling price minus cost price: 8 − 4,60 = R3,40.

PERCENTAGE PROFIT expresses that margin against the cost price: profit divided by cost price, times 100. Here: 3,40 ÷ 4,60 × 100 = 73,9%. The denominator matters and is examinable: percentage profit is reckoned on COST, not on selling price — 3,40 against 8 would give 42,5%, a different and wrong figure for this definition. Always state the base you used.

Fundraisers and large organisations run the same arithmetic at scale, with two extra habits. They compare YEAR against YEAR: takings of R7 120 this year against R6 400 last year is an increase of 720 ÷ 6 400 × 100 = 11,25%. And they compare PROJECTED against ACTUAL: a budget that projected R500 for ingredients against an actual R460 spent shows a saving of R40, and writing one sentence on the difference — under budget, and why — is the analysis the paper wants. The questions for this section are with you now; keep cost price and selling price in separate columns and name the base of every percentage.

## Subtopic: Break-even: Where Loss Turns into Profit

Some costs do not care how many vetkoek are sold. The stall pays R300 for its pitch at the market — a FIXED COST, spent before the first customer arrives. The ingredients are VARIABLE COSTS: R4,60 more for every vetkoek made. Total cost for n vetkoek: 300 + 4,60 × n. Income at R8 each: 8 × n.

The BREAK-EVEN point is where income first equals total cost — where the stall has lost nothing and gained nothing. Set the two expressions equal: 8 × n = 300 + 4,60 × n. Each vetkoek contributes 8 − 4,60 = R3,40 toward the fixed cost, so the number needed is 300 ÷ 3,40 = 88,2. Round UP: 89 vetkoek, because at 88 the stall is still R0,80 short — income 704 against costs 704,80 — while at 89 it is R2,60 ahead.

On a graph the story is visible: the income line climbs from zero at R8 per unit; the cost line starts at R300 and climbs more gently at R4,60 per unit. They cross at the break-even point; left of the crossing the cost line rides higher and the gap is loss, right of it the income line rides higher and the gap is profit. Papers give the graph and ask you to read the crossing, the loss at a given quantity, or the profit at full capacity — all vertical gaps between the two lines.

Break-even also prices decisions. A cheaper pitch at R204 drops the break-even to 204 ÷ 3,40 = 60 vetkoek; raising the price to R9 lifts the contribution to R4,40 and drops it to 300 ÷ 4,40 = 68,2, so 69. Every change in price or cost moves the crossing, and saying WHICH way it moves is the understanding being tested. Your questions for this section are ready; identify fixed against variable first, and round break-even answers up with a reason.

## Subtopic: Interest and Banking, Year by Year

The stall banks its money, and grade 11 handles the bank's arithmetic WITHOUT formulae — building the growth year by year in a table, which is both the curriculum's requirement and the honest way to see what compounding is.

The fundraising committee invests R6 000 of profit in a FIXED DEPOSIT at 7% per year, interest added to the account each year. Year one: interest is 6 000 × 0,07 = R420, balance 6 420. Year two: the 7% now works on 6 420 — interest 449,40, balance 6 869,40. Year three: 7% of 6 869,40 is 480,86, balance R7 350,26. Each year's interest is larger than the last because the base grows: that is COMPOUND interest, interest earning interest.

Compare simple interest at the same rate: R420 a year, every year, totalling 1 260 over three years — final amount R7 260. Compounding beat it by 7 350,26 − 7 260 = R90,26. Small over three years; decisive over twenty.

The same table runs in reverse for a LOAN: borrow R6 000 at 7% compounded, repay nothing, and the debt walks the identical staircase — 6 420, 6 869,40, 7 350,26. What delights the saver terrifies the borrower.

Banking vocabulary completes the section. A SAVINGS account earns modest interest and allows withdrawals. A CHEQUE or CURRENT account is for daily transactions, earns little or nothing, and charges fees. A FIXED DEPOSIT locks money away for a term at a higher rate. A DEBIT account or card spends your own money; a CREDIT account or card spends the bank's, which must be repaid — with interest if late. Bank FEES — monthly account fees, per-transaction charges — are expenses in any budget the paper sets. The questions for this section follow now; build the table one year at a time and mark whose money grows.

## Subtopic: Inflation: When the Same Trolley Costs More

INFLATION is the general rise of prices over time, quoted as a percentage per year. Inflation of 6% means that what cost R100 a year ago typically costs R106 now — the same goods, the same trolley, more rand.

The calculation is a percentage increase. Next year's price = this year's price × (1 + rate). The stall's ingredient basket costs R1 850 today; at 6% inflation, next year it costs 1 850 × 1,06 = R1 961. The committee budgeting next year's fundraiser at this year's prices will be R111 short before a single vetkoek fries.

The reverse skill: given two prices, find the rate. A loaf of bread rose from R15,50 to R17,05: the increase is 1,55, and 1,55 ÷ 15,50 × 100 = 10% — the bread inflated by ten percent. The base is always the OLD price; dividing by the new one is the classic error and gives 9,1%, which is wrong.

Inflation compounds, exactly like the fixed deposit. Two years at 6% multiplies prices by 1,06 × 1,06 = 1,1236, not by 1,12: the R1 850 basket costs 1 850 × 1,1236 = R2 078,66 in two years, and adding 6% twice to the original — R2 072 — undercounts, because the second year's rise works on the first year's higher prices.

The sting in the tail connects the whole session: money in the bank grows, but so do the prices it must chase. A fixed deposit earning 7% while inflation runs 6% grows its BUYING power by only the sliver between them; savings under a mattress earning nothing LOSE 6% of their power each year. Rising prices are also why a rate of increase can be a rate of DECREASE in disguise — a price falling from R20 to R18 is a decrease of 2 ÷ 20 × 100 = 10%, same method, opposite direction. The last questions of this part are with you now: old price as the base, compound over multiple years, and one sentence on what the number means for the budget.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same vetkoek stall from behind the pan, where every number smells of oil and counts in singles.

## Subtopic: Pricing the Vetkoek Stall

Before the stall can charge anything, it must know what one vetkoek costs to make. Add up the whole batch: flour 95, oil 120, filling 185, gas 60 — R460 for 100 vetkoek. So each one costs R4,60 before it earns a cent. That is the cost price, and it is the floor: sell below it and every sale digs the hole deeper.

The selling price is your call. Too close to R4,60 and the profit is crumbs; too high and the queue walks to the next stall. The committee lands on R8 — under the tuck shop pie, over the cost. Each sale clears 8 minus 4,60: R3,40 of profit.

How good is R3,40? Measure it against what you spent, not what you charged: 3,40 out of 4,60 is 73,9%. That is the percentage profit, and the base matters — measured against the R8 selling price it would look like 42,5%, a different number answering a different question. The paper wants profit on COST unless it says otherwise; write the base next to the answer and no marker can quarrel.

And once the frying is done, the committee compares like a business: this year took R7 120 against last year's R6 400 — up 11,25%. The budget projected R500 of ingredients; the till says R460 — R40 under, because oil was on special. One sentence per comparison: what changed, which way, and by how much. Some questions on this section are with you right now — cost floor first, price decision second, percentage on the cost.

## Subtopic: How Many Before We Stop Losing

Here is the stall's uncomfortable truth at dawn: before one customer arrives, it is already R300 down — the pitch fee, paid whether the day goes well or badly. The first vetkoek sold does not make the stall rich; it makes the stall slightly less poor.

Watch the crawl back to zero. Each vetkoek sells for R8 but costs R4,60 to make, so each one pushes R3,40 against that R300 hole. How many pushes fill it? 300 divided by 3,40: 88,2. And here you round UP — at 88 sold, the stall has taken 704 but spent 704,80, still eighty cents down; the 89th vetkoek tips it R2,60 into profit. Number 89 is the break-even vetkoek: before it, every sale reduces a loss; after it, every sale IS profit, the full R3,40 a time.

Picture the graph like a race: the income line starts at zero and climbs R8 a step; the cost line starts at R300 up the wall and climbs R4,60 a step. Income climbs steeper, so it catches up — the crossing is break-even, and the gap between the lines at any point is that day's loss or profit. Read the gap, not the lines.

The crossing moves when the numbers move. Cheaper pitch at R204? Break-even falls to 60. Charge R9 instead of R8? Each sale pushes R4,40, and 69 vetkoek clears it. Every business decision — rent, price, recipe — is secretly a question about where the crossing sits. Questions on this section are coming your way now — fixed cost in the hole, contribution per item, divide, round up, and say why you rounded up.

## Subtopic: The Bank's Staircase and the Trolley That Grows

The fundraiser banked R6 000 in a fixed deposit at 7%, and the balance climbs a staircase. First step: 7% of 6 000 is R420 — balance 6 420. Second step: the bank pays 7% on the NEW balance, 6 420, which is R449,40 — balance 6 869,40. Third step: R480,86 — balance R7 350,26. Each step is taller than the last, because interest starts earning its own interest. That is compounding, and you build it exactly like this: one year at a time, in a table, no formula required.

Compare the flat staircase: simple interest pays R420 every year, full stop — R7 260 after three years. Compounding beat it by R90,26. Three years, small gap; twenty years, a canyon. And the staircase has a dark twin: borrow R6 000 at 7% compound and repay nothing, and your DEBT climbs the same steps — 6 420, 6 869,40, 7 350,26. The machine does not care whose money is in it.

Meanwhile, outside the bank, the trolley is climbing its own staircase. Inflation at 6% means this year's R1 850 basket of stall ingredients rings up at 1 850 times 1,06 next year: R1 961. Budget next year's fundraiser at this year's prices and you arrive R111 short. Two years out, the multiplier is 1,06 times 1,06 — 1,1236 — because the second rise stands on the first: R2 078,66.

Spot the rate from two prices by always dividing by the OLD one: bread from R15,50 to R17,05 is a rise of 1,55, and 1,55 over 15,50 is 10%. Old price on the bottom, every time. And put the two staircases side by side for the final truth: money earning 7% while prices climb 6% is only truly growing by the sliver between; cash in a drawer, earning nothing, quietly shrinks 6% a year in what it can buy. The final questions of the lesson are with you now — build the bank's table year by year, grow the trolley by 1,06, and check which staircase is climbing faster.
