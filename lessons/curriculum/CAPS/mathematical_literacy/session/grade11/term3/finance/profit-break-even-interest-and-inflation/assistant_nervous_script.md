Hey, it's Bianca — this topic sounds like a business degree, but it is four small machines, and each one runs on arithmetic you have done since primary school. Let me hand you the levers.

Machine one: pricing. Add up the batch costs, divide by the batch size — that is the cost price, your floor. Selling price minus cost price is profit per item, and percentage profit is profit over COST, times 100. Write the base next to the percentage and that mark is bolted down.

Machine two: break-even. Two kinds of cost, never shaken together: the fixed cost you owe before the first sale, and the variable cost riding inside each item. Each sale contributes selling price minus variable cost. Divide the fixed cost by the contribution — 300 over 3,40 gives 88,2 — and round UP with the reason written: at 88 you are still eighty cents short. The rounding sentence is a mark waiting for you.

Machine three: the interest staircase. No formulas at this level — a table, one year per row. Balance times the rate gives the row's interest; add it on; next row works on the NEW balance. That is all compound interest is: interest meeting its own children. The same table with the same steps is also a debt growing, so label whose money it is.

Machine four: inflation. Next year's price is this year's times 1,06. Two years is 1,06 times 1,06 — multiply, never add, because the second rise stands on the first. Finding the rate from two prices? The increase over the OLD price, always.

Cost floor, contribution, staircase, multiplier. Four levers, no mysteries. You've got this!
