**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! First one that comes up every single year: on the municipal account, is VAT worked out before or after the basic charge is added? People got different totals depending on the order.

**Tutor:** VAT comes last, and it covers everything the municipality is charging you for the service — the consumption charge and the basic charge together. So the order is fixed: consumption times tariff first, then add the basic charge to get the subtotal, and only then take fifteen percent of that whole subtotal. In our example, 320 units at R2,10 gave R672, the basic charge lifted it to R852, and the VAT of R127,80 was fifteen percent of the full R852, not of the R672 alone. If you take VAT on the consumption only and then add the basic charge afterwards, you have quietly left the basic charge untaxed and your total will be R27 short. A reliable habit is to draw a line under everything the supplier charges, write "subtotal" beside it, and let VAT operate on that line only. The one exception to watch for is a document that lists an amount already described as VAT inclusive — then the fifteen percent is already inside it, and adding VAT again is double taxation, which examiners love to catch.

**Bianca:** Next: the difference between fixed and variable tripped people up, because taxi fare feels completely fixed — it is the same R14,50 every single morning. Why does the lesson call it variable?

**Tutor:** Because the test is not whether the expense repeats, it is whether the month's total amount is guaranteed in advance. Rent is R3 800 whether the month has 28 days or 31, whether you are home or away — the landlord's number never moves, so it is fixed. The taxi costs R14,50 a trip, yes, but the number of trips is not promised to you: a public holiday removes two trips, a week of illness removes ten, a lift from a neighbour removes two more, and December's total looks nothing like March's. The amount you finally spend depends on how the month unfolds, and that dependency is what "variable" means. The same logic sorts income: a salary is fixed because the contract states the amount; wages are variable because the hours move; commission is variable because the sales move. And "occasional" is the third drawer, for things with no schedule at all — a uniform, a funeral contribution, a gift. In an exam, always give the reason with the label: "variable, because the amount depends on the number of trips actually taken." The label alone is usually only half the mark.

**Bianca:** This one caused an argument: for the fridge loan, someone worked out twelve percent of R8 000, got R960, and wrote that as the answer for the whole three-year loan. Where exactly does the "times three" come in, and why is it not doubling up?

**Tutor:** The R960 is the rent on the money for one year only. Simple interest charges that same rent again for every further year the debt stands, because the calculation always looks back at the original R8 000 and never at what has accumulated. Year one costs R960, year two costs another R960, year three another — three years of borrowing, three payments of rent, so 960 times 3 gives R2 880. It is not doubling up, because each R960 pays for a different year of the lender's patience. Where doubling up WOULD happen is if you charged interest on the interest — worked year two on R8 960 instead of R8 000. That is a different arrangement called compound interest, which you will meet properly later in the year; simple interest, the one this lesson uses, never does it. So the working always has three factors: principal, rate as a decimal, and time in years — 8 000 times 0,12 times 3. Leave out the time and you have priced one year; leave out the decimal conversion and you have priced nonsense. Then add the interest back onto the principal, because the examiner's favourite final question is the total repaid, R10 880, not the interest alone.

**Bianca:** Last one: in the budget, someone's expenses came to more than their income and they just wrote the subtraction answer as a negative number and moved on. What should a full-marks answer to a deficit actually look like?

**Tutor:** Three parts: the number, the name, and the consequence. First show the subtraction honestly — income of R11 000 minus expenditure of R11 750 gives negative R750, and writing it as such is correct arithmetic. Second, name it in words: "the household has a deficit of R750," because deficit and surplus are the terminology the paper is testing. Third — and this is the part that separates full marks — say what the deficit means and what could be done about it: a deficit means the month cannot be paid for from the month's income, so the shortfall must come from savings or borrowing, and borrowing adds interest on top. Then point at the budget lines that can actually move. Fixed expenses cannot be cut this month — the rent will not negotiate — so the suggestion must come from the variable lines: reduce airtime, trim groceries, walk one of the taxi legs. A suggestion to "earn more salary" is not a budget adjustment and earns nothing. The examiners are checking whether you can read a budget the way a household must: not as a subtraction exercise, but as a decision about which numbers are within your power to change.
