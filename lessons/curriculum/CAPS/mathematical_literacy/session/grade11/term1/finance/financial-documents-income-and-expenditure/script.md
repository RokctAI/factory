# Part 1 — Expert

Every household in South Africa runs on paper: a municipal account in the post box, a payslip at month end, a till slip from the spaza, a loan agreement with small print. This session reads those documents the way the curriculum requires — finding the terminology, checking the calculations, sorting income from expenditure — and then follows the money one step further, to the simple interest that decides what a loan really costs. The setting is one household in Katlehong: a salary, a side business, a municipal bill and a loan for a fridge.

## Subtopic: Reading a Municipal Account

A FINANCIAL DOCUMENT is any paper or electronic statement that records money owed, paid or earned: municipal accounts, till slips, payslips, bank statements, invoices and quotations. The examinable skill is twofold — knowing the words on the document, and checking its arithmetic line by line.

Take the municipal account first, because it carries the most vocabulary. The ACCOUNT NUMBER identifies the property. The BILLING PERIOD is the month being charged. CONSUMPTION is how much electricity or water was used, found by subtracting the previous meter reading from the current one. The TARIFF is the price per unit — per kilowatt-hour of electricity, per kilolitre of water. ARREARS are amounts unpaid from earlier months, and the DUE DATE is the last day to pay before interest or disconnection.

Now check the arithmetic on this month's electricity charge. The household used 320 kWh at a tariff of R2,10 per kWh: 320 × 2,10 = R672,00. Add the basic charge of R180 — a fixed amount payable even if no electricity is used — and the subtotal is R852,00. Then VAT, value-added tax, at 15%: 852 × 0,15 = R127,80. The total is 852 + 127,80 = R979,80.

That structure — consumption times tariff, plus fixed charge, plus 15% VAT — repeats on almost every account in the country, and examiners test it by leaving one line blank and asking for the missing value. Work down the document in order, and if the question gives the VAT-inclusive total instead, divide by 1,15 to recover the amount before VAT: 979,80 ÷ 1,15 = R852,00. The questions for this section are with you now; read each document line before touching the calculator, and name the line you are checking.

## Subtopic: Income: Fixed, Variable and Occasional

INCOME is money coming in, and the curriculum sorts it three ways. FIXED income arrives in the same amount at regular intervals: a monthly SALARY, a rental received, a social grant. VARIABLE income arrives regularly but in changing amounts: WAGES paid per hour or per day, COMMISSION calculated as a percentage of sales, the takings of a small business. OCCASIONAL income arrives now and then with no schedule: a gift, an inheritance, a bonus.

The household shows all three. Thabo earns a fixed salary of R9 500 a month as a storeman. His wife Palesa works part-time at R28,50 per hour; in a 40-hour week she earns 28,50 × 40 = R1 140, but in a short week the amount drops — that is what makes a wage variable. She also sells beaded jewellery and earns commission of 3% on whatever a gift shop sells for her: on sales of R42 000 in December, commission = 0,03 × 42 000 = R1 260. In February the shop sold nothing, and the commission was R0 — variable again, and a warning against budgeting on a good month.

Businesses have the same three categories with different names: SALES are the main income, usually variable; DONATIONS and GRANTS may be fixed if promised monthly or occasional if once-off.

Two examinable habits. First, classify before calculating — papers award marks for naming an income fixed, variable or occasional with a reason. Second, when a month's income must be totalled, use the realistic figure for variable income, not the best one ever earned. The questions for this section are ready; sort each income source into its category and justify the sorting in a phrase.

## Subtopic: Expenditure and the Household Budget

EXPENDITURE is money going out, and it sorts into the same three categories. FIXED expenses stay the same each month: rent, an insurance premium, a stokvel contribution, school fees. VARIABLE expenses recur but change in size: groceries, electricity, airtime, taxi fares. OCCASIONAL expenses arrive irregularly: a school uniform in January, a doctor's visit, a funeral contribution.

A BUDGET is a plan that lists expected income and expected expenditure side by side. Build the household's monthly budget. Income: Thabo's salary R9 500, Palesa's wages averaging R2 500 — total R12 000. Expenditure: rent R3 800; taxi fares at R14,50 a trip, two trips a day for 22 working days, which is 14,50 × 44 = R638; groceries R2 500; electricity R850; airtime and data R450; stokvel contribution R500; school costs R600. Total expenditure = 3 800 + 638 + 2 500 + 850 + 450 + 500 + 600 = R9 338.

Income minus expenditure: 12 000 − 9 338 = R2 662. A positive answer is a SURPLUS — money left over, available for saving or debt. A negative answer is a DEFICIT, and a deficit budget is a plan to go into debt, which is why examiners ask learners to suggest which variable expense to cut.

Notice the taxi line: it looks fixed because it repeats, but it is variable — a public holiday or a lift from a neighbour changes it. The test for fixed is whether the AMOUNT is guaranteed, not whether the expense recurs. Answer budget questions in sentences: "the household has a surplus of R2 662, so the fridge instalment of R302 is affordable." Your questions for this section follow now; total carefully, classify each line, and say surplus or deficit in words.

## Subtopic: Simple Interest: the Price of Borrowed Money

Borrowed money is never free, and the simplest price attached to it is SIMPLE INTEREST: interest calculated every year on the ORIGINAL amount only. The original amount is the PRINCIPAL. The formula in words: interest for one year equals principal times the interest rate; total simple interest equals principal times rate times the number of years.

The household buys a fridge with a loan of R8 000 at 12% per year simple interest, repaid over 3 years. Interest for one year: 8 000 × 0,12 = R960. For three years: 960 × 3 = R2 880. The total to repay is principal plus interest: 8 000 + 2 880 = R10 880. Spread over 36 equal monthly instalments: 10 880 ÷ 36 = R302,22 a month, with the final instalment adjusted by a few cents. The fridge with a price tag of R8 000 truly costs R10 880 — the interest is the price of not having the cash today.

Simple interest also works in the saver's favour. Palesa puts R5 000 into an account paying 6,5% per year simple interest: one year earns 5 000 × 0,065 = R325, two years earn R650, and the account holds R5 650.

Three disciplines carry the marks. Convert the percentage before multiplying — 12% is 0,12, and writing 8 000 × 12 earns nothing. Match the rate to the time: a rate PER YEAR needs the time in years, so 18 months is 1,5 years, not 18. And always finish with the total, principal plus interest, in a sentence — because the question behind every loan is what it costs altogether. The last questions of this part are with you now: rate times principal times time, then the honest total.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same household with the papers spread on the kitchen table, one envelope at a time.

## Subtopic: The Envelope from the Municipality

The municipal account looks frightening because it is crowded, but it only ever says three things: what you used, what that costs, and when to pay.

Start with what you used. The meter was read last month and again this month; the difference between the two readings is your consumption — the electricity or water that actually flowed. This month it was 320 units of electricity. Each unit costs R2,10, so 320 times 2,10 is R672. Then there is the basic charge, R180, which you pay just for being connected — use nothing, pay it anyway. That brings you to R852.

Then the government takes its slice: VAT, fifteen percent, on nearly everything you buy. Fifteen percent of R852 is R127,80, and the bottom line becomes R979,80. If a bill only shows you the bottom line and asks what it was before VAT, do not take fifteen percent off — divide by 1,15 instead, and R979,80 becomes R852 again.

Two more words and the document is yours. Arrears means last month's unpaid money riding on this month's bill — pay it first, because it grows. Due date means the last safe day; miss it and the account starts charging you interest for the delay. Some questions on this section are with you right now — find each line on the document before you calculate anything.

## Subtopic: Money In, Money Out

Think of the household as a bucket. Income pours in at the top; expenses leak out the bottom. The budget is just an honest list of both, written before the month starts.

The money coming in: Thabo's salary is the same R9 500 every month — fixed, you can build on it. Palesa's wages move with her hours, and her jewellery commission was R1 260 in December and nothing in February — variable, so plan on an ordinary month, never the best one. A gift from an uncle is occasional — nice, but never budget on it.

The money going out sorts the same way. Rent, stokvel, school fees: fixed, same every month. Groceries, electricity, airtime, taxi fare: variable — the taxi line repeats every day, but one public holiday changes the total, so it is variable even though it feels fixed. The uniform in January: occasional, and it ambushes families who forgot to plan for it.

Now the honest sum. In: R12 000. Out: rent 3 800, taxis 638, groceries 2 500, electricity 850, airtime 450, stokvel 500, school 600 — R9 338. What is left is 12 000 minus 9 338: R2 662. Left over is called a surplus; short is called a deficit, and a deficit means borrowing, which costs extra. Questions on this section are coming your way now — add the leaks, compare with the tap, and say in a sentence whether the bucket is filling or emptying.

## Subtopic: What the Loan Really Costs

The fridge costs R8 000 cash, but the household does not have R8 000 cash. So they borrow it, and borrowing has a price with a name: simple interest.

Simple means the interest is worked out on the original R8 000 every single year — the debt is charged the same rent each year for the money. Twelve percent of 8 000 is 960. That is the first year's interest. The second year: another 960. The third: another 960. Three years of borrowing costs 3 times 960, which is R2 880.

So what goes back to the lender is the original R8 000 plus the R2 880, which is R10 880. Divide by the 36 months of the agreement and the instalment is about R302 a month. Hold both numbers in your head at the shop: the sticker says R8 000, the truth says R10 880. That gap, R2 880, is what "12% per year over three years" means in rands, and deciding whether the fridge is worth it means looking at the truth, not the sticker.

Two traps before the questions. The rate must become a decimal before it multiplies — twelve percent is 0,12. And the time must be in years if the rate is per year — a loan of 18 months is 1,5 years. The same machine runs in reverse when you are the lender: R5 000 saved at 6,5% earns R325 a year, and after two years the bank owes you R5 650. The final questions of the lesson are with you now — principal, times rate, times years, then add it back on and say the full cost out loud.
