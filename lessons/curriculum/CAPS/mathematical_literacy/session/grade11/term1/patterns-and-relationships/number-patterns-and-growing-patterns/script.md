# Part 1 — Expert

A number pattern is a list of numbers that changes by a rule, and Mathematical Literacy asks one thing above all: find the rule, then use it. Tariffs that climb by the same amount, savings that double, stacked rows that grow by one more each time — the examination dresses patterns in real situations and pays you to see through the clothing. This session builds the three kinds of pattern the papers use, the difference-row test that identifies them in seconds, and the stacked-rows question that has caught matric candidates as recently as the latest national paper: rows of objects growing by one each row, and the total needed in a hurry.

## Subtopic: Three Kinds of Number Patterns

Every pattern question in this subject comes from one of three families, and naming the family is half the marks.

Family one: constant difference. Each term is found by adding the same amount to the one before. The list 13; 20; 27; 34 climbs by 7 every step. Real life supplies these endlessly: a delivery fee that grows by the same amount per kilometre, a savings account fed the same deposit every month, a stack of chairs where each chair adds the same height.

Family two: constant ratio. Each term is found by multiplying the one before by the same factor. The list 5; 10; 20; 40 doubles at every step — the ratio between consecutive terms is 2. Real life again: a rumour reaching twice as many people each hour, bacteria doubling, a chain message forwarded to the same number of friends by each receiver.

Family three: neither. The steps themselves change — but they change with their own smaller pattern. The famous example is 1; 3; 6; 10, where the steps are 2, then 3, then 4: a growing step. These are the patterns of triangles and stacks: one object in the first row, two in the second, three in the third, and the running total of rows.

The vocabulary the paper expects: the numbers in the list are terms; the position of a term matters, first term, second term and so on; and consecutive terms means terms standing next to each other. A question that says describe the pattern in words wants the family and the number: add 7 to each term; multiply each term by 2; the amount added grows by one each time.

The questions for this section are with you now: the three families, one everyday example for each, and the meaning of term and consecutive.

## Subtopic: The Difference Row — Your First Test

Here is the method that answers most pattern questions before panic can start. Write the terms in a row with space between them. Underneath each gap, write the difference: second term minus first, third minus second, and so on. That difference row is a diagnosis.

Run it on 13; 20; 27; 34. The differences: 20 minus 13 is 7; 27 minus 20 is 7; 34 minus 27 is 7. A constant difference of 7 — family one confirmed. Continuing the pattern is now mechanical: 34 plus 7 gives 41; 41 plus 7 gives 48. And a missing term in the middle is no harder: if the list reads 13; 20; blank; 34, the blank must be 20 plus 7, which is 27 — and check it from the other side, 34 minus 7 is 27. Both directions agree, so the answer stands.

The exam almost never leaves the pattern naked; it wraps it in a tariff. A courier charges R13 for the first kilometre and R7 for every kilometre after that, and the list 13; 20; 27; 34 is suddenly the cost of one, two, three, four kilometres. The rule in words: each extra kilometre adds R7. The question then asks for the cost of seven kilometres, and you extend the row: 41; 48; 55. Seven kilometres costs R55. Two habits protect these marks. First, answer inside the story — R55, with the rand sign, not a naked 55. Second, count positions on your fingers as you extend: it is humiliating to extend perfectly and stop one term early.

When the difference row is not constant, do not force it. That is the signal to test the other two families — and the test comes next.

Work this section's questions before moving on: the difference row on a clean list, the missing middle term checked from both sides, and the tariff wrapped around the pattern.

## Subtopic: Constant Ratio — When Patterns Multiply

Some lists refuse the difference test. Run it on 5; 10; 20; 40: the differences are 5, then 10, then 20 — not constant, and themselves doubling. That doubling in the difference row is the fingerprint of family two. Confirm it with the ratio test: divide each term by the one before. 10 divided by 5 is 2; 20 divided by 10 is 2; 40 divided by 20 is 2. A constant ratio of 2.

Continuing a ratio pattern is multiplication: 40 times 2 gives 80; 80 times 2 gives 160. In words, the rule is: multiply each term by 2 to get the next term. The papers love growth stories here. A community message is sent to 5 people in the first hour, and each hour the number reached doubles: after four hours, 40 people in that hour; after five, 80. Or a puddle of algae doubles its size every day — the drama being how slowly it seems to start and how violently it ends. From 5 to 80 took four doublings; the next four take it to 1 280. Multiplication patterns start on their knees and finish through the roof — a sentence worth writing in interpretation questions.

Keep the two families apart with one contrast: constant difference grows by the same amount each step; constant ratio grows by the same factor. A tariff that adds R7 per kilometre will never explode; a chain that doubles will never stay polite. When a question asks which grows faster in the long run, the ratio pattern always wins eventually, and saying eventually is what earns the reasoning mark — for the first few terms, the adding pattern may well be ahead.

Take the questions for this section now: the ratio test performed, the pattern continued by multiplying, and the same-amount against same-factor contrast.

## Subtopic: Growing Patterns and the Stacked-Rows Question

Now the family the latest national paper used to separate the prepared from the rest. A decoration is built from rows of circles: one circle in the first row, and each row after that has one more circle than the row before. Seven rows in all. How many circles altogether?

Diagnose first. The circles per row run 1; 2; 3; 4; 5; 6; 7 — a constant difference of 1, easy. But the question asks for the total, and the running totals run 1; 3; 6; 10; 15; 21; 28. Difference row on the totals: 2; 3; 4; 5; 6; 7 — growing by one each time. Family three: the steps themselves form a pattern. These totals are the triangular numbers, the numbers you get from stacking.

The safe method is the table. Two columns: row number, and total so far. Row one, total 1. Row two, total 1 plus 2, which is 3. Row three, total 3 plus 3, which is 6. Row four, 6 plus 4 is 10. Row five, 10 plus 5 is 15. Row six, 15 plus 6 is 21. Row seven, 21 plus 7 is 28. The decoration uses 28 circles. The table is slower than a formula and immune to nerves — every line is one small addition, and the final line is the answer with its position attached.

There is also a beautiful shortcut for adding one up to seven, worth knowing as a check. Pair the ends: 1 plus 7 is 8; 2 plus 6 is 8; 3 plus 5 is 8. Three pairs of eight, 3 × 8 = 24, and the unpaired 4 in the middle brings it to 28. Same answer by another road — which is exactly what a checking habit looks like.

Answer in the story's units: 28 circles. And read what the question counts: circles in the seventh row is 7; circles in all seven rows is 28. The single biggest mark-loser on this question type is answering the row when the total was asked.

Pause for this section's questions: the two lists told apart, the table built to row seven, the pairing check, and the row-against-total trap.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same three families from the taxi rank, the group chat and the shop shelf — no new maths, just familiar clothes.

## Subtopic: The Same Step Every Time

Think of climbing a staircase where every step is the same height. After the first step you know everything about that staircase: each step lifts you the same amount, so climbing is just adding, again and again. That is a constant difference pattern — the steady staircase of numbers.

Money walks this staircase all day. A minibus route charges R13 for the short hop and R7 more for each extra zone: 13; 20; 27; 34. A cold drink deposit refunded per bottle, a phone paid off at the same amount weekly, loyalty points that drip in equally — steady staircases, all of them. The test is one move: subtract neighbours. Twenty minus thirteen, seven. Twenty-seven minus twenty, seven. The moment the gaps agree, you have the step, and the future is yours to write: 34 plus 7 is 41, then 48, then 55.

Two street-smart habits finish the job. Habit one: when a term is missing in the middle, walk to it from both sides — up from the term before, down from the term after — and only trust the answer both walks agree on. Habit two: keep count of your steps out loud. If the question wants the seventh term, count seven positions on your fingers as you go. The pattern never betrays you; miscounting does.

And keep the units of the story. If the numbers are rands, the answer is R55, spoken and written with its sign. An answer without units is a staircase with no floor at the top.

The questions for this section are up now: the staircase test, the both-sides walk to a missing term, and the finger-count to term seven. Take them gently — this is the friendliest family of the three.

## Subtopic: The Pattern That Doubles Like a Rumour

Now a different animal. A juicy rumour reaches 5 people in the first hour. Each of those tells enough friends that the next hour reaches double: 10, then 20, then 40. Nobody is adding here — the rumour multiplies. Divide any hour's number by the hour before and the answer is always 2. That fixed multiplier is the constant ratio, and it is the second family's signature.

Feel how differently it behaves. The staircase pattern added 7 forever, politely. The rumour started smaller than the staircase — 5 against 13 — and for a while it stays behind. But doubling compounds: 80, then 160, then 320. The staircase is still trudging 55, 62, 69 while the rumour has left the neighbourhood. Whenever an exam asks which plan overtakes, which spreads faster, which grows fastest in the long run — the multiplying pattern wins in the end, and your written reason is exactly that: it multiplies by the same factor each step, while the other only adds the same amount.

The test order to run under pressure: subtract neighbours first. Gaps equal? Constant difference; done. Gaps unequal but growing suspiciously? Divide neighbours. Answers equal? Constant ratio; done. The division is the same one you use to check airtime — how many times does the old number fit into the new — and the moment it repeats, you have caught the multiplier.

Your questions for this section are ready: the rumour's ratio caught by division, the slow-start-fast-finish behaviour, and the test order that never leaves you guessing.

## Subtopic: Cans at the Shop and the Growing Step

Picture the pyramid of cans at the supermarket entrance. One can on top. Two in the row below. Then three, then four — every row one can wider than the row above. Seven rows down, the display is done. The stacker's question is the exam's question: how many cans were carried from the storeroom?

The rows themselves are easy — 1; 2; 3; 4; 5; 6; 7. But the total is a different list: after one row, 1 can; after two rows, 3; after three, 6; then 10; 15; 21; 28. Look at the gaps between totals: 2, then 3, then 4, then 5 — the step grows by one every time, because every new row is one can longer. This is the third family: not a broken pattern, a growing one. The steps are on their own little staircase.

The everyday method is the running total, exactly as the stacker would count. Say it as you build: one. Plus two, three. Plus three, six. Plus four, ten. Plus five, fifteen. Plus six, twenty-one. Plus seven, twenty-eight. Twenty-eight cans off the storeroom trolley. Written as a two-column table — row number, cans so far — it is bulletproof, and the marker can watch every mark being earned.

Want the show-off check? Pair the rows from the outside in: the top row of 1 with the bottom row of 7 makes 8; rows 2 and 6 make 8; rows 3 and 5 make 8. Three pairs of 8 and the lonely middle row of 4: 24 plus 4, 28 again. Two roads, one answer — that is certainty, not luck.

Last warning, and it is the one that costs real marks: the seventh row holds 7 cans, but the display holds 28. Read the question's last line slowly — a row, or the whole stack? — and give the number the story asked for, with its unit: 28 cans.

The final questions of the lesson are with you now: the growing step spotted in the gaps, the running total said out loud to row seven, the pairs-of-eight check, and the row-or-stack decision. Answer them at your own pace, and this family joins the other two in your pocket.
