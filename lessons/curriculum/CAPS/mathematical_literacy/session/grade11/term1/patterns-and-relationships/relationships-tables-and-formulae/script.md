# Part 1 — Expert

Every tariff sheet, wage slip and price list in this subject hides a rule. This session takes a table of real rand amounts, finds the rule inside it, writes that rule as a formula, draws it as a graph, and then uses it to answer the question the person actually asked: what will this cost me, and what can I afford? Grade 11 works with the four relationships the curriculum names — constant difference, inverse proportion, constant ratio, and combinations of these — and the marks go to the learner who can name which one is on the page and show the substitution that follows.

## Subtopic: Naming the Variables and Finding the Rule

Begin with the two variables, because every later step depends on telling them apart. The INDEPENDENT variable is the one you choose: hours booked, litres bought, kilometres driven, people in the car. The DEPENDENT variable is the one that follows from that choice — almost always the amount you pay. Say it as a sentence before you touch a calculator: "the cost depends on the number of hours." On a graph the independent variable goes on the horizontal axis and the dependent variable on the vertical axis. Examiners ask this directly, because a learner who swaps them plots the whole graph the wrong way round.

Now a real table. A plumber working in Kimberley charges a call-out fee of R350 and then R280 for every hour on site. One hour costs R630, two hours R910, three hours R1 190, four hours R1 470.

Test the table the way you test every table: subtract each value from the one before it. R910 minus R630 is R280. R1 190 minus R910 is R280. R1 190 to R1 470 is R280 again. The difference is CONSTANT, so the relationship is linear and its graph is a straight line. A constant difference always means "the same amount added for each extra unit" — here, the hourly rate.

Two numbers now build the formula. The constant difference R280 is the rate per hour. The starting value is the cost of zero hours — not zero rand, but R350, the call-out fee, which is charged before a single spanner turns. So: Cost in rand equals 350 plus 280 multiplied by the number of hours. Substitute to check: three hours gives 350 plus 280 times 3, which is 350 plus 840, which is R1 190 — the table agrees, so the formula is sound.

Three habits earn marks here and cost nothing: write the formula down before substituting, keep the rand sign and the unit "hours" attached to the numbers, and finish with a sentence — "the plumber's bill for three hours is R1 190." The questions for this section are with you now; give attention to which variable is independent, and to testing the differences before you name the relationship.

## Subtopic: When the Difference Is Not Constant

Not every table adds the same amount. When the differences refuse to settle, do not abandon the table — test it twice more, for a constant PRODUCT and for a constant RATIO.

First, inverse proportion. Six friends hire a bakkie and driver from Mthatha for R1 800 for the day and split the cost equally. One person pays R1 800. Two pay R900 each. Three pay R600. Four pay R450. Five pay R360. Six pay R300. The differences are R900, R300, R150, R90, R60 — nothing constant there. But multiply each pair: 1 times 1 800, 2 times 900, 3 times 600, 4 times 450 — every product is 1 800. That constant product is the signature of INVERSE PROPORTION: as one quantity increases, the other decreases in exact proportion. The formula is cost per person equals 1 800 divided by the number of people. Its graph is a smooth curve falling steeply and then flattening, approaching the axes without ever touching them — because no matter how many people climb in, each still pays something above zero.

Second, constant ratio between consecutive terms. A stokvel in Soshanguve holds R2 000 in an account paying 8% a year, left untouched. After one year the balance is R2 160. After two years R2 332,80. After three years R2 519,42. The differences grow — R160, then R172,80, then R186,62 — so it is not linear. But divide each value by the one before it: 2 160 divided by 2 000 is 1,08; 2 332,80 divided by 2 160 is 1,08 again. The RATIO is constant, and the rule is "multiply by 1,08 each year". Constant-ratio relationships climb slowly at first and then steeply — that upward curve is why money left alone for a long time grows so much faster in the later years than the earlier ones.

Build the test into a routine you can run under pressure. Differences constant? Linear. Products constant? Inverse proportion. Ratios constant? Constant ratio. None of the three? Look for a combination, which the fourth section handles. Your questions for this section are ready; keep the three tests in that order in your head, and be ready to justify your choice with the actual arithmetic, not a guess from the shape.

## Subtopic: One Relationship, Three Representations

A relationship can appear in three costumes, and the examination moves between them without warning. The FORMULA or equation is the rule in symbols or words. The TABLE is a list of matched input and output values. The GRAPH is the picture. All three describe the same relationship, and a complete answer can travel from any one of them to the others.

From formula to table: substitute chosen inputs. From the bakkie rule, eight passengers give 1 800 divided by 8, which is R225 each. From table to graph: plot each pair as a point, with the independent variable across and the dependent variable up, then join the points if the quantity between them is meaningful. This is the distinction between DISCRETE and CONTINUOUS data, and it is examinable. Passengers in a bakkie are discrete — there is no such thing as 3,5 people — so the points are plotted but not joined. Hours of plumbing are continuous, since the plumber can work an hour and a half, so those points are joined with a straight line.

From graph back to numbers: to read a value, go up from the horizontal axis to the line, then straight across to the vertical axis, and read the amount. Always state the units you read. And label everything — both axes with their quantity and unit, and a heading — because axis labels carry marks of their own.

The shapes are worth memorising because they let you check yourself. A constant-difference relationship is a straight line, lifted above the origin by any fixed starting charge. An inverse proportion is a falling curve that flattens out. A constant-ratio relationship is a rising curve that gets steeper. If your plotted points bend when your table said the differences were constant, the picture has just caught an arithmetic error for you. Turn to the questions for this section now, paying particular attention to discrete against continuous, and to reading a value off a graph correctly.

## Subtopic: Working Backwards on a Combination Tariff

Most real charges combine relationships, and the commonest combination is a fixed charge plus a rate. A municipality in the Eastern Cape bills water at a fixed basic charge of R145,00 per month plus R21,50 for every kilolitre used. The rule: monthly bill equals 145 plus 21,50 times the kilolitres used.

Forwards is straightforward. A household using 15 kℓ pays 145 plus 21,50 times 15, which is 145 plus 322,50, giving R467,50. A household using 25 kℓ pays 145 plus 537,50, giving R682,50.

Backwards is where the marks hide. Suppose the bill is R790,00 and the household wants to know how much water it used. Undo the rule in reverse order. First remove the fixed charge: 790,00 minus 145,00 leaves R645,00, which is the part that paid for water. Then divide by the rate: 645,00 divided by 21,50 gives 30. The household used 30 kilolitres. Check by substituting forwards: 145 plus 21,50 times 30 is 145 plus 645, which is R790,00 — correct.

That reversal generalises to every combination tariff you will meet: subtract the fixed part first, then divide by the rate. Learners lose marks by dividing the whole bill by the rate — 790 divided by 21,50 gives 36,7 kℓ, which is wrong by nearly seven kilolitres because the basic charge was never removed.

Two further disciplines. Round only at the end: carry the full value through the working, then round sensibly — to cents for money, to a whole unit where fractions make no sense. And answer in a sentence with its unit: "the household used 30 kilolitres of water in that month." A bare number at the foot of a page of correct work is an answer the marker cannot credit fully. The last questions of this part are in front of you now: expect a combination tariff read forwards, then read backwards, with the fixed charge handled separately from the rate.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same rules from the kitchen table, where a price list is not a pattern question but a bill somebody has to pay.

## Subtopic: The Plumber Who Charges Twice

When the plumber walks into your yard, you are already paying. That is the call-out — R350 just for arriving, whether the job takes five minutes or five hours. Then the clock starts, at R280 an hour. So one hour costs R350 plus R280, which is R630. Two hours: R910. Three hours: R1 190. Four: R1 470.

Look at what changes from one row to the next. R630 to R910 is R280 up. R910 to R1 190 is R280 up. Every single step, the same R280. When a table climbs by the same amount every time, you are looking at the simplest kind of rule: something fixed at the start, then something extra for each unit. Write it exactly how you would explain it to your aunt: "three hundred and fifty rand, plus two hundred and eighty rand for every hour."

There are two jobs in that sentence and they behave differently. The R350 sits still. The R280 multiplies. So the moment you see a price list, ask two questions: what do I pay before anything happens, and what does each extra unit add? Answer those and you have the rule — plumber, taxi, water bill or phone contract.

One more thing worth naming: the hours are what you choose, and the money is what follows. That is why hours go along the bottom of a graph and rands go up the side, never the other way round. Some questions on this section are with you right now; look for the fixed part and the per-unit part in each price list.

## Subtopic: Splitting the Bakkie, Growing the Stokvel

Two more rules, and neither one adds the same amount each time.

First, the bakkie. Hiring it costs R1 800 for the day, and whoever climbs in shares that cost. Alone, you pay the whole R1 800. Bring a friend: R900 each. Three of you: R600 each. Four: R450. Five: R360. Six: R300. Notice how the price per person drops fast at the start and then slows down. Going from one person to two saves you R900; going from five to six only saves you R60. The bakkie does not get cheaper, it just gets shared further. Here is the trick that proves it: multiply the people by the price every time — one times 1 800, two times 900, three times 600 — and you always land back on R1 800, because the bakkie's price never moved. That is what it means when two things are inversely proportional: one goes up, the other comes down, and their product stays put.

Second, the stokvel money. Two thousand rand sits in an account earning 8% a year and nobody touches it. Year one: R2 160. Year two: R2 332,80. Year three: R2 519,42. Now the jumps get BIGGER each year — R160, then R172,80, then R186,62. Why? Because in year two the interest is earned on the R2 160, not just the original R2 000. The rule is not "add something", it is "multiply by 1,08". Same multiplier, every year — which is why money left alone quietly speeds up.

So when a table will not add nicely, do not panic. Try multiplying across the pair and see if you keep getting the same total, like the bakkie. Then try dividing each number by the one before it and see if you keep getting the same multiplier, like the stokvel. One of the three tests almost always answers. Questions on these two patterns are coming to you now — test with the arithmetic, not with your eyes.

## Subtopic: Reading the Rule Backwards

The last move is the one people find hardest and it is really just undoing your shoes in the right order.

The municipality charges R145 every month before you open a single tap, then R21,50 for every kilolitre — that is a thousand litres. Use 15 kℓ and your bill is R145 plus fifteen lots of R21,50, which is R145 plus R322,50, so R467,50. Fine. But now flip it. The bill arrives at R790 and you want to know how much water the family actually used, because that number is the one you can change.

Take the fixed part off first: R790 minus R145 leaves R645. That R645 is pure water. Now share it out at R21,50 per kilolitre: R645 divided by R21,50 gives 30. Thirty kilolitres. And check yourself by going forwards again: R145 plus thirty lots of R21,50 is R145 plus R645, which is R790 exactly. When the check lands on the number you started with, you can stop worrying.

The mistake to avoid is dividing the whole R790 by R21,50, because that pretends the basic charge bought water. It did not — it bought the pipes, the meter and the connection. Take it off first, every time.

And finish like a person, not a calculator. Do not write "30" and stop. Write: "the household used 30 kilolitres of water that month." Keep the rands with the rands, the kilolitres with the kilolitres, and round only right at the end, once the work is done. The final questions of the lesson are with you now — expect a bill to be read forwards and then, the interesting way, backwards.
