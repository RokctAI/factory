**Bianca:** Before you move on — a few questions that come up a lot with this topic. First: how do you tell the three families apart quickly when the numbers look nothing like the examples?

**Tutor:** Run the same two tests in the same order, whatever the numbers look like. Test one: subtract neighbours. Write the gap between every pair of consecutive terms. If the gaps all agree, you have a constant difference pattern and you are finished diagnosing — the shared gap is your step. If the gaps disagree, look at how they disagree before moving on. Gaps that grow by the same amount each time — two, three, four, five — announce a growing-step pattern, the stacking family. Gaps that seem to double or triple send you to test two: divide neighbours. Divide each term by the one before it; if that answer repeats, you have a constant ratio pattern and the repeated answer is your multiplier. The order matters because subtraction is quicker and catches the most common family first, and the shape of the failed subtraction usually names the family anyway. Two tests, run in order, and every list the paper can print falls into one of the three drawers within a minute.

***

**Bianca:** Next: in Mathematical Literacy, do we ever need the nth-term formula, or is extending the table always enough?

**Tutor:** The table is enough, and it is the intended method. This subject examines whether you can use patterns inside real situations — tariffs, stacks, schedules — not whether you can build algebraic machinery. Every pattern question in these papers can be answered by writing the terms in a table and extending it carefully to the position asked for: term by term for a constant difference, multiplication by multiplication for a ratio, running total by running total for a stack. What the papers do reward is describing the rule in words — add seven each time, double each hour, each row adds one more — so practise saying rules as sentences. Where learners burn time is extending blindly to faraway positions, so borrow one shortcut for the stacking family only: to add one up to seven, pair the ends — one with seven, two with six, three with five — three pairs of eight plus the middle four gives twenty-eight. Use the table as your method and the pairing as your check, and you have full marks without a single formula.

***

**Bianca:** Third: the missing-middle-term questions. People find the step, fill the blank, and still lose the mark. What goes wrong?

**Tutor:** They walk to the blank from one side only, and the exam counts on it. Suppose the list reads thirteen, twenty, blank, thirty-four. A hurried candidate finds the step of seven, adds it to twenty, writes twenty-seven and moves on — correct this time, but only by luck, because they never checked that thirty-four agrees. The examiners love lists where a careless step survives the first check and collapses on the second: if the true pattern had a step of eight, the blank from the left would be twenty-eight, but thirty-four minus eight is twenty-six, and the disagreement shouts that the step itself was wrong. So make the both-sides walk a ritual. Add the step to the term before the blank; subtract the step from the term after it; only an answer both walks agree on goes in the answer book. It costs ten seconds, it catches wrong steps as well as wrong arithmetic, and it turns the shakiest question type in this topic into a guaranteed mark.

***

**Bianca:** Last one: the stacked-rows question from the recent paper — what exactly must go in the answer book to collect every mark?

**Tutor:** Show the build, then land on the total with its unit. The marker wants to see that you understood the structure — one object in the first row, one more in each row after — and that you accumulated it correctly. The cleanest presentation is the two-column table: row number down the left, total so far down the right. One, one. Two, three. Three, six. Four, ten. Five, fifteen. Six, twenty-one. Seven, twenty-eight. Every line is a visible small addition, so even under time pressure nothing hides. Then write the sentence the question actually asked for: the decoration uses twenty-eight circles — the number and the thing being counted, together. If you have a spare thirty seconds, run the pairing check beside your table so a slip cannot survive. And before any of that, underline what is being counted: the row or the whole stack. Seven is the seventh row; twenty-eight is the decoration. The candidates who lost this question mostly did the arithmetic right and answered the wrong count.
