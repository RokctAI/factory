**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! First one: how do we actually know which variable is independent when the question does not tell us? In the plumber example it felt obvious, but on a strange context we froze.

**Tutor:** Use the dependency sentence, and say it out loud in both directions to hear which one is nonsense. "The cost depends on the number of hours" makes sense; "the number of hours depends on the cost" does not, because the pipe takes as long as it takes regardless of what you are willing to pay. The one that follows is dependent; the one that is chosen or set is independent. A second cue is control: whoever is doing the buying, driving, or using controls the independent variable, and the till or the meter reports the dependent one. A third cue is the formula itself — the variable you substitute INTO the rule is independent, and the value that comes out is dependent. In almost every maths literacy context the money is dependent: rands come out at the end. Once you have decided, hold the decision through the whole question, because the graph's axes, the table's columns and the wording of your final sentence all depend on it. And when you plot, the independent variable is always horizontal, the dependent one vertical.

**Bianca:** Next: our table's differences were nearly constant — R280, R280, then R281 in one row. Do we call it linear or not?

**Tutor:** Check your arithmetic first, because in an examination table the differences are constructed to be exactly constant, and a single value that is one rand out is far more likely to be a subtraction slip than a real feature of the data. Redo that one subtraction. If it truly is one rand out and the table came from real collected data — a household's actual bills, a shop's actual takings — then the honest description is that the relationship is approximately linear, and you say so: the difference is constant at about R280 per unit, so the data is very close to a straight line. Real-world data is allowed to be slightly untidy; a designed pattern question is not. The distinction matters because your next move differs: for a designed table you may confidently write a formula and use it to predict any value, while for real data you write the formula as a model and treat predictions as estimates. Examiners reward that caution when the context is genuine measurement, and they reward exactness when the pattern is clean.

**Bianca:** This one caused an argument: someone said inverse proportion and constant ratio are the same thing because both graphs are curves. Are they?

**Tutor:** They are genuinely different, and the arithmetic separates them cleanly even when the pictures look vaguely similar. In inverse proportion, one quantity goes up while the other comes down, and the PRODUCT of the pair stays fixed: six people at R300 and three people at R600 both multiply to R1 800, because the total being shared never changed. Its graph falls, flattening as it goes, and it never reaches zero — sharing among more and more people makes each share tiny, but never nothing. In a constant-ratio relationship both the input and the output rise, and it is the QUOTIENT of consecutive outputs that stays fixed: each year's balance divided by the previous year's gives 1,08 every time. Its graph rises, and it rises faster and faster, because each multiplication acts on a bigger number than the last. So one is about a fixed total being divided up, the other about a fixed multiplier being applied again and again. Test with multiplication for the first and division for the second, and write down which test you used — that sentence is often worth a mark by itself.

***

**Bianca:** Last one: why do we bother with the graph at all if the formula gives the exact answer anyway?

**Tutor:** Three reasons, and each of them earns marks. First, the graph answers comparison questions faster and more convincingly than arithmetic: when two tariffs are drawn on the same axes, the point where the lines cross is the usage at which they cost the same, and which line sits lower on either side tells you immediately which option is cheaper for a light user and which for a heavy one. Second, the graph is a check on your working. If your table said the differences were constant but your plotted points bend, one of the values is wrong and the picture has just caught it — that is free error-detection. Third, the examination sets questions in both directions: it hands you a graph and asks for a value, and it hands you a formula and asks for a sketch. So you need the traffic to flow both ways. When you read a value off a graph, go up from the horizontal axis to the line and straight across to the vertical axis, then state the reading with its unit. And label both axes with quantity and unit and give the graph a heading — those labels carry their own marks, and they are the easiest marks in the topic to collect.
