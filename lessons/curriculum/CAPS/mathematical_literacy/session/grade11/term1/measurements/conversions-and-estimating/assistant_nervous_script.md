Hey, it's Bianca — conversions look like a pile of numbers to memorise, and that is exactly why they feel heavy. Here is the relief: the paper gives you the conversion table. You are not being tested on remembering 2,54 or 1,609. You are being tested on knowing which way to point them.

So learn the one question instead of the twenty numbers: which unit is BIGGER? A kilometre is bigger than a metre, so kilometres turn into lots of metres — multiply. A kilogram is heavier than a pound, so kilograms turn into more pounds — multiply. Go the other way and you divide. One question, every conversion, every time.

Temperature is the only one with a real order to it, and the order is the whole mark. Coming down from Fahrenheit: take the 32 off first, then times five, then divide by nine. Going up to Fahrenheit: times nine, divide by five, then add the 32 last. Write those two lines on the front page of your book and say them once a day this week.

Your mark-protecting habit here is small and boring and it works: convert everything into the same unit BEFORE you calculate, and write the unit next to every single number in your working. Half of all lost marks in measurement come from a metre and a centimetre meeting inside one multiplication. The other half come from an answer with no unit on it.

And use your common sense as a checker. A bath does not hold eight thousand litres. A person does not weigh seven hundred kilograms. If your answer is the wrong size for the world, go back — you will find the slip in about ten seconds.

One question about size, two lines about temperature, units on everything. That is the whole topic. You've got this!
