**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! First one: how do we know whether a question wants perimeter or area? Half of us used the wrong one in the test and lost everything that followed.

**Tutor:** Ask what the material physically does. If it runs along a line, it is perimeter: fencing, skirting board, curtain rail, a strip of edging bricks, ribbon around a cake, burglar bars along a boundary. If it lies flat and covers a surface, it is area: paint, tiles, paving, carpet, grass, plaster, wallpaper. The unit in the price is a second and very reliable clue. A price quoted per METRE — R289 per metre of fencing — is a perimeter price, because it is buying length. A price quoted per SQUARE METRE, or per litre of coverage, or per box covering so many square metres, is an area price. So read the price list before you read the shape, and the price list will tell you which calculation the examiner wants. A third clue is the wording itself: "around", "along the boundary" and "the edge of" signal perimeter; "cover", "lay", "paint" and "plant" signal area. Write the word you have chosen at the top of your working — "this is an area question" — and the rest of the method follows without hesitation.

**Bianca:** Next: why is the wall area the perimeter times the height? It looks like we are mixing a length answer with a length to get an area, and that felt like cheating.

**Tutor:** It is not cheating, and the reason is worth seeing once because you will never forget it afterwards. Imagine cutting the room's four walls apart at one corner and unrolling them flat, like unwrapping a label from a tin. What you end up with is a single long rectangle. Its height is the height of the walls, and its length is the total distance around the room — which is exactly the perimeter of the floor. So the wall area is that long rectangle's length times its height, which is perimeter times height. It is the ordinary area formula applied to one unrolled shape rather than four separate ones. You could also work out each wall separately: two walls of 4,2 by 2,5 and two of 3,6 by 2,5, which gives 21 plus 18, again 39 square metres. Same answer, more writing and more chances to slip. Use the shortcut when the room is a plain rectangle, and fall back on wall-by-wall when the room has an odd shape, a sloping ceiling or walls of different heights.

**Bianca:** This one caused real confusion: someone converted an area from square metres to square centimetres by multiplying by 100, and the whole answer was out. Why is it not 100?

**Tutor:** Because you are converting a covering, not a line, and a covering stretches in two directions at once. Picture one square metre drawn on the floor. Along the bottom edge it is 100 centimetres, and up the side it is also 100 centimetres. The little one-centimetre squares that fill it are therefore 100 across and 100 down, which is 100 times 100, or 10 000 of them. So one square metre is 10 000 square centimetres, and multiplying by only 100 leaves you a hundred times too small. The same logic runs through every squared unit: one square kilometre is 1 000 times 1 000, which is a million square metres. And it goes one step further for volume, where the stretching happens in three directions: one cubic metre is 100 times 100 times 100, which is a million cubic centimetres. A safe habit is to avoid the problem entirely — convert all the LENGTHS into the unit you want before you multiply, so the area comes out in the right unit from the start and no area conversion is needed at all.

**Bianca:** Last one: the wastage percentage confused us. Is 10% something we must memorise, and do we add it before or after working out the boxes?

**Tutor:** You never memorise it, because the question always states it — ten percent is common for tiling and paving, but a paper may specify five percent, fifteen percent, or none at all, and whatever the paper says is what you use. The order matters, though, and it is worth being precise. Add the wastage to the AREA first, then convert that increased area into packets. So the floor of 15,12 square metres becomes 15,12 times 1,10, which is 16,63 square metres, and only then do you divide by the 1,44 square metres a box covers, giving 11,55 boxes, which rounds up to 12. If you instead work out the boxes first and then add ten percent to the box count, you can land on a different number, because the rounding then happens in the wrong place. And that final rounding is never ordinary rounding: it is always upward for materials, because the shop cannot sell you a fraction of a box and a fraction short means a patch of bare floor. Write a short reason beside the rounded figure — "rounded up, since boxes are sold whole" — and the marker can see the decision was deliberate.
