Hey, it's Bianca — measurement questions look long and frightening because they come wrapped in a whole building project. But strip the story away and there are only three moves in the entire topic, and you already know two of them.

Move one: perimeter, the walk around the edge. Add all the sides, or for a rectangle add one long and one short and double it. That is what you buy fencing and skirting by. Move two: area, the covering over the top. Length times breadth for a rectangle, half base times height for a triangle, 3,14 times radius times radius for a circle. That is what you buy paint, tiles, paving and grass by. If you can say "am I buying the edge or the covering?", you have already chosen the right formula.

Move three is the one that separates the marks: turning the answer into a shopping list. The paper always tells you the coverage rate and the packet size, and it always expects you to use both. Paint covers so many square metres a litre. Tiles come in boxes. Add roughly 10% wastage for anything that gets cut, then round UP — never down — because eleven boxes on a twelve-box floor means bare floor and a second taxi to the shop.

Your mark-protecting habit here is subtraction. Look at the picture for what must come OFF before you multiply by a price: a gate off the fence, a door and a window off the walls, a flower bed off the paving. Examiners put one in almost every question, and it is worth a mark on its own.

And write the units on every single line — m for the edge, m² for the covering. Then finish with a sentence: "she must buy 12 boxes, costing R3 468."

Edge or covering, subtract the openings, round up, units on. That is the whole topic. You've got this!
