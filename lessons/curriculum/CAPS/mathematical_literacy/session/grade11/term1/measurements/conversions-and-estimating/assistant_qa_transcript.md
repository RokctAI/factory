**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! First: the paint question said 27 litres were needed, but the memo answer was about buying 30 litres. Two of us lost marks for writing 27. Why is 27 wrong when the arithmetic is right?

**Tutor:** The arithmetic is not wrong — it is incomplete, and the question asked something the arithmetic alone does not answer. There are really two questions hidden in one: how much paint does the wall need, and what must the painter buy? The first is answered by 27 litres and that value should be written down, because it carries its own mark. The second is answered by the shop's packaging, which is why the tin sizes and prices are printed in the question at all. In maths literacy nothing in the question is decoration — if a price list appears, the examiner intends you to use it. So the full answer names the purchase and the cost: one 20 ℓ drum and two 5 ℓ tins, which gives 30 litres for R2 407, and it goes further by comparing that with six tins at R2 574 and stating the R167 saving. A useful habit is to reread the question after you have a number and ask what a real person would still need to know before walking into the shop. If your answer does not let them act, it is not finished.

**Bianca:** Next one: with temperature, why can we not just use one multiplier the way we do with kilograms and pounds? It feels like an unnecessary extra rule.

**Tutor:** Because the two temperature scales measure from different starting points, and a single multiplier can only stretch a scale, not move it. Mass and length both start at a shared zero: zero kilograms is zero pounds, zero metres is zero feet, so converting is pure stretching and one multiplier does the job. Temperature is different. Water freezes at zero on the Celsius scale but at thirty-two on the Fahrenheit scale, so the two rulers are offset by 32 before any stretching happens. That is why the conversion has two operations: the 32 handles the offset and the nine-fifths handles the stretch. The order follows from that logic. Coming down from Fahrenheit you remove the offset first and then unstretch, so subtract 32, multiply by 5, divide by 9. Going up you stretch first and then add the offset, so multiply by 9, divide by 5, add 32. If you can say why the 32 is there, you will never put it in the wrong place, and it also explains the odd fact that minus forty is the same number on both scales — the point where the offset and the stretch happen to cancel.

**Bianca:** This one came up in a past paper: it asked us to round an answer of 22,5 eggs and someone rounded to 22 because normal rounding says round down below a half. Was that fair?

**Tutor:** It is a real and common loss, and the rule you need is that in maths literacy the CONTEXT decides the rounding, not the digit after the decimal comma. Ordinary rounding is a mathematical convention; rounding in a real situation is a decision with consequences. Buy 22 eggs when the recipe needs 22,5 and you cannot finish the last batch, so the honest answer is 23. The same logic governs paint, tiles, bricks, bags of cement, taxis for a school outing and packets of anything: when running short stops the job, round up. Money rounds to two decimals because that is what cents allow. Time rounds to whole minutes or hours depending on what a timetable can use. And a medicine dose is not rounded at all, because the number is a medical instruction rather than an estimate. The safest habit is to write one short sentence next to the rounded value explaining the choice — "23 eggs, since half an egg cannot be bought" — because that sentence shows the marker you rounded on purpose.

**Bianca:** Last one: how much of the estimating work is really examinable? It feels like the soft part of the topic that nobody tests.

**Tutor:** It is examined in two distinct ways, and both are worth marks. The first is direct: papers ask you to estimate a length, a mass or a volume from a photograph or a description, or to choose the most reasonable value from a list, and they expect you to reason from a known reference — a doorway is about two metres high, a bag of mealie meal is ten or twelve and a half kilograms, a cool drink bottle holds two litres, a normal walking step is close to a metre. Naming the reference in your answer is what turns a guess into an estimate. The second way is indirect and much more valuable: estimating is the error-check that runs over the whole paper. Every calculation you finish can be tested against the world in a moment — is this bath, this wall, this journey, this bill the right SIZE? A learner who checks that way catches misplaced decimal points, wrong-direction conversions and mixed units before the marker does, and those are the three most common errors in the entire measurement section. So the estimating skill is not decorative. It is the habit that protects everything else you calculate.
