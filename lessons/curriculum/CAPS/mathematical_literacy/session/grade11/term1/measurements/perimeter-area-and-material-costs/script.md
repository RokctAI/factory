# Part 1 — Expert

A building project is where measurement earns its keep. Somebody has to fence a yard, pave it, tile a floor and paint the walls, and every one of those decisions begins with a length or an area and ends with a rand amount. This session works through one project in Soweto — a rectangular yard and the room behind it — using perimeter, area, surface area and the costing that follows, with the formulae the curriculum requires and the rounding rules a hardware shop actually imposes.

## Subtopic: Perimeter: the Line Around the Job

PERIMETER is the total distance around the outside of a two-dimensional shape, measured in ordinary length units — millimetres, centimetres, metres. It answers questions about anything that runs along an edge: fencing, skirting board, curtain rail, a border of bricks, a strip of beading.

For a rectangle the formula is P = 2 × (length + breadth). The yard measures 12,5 m by 8 m, so P = 2 × (12,5 + 8) = 2 × 20,5 = 41 m. For a circle the perimeter has its own name, CIRCUMFERENCE, and its own formula: C = 2 × π × radius, or equivalently π × diameter. A circular flower bed of radius 1,5 m has C = 2 × 3,14 × 1,5 = 9,42 m of edging.

For any other shape, perimeter is simply the sum of all the sides — there is no formula to remember, only the discipline of walking the outline and not missing a side. In a composite shape, the sides that face inward still count if the edging runs along them.

Now the step that separates a maths answer from a useful one. The yard's perimeter is 41 m, but the fence is not 41 m, because a 3 m gate occupies part of that line and the gate is bought separately. Fencing needed = 41 − 3 = 38 m. At R289 per metre, the fencing costs 38 × 289 = R10 982. A learner who fences 41 m has overspent by R867 on material the yard cannot use.

That subtraction is the examinable habit: read the context for openings, gates and gaps before multiplying by a rate. Perimeter questions in this grade almost always include one. The questions for this section are with you now; check the formula, then check the context for what must be taken off the line.

## Subtopic: Area: Rectangles, Triangles and Circles

AREA is the amount of flat surface a shape covers, measured in SQUARE units — square metres, square centimetres. Three formulae carry the grade: rectangle A = length × breadth; triangle A = ½ × base × perpendicular height; circle A = π × radius squared.

The yard again: A = 12,5 × 8 = 100 m². The circular flower bed inside it: A = 3,14 × 1,5² = 3,14 × 2,25 = 7,07 m² rounded to two decimals. A triangular corner bed with base 4 m and height 2,5 m: A = ½ × 4 × 2,5 = 5 m².

COMPOSITE shapes are the grade 11 step up, and they are handled by adding or subtracting whole areas. The paving must cover the yard but not the flower bed, so the paved area is 100 − 7,07 = 92,93 m². Where a shape is an L or a T, cut it into rectangles, find each area, and add. Draw the cut and label the pieces — a marker can follow labelled pieces and award method marks even if one multiplication slips.

Two cautions. First, the triangle's height must be PERPENDICULAR to the base — the vertical drop, not the slanted side. Second, area units are squared, and converting them is not the same as converting lengths. Since 1 m is 100 cm, one square metre is 100 × 100 = 10 000 cm². Learners who divide an area in square centimetres by 100 instead of 10 000 are wrong by a factor of a hundred.

Write the formula, substitute with units, and state the answer in square metres. Turn to the questions for this section now, paying attention to composite shapes and to the perpendicular height of a triangle.

## Subtopic: Walls, Doors and Windows

Painting a room needs the area of its walls — a SURFACE AREA calculation, because the walls are the vertical surfaces of a box. There is a shortcut worth knowing: the total wall area of a rectangular room is the PERIMETER of the floor multiplied by the height of the walls. It works because unrolling the four walls gives one long rectangle whose length is the perimeter.

The room measures 4,2 m by 3,6 m, with walls 2,5 m high. Floor perimeter = 2 × (4,2 + 3,6) = 15,6 m. Wall area = 15,6 × 2,5 = 39 m².

Now subtract what is not painted. A door of 0,9 m by 2,0 m has an area of 1,8 m². A window of 1,5 m by 1,2 m also has an area of 1,8 m². Together they take away 3,6 m², leaving a paintable area of 39 − 3,6 = 35,4 m².

The specification says two coats, and a coat is a full covering of the surface, so the area to be covered is 35,4 × 2 = 70,8 m². This is the step most often skipped, and it doubles the paint bill when it is skipped in the shop rather than on the page.

The same logic extends to the surface area of any rectangular prism the curriculum sets — a storage box, a water tank, a cardboard carton. Total surface area = 2 × (length × breadth) + 2 × (length × height) + 2 × (breadth × height). Take each face in turn, name it, and add. Where a face is missing — an open-topped box, a tank with no lid — leave that face out and say in words that you have done so. Your questions for this section are ready; expect a room with openings to subtract and a number of coats to apply.

## Subtopic: From Square Metres to Rands

The final move converts area into money, and it has three parts: the coverage rate, the wastage allowance, and the packaging.

Coverage rate first. Paint covering 8 m² per litre must cover 70,8 m², so 70,8 ÷ 8 = 8,85 ℓ. The shop sells 5 ℓ tins at R525, so two tins are required, giving 10 ℓ at a cost of 2 × 525 = R1 050. Nobody sells 8,85 litres.

Wastage next. Tiles and paving are cut at the edges and some break, so the trade allows about 10% extra. The floor is 4,2 × 3,6 = 15,12 m². With 10% wastage, order 15,12 × 1,10 = 16,63 m². Each box of tiles covers 1,44 m², so 16,63 ÷ 1,44 = 11,55 boxes — round UP to 12 boxes, because 11 boxes leaves floor showing. At R289 a box, the tiles cost 12 × 289 = R3 468.

Paving the yard uses the same logic on a bigger area. The paved area was 92,93 m²; with 10% wastage that is 102,22 m², and paving bought by the square metre means ordering 103 m². At R145 per square metre, the paving costs 103 × 145 = R14 935.

Then total the project honestly: fencing R10 982, paving R14 935, tiles R3 468, paint R1 050 — R30 435 in materials, before labour. That total is the answer to the question the household is actually asking.

Three disciplines protect these marks. Round only at the end of each independent calculation, not part-way through. Round UP whenever running short would stop the job, and say why in a short phrase. And answer in a sentence with the rand amount, because "12" is not an answer and "12 boxes of tiles, costing R3 468" is. The last questions of this part are in front of you now: coverage rates, wastage and packaging, each turning an area into a number of things to buy.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same project from the pavement outside, where the tape measure is borrowed and the budget is real.

## Subtopic: Walking the Fence

Perimeter is just the walk around the edge. Stand at the corner of the yard and walk the whole way round, back to where you started — that distance is the perimeter, and it is what you pay for when you buy fencing, skirting or a strip of border bricks.

The yard is 12,5 m one way and 8 m the other. Walk it: 12,5 plus 8 plus 12,5 plus 8, which is 41 m. Or do what the formula does — add one long side and one short side, then double it: 12,5 plus 8 is 20,5, doubled is 41 m. Same walk, less writing.

Round things work the same way, they just have their own name. The distance around a circle is the circumference, and it is π — call it 3,14 — times the whole width across the middle. The flower bed is 1,5 m from the centre to the edge, so 3 m across, and 3,14 times 3 is 9,42 m of edging.

Now the part that catches everyone. The fence is not 41 m, because there is a 3 m gate in it, and the gate comes from a different shelf at a different price. So the fencing is 41 minus 3, which is 38 m. At R289 a metre, that is R10 982. Buy the full 41 m and you have wasted R867 on fencing that will stand where the gate must open.

So before you multiply anything by a price, look at the picture again and ask what is not fence: a gate, a driveway, a gap where the wall of the house already closes the line. Some questions on this section are with you right now — walk the whole outline, then take off what is not being bought.

## Subtopic: Covering the Floor and the Walls

Area is different from perimeter, and the difference is simple: perimeter is the walk around, area is the covering over. Fencing is perimeter. Tiles, paint, paving, grass — all area.

For a rectangle, multiply the two sides. The yard is 12,5 by 8, so 100 square metres. The room floor is 4,2 by 3,6, so 15,12 square metres. A triangle is half of a rectangle, so it is half of base times height — a corner bed 4 m along the bottom and 2,5 m up is half of 10, which is 5 square metres. A circle is 3,14 times the radius times the radius again — the flower bed at 1,5 m radius is 3,14 times 2,25, which is 7,07 square metres.

And when a shape is not one neat rectangle, cut it into pieces you recognise, work each one out, then add them. Or, if something must be left out — the flower bed in the middle of the paving — work out the big area and take the small one off: 100 minus 7,07 leaves 92,93 square metres to pave.

Walls are just more rectangles standing up. Here is the trick that saves time: walk the floor's edge to get the perimeter, then multiply by how high the walls are. The room's walk-around is 15,6 m, the walls are 2,5 m high, so 15,6 times 2,5 is 39 square metres of wall. Then take off the door, 0,9 by 2, which is 1,8, and the window, 1,5 by 1,2, also 1,8. That is 3,6 off, leaving 35,4 square metres to paint. And the tin says two coats — so you are really covering 35,4 twice, which is 70,8 square metres. Questions on this section are coming your way now; keep perimeter and area in separate pockets, and take the door and window off before you buy paint.

## Subtopic: The Shop Does Not Sell Half a Box

The last step turns square metres into a shopping list, and the shop has its own rules.

Paint first. The tin covers 8 square metres a litre and you need 70,8 covered, so 70,8 divided by 8 is 8,85 litres. Now walk into the shop and ask for 8,85 litres of paint. They will look at you. They sell 5 ℓ tins at R525, so you take two — 10 litres, R1 050 — and you finish the job with a bit left over for touch-ups.

Tiles next, and tiles bring a new idea: WASTAGE. You cut tiles at the walls, a few break, and the offcuts are useless. So the trade orders about 10% extra. The floor is 15,12 square metres; add 10% and you order 16,63. Each box covers 1,44, so 16,63 divided by 1,44 is 11,55 boxes. Round it UP to 12, always up, because eleven boxes leaves a patch of bare floor and a second trip to the shop. Twelve boxes at R289 is R3 468.

The paving is the same story on a bigger scale: 92,93 square metres to pave, plus 10% is 102,22, so order 103 square metres, and at R145 each that is R14 935.

Then add it all up like a person planning a real job: fence R10 982, paving R14 935, tiles R3 468, paint R1 050 — thirty thousand four hundred and thirty-five rand in materials, before anybody is paid to lay it.

Two rules to carry out of here. Round UP whenever running out would stop the work, and write the reason next to it in a few words. And finish with a sentence someone could act on: "she must buy 12 boxes of tiles, which will cost R3 468." The final questions of the lesson are with you now — turn each area into a number of tins, boxes or square metres, then into rands.
