**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! First one: how do we know whether a question wants perimeter or area? Half of us picked the wrong one and lost everything downstream.

**Tutor:** Ask what the material physically does. If it runs along a line, it is perimeter: fencing, skirting board, curtain rail, a row of edging bricks, ribbon around a cake, burglar bars along a boundary. If it lies flat and covers a surface, it is area: paint, tiles, paving, carpet, grass, plaster, wallpaper. The unit inside the price is a second, very dependable clue. A price quoted per METRE — R315 per metre of fencing — is a perimeter price, because it buys length. A price per SQUARE METRE, or per litre of coverage, or per box covering so many square metres, is an area price. So read the price list before you read the shape, and the price list will reveal which calculation is wanted. A third clue is the wording itself: around, along the boundary, and the edge of all signal perimeter; cover, lay, paint and plant all signal area. Write your chosen word at the top of the working — this is an area question — and the rest of the method falls into place without hesitation.

**Bianca:** Next: why is the wall area the perimeter times the height? It looks like multiplying two lengths to sneak out an area, and that felt like cheating.

**Tutor:** It is not cheating, and seeing why once means never forgetting it. Imagine slicing the room's four walls apart at one corner and unrolling them flat, the way you peel a label off a tin. You are left with a single long rectangle. Its height is the wall height, and its length is the total distance around the room — which is precisely the perimeter of the floor. So the wall area is that long rectangle's length times its height: perimeter times height, the ordinary area formula applied to one unrolled shape instead of four separate ones. You can always verify wall by wall: two walls of 3,9 by 2,6 give 10,14 each, two of 3,2 by 2,6 give 8,32 each, and 20,28 plus 16,64 is the same 36,92 square metres. Same answer, more writing, more places to slip. Use the shortcut when the room is a plain rectangle, and go wall by wall when the room is oddly shaped, has a sloping ceiling, or walls of different heights.

**Bianca:** This one caused real confusion: someone converted square metres to square centimetres by multiplying by 100, and the whole answer collapsed. Why is the factor not 100?

**Tutor:** Because a covering stretches in two directions at once, and the conversion must stretch with it. Picture one square metre chalked on the floor. Its bottom edge is 100 centimetres, and its side is also 100 centimetres, so the little one-centimetre squares filling it run 100 across and 100 up — 100 times 100, which is 10 000 of them. One square metre is 10 000 square centimetres, and multiplying by only 100 leaves you a hundred times short. The same logic threads through every squared unit: a square kilometre is 1 000 times 1 000, a million square metres. It escalates once more for volume, where the stretching happens three ways: one cubic metre is 100 times 100 times 100, a million cubic centimetres. The safest habit sidesteps the problem entirely — convert every LENGTH into the target unit before multiplying, so the area arrives in the right unit from the start and no area conversion is ever needed.

**Bianca:** Last one: the wastage percentage confused us. Do we memorise ten percent, and does it get added before or after working out the boxes?

**Tutor:** Never memorise it, because the question always states it — ten percent is typical for tiling and paving, but a task may specify five, fifteen, or none, and whatever is stated is what you use. The order, though, matters and rewards precision. Add the wastage to the AREA first, then convert the enlarged area into packets. The floor of 12,48 square metres becomes 12,48 times 1,10, which is 13,73 square metres, and only then do you divide by the 1,2 square metres a box covers, giving 11,44 boxes, which rounds up to 12. Work out the boxes first and add ten percent to the box count afterwards, and you can land on a different number, because the rounding then happens in the wrong place. And that final rounding is never ordinary rounding: for materials it always goes upward, because the shop cannot sell a fraction of a box and a fraction short means bare floor. Write a short reason next to the rounded figure — rounded up, since boxes are sold whole — so the marker sees a deliberate decision rather than a lucky one.
