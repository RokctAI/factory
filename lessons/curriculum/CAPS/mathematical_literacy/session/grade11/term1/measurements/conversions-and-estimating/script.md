# Part 1 — Expert

Measurement questions in grade 11 rarely ask for a conversion on its own. They hand over a recipe written in Fahrenheit, a tape measure marked in inches, a paint tin whose label promises square metres per litre, or a bus timetable in the twenty-four-hour clock, and they ask what a person should DO. This session builds the four conversion skills the curriculum names — within the metric system, between metric and imperial, temperature and time, and equivalent-proportionality conversions such as paint coverage and medicine doses — together with the measuring and estimating that surrounds them.

## Subtopic: The Metric Ladder and the Marks in Units

The metric system is built on one thousand. One metre is 1 000 millimetres; one kilometre is 1 000 metres; one kilogram is 1 000 grams; one tonne is 1 000 kilograms; one litre is 1 000 millilitres; one kilolitre is 1 000 litres. The centimetre is the exception worth memorising separately: 1 metre is 100 centimetres, and 1 centimetre is 10 millimetres.

Decide the direction before you touch the calculator. Converting to a SMALLER unit means more of them, so you multiply; converting to a LARGER unit means fewer of them, so you divide. A hosepipe 2,4 km long is 2,4 × 1 000 = 2 400 metres. A 750 g packet of rice is 750 ÷ 1 000 = 0,75 kg. A household using 18 kℓ of water in a month used 18 × 1 000 = 18 000 litres. If your converted number moved the wrong way — a kilometre answer bigger than the metre answer — you have divided when you should have multiplied, and a glance catches it.

Two examination habits sit on top of this. First, convert BEFORE you calculate, never during. If a question gives a room as 4,2 m by 340 cm, put both into metres — 4,2 m by 3,4 m — and only then multiply. Mixing units inside one calculation produces answers wrong by factors of a hundred. Second, write the unit at every line of working: markers credit a correctly converted value even when the final answer is spoiled later, but only if they can see which unit each number carries.

Estimating belongs here too, because it is the check that costs nothing. Keep a few anchors: a little fingernail is about a centimetre wide, a walking pace is close to a metre, a cool drink bottle holds two litres, a bag of mealie meal is 10 kg or 12,5 kg. A bath that holds 8 000 litres or a person of 700 kg is a signal to check the arithmetic, not a result to write down. The questions for this section are with you now; hold the direction rule in mind — smaller unit means multiply — and keep the units attached.

## Subtopic: Crossing Into Imperial: Inches, Pounds and Miles

South Africa is metric, but imperial units survive everywhere: television screens and pipes measured in inches, height given in feet, weights in pounds on imported labels, engine and travel figures in miles. Examination papers always supply the conversion table you need, so the skill is not memorising it — the skill is using it in the right direction and rounding sensibly at the end.

The common conversions are these: 1 inch is 2,54 cm; 1 foot is 12 inches, which is 30,48 cm; 1 mile is about 1,609 km; 1 kilogram is about 2,2 pounds; and 1 ounce is about 28,35 g.

Work an example in each direction. A pocket of cement weighs 50 kg. In pounds that is 50 × 2,2 = 110 pounds. Going the other way, an imported bag of dog food labelled 33 pounds is 33 ÷ 2,2 = 15 kg. A man described as six foot tall is 6 × 30,48 = 182,88 cm, which is 1,83 m rounded to two decimals — a sensible height, which is the estimate check doing its job. And a speed limit posted as 60 miles per hour is 60 × 1,609 = 96,54 km/h, close enough to our own 100 km/h freeway limit to make sense.

The direction trap is the whole difficulty. Ask which unit is BIGGER. A kilogram is bigger than a pound, so a mass in kilograms becomes a bigger NUMBER of pounds: multiply. A mile is bigger than a kilometre, so miles become a bigger number of kilometres: multiply. Reverse either and you divide instead. State the reasoning in one line — "1 kg is more than 1 lb, so the number of pounds must be larger" — and round only at the end, to something the context can use. Your questions for this section are ready now; watch the direction of each conversion and justify it before you calculate.

## Subtopic: Temperature, Time and Timetables

Temperature converts by formula, not by a single multiplier, because the two scales do not start at the same place. To go from Celsius to Fahrenheit: multiply by 9, divide by 5, then add 32. To go from Fahrenheit to Celsius: subtract 32 first, then multiply by 5 and divide by 9.

An American recipe calls for an oven at 350 °F. Subtract 32 to get 318. Multiply by 5 to get 1 590. Divide by 9 to get 176,67 °C — so you set a South African oven to 180 °C, the nearest setting the dial offers. Going the other way, a child with a temperature of 38,5 °C is, in Fahrenheit, 38,5 × 9 = 346,5, divided by 5 = 69,3, plus 32 = 101,3 °F. The order is not negotiable: subtracting the 32 in the wrong place is the single most common error in this section, and it produces answers that are wildly wrong.

Time carries its own rules. The twenty-four-hour clock runs from 00:00 to 23:59, so 14:20 is twenty past two in the afternoon. Duration is found by counting forwards in whole hours first and then the remaining part of an hour, never by subtracting as if the numbers were decimals: from 14:20 to 16:05 is one hour to 15:20, then a further forty-five to 16:05, giving one hour forty-five. Subtracting 14,20 from 16,05 on a calculator gives 1,85, which is not a time at all.

Timetables put both skills together. A long-distance bus leaves Mthatha at 05:45 and the journey takes six hours forty. Add the whole hours: 05:45 becomes 11:45. Add the remainder: forty past 11:45 is 12:25, so the bus arrives at 12:25. The minutes rolled over the hour — 45 plus 40 is 85, which is one hour and 25 — and that rollover is where marks are lost. Take the questions for this section now, with attention to the order of operations in the temperature formula and to counting a duration forwards rather than subtracting it.

## Subtopic: Proportional Conversions: Coverage, Doses and Recipes

The fourth kind of conversion has no fixed table at all. Instead the context supplies a rate — this much of one thing corresponds to that much of another — and you scale it. The curriculum's own examples are a tin of paint that covers a stated area and a teaspoon that holds about 5 mℓ.

Paint first. A label reads: 5 ℓ covers 20 m². Reduce it to a unit rate: 20 ÷ 5 = 4, so one litre covers 4 m². A wall measures 54 m² and needs two coats, so the total area to cover is 54 × 2 = 108 m². Paint required is 108 ÷ 4 = 27 ℓ. Now the part learners forget: the shop does not sell 27 litres. It sells 5 ℓ tins at R429 and 20 ℓ drums at R1 549. Buying one drum and two tins gives 30 ℓ for 1 549 + 858 = R2 407; buying six tins gives the same 30 ℓ for R2 574. The drum-and-tins combination saves R167, and the answer to the question "what should she buy" is that combination, stated in a sentence.

Medicine next. A syrup is dosed at 7,5 mℓ three times a day for five days. Total needed is 7,5 × 3 × 5 = 112,5 mℓ, so a single 100 mℓ bottle is not enough and two must be bought. And with no medicine spoon in the house, convert to what there is: at about 5 mℓ per teaspoon, 7,5 mℓ is one and a half teaspoons.

Recipes work identically. A recipe serving 4 must serve 30, so every quantity multiplies by 30 ÷ 4 = 7,5. The 250 mℓ of milk becomes 1 875 mℓ, which is 1,875 ℓ. The 3 eggs become 22,5, and since half an egg cannot be bought, 23 eggs. That last step is the lesson of this section: rounding obeys the context. Paint and eggs round UP, because running short stops the job; money rounds to cents; a dose is never rounded at all. The final questions of this part are in front of you now: expect a coverage rate, a scaling calculation, and a decision about what to buy.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same conversions from the kitchen and the hardware shop, where getting it wrong means a second trip and money you did not plan to spend.

## Subtopic: Moving the Decimal Point

Everything metric is built on a thousand, and once you see that, half the fear goes. A thousand millimetres in a metre. A thousand metres in a kilometre. A thousand grams in a kilogram. A thousand millilitres in a litre. A thousand litres in a kilolitre. Same number, over and over. Only the centimetre breaks the rhythm: a hundred of them in a metre.

Now, which way? Forget rules for a second and think about size. A kilometre is a long way; a metre is one big step. So there must be LOTS of metres in a kilometre — 2,4 km becomes 2 400 m. Going back the other way there must be FEWER kilometres than metres, so you divide. Smaller unit, bigger number. Bigger unit, smaller number. If your answer breaks that, you have gone the wrong way, and you can fix it before anyone marks it.

Then the check nobody teaches and everybody needs: does the answer make sense in the real world? Your little fingernail is about a centimetre wide. One walking step is roughly a metre. A big cool drink bottle holds two litres. A bag of mealie meal is ten or twelve and a half kilograms. So if your working says the bath holds eight thousand litres, or your cousin weighs seven hundred kilograms, stop — something went wrong three lines ago.

Last thing, small but it pays: if one measurement is in centimetres and another in metres, fix that FIRST, before you multiply anything. Questions on this section are with you now — check the direction, then check the size against something real.

## Subtopic: Granny's Recipe in Fahrenheit

Some measurements come from another world entirely. An old recipe book says bake at 350 degrees — but that is Fahrenheit, and your oven speaks Celsius. A tape measure from the hardware shop has inches down one edge. Your uncle says he is six foot. Imported dog food is labelled in pounds.

For length and weight it is one multiply or one divide, and the paper gives you the number. A pocket of cement is 50 kg; in pounds that is 50 times 2,2, which is 110 pounds. That imported bag saying 33 pounds is 33 divided by 2,2, which is 15 kg. Six foot is 6 times 30,48 centimetres, which is 182,88 cm — call it 1,83 m, and yes, that is a tall man, so the answer passes the sense check.

Which way, again? Ask which unit is bigger. A kilogram is heavier than a pound, so one kilogram must be worth SEVERAL pounds — multiply. Same logic as rands and cents: the bigger unit always turns into more of the smaller one.

Temperature is the one that needs care, because the two scales do not start together. Celsius starts freezing at 0, Fahrenheit starts freezing at 32. So to go from Fahrenheit down to Celsius, take that 32 off FIRST, then times 5, then divide by 9. Granny's 350 degrees: 350 take away 32 is 318; times 5 is 1 590; divide by 9 is 176,67 — so set the oven to 180 and the cake will be fine. Going the other way for a sick child at 38,5 °C: times 9 is 346,5, divide by 5 is 69,3, add 32 is 101,3 °F. Take the 32 off first coming down, put it on last going up. Some questions on this section are coming to you now — go slowly through the order of the steps.

## Subtopic: How Much Paint, How Much Medicine

The last kind of conversion has no table. The tin itself tells you the rate, and you do the sharing.

The label says 5 litres covers 20 square metres. Break it down to one litre: 20 divided by 5 is 4, so one litre covers 4 m². Your wall is 54 m² and it needs two coats, so really you are covering 54 times 2, which is 108 m². Paint needed: 108 divided by 4, which is 27 litres.

But here is where real life arrives. The shop does not sell 27 litres. It sells 5 ℓ tins at R429 and a 20 ℓ drum at R1 549. One drum plus two tins is 30 litres for R2 407. Six tins is also 30 litres, but it costs R2 574. Same paint, R167 difference — and that R167 is why this is a maths literacy question and not an arithmetic exercise. The answer is not "27 litres". It is "buy one 20 ℓ drum and two 5 ℓ tins, which costs R2 407 and is R167 cheaper than six tins."

Same thinking with medicine. The dose is 7,5 mℓ, three times a day, for five days: 7,5 times 3 is 22,5 mℓ a day, times 5 days is 112,5 mℓ. The bottle holds 100 mℓ, so one will not finish the course — buy two. And with no medicine spoon in the house, a teaspoon holds about 5 mℓ, so 7,5 mℓ is one and a half teaspoons.

Cooking for a crowd is the same move. A recipe feeds 4, the fundraiser needs 30, so everything multiplies by 30 divided by 4, which is 7,5. The 250 mℓ of milk becomes 1 875 mℓ — nearly two litres. The 3 eggs become 22,5, and since nobody sells half an egg, you buy 23. Round paint and eggs UP, round money to cents, never round a dose. The last questions of the lesson are with you now — read the rate off the label first, then scale it, then decide what a person actually buys.
