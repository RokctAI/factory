# Part 1 — Expert

Perimeter and area questions in Paper 2 always arrive wearing work clothes: a fence to price, a floor to pave, a round bed to plant. The formulae are printed on the exam paper — your job is to choose the right one, feed it correctly, and turn the answer into money. Our project is a community food garden: a rectangular plot 12 metres long and 8 metres wide, holding a triangular seedling bed with base 6 metres and height 4 metres, and a semicircular herb bed of radius 2 metres against the north wall. We fence the plot, plant the lawn strips, and price everything — including the trap that catches more candidates than any other in this section: the flat edge of a fraction of a circle. Throughout, pi is taken as 3,142.

## Subtopic: Perimeter and Area — the Fence and the Floor

Two different measurements, two different units, two different jobs.

Perimeter is the distance around the outside — a length, measured in metres. It answers fencing, edging, skirting and framing questions. Area is the amount of flat surface inside — measured in square metres — and it answers paving, painting, planting and tiling questions. Before any formula, ask: is this question walking around the edge, or covering the inside? That single decision is the first mark.

The rectangular plot: length 12 metres, width 8 metres. Perimeter: twice the sum of length and width — 2 times 12 plus 8, which is 2 times 20: 40 metres of edge. Area: length times width — 12 times 8: 96 square metres of ground.

Context immediately complicates the clean numbers, and that is deliberate. The garden needs a gate 1,5 metres wide, and nobody fences a gateway. Fencing needed: 40 minus 1,5 gives 38,5 metres. Exam questions almost always subtract an opening — a gate, a doorway, a driveway — and the learners who lose marks are the ones who fenced straight across the gate.

Units guard the whole calculation. Perimeter answers in metres; area answers in square metres. If an answer to a fencing question says 96, the units have already caught the error: 96 what? Square metres cannot be bought as fencing. Say the unit with the number, every time, and wrong-formula errors announce themselves.

The questions for this section are with you now. Each one asks you first to decide: edge or inside — and then to respect the gate.

## Subtopic: Triangles and Circles with Given Formulae

The seedling bed is a triangle with base 6 metres and height 4 metres. The given formula: area equals half times base times height. Substitute: half of 6 times 4 — half of 24 — 12 square metres. The height must be the perpendicular height — the straight-up distance from base to the top point, not the slanted side. Exam diagrams often print both lengths precisely to see whether you grab the slant by mistake; the formula wants the upright one.

Now the circle, with its two given formulae. Circumference — the circle's word for perimeter — equals 2 times pi times the radius. Area equals pi times the radius squared. One vocabulary pair first: the radius runs from the centre to the edge; the diameter runs edge to edge through the centre, so the diameter is twice the radius. Questions hand you the diameter hoping you will use it as the radius — halve it first, always.

Take a round pond of radius 3,5 metres. Circumference: 2 times 3,142 times 3,5. Compute: 2 times 3,5 is 7, and 7 times 3,142 gives 21,994 — about 21,99 metres of edging. Area: 3,142 times 3,5 squared. Square first: 3,5 times 3,5 is 12,25. Then 3,142 times 12,25 gives 38,4895 — about 38,49 square metres.

Watch the order in that area calculation: the radius is squared BEFORE multiplying by pi. Squaring 3,5 gives 12,25; multiplying 3,142 by 3,5 first and then squaring would give a wildly wrong answer. The formula reads pi times radius-squared: the little raised two belongs to the radius alone.

Work through this section's questions. They test the perpendicular height, the radius-diameter halving, and the square-before-multiply order.

## Subtopic: Fractions of Circles — the Trap in the Perimeter

Grade 10 measures quarter circles, semicircles and three-quarter circles, and this is where the section's biggest trap lives.

Area behaves politely. A semicircle is half a circle, so its area is half the full area. The herb bed: radius 2 metres. Full circle area: 3,142 times 2 squared — 3,142 times 4 — 12,568. Half of that: 6,284 — about 6,28 square metres of herbs. A quarter circle of the same radius: a quarter of 12,568 is 3,142 square metres. Three-quarter: 9,426 square metres. Take the fraction of the area, done.

Perimeter does NOT behave. Here is the trap: the perimeter of a semicircle is not half the circumference. Walk it and feel why. The full circumference of the radius-2 circle is 2 times 3,142 times 2 — 12,568 metres. Walking the curved edge of the semicircle covers half: 6,284 metres. But you are not back where you started — you are standing at one end of the flat side, and the walk around any shape ends where it began. The flat side is the diameter, 4 metres. True perimeter: 6,284 plus 4 gives 10,284 — about 10,28 metres. Forget the straight edge and the edging order comes up 4 metres short.

The quarter circle is worse, with TWO straight edges. Curved part: a quarter of 12,568 is 3,142. Plus two radii: 2 plus 2. Perimeter: 3,142 plus 4 gives 7,142 — about 7,14 metres. The three-quarter circle: curved part 9,426, plus the two radii where the missing quarter was cut out: 9,426 plus 4 gives 13,426 — about 13,43 metres.

The rule that holds all of this: area takes the fraction and stops; perimeter takes the fraction of the curve, then walks home along the straight edges. Every semicircular window frame, every curved flowerbed border in every past paper turns on that rule.

This section's questions are in front of you. For each shape, trace the walk in your mind and count every edge underfoot — curved and straight.

## Subtopic: From Measurements to Money

Now the part that makes this Mathematical Literacy: the shop. Materials are sold in fixed units — rolls, bags, boxes — and the shop does not sell fractions. The rounding rule of the entire Measurement section: material quantities always round UP.

Price the fence. We need 38,5 metres of fencing, sold in 10-metre rolls at R350 per roll. Divide: 38,5 over 10 gives 3,85 rolls. The shop sells 4 rolls or 3 — and 3 rolls is 30 metres, which leaves an 8,5-metre hole guarded by hope. Round up: 4 rolls. Cost: 4 times 350 gives R1 400. Rounding 3,85 down, or to the nearest whole, is the single most-penalised move in this question type; with materials, 3,85 becomes 4 even though 3,85 is nearer to 4 anyway — and 3,1 would ALSO become 4.

Price the lawn. Grass seed goes on the plot area not occupied by beds: 96 minus the triangle's 12 minus the semicircle's 6,28 gives 77,72 square metres. One bag covers 20 square metres and costs R120. Divide: 77,72 over 20 gives 3,886 bags. Round up: 4 bags. Cost: 4 times 120 gives R480. Total so far: 1 400 plus 480 gives R1 880 — and an exam question will happily add cement, labour at a daily rate, or VAT on top.

The complete method, six steps. One: decide edge or inside — perimeter or area. Two: choose the printed formula and identify each letter's value, halving diameters as needed. Three: substitute and compute in order — squares before multiplication, fractions of circles handled curve-plus-straight-edges for perimeter. Four: subtract openings like gates, add straight edges of part-circles. Five: convert to purchase units and round UP. Six: multiply by price, state the answer in rand, and sense-check the size.

The closing questions of this part are with you now. They run the full pipeline — shape to formula to metres to rolls to rand.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Same garden, same numbers — now with mud on our boots.

## Subtopic: Walking the Edge, Painting the Floor

Stand at the garden gate. There are only two questions any measurement problem can ask about this plot, and your feet can tell them apart.

Question one: how far do you WALK to go all the way around? That is perimeter. It is a walk, so it is measured in metres, and it prices anything that follows the edge — fencing, plastic edging, rope.

Question two: how much ground would you PAINT if you painted everything inside the fence? That is area. It is a surface, measured in square metres — imagine metre-by-metre carpet tiles laid out — and it prices anything that covers — seed, paving, fertiliser.

The walk around our 12 by 8 plot: down a long side, 12. Across the short side, 8. Back along the other long side, 12. Home across the last side, 8. Total: 40 metres. The paint job: 12 rows of 8 carpet tiles each — 96 tiles — 96 square metres.

Then reality edits the sum: the gate. It is 1,5 metres of the walk where no fence goes — so the fence shopping list reads 38,5 metres, not 40. Almost every exam plot has a gate, a doorway or a gap, and it is always mentioned in the words, not always shown clearly. Read for the opening before you price the edge.

Feet for the edge, paint for the inside — the questions for this piece are with you now. Ask which one before touching any formula.

## Subtopic: Half a Pizza Still Has a Straight Side

The semicircle trap makes sense the moment you think about pizza. A full pizza has a crust all the way around — that crust is the circumference. Now take half the pizza. How much crust? Half, obviously — the curve. But run your finger around the edge of that half-pizza: curve... and then the straight cut edge. The half-pizza's full edge is half the crust PLUS the straight cut. That straight edge is exactly what learners forget when a question asks for the edging around a semicircular flowerbed.

Numbers for the herb bed, radius 2 metres. Whole crust: 2 times 3,142 times 2 — 12,568 metres. Half the crust: 6,284. The straight cut is edge to edge through the centre — the diameter — 4 metres. Full edge of the bed: 6,284 plus 4, which is 10,28 metres of edging, said to two decimals. Order 6,28 and the bed lies open along its whole straight side.

Cut the pizza again into a quarter and now there are TWO straight sides, each one a radius. Quarter crust: 3,142. Two straight sides: 2 and 2. Edge: 7,14 metres. And the three-quarter pizza — the one with a slice missing — keeps three-quarters of the crust, 9,426, plus the two straight sides of the missing slice's cut: total 13,43 metres.

The filling, though, never tricks anyone: half a pizza has half the topping, a quarter has a quarter. Area just takes the fraction. It is only the EDGE — the walk, the crust — that picks up straight sides. Curve plus cuts: say it every time you meet a part-circle.

Your questions for this section are up. Trace the crust with your finger, then count the cuts.

## Subtopic: The Shop Sells Rolls, Not Metres

The garden maths says 38,5 metres of fencing. Now stand at the hardware counter and hear the assistant say: it comes in 10-metre rolls, R350 each. The shop does not cut rolls. So how many rolls?

Divide first: 38,5 over 10 is 3,85 rolls. And here is the one place in all of mathematics where your rounding teacher's rule gets overruled: 3,85 rounds UP to 4 — and so would 3,1. Because 3 rolls is 30 metres, and a 30-metre fence around a 38,5-metre job is a fence with a hole in it. When buying materials, the question is never which whole number is nearest; it is what is the smallest amount that finishes the job. That is always the round UP. Four rolls: R1 400.

Same story at the seed shelf. The lawn is the plot minus the beds: 96 minus 12 minus 6,28 leaves 77,72 square metres. A bag covers 20 square metres: 77,72 over 20 is 3,886 bags. Round up: 4 bags at R120 — R480. Yes, there will be seed left over. Leftovers are the price of a finished lawn; a shortfall is a bald patch in the middle of the garden.

So the money pipeline is always: measurement, divide by pack size, round UP, multiply by price. Fence: 38,5 becomes 4 rolls becomes R1 400. Seed: 77,72 becomes 4 bags becomes R480. Project total: R1 880 — and if the question adds a labourer at R250 a day for two days, that is R500 more, giving R2 380. Every step shown, every unit named, every rounding upward.

Last questions of the lesson are with you now. Measure, divide, round up, price — and read the packaging line in the question as carefully as the shape itself.
