**Thandi:** Time for the questions that come up a lot in this section! First: the formula sheet gives me every formula anyway, so what is actually being tested?

**Tutor:** Three decisions the sheet cannot make for you. First, choosing WHICH formula — and that comes from reading the job in the question: fencing, edging and bordering are perimeter jobs; paving, planting, painting and tiling are area jobs. The formula sheet lists both and waits for you to pick. Second, feeding the formula correctly: identifying the base and the perpendicular height on the triangle, halving a diameter before using it as a radius, squaring before multiplying in pi times radius squared. Third, finishing in context: subtracting the gate from the fence, adding the straight edges to a semicircle's border, rounding 3,85 rolls up to 4, multiplying by the price. Notice that all three decisions live in the words of the question, not in the formula. That is why strong candidates read a measurement question twice before touching the calculator: once for the shape, once for the job. The formula is the free part; the marks are paid for the decisions around it.

***

**Thandi:** Next one, the classic: why is the perimeter of a semicircle not just half the circumference? Half a circle, half the edge — it feels completely logical.

**Tutor:** It feels logical because half the CURVE is indeed there — the error is forgetting that cutting a circle in half creates a brand-new edge that the full circle never had: the straight cut along the diameter. Perimeter means the complete boundary you would walk to end up back where you started. Walk the herb bed of radius 2: the curved half of the circumference is half of 12,568, which is 6,284 metres, but that walk strands you at one end of the flat side. To get home you walk the diameter, 4 metres more. So the true perimeter is 6,284 plus 4 — 10,28 metres. The area, by contrast, genuinely is half, 6,28 square metres, because cutting the circle creates no new surface, only new edge. That is the deep difference: cuts create edges, not area. It is also why the quarter circle picks up two radii — two cuts — and the three-quarter circle likewise gains the two radii where the missing slice was removed. Whenever a shape has been cut from a circle, trace its boundary with a finger and count every straight edge the cutting created.

***

**Thandi:** Third: with the fencing rolls, 3,85 rounds to 4 and I accept that — but my friend rounded 3,1 rolls up to 4 as well and was marked correct. Why?

**Tutor:** Because materials questions replace the nearest-number rule with a different question entirely: what is the smallest whole number of packs that finishes the job? Three rolls give 30 metres against a 38,5-metre fence line — the job fails, so three is not an option no matter how close 3,1 sits to 3. Four rolls is the first quantity that covers the need, so four is the answer for 3,85 rolls AND for 3,1 rolls alike. The nearest-rounding habit comes from pure arithmetic, where the number itself is the answer; here the number is a purchase decision, and an undersupplied purchase is a wrong answer in the real world before it is wrong on the memo. The same logic runs through the seed bags: 3,886 becomes 4, but so would 3,2. One caution though — this rule belongs to buying materials only. When a question asks how many full boxes CAN be filled from a given supply, the rounding flips downward, because a part-filled box is not a full box. Read whether you are buying to cover a need or filling from a stock, and the rounding direction chooses itself.

**Thandi:** Last one: my final answer to the fence-cost question was just R1 400 and I lost method marks. What should the page have shown?

**Tutor:** The marker needed the pipeline, because each stage carries its own mark. Stage one: the perimeter formula and its substitution — 2 times 12 plus 8, giving 40 metres. Stage two: the context adjustment — 40 minus the 1,5-metre gate, giving 38,5 metres of actual fencing. Stage three: the purchase conversion — 38,5 divided by 10 gives 3,85 rolls, rounded up to 4, with the words rounded up written next to it so the marker sees the decision rather than an accident. Stage four: the money — 4 times R350 gives R1 400. Four lines, roughly four marks, and if a slip happens in one line, the lines after it can still earn follow-through marks because your method is visible. A bare R1 400 gambles everything on one number being perfect. In measurement questions the working IS the answer; the final rand figure is just its last line.
