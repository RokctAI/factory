# Part 1 — Expert

Substitution into formulae quietly carries marks on both Mathematical Literacy papers every year — in Measurement, where perimeter, area and volume formulae are printed for you, and in Finance, where tariff formulae do the same work. A formula is a machine: values go in, an answer comes out. This session runs one machine in both directions. The machine is a car-hire tariff — cost equals R450 plus R12,50 times the number of kilometres driven. Forwards, it prices a 340 kilometre trip. Backwards, it answers the harder question of how far a budget of R1 200 will take you. Between the two directions sit the errors that cost the most marks: breaking the order of operations, and dividing a budget before removing the fixed charge.

## Subtopic: Reading a Formula Like a Sentence

Cost = R450 + R12,50 × km. Before any calculating, translate the symbols into the story they tell, because a formula you can narrate is a formula you cannot misuse.

The R450 stands alone — no kilometres multiply it. It is the fixed charge: hand over the keys and R450 is owed before the wheels turn. Perhaps it covers admin and insurance; whatever the reason, it does not care how far you drive.

The R12,50 is glued to the kilometres by multiplication: every kilometre adds R12,50 to the bill. It is the rate — the variable part, the part that grows with usage. Drive nothing, pay nothing here; drive far, and this term does the damage.

So the formula says, in words: the total cost is the R450 entrance fee, plus R12,50 for every kilometre driven. Fixed plus rate-times-usage — the same skeleton as electricity tariffs, cellphone contracts, plumber call-out fees, delivery charges. Learn this skeleton once and you will recognise it in half the Finance and Measurement questions you ever meet.

Two vocabulary notes exams rely on. The quantities that can change — cost and kilometres — are variables. The kilometres, which we choose, is the independent variable; the cost, which responds, is the dependent variable — the cost depends on the distance. Say it in the direction the dependence flows and you will never mix up which value to substitute where.

The questions for this section are with you now. They test whether you can name each part of the formula in words: which number is the fixed charge, which is the rate, and which of the two variables depends on the other.

## Subtopic: Forward Substitution — Pricing the 340 km Trip

The first question: what does a 340 kilometre trip cost? The kilometres are known; the cost is wanted. When the known value slots straight into the right-hand side, that is forward substitution — the easy direction, if you respect the order of operations.

Write the formula first, always: cost = 450 + 12,50 × km. In an exam, that written line is usually a mark all by itself.

Substitute, replacing the variable with its number: cost = 450 + 12,50 × 340. Write this substitution line too — another mark, and your safety net: an examiner can follow your thinking even if a calculator slip follows.

Now compute — and here stands the great trap of forward substitution: the order of operations. Multiplication BEFORE addition, always. The wrong road adds first: 450 plus 12,50 gives 462,50, times 340 gives a monstrous R157 250 — a hire-car bill that buys the car. The right road multiplies first.

So: 12,50 times 340. Break it apart: 12 times 340 is 4 080; 0,50 times 340 is 170; together 4 250. The distance charge is R4 250.

Then add the fixed charge: 450 plus 4 250 equals 4 700.

The 340 kilometre trip costs R4 700. Sense-check it against the story: the trip charge should dwarf the entrance fee on a long drive — R4 250 of driving against R450 of admin. Reasonable. And units: rands out of a rand formula. Done.

Work through this section's questions before we turn the machine around. Between them they cover the order of operations, the value of the substitution line, and the sense-check that would have caught the R157 250 disaster before it reached the marker.

## Subtopic: Reverse Substitution — How Far Does R1 200 Go?

Now the direction that separates candidates: I have R1 200 — how many kilometres does it buy? This time the COST is known and the kilometres are wanted. The known value belongs to the left-hand side, and the unknown hides inside the right. Substitute what you know, then dig the unknown out.

Substitute: 1 200 = 450 + 12,50 × km.

Read what the equation says before touching it: the R1 200 covers the R450 fixed fee plus some driving. So the first move almost states itself: remove the fixed fee to see what money remains for actual kilometres. Subtract 450 from both sides: 1 200 minus 450 is 750. So 750 = 12,50 × km. In money language: after paying the entrance fee, R750 remains for driving.

Second move: each kilometre swallows R12,50, so ask how many R12,50s fit into R750. Divide both sides by 12,50: km = 750 over 12,50. Compute: 12,50 times 60 is 750 — exactly. So km = 60.

R1 200 buys a 60 kilometre trip.

Notice what we did in algebra terms: the formula was built by multiplying then adding; we unwound it by subtracting then dividing — inverse operations, applied in reverse order, like taking off shoes before socks. That unwinding IS the method for every reverse question: peel away the fixed part first, then divide by the rate.

Verify — and reverse questions practically beg for verification, because you can run the machine forwards with your answer. Substitute 60 back: cost = 450 + 12,50 times 60 = 450 + 750 = 1 200. Exactly the budget. The answer is proven, not just hoped.

This section's questions are in front of you. Watch the option offering 96 kilometres — it is the entire trap compressed into one line — and be ready to state how a reverse answer proves itself.

## Subtopic: The General Method, the Classic Traps, and Where This Skill Lives

The complete method, six steps, for any substitution question in any context. One: write the formula down unchanged. Two: identify which variable is known and which is wanted. Three: substitute the known value, writing the full line. Four: forwards — compute respecting order of operations; backwards — unwind with inverse operations, fixed part first, rate last. Five: state the answer WITH its unit — R4 700; 60 kilometres. Six: verify — sense-check the size, and for reverse answers, substitute back.

Now the traps, ranked by the damage they do. Trap one: order of operations — adding the fixed charge to the rate before multiplying. The R157 250 disaster. Your defence: multiplication first, always, and a sense check that would have screamed at a six-figure car hire. Trap two, in reverse questions: dividing the whole budget by the rate without removing the fixed charge — 1 200 over 12,50 gives 96 kilometres, wrong, because R450 of that budget never bought a single kilometre. Peel the fixed fee first. Trap three: dropping units or mixing them — if a formula wants kilometres and the question supplies metres, convert before substituting. Trap four: rounding midway — carry full values through and round only the final answer, and with money, sensibly: to the cent, or as the context demands.

Where does this skill live in your exams? Everywhere. Measurement, Question 3 of Paper 2: perimeter, area, volume formulas — substitution. Finance, Question 2 of Paper 1: tariff formulas exactly like today's — substitution, both directions. Conversion formulas between units, BMI calculations, distance-speed-time — substitution again. The formula changes costume; the six steps never change.

One last encouragement: substitution is the rare skill where the exam GIVES you the mathematics — the formula is printed on the paper. Your job is only to feed it carefully and unwind it patiently. Write every line, respect the order, check the answer. Do that, and these are marks you collect every single time.

The closing questions of this part are with you now. They run across the unwinding order, unit conversion before substitution, and when rounding is permitted — three habits that protect marks in every formula question you will ever be set.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same hire car from the counter at the rental desk — same R450, same R12,50 a kilometre, same two answers, told the way you would work it out for a family trip.

## Subtopic: The Gate Fee and the Meter

Think about going to a soccer match. There are two completely different kinds of money in your pocket that day. There is the gate fee — you pay it once, at the entrance, and you pay it whether you watch the whole match or leave at half-time. Then there is the money you spend inside, which depends entirely on how many pies you buy. One is fixed. The other grows.

Hiring the car is exactly that. The R450 is the gate fee: it is charged the moment they hand you the keys, and it is the same R450 whether you drive to the corner shop or to Bloemfontein. The R12,50 is the pie money: every single kilometre you drive costs you another R12,50. So the bill is always the gate fee plus the driving money.

Notice how the formula says the same sentence. Cost equals R450 plus R12,50 times the kilometres. The R450 sits by itself with nothing attached to it — that is your clue that it is the once-off charge. The R12,50 is stuck to the kilometres by a multiplication sign — that is your clue that it repeats, once for every kilometre.

Once you can hear that sentence in a formula, you can hear it everywhere. The plumber who charges to come out and then charges by the hour. The prepaid electricity meter with a service fee. The cellphone contract with a monthly subscription and then a rate per gigabyte. Same skeleton: one amount that never moves, and one amount that grows with how much you use.

And one last piece of exam vocabulary, which is easier than it sounds. The cost depends on how far you drive — you choose the kilometres, and the bill answers you. So the kilometres are called the independent variable, because they go first and choose freely, and the cost is called the dependent variable, because it waits to see what you did.

Good — the questions for this piece are with you now. All they want is that you can point at each number in the formula and say, in ordinary words, which one is the gate fee and which one is the meter running.

## Subtopic: Running the Machine Forwards — and the R157 250 Disaster

You are driving 340 kilometres to a family funeral. What does the hire car cost?

Take it in the order the day happens. First the driving: 340 kilometres at R12,50 each. Do it in two friendly pieces — 340 twelves is 4 080, and 340 half-rands is another R170 — so the driving comes to R4 250. Then, at the very end, the gate fee: R450. Add them: R4 250 plus R450 is R4 700. The trip costs R4 700.

Now the famous disaster, because the exam will offer it to you as a tempting option. Somebody reads left to right, adds the R450 to the R12,50 first to get R462,50, and multiplies that by 340. Out comes R157 250. That is not a hire car, that is buying the car.

Here is what actually went wrong, in plain language. Adding first is like paying the stadium gate fee once for every pie you buy. You bought 340 kilometres, so you paid the R450 gate fee 340 times over — and 340 times R450 really is R153 000. Add the honest R4 250 of driving and there is your R157 250, phantom gate fees and all. Nobody is charged to walk in 340 times. The gate fee happens once, at the end of the sum, and never gets multiplied.

So the rule everybody teaches as "multiply before you add" is not an arbitrary maths law here. It is just the story: work out the part that repeats, then add on the part that happens once.

Finish with the check that costs you nothing. Does R4 700 sound like a hire car for a long trip? Yes — most of it is driving, R4 250, with a small admin charge on top. Does R157 250 sound like a hire car? Absolutely not, and that alarm should ring before you circle anything.

Your questions on this section are up now. Do the multiplying part on its own first, then add the once-off, and let your common sense look at every answer before you commit to it.

## Subtopic: Running It Backwards — R1 200 in Your Pocket

Now flip the machine around, because this is the version real life asks. You have R1 200. How far can you actually go?

Do not touch the R12,50 yet. First ask what you have to pay before you drive anywhere: R450, at the counter, gone. So from your R1 200, only R1 200 minus R450 — that is R750 — is money that can actually turn into kilometres. It is like walking into a shop with R1 200 in your hand after paying R450 for the taxi to get there: the shelves can only see R750.

Now the easy part. Each kilometre costs R12,50, so how many R12,50s fit into R750? Twelve fifty times sixty is exactly seven hundred and fifty, so sixty kilometres. R1 200 buys you a 60 kilometre trip.

Here is where people lose it. If you skip the peeling step and just divide the whole R1 200 by R12,50, you get 96 kilometres and you feel confident. But look at the extra 36 kilometres you invented: that is exactly what R450 would buy if the counter had let you spend it on driving — R450 divided by R12,50 is 36. Those kilometres were never on offer. Your error explains itself right down to the number.

The order matters and it is worth saying out loud, because it is backwards from how the bill was built. The bill was built by multiplying and then adding. So you undo it by subtracting and then dividing — like getting undressed at night: shoes come off before socks, in the opposite order from how they went on.

And now the best habit in the whole lesson: prove your own answer. Take the 60 kilometres and run the machine forwards on it. Sixty times R12,50 is R750, plus the R450 gate fee is R1 200 — precisely your budget. Nothing left over, nothing missing. When your answer walks back through the machine and lands exactly on the number the question gave you, you are not hoping any more; you know.

Last questions of the lesson are with you now. Peel the once-off charge first, divide second, then send your answer back through the formula to check it — and formula questions will start feeling like free marks, because that is exactly what they are.
