**Student:** How do I know when to multiply first? Sometimes I look at the formula and just... add left to right.

**Thandi:** Left-to-right reading is perfect for storybooks and a disaster for formulas! Maths has a fixed pecking order: brackets first, then multiplication and division, then addition and subtraction — no matter where they sit in the line. In our formula, the 12,50 × km are glued together as one package; the + 450 waits outside until that package is finished. Try this habit: before calculating, circle the multiplication like it's one object. Then the formula reads "450 + (one circled thing)" and you'd never dream of breaking into the circle to add first. Circle, multiply, then add.

**Student:** In the R1 200 question, my answer was 96 km and I was so confident. Where did the extra 36 km come from?

**Thandi:** Ah, the phantom kilometres! You divided the whole R1 200 by R12,50 — but R450 of that money never bought a single kilometre; it paid the fixed hire fee at the door. Only R750 actually went to driving, and R750 ÷ R12,50 = 60 km. Your extra 36 km is exactly what R450 would buy IF it went to distance: 450 ÷ 12,50 = 36. See how neatly the error explains itself? The cure: in any budget question, first ask "what gets paid before I use anything?" Peel that off, then divide.

**Student:** Do I really have to write the formula and the substitution line every time? It's the same formula the paper just gave me!

**Thandi:** I know it feels like copying homework you've already done — but yes, write both lines, every time, and here's the selfish reason: those lines are usually worth marks *on their own*. Memos typically award a mark for the correct substitution before any arithmetic happens. Even better, they're your insurance policy: if your finger slips on the calculator, the marker can see your method was right and give you the method marks anyway. Two lines of writing, potentially half the question's marks protected. Best deal in the exam hall.

**Student:** What if the formula in the exam looks scary — like something with brackets and squares I've never seen?

**Thandi:** Here's the secret: you don't need to understand WHY a formula works to use it perfectly. BMI, volume of a cylinder, a bank's interest formula — the six steps never change: write it, identify known and wanted, substitute carefully (units converted first!), compute in the right order, answer with units, check the size makes sense. The formula is a recipe; you're the cook, not the chef who invented it. Scary-looking formulas are actually GIFTS — everyone else panics, while you calmly feed the machine and collect the marks.
