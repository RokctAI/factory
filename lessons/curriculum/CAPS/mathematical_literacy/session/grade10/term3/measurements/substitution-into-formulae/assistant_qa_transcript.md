**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! The first one is about the order of operations, and it is asked with real frustration: how am I supposed to know to multiply first? My eyes read left to right, so I add the R450 first and it feels natural.

**Tutor:** Then stop thinking of it as a rule and think of it as the story the bill is telling. The R12,50 is charged once for every kilometre; the R450 is charged once, full stop, at the counter. If you add them together before multiplying, you are multiplying the R450 by the distance as well — which means charging the counter fee separately for each of the 340 kilometres. That is 340 times R450, which is R153 000 of fees that nobody asked for, and it is exactly the difference between the honest R4 700 and the ridiculous R157 250. Once you hear the sentence, the order takes care of itself: do the part that repeats first, then add the part that happens only once. If you would rather have a mechanical habit, treat the multiplication as a single sealed parcel — the formula reads "450 plus one parcel", and you would never break open a parcel to add something into it before it is finished. Parcel first, then add.

***

**Thandi:** Next question, from a learner who is genuinely puzzled. On the budget question she got 96 kilometres and was completely confident. Where did the extra 36 kilometres come from?

**Tutor:** They came from spending the counter fee twice. She took the whole R1 200 and divided it by the rate of R12,50, which quietly assumes that every rand in her pocket is available to buy distance. It is not. R450 of it is claimed the moment the keys change hands, before a single wheel turns, so only R750 is left to convert into kilometres. R750 divided by R12,50 is 60 kilometres. Now look at her error and watch it explain itself: R450 divided by R12,50 is 36 — precisely the kilometres she gained. The mistake was to let the fixed fee buy driving. The general habit that prevents it is a single question asked before any dividing: what has to be paid before I use anything at all? Remove that first, then divide what remains by the rate. Peel, then divide — in that order, every time.

***

**Thandi:** Here is a good one. How do I know which value to substitute where? Sometimes I look at a question and cannot tell what is known and what is wanted.

**Tutor:** Let the question's own words sort it for you. Read for the value the question hands you and the value it asks for. "What does a 340 kilometre trip cost?" hands you a distance and asks for money — so the distance goes into the formula, and the cost is what comes out; that is the forwards direction. "How far will R1 200 take me?" hands you money and asks for distance — so the R1 200 goes where the cost sits, and the distance is what you dig out; that is the backwards direction. There is a second check built into the language of the formula. Cost depends on distance, never the other way round, so distance is the value you feed in and cost is the value that answers. And before anything is substituted, make the units agree with the formula. If the formula expects kilometres and the question offers 4 500 metres, that is 4,5 kilometres — convert first, because a formula cannot tell that you fed it the wrong unit, and it will hand you a confident, wrong answer.

***

**Thandi:** Last one, and it is a fear rather than a question. What happens if the exam gives me a formula I have never seen — something with brackets and squares in it?

**Tutor:** You use it perfectly without understanding where it came from, because that is what substitution is for. Nobody expects you to know why the volume of a cylinder involves pi, or why the body mass index divides by height squared. The six moves do not change with the formula. State the formula exactly as the paper gives it. Decide which quantity is known and which is wanted. Check that your units match what the formula expects, and convert if they do not. Substitute the known value in place of its symbol. Then compute in the proper order — brackets first, then any squaring or square-rooting, then multiplication and division, and only then addition and subtraction. Finish with the unit attached, and ask whether the size of the answer is believable for the situation. An unfamiliar formula is actually a gift: the mathematics has already been done for you and printed on the paper, while everyone else is panicking. Feed the machine carefully and collect the marks.
