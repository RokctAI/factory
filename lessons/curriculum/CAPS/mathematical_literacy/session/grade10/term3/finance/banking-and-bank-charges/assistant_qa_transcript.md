**Thandi:** Time for the questions that come up a lot here! First one: debit card, credit card, debit column, credit column — the same two words seem to point in opposite directions. How do I keep them straight?

**Tutor:** Separate the cards from the statement, because they are answering different questions. The cards describe whose money is being spent: a debit card spends your own money straight from your account, while a credit card spends the bank's money, which you must repay later, with interest if you are slow. The statement columns describe direction of flow for your account: debit means money left the account, credit means money arrived. So when your salary or stall takings land, the statement shows a credit; when a fee or a withdrawal happens, the statement shows a debit — even if you used a debit card to do it. In the exam, statement questions dominate, so anchor this sentence: on a statement, debit is out, credit is in. If a question shows a statement line reading debit R70,00, that is R70 gone — probably that expensive branch-counter withdrawal — and the balance column immediately to the right should have dropped by exactly R70. Reading the balance column as a running check is also how you catch the bank statement errors that exam questions love to hide.

***

**Thandi:** Next: with the fee of R2,00 plus R1,00 per R100 or part thereof, why is the fee on R250 not R4,50? Two and a half hundreds seems obvious.

**Tutor:** Because the phrase part thereof forbids halves. The fee structure does not measure your withdrawal as a decimal number of hundreds; it counts how many R100 units the amount touches, and any partly-used hundred counts in full. R250 touches three hundreds: the first hundred, the second hundred, and fifty rand into the third — so the count is three, and the fee is 2,00 plus 3 times 1,00, which is R5,00. Notice the direction of the rounding: always up, always in the bank's favour, never yours. This is the same rounding logic as taxi fare per kilometre started, or a parkade charging per hour or part of an hour — begin the unit, owe the unit. In calculations, do the count first and write it down: R250 means three hundreds; R601 means seven hundreds, because that single rand past six hundred opens the seventh; R1 000 exactly means ten hundreds, no rounding needed since nothing is partial. The exam's favourite wrong option is always the decimal version — 4,50 in this case — sitting there looking reasonable.

***

**Thandi:** Third one: in the account comparison my totals were right but I still lost a mark. The memo wanted a sentence. What sentence?

**Tutor:** The comparison question is only finished when you announce the decision the numbers support. Computing R34,50 for EasySave and R55,00 for the Bundle earns the calculation marks, but the final mark sits in the conclusion: EasySave is cheaper for this month by R20,50, so Amahle should choose EasySave. Three ingredients — the winner, the margin, and the recommendation tied to the given month of use. Leave out the margin and you have asserted rather than compared; leave out the winner and the marker must guess your conclusion. And be careful to answer for the month the question described, not the month you imagine: if the question then changes the usage to twelve heavy withdrawals, the totals become R150,50 against R55,00 and the recommendation legitimately flips to the Bundle, saving R95,50. Examiners deliberately run both scenarios in one question to test whether your advice follows the arithmetic rather than habit. Numbers, then sentence: that pairing is the full-mark shape of every comparison answer.

**Thandi:** Last one: the graph question says describe where the two fee lines cross and what it means. What are the marks actually looking for?

**Tutor:** Three specific observations, each in plain words. First, the shapes: the Bundle account is a horizontal line at R55, because its cost ignores how many transactions happen, while EasySave is a rising line that starts at R6,50 — the admin fee charged even for an idle month — and climbs R7 with each R500 withdrawal. Second, the crossing: somewhere between six and seven withdrawals the rising line passes the flat one; at six withdrawals EasySave totals R48,50, still below 55, and at seven it totals R55,50, just above. Third, the meaning on each side: to the left of the crossing, the rising line is lower, so the pay-per-use account is cheaper for light users; to the right, the flat line is lower, so the bundle is cheaper for heavy users. The crossing point itself is the break-even, where the two accounts cost the same. Say those three things with the numbers attached and you have every mark — the graph question is the comparison question wearing a picture.
