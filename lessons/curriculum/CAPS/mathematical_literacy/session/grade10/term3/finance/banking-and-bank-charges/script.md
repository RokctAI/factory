# Part 1 — Expert

Banks charge for almost everything they do, and Paper 1 loves to ask exactly what a month of banking really costs. This lesson opens a first bank account alongside Amahle, who has been running a weekend snack stall and is tired of keeping cash in a biscuit tin. She must choose between two accounts: EasySave, which charges a small fee for every transaction, and the Bundle account, which charges one flat monthly amount for unlimited transactions. Between those two offers sits every skill the syllabus wants: banking vocabulary, reading a fee table, computing a transaction fee from a fixed-plus-rate structure, totalling a month of charges, and describing the graph where the two accounts cross.

## Subtopic: The Language of Banking

Banking questions begin as reading questions, so the vocabulary comes first.

Types of account. A savings account holds money safely and pays a little interest — Amahle's starting point. A current account, also called a cheque account, is built for many transactions in and out, usually with more fees and little interest. A fixed deposit locks a lump sum away for a fixed time at a better interest rate — strong for saving, useless for daily spending. And a credit account or credit card lets you spend the bank's money first and repay later, with interest charged if you are slow.

Movements of money. A deposit puts money into the account. A withdrawal takes it out. An EFT — electronic funds transfer — moves money between accounts without cash. A debit order is a standing permission for a company to take a regular payment, like a phone contract, from the account each month. On a bank statement, money out appears in the debit column and money in appears in the credit column, and the balance is what stands in the account after each transaction.

One warning about those two famous words: debit means money leaving your account, credit means money arriving. Learners mix them because a debit CARD spends your own money while a credit CARD borrows the bank's — but on the statement itself, debit is out, credit is in. Fix the statement meaning now, because statement-reading questions are guaranteed marks.

The questions for this section are with you now — pure vocabulary, the cheapest marks in the whole topic, lost only by guessing instead of learning.

## Subtopic: What a Withdrawal Really Costs

Now the fee table for Amahle's EasySave account, read line by line. Monthly admin fee: R6,50. Cash withdrawal at the bank's own ATM: R2,00 plus R1,00 per R100 or part thereof. Cash withdrawal at the till of a supermarket — called cashback — R1,60 flat. Withdrawal inside a branch, over the counter: R70,00. Those four lines carry three different pricing shapes: fixed plus rate, flat fee, and painful flat fee.

Work the ATM line. Withdrawing R500: the fixed part is R2,00, and R500 contains five full R100s, so the rate part is 5 times R1,00. Total: 2 plus 5 gives R7,00. Withdrawing R1 000: 2 plus 10 gives R12,00.

The phrase that examiners test: per R100 OR PART THEREOF. Withdraw R250. That is two full hundreds and a part of a third — and a part counts as a whole. So the rate part is 3 times R1,00, and the fee is 2 plus 3, R5,00. Not 2 plus 2,50. The bank rounds the hundreds UP, always in its own favour, and the exam checks whether you noticed.

Now compare channels for the same R500 in cash. Own ATM: R7,00. Cashback at the till while buying bread: R1,60. Over the counter in the branch: R70,00 — ten times the ATM, forty times the till. Same rand notes in your hand, wildly different prices. Choosing the cheap channel is not stinginess; over a year of weekly withdrawals, the gap between the till and the counter is thousands of rand.

Work through this section's questions. Watch every option for the part-thereof trap and for fees quoted at the wrong channel.

## Subtopic: Two Accounts Head to Head

The Bundle account makes the opposite offer: R55,00 flat per month, all transactions included, no admin fee on top. Which account should Amahle choose? The only honest answer comes from her own month of banking.

Her typical month: four ATM withdrawals of R500 each, and nothing else unusual. EasySave: each withdrawal costs R7,00, so 4 times 7 gives R28,00, plus the monthly admin fee of R6,50. Total: R34,50. The Bundle at R55,00 would cost her R20,50 more. EasySave wins — for THIS pattern of use.

Now a heavier month, the kind a busier stall might bring: twelve ATM withdrawals of R1 000. EasySave: each costs R12,00, so 12 times 12 gives R144,00, plus 6,50 — R150,50. The Bundle still costs R55,00. Bundle wins by nearly R100.

So neither account is simply better; the break-even point decides. Ask: how many R500 withdrawals make EasySave's total reach R55? Fees: 6,50 plus 7 per withdrawal. Six withdrawals: 6,50 plus 42 gives R48,50 — still cheaper than the Bundle. Seven withdrawals: 6,50 plus 49 gives R55,50 — now just past the Bundle. So at six or fewer withdrawals a month, pay per transaction; at seven or more, the flat fee is the bargain. This is exactly the reasoning the exam wants written out: compute both totals for the given month, compare, and state the winner with its margin.

The deeper lesson: a flat fee is insurance for heavy users and a waste for light users, and a per-use fee is the reverse. Banks design both and profit either way; the customer's job is arithmetic.

This section's questions are in front of you — every one is a small month of banking to cost out, so keep the fee table in your head as you go.

## Subtopic: The Fee Graph, and the Method for Any Charges Question

Picture the two accounts as a graph, money on the vertical axis, number of R500 withdrawals along the horizontal. The Bundle draws a horizontal line at R55 — flat, unchanging, the same for zero withdrawals as for thirty. EasySave starts lower, at R6,50 — the admin fee is charged even in a month of no withdrawals — and climbs by R7 for every withdrawal: a staircase rising from left to right. On the left of the picture, the EasySave line runs below the Bundle line: light users pay less per-use. Between six and seven withdrawals the rising line climbs past the flat one — the crossing is the break-even — and to the right of it the flat Bundle line is the cheaper of the two. Every tariff-comparison graph in this course tells this same story: a rising line and a flat line, and the crossing point where the advice changes.

The method for any bank-charges question, five steps. One: read the fee table and classify each fee's shape — flat, fixed plus rate, or percentage. Two: for fixed-plus-rate fees, count the R100s, rounding parts UP, then fee equals fixed part plus rate times the count. Three: total a month by multiplying each fee by how many times it happened, and never forget the monthly admin fee — it is charged for merely existing. Four: to compare accounts, compute each account's total for the same month of use, then state the cheaper one and by how much. Five: for a break-even question, step the number of transactions up until the totals cross, and report the crossing honestly: cheaper at six, dearer at seven.

The traps: treating R250 as two-and-a-half hundreds instead of three; forgetting the admin fee; comparing accounts on different months of use; and reading the branch-counter fee as if it were the ATM fee. Every one of them appears in past papers, dressed as a plausible option.

The closing questions of this part are with you now. Take each month slowly: fee shape first, count second, total third.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Same biscuit tin, same two accounts — now in the language of the shop counter.

## Subtopic: The Bank Is a Shop That Sells Services

Amahle's biscuit tin never charged her a cent, so the first shock of banking is that everything has a price. Think of the bank as a shop. Keeping your money safe: that is the monthly admin fee, R6,50 — like a locker rental. Giving you your own cash through a machine: R2,00 plus R1,00 for every R100 — a small service with a small price. Giving you cash through a human being behind a counter: R70,00 — the deluxe service you almost never need. The bank is not being sneaky when it charges — the prices are printed on the fee table, like a menu. The sneakiness only wins when nobody reads the menu.

And the words on the statement are simpler than they look. Deposit: money in. Withdrawal: money out. Debit column: out. Credit column: in. Debit order: you gave a company permission to help itself to a payment every month — useful for the phone contract, dangerous to forget about. Balance: what is standing in the account right now, after everything in and out.

Accounts are just different lockers. Savings: an ordinary locker that pays you a little rent. Fixed deposit: a locker with a time lock — better rent, but no access. Current account: a locker with a revolving door, built for business. Credit: not a locker at all — that is the shop lending you ITS money, and its rent runs against you.

The questions for this piece are with you now. Every term is on the menu you just read — no guessing needed.

## Subtopic: Small Fees Eat Big Money

Nobody loses their savings to one giant fee. They lose them R7 at a time, without watching.

Follow one R500 note out of Amahle's account by three different doors. Door one, the supermarket till: she buys bread, asks for R500 cashback, pays R1,60. Door two, the bank's ATM: R2,00 plus R5,00 — that is R7,00, four times the till. Door three, the branch counter: R70,00 — enough to buy the bread forty times over. The cash is identical; only the door changed.

Now run the year. Weekly cash at the till: about 52 times R1,60 — roughly R83 for the year. Weekly cash at the counter: 52 times R70 — R3 640. The difference could stock her stall for a month. Same money, same year — the only variable was which door she made a habit of.

And the sneaky phrase "per R100 or part thereof" is the shop rounding in its own favour. Draw R250 and the machine does not see two and a half hundreds; it sees three, because a part counts as a whole. Fee: 2 plus 3 — R5,00. The bank never rounds toward you. Expect it, count the hundreds up, and the fee table holds no surprises.

Your questions for this section are up. For each one, just ask: which door, and how many hundreds — counting the parts as wholes?

## Subtopic: Flat Rate or Pay-As-You-Go

You already understand the Bundle account, because you buy data. Pay-as-you-go airtime is perfect when you barely use the phone — and ruinous the month you stream every night. A big monthly data bundle is wasteful for a light user and a bargain for a heavy one. Bank accounts copy this exactly. EasySave is pay-as-you-go: R6,50 to exist, then R7 per R500 withdrawal. The Bundle is the flat package: R55, all you can transact.

So the choice is not about which account is better — it is about which month is yours. Amahle's quiet month has four withdrawals: pay-as-you-go costs 28 plus 6,50, R34,50, and the flat R55 would waste R20,50. Her busy season has twelve withdrawals of R1 000: pay-as-you-go balloons to R150,50, and the flat R55 saves nearly R100.

Where does the advice flip? Step it up one withdrawal at a time: six withdrawals cost R48,50 — still under 55. Seven cost R55,50 — just over. So six or fewer, stay pay-as-you-go; seven or more, buy the bundle. That crossing point is the whole exam question, and you find it exactly the way you just did: count upward until the totals swap places.

Last habit, the one that saves real families real money: check the pattern once a year. A quiet stall becomes a busy one, four withdrawals become fifteen, and the account that was right becomes the account that leaks. The bank will not phone to tell you — the fee table is public, but the arithmetic is yours.

Last questions of the lesson are with you now. Cost out both accounts for the month described, compare, and say the winner and the margin out loud — that full sentence is the full marks.
