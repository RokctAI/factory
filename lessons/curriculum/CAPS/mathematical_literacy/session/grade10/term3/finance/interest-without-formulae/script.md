# Part 1 — Expert

Interest questions in Grade 10 come with a gift attached: no formulae are required or allowed. Everything is done with percentages you already own, one period at a time. What the examiner actually tests is whether you know the difference between two easily-confused things — the interest rate, which is a percentage, and the interest, which is an amount of rand — and whether you can grow or charge money step by step. Our story follows R3 000 down two different roads. Thabo puts R3 000 into a savings account earning 8 percent per year. His cousin Sipho borrows R3 000, first from a bank at 20 percent per year, then — in the cautionary tale — from an informal moneylender at 30 percent per month. Same R3 000, very different endings.

## Subtopic: Interest and Interest Rate — a Percentage Is Not Money

Two definitions, and the exam's favourite trick lives in the gap between them.

The interest rate is a percentage per time period — 8 percent per year, 30 percent per month. It is a speed, not an amount. On its own it buys nothing.

The interest is the actual rand amount that the rate produces when it acts on a sum of money. Thabo's 8 percent per year acting on R3 000 produces 0,08 times 3 000, which is R240 of interest in one year. The rate is 8 percent; the interest is R240. Write the units and you can never confuse them: percent for the rate, rand for the interest.

Direction matters just as much. When you save or invest, the bank is using your money, so the bank pays you interest — it arrives as a gain. When you borrow, you are using the bank's money, so you pay the interest — it lands as a cost. Same word, opposite pockets. An exam question that asks who receives the interest is testing exactly this.

One more term: the original amount that the rate acts on — the deposit or the loan — is called the principal. Rate acts on principal to produce interest. Say that sentence until it is boring.

The questions for this section are with you now. They check the rand-versus-percent distinction and which direction the money flows for a saver and for a borrower.

## Subtopic: Growing a Saving Year by Year

Thabo deposits R3 000 at 8 percent per year and leaves it alone. No formula — just take the percentage, one year at a time.

Year one: 8 percent of R3 000. Compute it: 10 percent of 3 000 is 300, so 1 percent is 30, and 8 percent is 8 times 30 — R240. The account grows to 3 000 plus 240, which is R3 240.

Year two is where thinking one year at a time pays off. If Thabo left the R240 in the account, the bank now pays 8 percent on the NEW balance of R3 240, not on the original R3 000. Compute: 1 percent of 3 240 is 32,40, times 8 gives R259,20. The balance becomes 3 240 plus 259,20, which is R3 499,20.

Look at what just happened: year two's interest, R259,20, is bigger than year one's R240, because last year's interest itself started earning. Interest on interest — money gathering speed. Grade 11 will give this a name and a formula; Grade 10 simply asks you to compute it step by step, and the step-by-step version is the one you will actually understand.

Compare the alternative: suppose Thabo withdrew the R240 each year and spent it. Then every year starts back at R3 000, and every year pays the same flat R240. After two years he has received 240 plus 240 — R480 — while the leave-it-alone version earned 240 plus 259,20, which is R499,20. The difference, R19,20, is exactly 8 percent of the first R240: the interest the withdrawn money never got to earn. Small after two years; after twenty, enormous.

Work through this section's questions now. They follow the two-year growth line by line and probe why the second year outgrows the first.

## Subtopic: The Cost of Borrowing — Bank versus Mashonisa

Now the other road. Sipho borrows R3 000 from the bank at 20 percent per year, to be repaid after one year.

The interest: 20 percent of 3 000 is 0,20 times 3 000 — R600. So after a year Sipho repays the principal plus the interest: 3 000 plus 600 gives R3 600. Borrowing R3 000 for a year cost him R600. That is the whole calculation — percentage of principal, added on.

Now the cautionary tale. The bank says no, and an informal moneylender — a mashonisa — offers the same R3 000 at 30 percent per month. The number 30 sits close to 20, and that closeness is the trap, because the periods are different. Per month, the interest is 30 percent of 3 000: R900. Every single month.

Put the two side by side over three months. The bank's 20 percent per year charges R600 for a whole year — three months would be a fraction of that. The mashonisa charges R900 in month one, R900 in month two, R900 in month three: R2 700 of interest in three months, and if Sipho has only been paying the interest, the R3 000 principal is still fully owed. He has paid nearly the whole loan again and owes as much as the day he started.

To compare rates honestly, put them on the same time period. Thirty percent per month, over twelve months of simple charging, is 30 times 12 — 360 percent per year. Against the bank's 20 percent per year, the mashonisa is eighteen times more expensive. A rate means nothing until you read its time period; that one sentence is both an exam skill and a life skill.

This section's questions are in front of you. Watch the per-month and per-year labels in every option — the wrong answers all come from ignoring them.

## Subtopic: Finding the Rate, and the Full Method

Exams also run the calculation backwards: here is the interest, find the rate. Suppose a different lender charged R450 interest on a R3 000 loan over one year. What was the rate?

The rate is the interest as a percentage of the principal: 450 over 3 000, times 100. Compute: 450 divided by 3 000 is 0,15, and times 100 gives 15 percent per year. Always divide by the PRINCIPAL — the original amount — never by the total repaid. Dividing by the repayment of 3 450 gives around 13 percent, wrong, because the rate acted on what was borrowed, not on what was returned.

The complete method for any Grade 10 interest question, five steps. One: identify the principal, the rate and the time period, and underline whether the rate is per year or per month. Two: decide the direction — is this interest earned by a saver or paid by a borrower? Three: compute one period's interest as the percentage of the current balance. Four: for multiple periods, move one period at a time, and ask whether interest stays in the account and grows the balance, or is paid out or paid off, so the balance resets. Five: answer the actual question — sometimes the interest, sometimes the new balance, sometimes the total repaid, sometimes the rate found by dividing interest by principal and multiplying by 100.

The traps, in order of expense. Mixing periods — treating 30 percent per month as if it competed with 20 percent per year. Growing from the wrong balance — using R3 000 in year two when the account holds R3 240. Dividing by the repayment instead of the principal when finding a rate. And writing a rate as rand or interest as a percent — the units are the meaning.

The closing questions of this part are with you now. They mix savers and borrowers, forwards and backwards, and every single one is solved by a percentage and a clear head.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Same R3 000, same two roads — now told the way you would warn a friend.

## Subtopic: Renting Money

Interest stops being mysterious the moment you see it as rent. When you live in someone's room, you pay rent for using it. When you use someone's money, you pay rent for that too — and money-rent is called interest.

Borrow R3 000 from the bank for a year and the rent is 20 percent of what you borrowed: R600. You return the R3 000 — it was never yours — plus R600 for having used it. Total repaid: R3 600.

Savings are the same picture reversed. When Thabo deposits R3 000, the bank is renting HIS money — using it while it sits there. So the bank pays him rent: 8 percent per year, which is R240. Saver receives rent; borrower pays rent. One idea, two directions, and now you always know who pays whom.

And the rate versus the interest? The rate is like the price per month on a to-let sign — a percentage, just information. The interest is the actual rent money that changes hands — rand. A sign saying 8 percent has paid nobody anything yet; R240 landing in the account is the event. In every exam answer, rate gets a percent symbol and interest gets a rand sign, and if your answer has the wrong one, you have answered a different question.

The questions for this piece are with you now. For each one just ask: who is renting whose money, and is this line a price tag or an actual payment?

## Subtopic: The Snowball in the Savings Account

Rolling a snowball along wet grass, it picks up more grass with every roll — because each layer it gains makes it bigger for the next roll. Thabo's savings account is that snowball.

Year one: R3 000 earns its 8 percent — R240 — and he leaves it in. The ball is now R3 240.

Year two: the bank pays 8 percent again, but on the bigger ball. Eight percent of R3 240 is R259,20. More than last year — R19,20 more — and nobody worked harder for it. Last year's interest earned its own interest. The ball is now R3 499,20.

Now the friend who does it the other way: she withdraws her R240 every year to spend. Nothing wrong with that — but her ball never grows. Every January she is back at R3 000, and every December she gets the same flat R240. After two years: R480, against Thabo's R499,20. The gap looks tiny. But gaps like this are baby snowballs — give them twenty years and Thabo's account has pulled far ahead, all from interest earning interest.

One habit makes every exam version of this easy: move ONE year at a time, and before each year, write down the balance the year starts with. Year two starts at R3 240 — so year two's interest is 8 percent of R3 240, full stop. Learners who lose marks here all made the same move: they took 8 percent of the original R3 000 twice. The snowball reminds you — measure the ball as it is NOW, not as it was when you started rolling.

Your questions for this section are up. Balance first, then percentage, one year at a time.

## Subtopic: Which Loan Hurts Less

Two people offer to lend you R3 000. The bank says 20 percent. The mashonisa down the road says 30. Thirty against twenty — sounds close, right? Read the small print out loud: the bank's is 20 percent PER YEAR. The mashonisa's is 30 percent PER MONTH. Those are not neighbours; they are different planets.

The bank's rent for a whole year: R600. The mashonisa's rent for one month: R900. In his first MONTH you pay more than the bank asks for a whole YEAR.

Run three months, which is how these stories really go. Month one: R900. Month two: R900. Month three: R900. That is R2 700 paid in rent — and here is the part that traps real families: if you were only paying the interest each month, the R3 000 itself is still owed, in full. You have paid nearly the price of the loan and the loan is still there, staring at you.

Want the honest comparison? Stretch the mashonisa's rate to a year: 30 percent a month, twelve months, is 360 percent a year. The bank charges 20. Eighteen times more expensive, hiding behind a number that looked only a little bigger.

So the rule, for exams and for life: never compare two rates until they wear the same time period. Per year against per year, per month against per month. The examiner builds wrong options out of exactly this confusion, and the mashonisa builds a business out of it.

Last questions of the lesson are with you now. Check every rate's time period before you touch the calculator — that one glance is where all the marks live.
