**Thandi:** Time for the questions that come up a lot with this topic! First one: if Zanele still has ten vetkoek at closing time, can she count them as income since they are worth R6 each?

**Tutor:** No — and this is the single most common wrong instinct in the whole topic. Income is money that actually arrived, and those ten vetkoek brought in nothing. Worse, they already took money out: the flour, oil and gas inside them cost R2,50 each, so R25 of expenditure is sitting on that plate with no sale to answer for it. Value and income are different things. A shelf of unsold stock might have a selling value of R60, but value only becomes income at the moment a customer pays. That is why the statement records 190 vetkoek sold at R6 — R1 140 — and not 200. If she sells the ten tomorrow at half price, tomorrow's income records R30. The exam tests this by sneaking the words "made 200" into a question where only 190 were sold, hoping you multiply the wrong number. Read for the word sold, every time.

***

**Thandi:** Next one: profit and surplus, loss and deficit — are those four words two pairs of twins, or do they mean different things?

**Tutor:** They are twins in the arithmetic and different in the setting. Both pairs come from the same subtraction: total income minus total expenditure. A business talks about profit and loss, because its purpose is to trade. A household, a school, a stokvel or a sports club talks about surplus and deficit, because their purpose is not trading — a family does not make a profit on its own groceries, but its month can still end with money left over, and that is a surplus. In the exam, use the vocabulary of the document in front of you. If the question shows a household income-and-expenditure statement and asks for the bottom line, write surplus or deficit; if it is Zanele's tuckshop as a business, profit or loss is the natural pair. The number carries most of the marks, but the correct naming word usually carries one — and it is the cheapest mark on the paper.

***

**Thandi:** This one arrives every year: the till slip shows the total of R897 but the VAT line is smudged. How do I rebuild it?

**Tutor:** Use the relationship that never changes: subtotal plus VAT equals total. You have two of the three numbers, so subtraction rebuilds the third — 897 minus the subtotal of 780 gives the missing VAT of R117. Then verify it independently: VAT should be 15 percent of the subtotal, and 0,15 times 780 is indeed 117. That double route is the habit to build, because the exam can smudge any of the three lines. Missing total: add 780 and 117. Missing VAT: subtract, then check with the percentage. Missing subtotal: subtract the VAT from the total, or, in the harder version where only the total is given, divide 897 by 1,15 to get 780 — because a VAT-inclusive total is always the subtotal multiplied by 1,15. One relationship, four question styles, same three numbers.

**Thandi:** Last one: my statement balances perfectly, but the question asks me to suggest how the tuckshop could improve its profit. Is there a method for that?

**Tutor:** There is, and it comes straight off the statement's two halves: profit rises when income rises or when expenditure falls, so give one suggestion from each side and tie it to a real line item. On the income side: sell the ten vetkoek that currently go unsold, perhaps at a discount late in the day — even R3 each turns R25 of wasted ingredients into R30 of income. Or adjust the selling price, with the caution that a higher price can reduce the number of customers. On the expenditure side, aim at a line you can actually change: buy cans in bigger bulk for a lower unit price, or combine taxi trips to cut the R120 of transport. Do not suggest cutting the rent — a fixed cost set by the school is not hers to change, and examiners reward suggestions that respect which costs are controllable. Number plus reason, one from each side: that is a full-mark answer.
