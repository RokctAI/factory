# Part 1 — Expert

Profit and loss questions open the Finance section of Paper 1 nearly every year, and they are built on one subtraction: money in, minus money out. The trading story that carries this lesson is Zanele's school tuckshop over one ordinary month. She fries vetkoek, sells cans of cooldrink, pays the school for her stall, and at month end she wants one honest number: did the tuckshop make money or lose it? Along the way we read the financial documents that feed that number — the supplier's till slip with its VAT line, the invoice for stock — and we sort every amount into fixed, variable or occasional, because that sorting is worth marks on its own. The month ends with a full income-and-expenditure statement, and then a second, harder month where the same statement turns negative.

## Subtopic: Income, Expenditure and the Profit Line

Three words carry the whole topic. Income is money flowing in. Expenditure is money flowing out. Profit is what remains when expenditure is subtracted from income — and when that subtraction goes negative, the negative answer has its own name: a loss.

Zanele's month, income first. She made 200 vetkoek and sold 190 of them at R6 each: 190 times 6 is R1 140. She also sold 150 cans of cooldrink at R10 each: 150 times 10 is R1 500. Total income: 1 140 plus 1 500 gives R2 640.

Now expenditure. The vetkoek ingredients cost R2,50 per vetkoek, and she made 200, so 200 times 2,50 is R500. The cans cost R6 each from the wholesaler: 150 times 6 is R900. Stock therefore cost R1 400. Add the stall rent of R250 she pays the school, the gas refill of R180, and taxi fare of R120 to fetch stock. Total expenditure: 1 400 plus 250 plus 180 plus 120 gives R1 950.

The profit line: income minus expenditure, 2 640 minus 1 950, equals R690. Positive, so the tuckshop made a profit of R690 this month.

One more view examiners love: profit per item. A vetkoek costs R2,50 to make and sells for R6, so each one sold carries a profit of R3,50. But notice the ten vetkoek she made and did not sell — their ingredients cost 10 times 2,50, which is R25, and that money is spent whether or not anyone buys. Unsold stock quietly eats profit, and questions probe exactly that.

The questions for this section are with you now. They test the three definitions, the R690, and what those ten unsold vetkoek did to it.

## Subtopic: Reading the Financial Documents

Every number in a trading story arrives on a document, and Mathematical Literacy expects you to read those documents line by line. Take the till slip from the wholesaler where Zanele bought her stock. Near the bottom sit three lines that matter more than all the rest: a subtotal of R780,00, then a line reading VAT at 15 percent, R117,00, then a total due of R897,00.

Decode them. The subtotal is the price of the goods before tax. VAT — value-added tax — is the 15 percent the government adds to most goods: 15 percent of 780 is 0,15 times 780, which is 117, exactly the slip's VAT line. The total due is subtotal plus VAT: 780 plus 117 gives 897. Every till slip in the country follows this pattern, and a favourite exam question blanks out one of the three lines and asks you to rebuild it.

An invoice adds two ideas. First, quantity times unit price: the invoice for cooldrink reads quantity 150, unit price R6,00, amount R900,00 — and you should always check that multiplication, because a mistyped invoice is a classic exam scenario. Second, terms of payment: an invoice often says the amount is due within 30 days, which is a promise to pay, where a till slip records money already paid.

Vocabulary that earns direct marks: the balance is the amount currently owed on an account. Amount due is what must be paid now. A statement is the document that lists a month's transactions and ends with that balance. When the municipal account or the shop account asks for these words, they are asking for reading, not arithmetic — the easiest marks on the paper, lost only through skimming.

Work through this section's questions. Each one hands you a document line and asks what it means or whether it is correct — exactly the exam's habit.

## Subtopic: Fixed, Variable and Occasional Expenses

Examiners constantly ask you to classify expenses, because planning depends on it. Three categories.

A fixed expense is the same amount every period, no matter how business goes. Zanele's stall rent of R250 is fixed: brilliant month or terrible month, the school wants R250.

A variable expense changes with how much you do. Ingredients and stock are the purest examples: fry more vetkoek, buy more flour. The R1 400 of stock, the R180 of gas — these rise and fall with trade. So does her taxi fare when extra stock runs mean extra trips.

An occasional expense arrives rarely and irregularly. When Zanele's cooler box cracked two months ago, replacing it cost R350 — once. You cannot schedule it monthly, but a wise budget still sets something aside for it.

Why the sorting matters: fixed expenses set the floor. Before Zanele sells a single vetkoek, R250 of rent is already owed. Variable expenses scale with income, so they behave; it is the fixed ones that sink a slow month, and the occasional ones that ambush a good one. An exam question will give a list — rent, airtime, stock, a burst geyser repair, a monthly insurance debit order — and ask you to place each in its category and say why. The reason is the mark: fixed because the amount never changes; variable because it depends on how much is sold; occasional because it is unpredictable and rare.

This section's questions are ready for you. For every expense you classify, rehearse the one-line reason — the category without the reason is half the marks.

## Subtopic: The Income-and-Expenditure Statement — Surplus, Deficit and the Method

Now assemble the month onto one page: the income-and-expenditure statement. Its shape never changes. Income items listed and totalled. Expenditure items listed and totalled. Then the bottom line: total income minus total expenditure.

Zanele's statement reads: income — vetkoek sales R1 140, cooldrink sales R1 500, total income R2 640. Expenditure — stock R1 400, rent R250, gas R180, transport R120, total expenditure R1 950. Bottom line: 2 640 minus 1 950 gives R690. When income exceeds expenditure the result is a surplus — a business would call it profit. Statements for households and clubs prefer the word surplus; hold both words.

Now the harder month. Exams love the December scenario: school closes early, so Zanele trades two weeks instead of four. Income falls to R1 800. But look at the expenditure side with your categories ready: stock shrinks because she buys less, yet the rent does not shrink, and suppose the month's expenditure still totals R1 950. The bottom line becomes 1 800 minus 1 950, which is negative 150 — a deficit of R150. The tuckshop lost money, and the statement shows exactly why: income halved while fixed costs stood still.

The method for any statement question, five steps. One: separate every amount into income or expenditure — read each item's direction, in or out. Two: total each side, showing the addition. Three: subtract expenditure from income, in that order. Four: name the result — profit or surplus if positive, loss or deficit if negative. Five: answer the why question with categories: fixed costs are the usual villain of a loss.

The traps. Counting the value of unsold stock as income — nothing sold, nothing in. Forgetting an expense the question tucked into the story a paragraph earlier. Subtracting in the wrong order and declaring a loss a profit. And rounding money anywhere except the final answer.

The closing questions of this part are with you now. They walk the full statement, the deficit month, and the reasons behind both bottom lines.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same tuckshop month again, told the way you would explain it at the kitchen table — three piles of money and one page that settles every argument.

## Subtopic: Money In, Money Out, and What Is Left

Picture two shoeboxes under the counter. Every rand a customer pays goes into the IN box. Every rand Zanele pays out — flour, cans, rent, gas, taxi — comes from the OUT box. At month end, one question: which box worked harder?

The IN box: 190 vetkoek at R6 is R1 140, and 150 cans at R10 is R1 500. Together, R2 640 came in.

The OUT box: R500 of vetkoek ingredients, R900 of cans from the wholesaler, R250 rent to the school, R180 of gas, R120 of taxi rides. Together, R1 950 went out.

Left over: 2 640 minus 1 950 is R690. That R690 is the profit — the money the month actually earned her. It is not the money in the cash tin; the cash tin held R2 640 at some point. Profit is what survives after every cost has taken its bite.

And here is the bite people forget. Ten vetkoek never sold. Nobody refunds the flour and oil inside them — that R25 was spent the moment she fried them. Making things does not earn money; selling them does. That single sentence answers half the trick questions in this topic.

The questions for this piece are with you now. Keep the two boxes in your head and every one of them becomes a sorting job.

## Subtopic: The Bills That Never Budge and the Ones That Follow the Crowd

Sort Zanele's costs the way you sort washing — three piles.

Pile one: the bills that never budge. The R250 stall rent is the same in a bumper month and a dead month. The school does not ask how sales went. That is a fixed expense, and it is the dangerous pile, because it must be paid out of the first money earned, before any profit exists.

Pile two: the costs that follow the crowd. Busy month, more flour, more cans, more gas, more taxi trips to the wholesaler. Quiet month, less of all four. These are variable expenses, and they are the polite pile — they shrink when times are hard.

Pile three: the ambushes. The cooler box that cracks. The R350 that one repair cost arrives maybe once a year, never on schedule. Occasional expenses. You cannot predict them, but you can expect them — like knowing that somewhere this winter, somebody in the house will need new shoes.

Why bother sorting? Because it answers the question every failing business asks: where did the money go? A slow month does not lose money because of the variable pile — that pile shrank along with sales. It loses money because the fixed pile stood there, arms folded, demanding its R250. When an exam asks why the December statement shows a loss, the sentence they want is: income fell, but the fixed expenses stayed the same.

Your questions for this section are up. Every cost in them belongs to one pile — name the pile, then say the reason out loud.

## Subtopic: One Page That Settles the Argument

Suppose Zanele's mother says the tuckshop is a waste of time, and her brother says it is a goldmine. The income-and-expenditure statement is the one page that ends the debate, because it holds nothing back.

Top half: everything that came in, item by item. Vetkoek R1 140. Cooldrinks R1 500. Total in: R2 640. Bottom half: everything that went out, item by item. Stock R1 400. Rent R250. Gas R180. Transport R120. Total out: R1 950. Last line: in minus out, R690 to the good. A surplus — the polite bookkeeping word for profit. Not a goldmine, not a waste of time: R690 of truth.

Then December tells the other story. Two trading weeks instead of four. Money in: R1 800. Money out: still R1 950, because rent and the rest held firm. Last line: minus R150. A deficit — the month ran at a loss, and the page shows the exact reason sitting in the expenditure column.

When you build one of these in an exam, three habits protect your marks. Put every amount on the correct side — one misplaced item poisons both totals. Show both totals before you subtract, because each total usually carries its own mark. And name the bottom line in words: surplus or deficit, profit or loss. A bare number is an unfinished answer.

Last questions of the lesson are with you now. Build the page top to bottom, in before out, totals before the subtraction — and the bottom line will defend itself.
