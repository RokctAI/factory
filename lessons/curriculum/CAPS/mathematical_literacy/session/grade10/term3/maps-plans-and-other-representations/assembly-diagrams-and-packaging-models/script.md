# Part 1 — Expert

Two humble documents close the Maps and Plans topic: the assembly diagram that falls out of a flat-pack box, and the packaging problem of fitting round cans into rectangular crates. Both are pure Mathematical Literacy — reading a technical picture, checking quantities by multiplication, and choosing the cheapest way to do a real job. Our storyline: Lwazi buys a flat-pack bookshelf for his tuckshop stockroom and must assemble it from the diagram, and then he must choose between two crate sizes for transporting cans of cooldrink. The exam versions of these questions reward slow reading and small multiplications, and punish nothing so much as skipping the parts list.

## Subtopic: Reading an Assembly Diagram

An assembly diagram is a set of instructions that mostly refuses to use sentences. It communicates through numbered parts, exploded views and symbols, and the first skill is translating each of those into words.

The parts list comes first, and it is a table: each component has a letter or number, a name, a picture and a quantity. Lwazi's bookshelf lists: part A, side panel, quantity 2. Part B, shelf, quantity 4. Part C, bracket, quantity 8. Part D, screw, quantity 16. Part E, back board, quantity 1. Before any assembly, the instructions say: check the contents against this list. That instruction is arithmetic, and exams test it directly.

The exploded view is the diagram's main picture: the product drawn as if gently pulled apart, each part floating in the position it will occupy, with dotted lines showing what slides or screws into what. Read it as a frozen explosion: follow any dotted line and it tells you that this screw travels through this bracket into that panel.

The symbols carry the rest. A numbered circle marks a step: step 1, step 2, in order — and the order matters, because a back board attached too early can block the last shelf. An arrow shows direction of movement or turning; a curved arrow around a screw means turn to tighten. A magnified circle zooms into a detail — which way a bracket faces, which hole to use. A crossed-out picture shows the common MISTAKE — the panel upside down — and is often the most valuable image on the page.

Two habits make these questions easy: read the steps in number order, and keep one finger on the parts list so every letter in a step can be named aloud: step 3 says fix part C to part A — a bracket to a side panel.

The questions for this section are with you now. All of them ask you to translate a symbol, a dotted line or a step into plain words.

## Subtopic: Counting Parts and Catching Mistakes

The quantities on a parts list are not decoration — they interlock, and checking that they interlock is a classic exam question.

Do Lwazi's numbers agree? Four shelves, and the design fixes each shelf with 2 brackets: 4 times 2 gives 8 brackets — matching part C's quantity of 8. Each bracket takes 2 screws: 8 times 2 gives 16 screws — matching part D. The list is internally consistent: every bracket has a shelf, every screw has a bracket.

Now the exam's favourite twist: the packet actually contains 14 screws. How many complete brackets can be fixed? Each needs 2 screws: 14 divided by 2 gives 7 brackets — but 8 are needed. Short by one bracket means one shelf corner unfixed. The follow-up asks: how many screws are missing? Needed 16, have 14: 2 short. These are one-step calculations, but only for the candidate who first built the chain shelves-to-brackets-to-screws.

The same chain logic prices the job. If replacement screws sell in packets of 6 at R18 per packet, and Lwazi is 2 short, he must still buy 1 full packet — the round-up rule of all purchasing — and pay R18 for two needed screws and four spares.

And a consistency check protects against misprints, which examiners simulate: a list claiming 4 shelves, 8 brackets and 12 screws contradicts itself — 8 brackets at 2 screws each demands 16. The question asks: is the list correct, and if not, what should the screw quantity be? Answer with the chain: 4 times 2 is 8, 8 times 2 is 16, so 12 is a misprint for 16.

Work through this section's questions — every one is a small chain of multiplications hiding in a parts table.

## Subtopic: Packing Cans into Crates

Now the packaging model. Lwazi transports cans that are 6 centimetres in diameter and 12 centimetres tall, and the wholesaler offers a crate whose inside measures 30 centimetres long, 24 centimetres wide and 12 centimetres high. How many cans fit?

Cans stand upright in rows, so work one direction at a time. Along the 30-centimetre length, each can claims its 6-centimetre diameter: 30 divided by 6 gives 5 cans. Across the 24-centimetre width: 24 divided by 6 gives 4 cans. One layer therefore holds 5 times 4 — 20 cans. Height: the crate is 12 centimetres high, the can 12 tall, so exactly 1 layer. Crate capacity: 20 cans.

The bigger crate measures 36 by 30 by 24. Length: 36 over 6 gives 6. Width: 30 over 6 gives 5. Layer: 30 cans. Height: 24 over 12 gives 2 layers. Capacity: 60 cans.

Three rules keep this honest. First, divide dimension by dimension — length by diameter, width by diameter, height by can height — and never volume by volume. Dividing crate volume by can volume pretends cans are liquid; round cans in square corners always waste space, so the true count is the dimension-by-dimension one. Second, when a division leaves a remainder, round DOWN: a crate 32 centimetres long still fits only 5 cans of diameter 6, because 5 cans use 30 centimetres and the leftover 2 centimetres holds no sixth can. Partial items do not exist in packing. Third, match units before dividing — a can labelled 60 millimetres across is 6 centimetres; divide 30 by 6, not 30 by 60.

Notice the two rounding worlds in one topic: buying materials rounds UP; fitting objects into a space rounds DOWN. The question's verb — buy, or fit — chooses the direction.

This section's questions are in front of you. Take each crate one direction at a time, and watch the millimetre labels.

## Subtopic: Choosing Cost-Effective Packaging

The final skill is judgement: which crate should Lwazi use? Cost-effective means the most service for the least money, and the standard tool is cost per item.

The small crate costs R4,50 and holds 20 cans: 4,50 divided by 20 gives 22,5 cents per can. The large crate costs R9,60 and holds 60: 9,60 divided by 60 gives 16 cents per can. Per can, the large crate is clearly cheaper — 6,5 cents saved on every can, which across 600 cans a month is R39 of packaging saved. On pure unit cost, the big crate wins.

But cost-effectiveness always faces a second question: how much do you actually need? Suppose Lwazi moves only 25 cans a week. One large crate holds all 25 with 35 empty spaces — R9,60 spent. Two small crates hold 40 with 15 empty spaces — 2 times 4,50 gives R9,00. For THIS weekly job the small crates are cheaper in actual rand, 60 cents saved, despite the worse cost per can. Cheapest per unit and cheapest for the job are different questions, and the exam asks both — read which one is in front of you.

The full method for any packaging decision, five steps. One: compute each container's true capacity, dimension by dimension, rounding down. Two: compute cost per item — container price over capacity. Three: read the actual requirement, and compute the real cost of meeting it with each option, rounding containers UP to cover the need. Four: compare like with like — rand with rand — and state the winner with its margin. Five: mention the practical factor if asked — wasted space invites crushed stock, and a 60-can crate of full cans may simply be too heavy to lift safely.

The closing questions of this part are with you now. They run capacity, unit cost and job cost against each other — exactly where marks are won and lost.

## Subtopic: The Instruction Sheet Is a Recipe

An assembly diagram is a recipe that swapped its words for pictures. A recipe starts with ingredients: the parts list is exactly that — 2 side panels, 4 shelves, 8 brackets, 16 screws, 1 back board, each with its own letter like ingredients with their own labels. And what does every good cook do first? Check the ingredients are actually in the kitchen. Same here: count the parts against the list before starting, because discovering a missing bracket at step 5 is how furniture ends up half-built in the passage for a month.

Then come the steps, numbered like a recipe's method — and, like baking, order is everything. You cannot ice a cake before it is baked; you cannot screw on the back board before the shelves are in, or it blocks the last one.

The pictures do the talking. The exploded view is the dish photographed mid-air — every part floating exactly where it belongs, dotted lines showing what slides into what. Arrows say which way something moves; a curly arrow around a screw says turn it. A little magnifying circle is the recipe saying pay attention here — the detail everyone gets wrong. And the crossed-out picture is the burnt version: this is what failure looks like, do not do this. Diagrams put the mistake on the page because it is cheaper to draw it than to answer ten thousand phone calls about it.

So when an exam hands you an assembly diagram, read it like a recipe: ingredients first, steps in order, zoom-boxes respected. Every question about it becomes show me where the sheet says so.

The questions for this piece are with you now — each one just asks what a picture is saying in words.

## Subtopic: Eggs in an Egg Box

Fitting cans into a crate is the egg-box problem. Nobody pours eggs into a box and hopes; the box has spots — so many along, so many across. Work out the spots and you know the capacity.

Cans of diameter 6 in a crate 30 long: how many 6-centimetre spots fit along 30? Five. Across the 24 width? Four. So the floor of the crate is a grid of 5 by 4 — 20 spots. The crate is 12 high and each can is 12 tall: one storey only. Twenty cans, exactly like counting egg spots.

The big crate is a double-storey egg box: 6 spots by 5 spots gives 30 per floor, and its 24 of height over the can's 12 gives 2 floors: 60 cans.

Two things eggs teach us. First: leftover space is nothing. If the crate were 32 long instead of 30, could a sixth can squeeze in? A sixth can needs 6 centimetres and only 2 remain — so no, and the answer stays 5. When fitting things in, always round DOWN. Contrast with the fence rolls from measurement: when BUYING, round up; when FITTING, round down. The verb in the question tells you the direction.

Second: never divide volumes. The clever-sounding shortcut — crate volume over can volume — overcounts, because cans are round and crates are square: the corners around every can are dead air that liquid maths would happily fill. Count spots along each direction instead — that is the count that matches reality.

Your questions for this section are up: one direction at a time, whole spots only.

## Subtopic: Big Box, Small Box, Best Box

The shop offers two crates. Small: R4,50, holds 20. Large: R9,60, holds 60. Which is better? The trick is that better has two meanings, and the exam swaps them.

Meaning one: better value per can. Share the crate's price across its cans. Small: 4,50 over 20 — 22,5 cents per can. Large: 9,60 over 60 — 16 cents per can. The big crate carries each can more cheaply, the same way the big bag of maize meal costs less per kilogram. Bulk usually wins the per-unit contest.

Meaning two: better for the actual job. Lwazi's job is 25 cans this week. The large crate does it in one: R9,60, with 35 spots wasted. Two small crates do it: R9,00, with 15 spots wasted. This week, the small option is cheaper in real rand — 9,00 against 9,60 — even though the big crate is cheaper per can. The bulk deal is only a deal if you use the bulk. It is the two-litre milk that goes sour before a small household finishes it: cheaper per litre, dearer per week.

So answer the question actually asked. Cost per can: divide price by capacity, and the big crate wins with 16 cents. Cost for 25 cans this week: count the crates each option needs — rounding crates UP, because a 25-can job cannot travel in a fraction of a crate — then price the count and compare: R9,00 beats R9,60. Two different winners from the same numbers, both correct, because two different questions were asked.

And when the exam adds and give one practical reason — mention weight or wasted space: sixty full cans is a heavy lift, and empty spots let cans rattle and dent.

Last questions of the lesson are with you now. Read which better is being asked for, divide the right things, and let the numbers pick the box.
