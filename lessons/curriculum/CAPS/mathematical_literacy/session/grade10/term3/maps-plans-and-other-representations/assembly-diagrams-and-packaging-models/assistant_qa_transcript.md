**Thandi:** Time for the questions that come up a lot with this topic! First: assembly diagrams have no words — how can an exam even ask questions about a picture?

**Tutor:** The exam asks you to be the translator, and the questions follow four reliable patterns. Pattern one: what does this symbol mean — the numbered circle is a step, the arrow is a direction of movement, the curved arrow means turn, the magnified circle is a zoomed detail, the crossed-out image is the mistake to avoid. Pattern two: what does this step do in words — and you answer by pairing the step's letters with the parts list, so fix C to A becomes fix a bracket to a side panel. Pattern three: why is the order important — because a part attached too early can block a later one, like a back board trapping the final shelf. Pattern four: the quantity questions, which are really arithmetic wearing overalls — how many screws for all the brackets, are there enough parts in the box. If you can name every part, say every step aloud and multiply along the chain of quantities, there is nothing else the diagram can ask you. The candidates who struggle are the ones who try to answer from the picture alone without keeping a finger on the parts list.

***

**Thandi:** Next one: in the crate question I divided the crate's volume by the can's volume and got a bigger number than the memo. Where did the extra cans come from?

**Tutor:** From the corners — from space that exists but that no can may occupy. Volume division treats the cans as liquid: it imagines melting them down and pouring cooldrink into every cubic centimetre, including the little pockets around each round can and the leftover strip along a wall. Real cans are rigid cylinders standing in a rectangular box, so the honest count goes dimension by dimension: the 30-centimetre length takes 5 cans of diameter 6, the 24-centimetre width takes 4, so a layer holds 20, and the height decides the number of layers. Any remainder in those divisions is dead space — a crate 32 centimetres long still takes only 5 cans along, because the spare 2 centimetres cannot hold a sixth. That is also why packing questions round DOWN while buying questions round UP: leftover space fits nothing, but a leftover need must still be covered. When your dimension-count and a volume-count disagree, the dimension-count is always the true capacity, and the difference between them is exactly the wasted air the question may ask you to comment on.

***

**Thandi:** Third: the big crate is cheaper per can, but the memo chose the small crates. Are the memos inconsistent?

**Tutor:** No — two different questions were asked, and each answer is correct for its own question. Cost per can shares the crate's price across its full capacity: 4,50 over 20 gives 22,5 cents against 9,60 over 60 giving 16 cents, so on unit cost the big crate genuinely wins. But a cost-for-the-job question locks you to the real quantity: for 25 cans, the big crate costs R9,60 with 35 spots empty, while two small crates cost R9,00 with only 15 spots empty — the small option wins by 60 cents, because the big crate's advantage lives in capacity that this job never uses. Bulk is only a bargain when the bulk is used. So before calculating, underline what the question is asking for: per can, or for this order. If it asks which is more cost-effective with no quantity given, unit cost is the tool; the moment a real weekly or monthly quantity appears, cost the actual job with each option, rounding containers up to cover the need, and compare rand with rand. State the winner, the margin and the reason, and both styles of memo will agree with you.

***

**Thandi:** Last one: the question gave the can width in millimetres and the crate in centimetres, and half the class divided anyway. What is the safe habit?

**Tutor:** Convert everything onto one unit before any division, and write the conversion as its own line. A can of 60 millimetres is 6 centimetres — divide 60 by 10, since ten millimetres make a centimetre. Then 30 centimetres over 6 centimetres gives the honest 5 cans. The learners who divided 30 by 60 got half a can and knew something was wrong; the more dangerous case is dividing 300 by 60 after converting the wrong quantity, getting a confident 5 by accident in one direction and nonsense in another. The protection is mechanical: before dividing, glance at the two units and say them aloud — centimetres over centimetres, or millimetres over millimetres, never mixed. The same discipline saves the height count, where a crate quoted at 0,24 metres must become 24 centimetres before dividing by a 12-centimetre can. Unit first, division second — in packing questions the conversion line is often a mark on its own, and it is the cheapest insurance in the section.
