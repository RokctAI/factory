# Part 1 — Expert

A family in Kimberley is adding a room to the back of the house, and the builder hands them two sheets of paper. One looks down on the new room from above — walls, a door, a window, a stoep. The other shows the same room from outside, standing on the lawn. Between those two drawings and a ruler lies every number the family needs: how long the room really is, how much carpet to buy, how many paving blocks for the stoep. This session builds scale, floor plans and elevation plans — and the arithmetic that turns a drawing into a quotation.

## Subtopic: Two Kinds of Scale and What They Promise

A SCALE states the relationship between a length on the drawing and the matching length in reality. It comes in two forms, and CAPS examines both.

A NUMBER SCALE is written as a ratio, such as 1 : 50. It reads: one unit on the plan represents fifty of the same units in reality. The units must match on both sides — that is the whole discipline of it. So on a 1 : 50 plan, 1 cm represents 50 cm, which is 0,5 m. On a 1 : 100 plan, 1 cm represents 100 cm, which is 1 m. On a road map at 1 : 2 000 000, 1 cm represents 2 000 000 cm, which is 20 000 m, which is 20 km.

A BAR SCALE is a small ruler printed on the drawing itself, divided into segments, each labelled with the real distance it stands for. On our plan, each segment measures 2 cm on the page and is labelled 1 m — which is the same as saying 1 : 50, since 2 cm on the page stands for 100 cm in reality.

Now the question examiners love: why print a bar scale at all when the ratio is already there? Because the ratio is a promise about the page at its original size. Photocopy the plan at 80 percent, or open it on a phone, and the ratio quietly becomes a lie — 1 cm no longer means 50 cm. The bar scale shrinks or stretches with the drawing, so it stays true whatever size the page becomes. That is why builders trust the bar and treat the ratio as a label.

Two more facts. The bigger the second number, the SMALLER the drawing relative to reality: 1 : 100 shows less detail than 1 : 50 on the same page. And the ratio has no units of its own — it works in millimetres, centimetres or metres, as long as both sides match.

This section's questions are with you now: convert a ratio into "1 cm represents …", and be ready to explain why a bar scale survives a photocopy when a number scale does not.

## Subtopic: From Ruler to Reality — and Back

Scale questions run in two directions, and both appear on every paper.

Forwards, from plan to reality: measure, then multiply. On the 1 : 50 plan the new bedroom measures 8,4 cm along one wall. Actual length = 8,4 × 50 = 420 cm = 4,20 m. Write the units at every step, because 420 without "cm" is worth nothing. The shorter wall measures 6,0 cm: 6,0 × 50 = 300 cm = 3,00 m.

Backwards, from reality to plan: divide. The stoep is to be 5,5 m long, and the architect is drawing at 1 : 100. Convert first: 5,5 m = 550 cm. Then divide by the scale: 550 ÷ 100 = 5,5 cm on the page. The order matters — convert to a common unit, then apply the ratio.

Now turn the room into a quotation, because that is what the topic is for. Floor area = 4,20 × 3,00 = 12,6 m². Carpet costs R189,00 per square metre, so 12,6 × 189 = R2 381,40.

Skirting board runs around the floor's edge, so it needs perimeter, not area: 2 × (4,20 + 3,00) = 14,4 m. The 0,9 m doorway takes no skirting, so 14,4 − 0,9 = 13,5 m at R42,00 a metre gives R567,00.

And the stoep outside: 5,5 m by 2,0 m is 11 m². Paving blocks measure 0,2 m by 0,1 m, so each covers 0,02 m². Blocks needed: 11 ÷ 0,02 = 550. Add 5 percent for breakage and cutting: 550 × 1,05 = 577,5, and materials round up — 578 blocks.

Note the two traps. Careless measuring: a millimetre of ruler error becomes 5 cm of real wall at 1 : 50. And mixing units mid-calculation: never multiply centimetres on the page by a scale and then call the answer metres.

## Subtopic: Reading a Floor Plan

A FLOOR PLAN is a view from directly above, as though the roof were lifted off, drawn to scale. It shows the layout: where the walls run, where the openings are, and how the rooms connect.

Learn the standard symbols, because questions test them directly. Walls appear as two parallel lines with the space between them shaded — the thickness of the wall itself. A DOOR is a gap in the wall with a straight line for the leaf and a quarter-circle arc showing which way it swings; the arc is not decoration — it tells you the door will not open into the wardrobe. A WINDOW is a thinner break, usually three parallel lines. Fixtures appear in outline from above: a rounded rectangle for a bath, a circle for a basin, an oval for a toilet. Stairs are parallel lines with an arrow marked UP.

Then the notation. Dimension lines run alongside walls with arrows at each end and the real measurement written on them, usually in millimetres on a builder's plan — so 4 200 on a plan means 4 200 mm, which is 4,2 m. Rooms carry abbreviations: BED, KIT for kitchen, BATH, WC for the toilet, and sometimes SD for sliding door. A NORTH ARROW sits in a corner, and it is doing real work — it tells you which rooms get morning sun and which face the prevailing weather.

Analysing a layout is an examinable skill in its own right. Ask what a family would ask. Does the door swing block a cupboard? Is the toilet reachable without walking through a bedroom? Is the kitchen beside the dining area? Does the north-facing wall carry the biggest window, so the room is warm in winter? An answer that proposes an alternative — "move the door to the other side of the wall so it opens against the wall and not into the passage" — earns more than one that only describes.

Pause for this section's questions now: name the symbols, read a dimension in millimetres and convert it, and justify one improvement you would make to the layout.

## Subtopic: Elevation Plans and What They Add

An ELEVATION is a straight-on view of one side of a building, drawn to scale as though you are standing directly in front of that side. There is no perspective and no angle — vertical lines stay vertical and nothing shrinks with distance, so every length on an elevation can be measured with a ruler and scaled up.

Elevations are named by the direction the wall FACES. The north elevation is the side facing north — the view you get standing to the north and looking back at the house. That naming trips people, so read it as "the face pointing that way".

What an elevation shows that a floor plan cannot: the HEIGHT of the walls, the shape and slope of the roof, the height and vertical position of windows and doors, steps, gutters and a chimney. What a floor plan shows that an elevation cannot: the arrangement of rooms, floor areas, wall thicknesses and how spaces connect. Neither drawing is complete on its own, and the marks come from saying which one answers a given question.

Read our elevation. The wall height scales to 2,4 m and the window sill sits 0,9 m above the floor with the window 1,2 m tall, so the head of the window is at 2,1 m — with 0,3 m of wall above it before the roof. The roof rises from the front wall to the back, which tells you the rain runs off to the back gutter.

Then combine the two drawings, which is what a full question asks. The floor plan gives a perimeter of 14,4 m; the elevation gives a wall height of 2,4 m. Wall area = 14,4 × 2,4 = 34,56 m². Subtract the door, 0,9 × 2,1 = 1,89 m², and the window, 1,2 × 1,2 = 1,44 m²: 34,56 − 1,89 − 1,44 = 31,23 m² to be plastered or painted. Neither drawing could have produced that number alone.

Say the answer in a sentence, with units: "The new room has 31,23 m² of wall surface once the door and window are deducted."

The final questions of this part are in front of you now — match a described feature to the drawing that shows it, and combine a plan measurement with an elevation measurement to produce one area.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same two drawings on the kitchen table, where nobody says "elevation" — they say "this is looking down on it, and this is what it looks like from outside".

## Subtopic: The Shrunk House

A plan is a house that has been shrunk to fit on a page, and the scale is the honest label saying by how much.

1 : 50 means the drawing is fifty times smaller than the real thing. So every centimetre you measure on the paper is fifty centimetres of real wall — half a metre. 1 : 100 means a hundred times smaller: one centimetre on the page is a whole metre of wall. Read it as a shrinking factor and it stops being mysterious.

To go from page to reality, MULTIPLY. Measure the long wall of the new bedroom: 8,4 cm. Times 50 is 420 cm, and 420 cm is 4,20 m. Do the short wall too: 6,0 cm times 50 is 300 cm, which is 3,00 m. Now you know the room without ever visiting it.

To go the other way — reality to page — DIVIDE. The stoep will be 5,5 m long, and the plan is 1 : 100. Turn the metres into centimetres first: 550 cm. Divide by 100: it goes on the page as a 5,5 cm line.

One last thing about scale, and it is the question examiners keep asking. Why do plans carry a little printed ruler as well as the ratio? Because a photocopier does not know about ratios. Shrink the page to 80 percent and "1 : 50" is now a lie, but the printed ruler shrank too — so it still tells the truth. That is why the builder trusts the little ruler.

Some questions on shrinking and growing are with you right now — page to reality you multiply, reality to page you divide, and the units come along for the ride.

## Subtopic: The Bird's Eye and the Standing-in-Front View

Two drawings, two completely different jobs, and the whole confusion clears once you know which is which.

The floor plan is the bird's-eye view — the roof lifted off, looking straight down. It answers "where is everything?" You can see the shape of the room, where the door and window sit in the wall, how big the floor is, and whether you must walk through the kitchen to reach the toilet.

The symbols are just shorthand. Two lines with shading between them is a wall. A gap with a little quarter-circle sweeping across it is a door, and that curve shows which way it swings — a door that opens into a cupboard is a real problem someone pays to fix. A thin triple line is a window. A rounded rectangle is a bath, a small circle a basin. Numbers along the walls are usually millimetres, so 4 200 means 4,2 m.

The elevation is the standing-in-front view. Imagine walking outside and looking straight at one wall, not from an angle. That is what an elevation draws. It answers "how high?" — how tall the walls are, how the roof slopes, how far off the floor the window sits.

So each drawing knows things the other cannot. Ask the floor plan about layout and floor space. Ask the elevation about heights and the roof. And notice the naming: the north elevation is the wall FACING north — the side that points that way.

Questions on the two views are here now. For each one, decide first which drawing could possibly answer it — bird's eye, or standing in front.

## Subtopic: Turning the Plan into a Shopping List

Here is why any of this matters: two sheets of paper plus a ruler equal a quotation, and you can check the builder's numbers before you sign anything.

The floor first. The room is 4,20 m by 3,00 m, so the floor is 4,20 × 3,00 = 12,6 m². Carpet at R189 a square metre is 12,6 × 189 = R2 381,40.

Then the edge, which is a different measurement. Skirting board runs around the bottom of the walls, so it is a walk-around, not a cover: 4,20 and 3,00 and 4,20 and 3,00 is 14,4 m. Take off the 0,9 m doorway, because no skirting crosses a door: 13,5 m at R42 a metre is R567,00.

Now the stoep. It is 5,5 m by 2,0 m, so 11 m² to be paved. Each block is 20 cm by 10 cm — that is 0,2 m by 0,1 m, which covers 0,02 m². How many blocks fit into 11 square metres? 11 ÷ 0,02 = 550. Add five percent because blocks chip and some must be cut: 550 × 1,05 is 577,5, and you round materials up — 578 blocks.

Last, the walls, and this is where the two drawings shake hands. The floor plan tells you the walk-around: 14,4 m. The elevation tells you the height: 2,4 m. Multiply and you have the wall surface: 14,4 × 2,4 = 34,56 m². Take out what you do not plaster — the door, 0,9 by 2,1, which is 1,89 m², and the window, 1,2 by 1,2, which is 1,44 m² — and you are left with 31,23 m².

Neither piece of paper could have told you that alone. That is the whole point of having two.

The last questions of the lesson are with you now. Measure carefully, write the unit on every line, keep the door and window subtractions visible, and finish with a sentence a builder could actually quote from.
