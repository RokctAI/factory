**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! First: I keep getting scale conversions the wrong way round. How do I know whether to multiply by 50 or divide by 50?

**Tutor:** Ask yourself which world you are moving towards, and let common sense referee the answer. The real house is enormous and the paper is small, so numbers must GROW when you travel from the page to reality, and SHRINK when you travel from reality onto the page. Page to real: multiply. Real to page: divide. Now test it, because a sense check makes the rule permanent. The bedroom wall measures 8,4 centimetres on a 1 : 50 plan. Multiply: 8,4 times 50 is 420 centimetres, which is 4,20 metres. Is a bedroom wall about four metres long? Yes — that is an ordinary room. Divide instead and you would get 0,168 centimetres, a wall thinner than a pencil line, which no house has ever had. Going the other way: the stoep is 5,5 metres long and the plan is 1 : 100. Convert to centimetres first, 550, then divide by 100 to get 5,5 centimetres — a line that comfortably fits on a page. Two habits make this automatic. Convert both quantities to the same unit before applying the ratio, and after every answer ask whether a real house could look like that.

**Thandi:** Next one: the dimensions on our plan are written as 4200 with no unit at all. Is that centimetres? It cannot be metres, surely.

**Tutor:** It is millimetres, and building plans use them almost everywhere for a very practical reason. Millimetres let a builder write a whole wall as a round whole number with no decimal comma at all — 4200, 3000, 900 — and a decimal comma is exactly the sort of thing that gets lost on a dusty site or smudged in a photocopy. So the convention is to drop the unit and understand millimetres throughout. Your job in an exam is to convert before you use the number in any area or cost calculation. Divide by 1 000 to get metres: 4200 millimetres is 4,2 metres, 3000 is 3 metres, 900 is 0,9 metres — that last one being a standard doorway width, which is a useful thing to recognise. The danger is treating 4200 as centimetres, which would make the bedroom 42 metres long, or as metres, which would make it four kilometres. Both are absurd, and that absurdity is your protection: a domestic room is roughly 3 to 5 metres across, a door is about 0,9 metres wide and about 2,1 metres high, and a ceiling is around 2,4 to 2,7 metres. Carry those reference sizes in your head and a misread unit announces itself immediately.

**Thandi:** This one confused half the class. Why is the north elevation the side facing north? I thought it meant you are standing in the north looking at the house.

**Tutor:** You have described the view exactly right, and the naming still works the way the class was taught — those two things are the same thing, which is why it feels slippery. Picture it properly. If you stand to the north of the house and look back at it, the wall you are looking at is the wall that points north. So "north elevation" names the face, and standing in the north is simply how you get to see that face. The reason it matters is not vocabulary but sunlight and weather, which is why architects care. In South Africa the sun sits in the northern half of the sky, so a north-facing wall receives sun through most of the day. Put the big living-room windows there and the room is warm and bright in winter without paying for heating; put them on the south face and the room stays cold and dim all year. So when a question asks you to comment on a layout, the north arrow on the floor plan is not decoration — it is evidence. A good answer says something like: the bedroom windows face north, so the room gets morning and midday sun, which suits a home in a cold-winter area, while the bathroom on the south side stays cool and takes the weather. That kind of reasoning earns the interpretation marks.

**Thandi:** Last one: how do I know when to use the floor plan and when to use the elevation? In the wall-area question I did not know which one to measure.

**Tutor:** That question is a small trap, because the honest answer is that you need both — and recognising that is what the marks are for. Sort the information by asking what kind of quantity you are chasing. Anything horizontal and flat lives on the floor plan: room lengths and breadths, floor area, the walk-around distance for skirting, where a door sits along a wall, whether the kitchen touches the dining area. Anything vertical lives on the elevation: wall height, the pitch of the roof, how far above the floor a window sill sits, the height of a step. Wall area is horizontal times vertical, so it needs one number from each. In our room the floor plan gives a perimeter of 14,4 metres and the elevation gives a height of 2,4 metres, so the wall surface is 14,4 times 2,4, which is 34,56 square metres. Then subtract the openings, because nobody plasters a doorway: the door is 0,9 by 2,1, which is 1,89, and the window is 1,2 by 1,2, which is 1,44, leaving 31,23 square metres. Write that subtraction as its own line — markers award it separately — and finish with a sentence stating the area and its unit.
