Hey, it's Thandi — plans can look like an alien language the first time, all little arcs and shaded lines and numbers with no units. Let me shrink the whole topic down for you, because there is honestly less here than it looks.

The scale is one sentence: 1 : 50 means the drawing is fifty times smaller than the real thing. That is it. Going from the page to the real world, you MULTIPLY. Going from the real world onto the page, you DIVIDE. Say those two directions out loud once — "page to real, times; real to page, divide" — and half the topic is already yours.

The two drawings are just two ways of looking at the same room. The floor plan is the bird's-eye view: the roof lifted off, looking straight down, answering "where is everything?" The elevation is the standing-in-front view: you outside on the lawn, looking straight at one wall, answering "how high?" When a question asks about layout or floor space, go to the plan. When it asks about wall height or the roof, go to the elevation. When it asks for wall AREA, you need both — the walk-around from the plan and the height from the elevation.

The habit that protects your marks here is small and boring and worth a lot: write the unit on every single line. Measure 8,4 cm, times 50, is 420 cm, is 4,20 m. Four lines, four units. A learner who writes 420 and leaves it there has done the maths and lost the mark.

And you already read plans. You have looked at a room and thought "that couch will not fit through there". Same skill, now with a ruler. You've got this!
