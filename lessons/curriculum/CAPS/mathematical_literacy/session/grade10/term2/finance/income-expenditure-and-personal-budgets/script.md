# Part 1 — Expert

A household in Katlehong has a payslip, a room let out at the back, a sewing machine that earns on weekends, and eleven separate things to pay for every month. At the end of that arithmetic sits one number that decides whether the family sleeps easily: the difference between what came in and what went out. This session builds that number properly — reading gross and net income off a payslip, sorting expenditure into fixed, variable and occasional, laying out a household budget, and then turning the same logic on a small business to find the point at which it starts to make money.

## Subtopic: Income — Gross, Deductions and What Actually Arrives

INCOME is money received. On a payslip it appears twice, and confusing the two figures is the most expensive error in this topic.

GROSS income is the full amount earned before anything is taken off. DEDUCTIONS are amounts the employer removes and pays over on the employee's behalf. NET income — also called take-home pay — is gross income minus total deductions, and it is the only figure a budget may use, because it is the only money that reaches the household.

Read the payslip. Gross salary: R9 500,00. Deductions: PAYE, which is income tax, R760,00; UIF — the Unemployment Insurance Fund, contributed at one percent of earnings — R95,00; and a pension fund contribution of five percent, R475,00. Check that percentage: five percent of R9 500 is 0,05 times 9 500, which is R475,00, so the payslip is consistent.

Total deductions: R760 plus R95 plus R475 gives R1 330,00. Net income: R9 500 minus R1 330 gives R8 170,00.

A household usually has more than one income stream, and all of them belong in the budget. This family adds R900,00 rent from the back room and R1 130,00 from weekend sewing. Total household income: R8 170 plus R900 plus R1 130 gives R10 200,00 a month.

Two points examiners press. First, deductions are not expenditure — they never enter the household, so they must not be listed again among the expenses; doing so subtracts the same money twice. Second, income that varies must be treated cautiously: sewing income of R1 130 was last month's figure, not a promise, and a careful budget either uses the lowest recent month or plans for its absence.

This section's questions are with you now — read gross and net off the payslip correctly, and be ready to say why only one of them may enter a budget.

## Subtopic: Expenditure — Fixed, Variable and Occasional

EXPENDITURE is money paid out, and CAPS sorts it into three kinds because each kind behaves differently when money gets tight.

FIXED expenses are the same amount every month, whether the month goes well or badly: rent R3 000,00, funeral policy R180,00, school fees R450,00, and the phone contract R199,00. Their total is R3 000 plus R180 plus R450 plus R199, which gives R3 829,00. These are contracts and commitments; they cannot be reduced this month by deciding to use less.

VARIABLE expenses change with how much is used or bought: groceries R2 600,00, prepaid electricity R750,00, taxi fare R880,00, airtime and data R240,00, and toiletries and cleaning R320,00. Their total is R2 600 plus R750 plus R880 plus R240 plus R320, which gives R4 790,00. These are the household's only fast lever — the amounts that can actually be changed within a month.

OCCASIONAL expenses arrive a few times a year but must be paid monthly in advance if they are not to cause a crisis: school uniforms and stationery, set aside at R400,00 a month, and December and family events at R500,00 a month, giving R900,00. Setting money aside monthly for a known future cost is called PROVISIONING, and it is the difference between a planned December and a borrowed one.

Note the distinction examiners test hardest. Electricity is variable even though it arrives every month — the amount changes with use. Rent is fixed even if you can move house next year — within the budget period the amount does not respond to your behaviour. The test is not "does it come every month?" but "does the amount change with how much I use?"

Total expenditure: R3 829 plus R4 790 plus R900 gives R9 519,00.

Work through this section's questions now, classifying each item and giving the reason for the classification — the reason is where the marks sit.

## Subtopic: The Budget and Its Bottom Line

A BUDGET is a plan: expected income against expected expenditure, for a stated period, prepared BEFORE the period begins. An INCOME AND EXPENDITURE STATEMENT is the record of what actually happened afterwards. Both are examinable, and the difference in tense is the difference between them.

Set the budget out. Income: R10 200,00. Expenditure: R9 519,00. Subtract: R10 200 minus R9 519 gives R681,00. Income exceeds expenditure, so the household has a SURPLUS of R681,00. Had expenditure exceeded income, the shortfall would be a DEFICIT, and a deficit must be funded — from savings, from borrowing, or by not paying something.

Now interpret, because a budget that is only added up has not been used. Express the big items as percentages of income. Rent: 3 000 divided by 10 200 gives 0,2941, which is 29,4 percent. Groceries: 2 600 divided by 10 200 gives 0,2549, which is 25,5 percent. Transport: 880 divided by 10 200 gives 0,0863, which is 8,6 percent. Housing and food together take more than half of everything the household earns — which is the honest shape of most South African household budgets and the reason a rent increase hurts so immediately.

Then stress-test it. Suppose the sewing work dries up: income falls by R1 130 to R9 070,00, while expenditure stays at R9 519,00. The bottom line becomes R9 070 minus R9 519, which is negative R449,00 — a deficit of R449,00.

Close it in the right order. Fixed costs cannot move this month, so cut variables: airtime and data from R240 to R120 saves R120; groceries reduced by R200 saves R200; the December provision trimmed from R500 to R350 saves R150. Total saved: R120 plus R200 plus R150 gives R470,00. New expenditure: R9 519 minus R470 gives R9 049,00, against income of R9 070,00 — a surplus of R21,00. Thin, but positive, and achieved without missing a contractual payment.

Take on this section's questions now: compute a bottom line, name it surplus or deficit, and justify which items you would adjust first.

## Subtopic: Break-Even — When the Stall Starts Making Money

The same three ideas run a business. A vetkoek stall at the taxi rank pays a table rental of R400,00 a month and R200,00 for a gas refill — FIXED COSTS of R600,00, payable whether one vetkoek is sold or none. Each vetkoek costs R3,50 in flour, oil and filling — a VARIABLE COST, incurred only when one is made. The selling price is R8,00.

INCOME is selling price times number sold. TOTAL COST is fixed cost plus variable cost times number sold. PROFIT is income minus total cost, and a negative profit is a LOSS.

The BREAK-EVEN POINT is the number sold at which income exactly equals total cost — where the stall stops losing and has not yet gained. Find it by asking what each vetkoek contributes to the fixed costs: R8,00 received minus R3,50 spent leaves R4,50 per vetkoek towards the R600. So the number needed is 600 divided by 4,50, which is 133,33.

Now round with judgement, which is the examinable step. You cannot sell a third of a vetkoek, and rounding DOWN to 133 leaves her short: income 133 times R8,00 is R1 064,00, while total cost is R600 plus 133 times R3,50, which is R600 plus R465,50, or R1 065,50 — a loss of R1,50. Rounding UP to 134: income R1 072,00 against total cost R600 plus R469,00, which is R1 069,00 — a profit of R3,00. Break-even is therefore 134 vetkoek a month. When a break-even calculation gives a decimal, always round up.

Then the real month. She trades 26 days and sells about 12 a day: 26 times 12 gives 312 vetkoek. Income: 312 times R8,00 is R2 496,00. Total cost: R600 plus 312 times R3,50, which is R600 plus R1 092,00, or R1 692,00. Profit: R2 496 minus R1 692 gives R804,00 a month.

Answer in a sentence, always: "The stall breaks even at 134 vetkoek a month and, selling about 312, makes a profit of R804,00." The number alone is half an answer; the sentence is the whole one.

The final questions of this part are in front of you now — separate fixed from variable costs before anything else, and round break-even upward with a reason.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same month at the kitchen table, with the payslip on one side, the bills on the other, and the question every household actually asks: is there anything left?

## Subtopic: Money That Walks In, Money That Walks Out

Put two bowls on the table. Everything that comes IN goes in the first bowl. Everything that goes OUT goes in the second. That is a budget. Everything else is detail.

Now the first surprise on any payslip. It says R9 500 at the top, but that money never reaches your hand. Before you see it, three bites are taken: tax, R760; a small slice for the unemployment fund, R95, which is one rand for every hundred you earn; and R475 into a pension for the you of the future. Those bites come to R1 330, and what actually lands in the bank is R9 500 take away R1 330 — R8 170.

That R8 170 is the only money you may put in the first bowl. Budgeting with the R9 500 is like planning supper around a chicken your neighbour is holding. And here is the mistake that follows: do not then write "tax" in the out-bowl. It never came in, so it cannot go out. Counting it twice is how a budget goes quietly wrong.

Anything else that comes in belongs in the first bowl too. R900 from the room let at the back. R1 130 from the sewing machine on Saturdays. Add all three: R8 170 plus R900 plus R1 130 — R10 200 walked in this month.

One honest warning about that sewing money: it is not a salary. It came last month and may not come this month. A careful household plans on the money it is sure of, and treats the rest as a bonus.

Some questions on the in-bowl are with you right now — remember to use what lands in the bank, not what the payslip says at the top.

## Subtopic: The Bills That Don't Care How Your Month Went

Now fill the out-bowl, and notice the bills are not all the same animal.

Some bills do not care about you at all. Rent, R3 000. The funeral policy, R180. School fees, R450. The phone contract, R199. Whether you are careful or careless, they arrive at the same size — R3 829 of them, together. You cannot fix a bad month by using less rent.

Other bills listen to you. Groceries, R2 600. Prepaid electricity, R750. Taxi fare, R880. Airtime and data, R240. Toiletries and cleaning, R320 — R4 790 in all. Use less, pay less. This is the only part of the out-bowl you can move this week, which is why it is the first place you look when money is short.

And then the sneaky ones — the bills that do not come every month but come like a truck when they come. School uniforms and stationery in January. December. A funeral, a wedding, tyres. The trick every grandmother knows is to pay them in slices before they arrive: R400 a month towards uniforms, R500 a month towards December, R900 set aside so those months arrive as a plan and not a panic.

Here is the test that keeps you out of trouble in the exam. Do not ask "does it come every month?" Ask "does the amount change when I change what I do?" Electricity comes every month and still changes — so it listens to you. Rent is the same every month no matter what you do — so it does not.

Questions on sorting the bills are here now. For each one, say which kind it is and, more importantly, WHY.

## Subtopic: The Vetkoek That Pays for the Table

Now the bottom line, which is just one subtraction. R10 200 walked in; R9 519 walked out. R10 200 take away R9 519 leaves R681 still standing at the end of the month. Money left over is a surplus; if the out-bowl had been heavier, that would be a deficit, and a deficit always has to be paid for by somebody — savings, a loan, or an unpaid bill.

Look at the shape of the month, not just the leftover. Rent takes R3 000 out of R10 200, which is nearly 30 out of every 100 rands. Groceries take R2 600, about 25 in every 100. So the roof and the food together swallow more than half of everything this family earns — which is exactly why a rent increase is felt the same week it happens.

Now the same thinking for a small business, because it is identical. A woman sells vetkoek at the taxi rank. Before she sells anything she owes R600 a month — R400 for the table, R200 for gas. Every vetkoek costs her R3,50 to make and sells for R8,00, so each one puts R4,50 in her hand towards that R600. How many must she sell before she stops going backwards? R600 shared into lots of R4,50 gives 133 and a bit. You cannot sell a bit of a vetkoek, and 133 still leaves her short by a rand and a half — so she needs 134. When the answer to a break-even question has a decimal, always go UP to the next whole one.

Then the real month: 26 trading days, about 12 vetkoek a day, is 312 sold. R8,00 each brings in R2 496. Her costs are the R600 plus 312 lots of R3,50, which is R1 092 — R1 692 altogether. R2 496 take away R1 692 leaves R804 profit. She is well past her 134, and now she knows exactly how far.

Your last questions are with you now. Do the subtraction, put the rand sign and the cents on it, and finish with one plain sentence saying what the household or the stall should actually do — because a bottom line without a decision is only half the work.
