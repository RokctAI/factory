Hey, it's Thandi — a payslip, eleven bills, three kinds of cost, and something called break-even. It looks like a whole accounting course landed in your Mathematical Literacy paper. It did not. This topic is one subtraction with good bookkeeping in front of it.

Start at the top. Money in, money out, subtract. That is the entire answer to almost every budget question in the paper. Everything else — gross, net, fixed, variable, occasional, surplus, deficit — is just labelling which pile a number belongs to before you subtract.

The payslip is where most marks are lost, so pin it down. Gross is what you earned. Deductions are what was taken off before you saw it. Net is what actually arrived, and NET is the only figure that may go into a budget. Then the follow-on rule that saves you again: because the deductions never arrived, never list them as expenses too. Count them once, on the way past.

Sorting the bills has exactly one test, and it is not the one people expect. Do not ask "does it come every month?" Ask "does the amount change with what I do?" Electricity arrives every month and still changes, so it is variable. Rent arrives every month and never changes, so it is fixed. Say the test aloud with each item and you will not misclassify.

For break-even, hold one sentence: each item sold contributes selling price minus its own cost, and you keep selling until those contributions have covered the fixed costs. R8,00 minus R3,50 is R4,50 a vetkoek; R600 divided by R4,50 gives 133,33; and you round UP, because 133 still leaves her short. Rounding up on break-even is a rule with a reason, and saying the reason earns the mark.

Last habit, and it is the one that turns a right answer into a full answer: finish with a sentence. Not "R681" — "The household has a surplus of R681, which should go towards the December provision." A number without a decision is only half the work. You've got this!
