# Part 1 — Expert

A shopper in Mthatha puts a basket down at the till: maize meal, milk, brown bread, washing powder, a cooldrink and chips. The slip carries three numbers she has to understand — the total, the VAT, and the amount that was never taxed at all. Later that week the same household must decide whether to buy a fridge on today's sale price or leave the money earning interest. This session builds the three tools that settle both questions — VAT, discount and simple interest — each run forwards and backwards, because examiners ask for both directions.

## Subtopic: VAT — the Tax Already Inside the Price

VALUE-ADDED TAX is a tax on goods and services, charged in South Africa at 15 percent and collected by the seller on behalf of SARS. Two terms decide every VAT question. A price EXCLUSIVE of VAT is the price before the tax is added. A price INCLUSIVE of VAT is the price with the tax already inside it — and by law the price on a shelf in a South African shop is the inclusive price.

Forwards, from exclusive to inclusive, is the easy direction. A wholesaler quotes a cooler box at R690,00 excluding VAT. The VAT is 690 × 0,15, which is R103,50, so the inclusive price is R793,50. One step does the same job: 690 × 1,15 gives R793,50.

Backwards is where marks are lost. A till slip shows R1 288,00 including VAT — find the amount before VAT. Subtracting 15 percent is wrong, because the 15 percent was worked out on the smaller exclusive price, not on the R1 288. Undo the multiplication instead: 1 288 ÷ 1,15 gives R1 120,00. The VAT is then 1 288 − 1 120, which is R168,00, and the check confirms it: 1 120 × 0,15 is R168,00.

Now the part the basket forces you to notice: not everything is taxed. ZERO-RATED goods carry VAT at zero percent so that basic food stays reachable — brown bread, maize meal, rice, samp, dried beans, milk, eggs, fresh fruit and vegetables, vegetable oil, tinned pilchards and illuminating paraffin sit on that list.

Work the basket. Zero-rated: maize meal R130,00, milk R38,00, brown bread R34,00 — R202,00 carrying no VAT. Standard-rated: washing powder R92,00, cooldrink R23,50, chips R57,00 — R172,50, VAT already included. The VAT inside that R172,50 is 172,50 ÷ 1,15, which is R150,00, so the VAT is 172,50 − 150,00, or R22,50. Basket total: R202,00 plus R172,50 gives R374,50, of which R22,50 is VAT.

The trap is now visible: 15 percent of the R374,50 total would give R56,18 — more than double the true VAT — because it taxes the maize meal and the milk. Separate the zero-rated items before any VAT calculation begins.

The questions for this section are with you now: distinguish inclusive from exclusive, divide by 1,15 rather than subtracting, and check whether an item is zero-rated before you tax it.

## Subtopic: Discount — What Comes Off, and Off What

A DISCOUNT is a reduction on the MARKED PRICE, and the answer depends entirely on what the percentage is taken of. A tracksuit is marked R640,00 and the sign says 30 percent off. The discount amount is 640 × 0,30, which is R192,00; the SALE PRICE is 640 − 192, or R448,00. The single-multiplier version is quicker and safer: a 30 percent decrease leaves 70 percent, so 640 × 0,70 gives R448,00. Decrease means one minus the decimal; increase means one plus it.

Now the sign that catches people. The rail says 20 percent off, and at the till a poster promises a further 10 percent. Learners add: 30 percent. The shop knows better. Step one: 640 × 0,80 gives R512,00. Step two, on the new price: 512 × 0,90 gives R460,80. Combine the multipliers instead — 0,80 × 0,90 gives 0,72 — and the honest headline appears: the shopper pays 72 percent of the marked price, a discount of 28 percent, not 30. The saving is R179,20, not R192,00. Successive percentages never add, because each one bites what the previous one left.

Two more facts the examiner leans on. Because the marked price already includes VAT, discounting shrinks the VAT with it: at R460,80 the exclusive price is R400,70, so the VAT is R60,10 rather than the R83,48 hiding inside R640,00. And read the wording: "buy two, get one free" is not 50 percent off — three for the price of two is a third off, 33,3 percent.

Reverse discounts work like reverse VAT. If R460,80 follows a 28 percent discount, the marked price is 460,80 ÷ 0,72, which is R640,00. Undo a multiplier by dividing by it — always.

Take on this section's questions now, and be strict about the base: name the amount each percentage is being taken of before you press a single key.

## Subtopic: Simple Interest — Growth in a Straight Line

INTEREST is the price of money — paid to you when you lend it to a bank, paid by you when you borrow. The INTEREST RATE is the percentage; the INTEREST is the rand amount, and confusing the two loses marks with monotonous regularity. SIMPLE INTEREST is calculated on the ORIGINAL amount every period, never on interest already earned: interest equals principal times rate times time, with the rate as a decimal and the time in YEARS.

A learner puts R4 500,00 from holiday work into a fixed deposit paying 7,5 percent simple interest per year, for three years. Write the substitution, because that line carries a mark on its own: interest = 4 500 × 0,075 × 3. In order: 4 500 × 0,075 gives R337,50 — one year's interest — and R337,50 × 3 gives R1 012,50. The closing balance is R5 512,50.

Notice the shape. The interest is R337,50 in year one, in year two and in year three — identical, because every year's calculation returns to the same R4 500. Plotted, simple interest is a straight line, and that is the whole meaning of the word "simple".

Time must be in years, and this is the topic's most common slip. Eight months is 8 ÷ 12, or 0,667 of a year; eighteen months is 1,5; six months is 0,5. Substitute 8 for eight months and the answer is twelve times too large.

Work one backwards, since exams do. An amount grows from R4 500,00 to R5 062,50 in two years — find the rate. The interest is R562,50. Substitute: 562,50 = 4 500 × rate × 2, so 562,50 = 9 000 × rate, and the rate is 562,50 ÷ 9 000, which is 0,0625 — 6,25 percent per year. Peel off what you know, divide by the rest, and say "per year", because the unit is part of the answer.

Work through this section's questions now: substitute in full, convert months to years before anything else, and round only at the end, to the cent.

## Subtopic: The Fridge Decision — Three Tools, One Answer

The topic exists to serve decisions, so finish with one. A fridge is marked R7 200,00 and this week the store takes 15 percent off. The family has R6 500,00 in a savings account paying 6 percent simple interest per year, and had planned to buy in six months' time.

Price the sale first. The sale price is 7 200 × 0,85, which is R6 120,00 — a saving of R1 080,00. The VAT inside it, should the question ask, is 6 120 − 5 321,74, or R798,26.

Now price the waiting. If they leave the money untouched for six months, the interest earned is 6 500 × 0,06 × 0,5 — six months is 0,5 of a year. That gives 6 500 × 0,03, which is R195,00. But in six months the sale is over and the fridge is back at R7 200,00.

Lay the options side by side. Buy now: R6 120,00 leaves the account, R380,00 stays behind, and the fridge runs for six months. Wait: savings grow to R6 695,00 while the fridge returns to R7 200,00 — leaving them R505,00 SHORT of buying it at all. Netting the two effects, waiting gains R195,00 of interest and gives up R1 080,00 of discount: a net loss of R885,00.

Answer in a sentence, always: "The family should buy during the sale, because the R1 080 discount is more than five times the R195 of interest waiting would earn — a net gain of R885 — and after six months their savings would still be R505 short of the full price."

Finally, the habits that convert working into marks here: write the multiplier or formula before substituting; keep the rand sign and the cents; divide rather than subtract when removing VAT or undoing a discount; convert months to years; round once, at the end, to two decimals; and close with a sentence answering the question that was actually asked.

The last questions of this part are in front of you now — compare two options in full, show both totals and the difference, and finish each answer with a recommendation in words.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same three tools from the till at the corner shop, where nobody says "exclusive of value-added tax" and everybody still knows exactly how much money left their hand.

## Subtopic: The Till Slip at the Corner Shop

Here is the thing about VAT that confuses everyone: it is already in the price. When the shelf says R92,00 for washing powder, R92,00 is what you pay. The tax is not waiting to jump on at the till — it is baked into the loaf already.

So "how much VAT did I pay?" really means "how much of this R92,00 was tax?" You cannot answer it by taking 15 percent of R92,00, because the 15 percent was added to a smaller number to make the R92,00 in the first place. Think of a glass of squash: the tax is the concentrate, already stirred in — you cannot measure it by taking 15 percent of the full glass.

The move that works is division. The price with tax is the price without tax multiplied by 1,15 — so go backwards by dividing. Try it on the taxed part of the basket: R92,00 plus R23,50 plus R57,00 is R172,50. Divide by 1,15 and you get R150,00 — the shop's own price. Take that away from R172,50 and there is the tax: R22,50.

And here is the kindness built into the system. The maize meal, milk and brown bread pay no tax at all — zero-rated, along with rice, samp, beans, eggs, vegetables, cooking oil, tinned pilchards and paraffin. That is R202,00 the taxman does not touch. So the basket is R374,50 and only R22,50 of it is tax. Take 15 percent of R374,50 and you get R56,18 — and you have just taxed the mielie meal, which nobody does.

Some questions on the slip are with you right now — for each one, first ask which items are taxed at all, then divide by 1,15 instead of subtracting.

## Subtopic: The Sale Sign That Says More Than It Gives

A tracksuit is marked R640,00 and the sign shouts 30 percent off. Ten percent of R640 is R64, so 30 percent is three lots of R64 — R192,00, and you pay R448,00. Faster way, and the one that saves you in every chained question: if 30 percent comes off, 70 percent stays on, so multiply by 0,70 straight away. R640 × 0,70 is R448,00. One press instead of two.

Now the sneaky sign. The rail says 20 percent off, and the poster at the till says another 10 percent off. Everybody's head says 30. It is not 30. The first sign takes 20 percent off R640, leaving R512,00. The second sign then takes 10 percent off the R512 — not off the R640 — leaving R460,80. Compare that with a true 30 percent off, which would be R448,00. You paid R12,80 more than the sign made you feel.

Why? Because the second discount arrives after the first has already shrunk the price, so it has less to bite. Percentages of different amounts are different amounts of money — the second percentage eats only what the first left on the plate.

One more sign to translate: "buy two, get one free" sounds like half price and is not. Three things for the price of two saves you one in three — a third off, about 33 percent — and only if you wanted three.

Your questions for this section are here now. Before you calculate, say out loud which price the percentage is coming off — the marked price or the already-reduced one.

## Subtopic: Money That Grows the Same Amount Every Year

Simple interest is the friendliest growth there is, because it never changes its mind. Every year the bank pays you the same amount, worked out on what you originally put in.

A learner banks R4 500,00 from holiday work at 7,5 percent a year — seven and a half rands for every hundred. Each year the bank pays 4 500 × 0,075, which is R337,50. Not more in year two, not more in year three. Three identical payments come to R1 012,50, and she ends with R5 512,50.

That is the whole idea: same amount, every year, straight line. Interest equals the money you started with, times the rate as a decimal, times the number of years.

One warning that costs more marks than anything else here: the time must be in YEARS. Six months is not 6, it is 0,5. Eight months is 8 divided by 12, which is 0,667. Put 6 where 0,5 belongs and your answer is twelve times too big — and a fixed deposit paying out more than you put in should stop you cold.

Now the decision that pulls all three tools together. A fridge is R7 200,00, on sale at 15 percent off: R7 200 × 0,85 is R6 120,00 — R1 080 saved. The family has R6 500 in savings earning 6 percent a year and were going to wait six months. That interest is 6 500 × 0,06 × 0,5, which is R195,00. So waiting earns R195 and gives up R1 080 — a net loss of R885,00. Worse: their savings would stand at R6 695 and the fridge back at R7 200 would be R505 out of reach. Buying now wins, and it is not close.

The last questions of the lesson are with you now. Show the working line before the answer, keep the rand sign and the cents on it, and end with one plain sentence saying what you would actually tell the family to do — because a number without a decision is only half of what the marker is looking for.
