**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! First: my group listed the R525 pension under expenses, because the family definitely does not get to spend that money. It was marked wrong. Why?

**Tutor:** Because that money has already been counted once, and counting it again makes the household look R525 poorer than it really is. Trace the money's path one time only. The employer works out R10 500 as gross, then removes R840 for tax, R105 for the unemployment fund and R525 for the pension — R1 470 altogether — and pays those amounts over before anyone goes to the ATM. What lands in the account is R9 030, and that deposited figure is what the budget records as income. The R1 470 is already reflected in it — by not being there. Write R525 in the expense column as well and you have subtracted the pension from money that never contained it, and the bottom line drops for no real-world reason. A picture that helps: deductions happen upstream of the household, expenses happen downstream. The budget starts at the point where the water reaches your tank. The one legitimate place deductions reappear is a question about the total cost of employing someone, or the value of a whole pay package — never inside a household budget built on take-home pay.

**Thandi:** Next: somebody said this family is fine because they have R941 left over. But then I remembered our own electricity going up last winter and I wasn't so sure. Is R941 actually fine?

**Tutor:** The budget is correct and it is thin, and telling those two things apart is a skill worth marks. R941 out of R11 200 is a margin of about eight and a half percent — the family spends roughly ninety-one cents of every rand that arrives. That is a plan with a small shock absorber, not a big one. One cold winter where prepaid electricity climbs a few hundred rand, or one month where the Saturday hair work dries up — worth R1 070 — and the cushion is gone. So a strong answer never stops at "surplus of R941". It says the surplus exists but is modest relative to income, points at the specific risks visible in the numbers — an income stream that is not guaranteed, and a variable electricity cost that rises in winter — and proposes a response: move the surplus into an emergency provision rather than letting it dissolve into spending, and provision for winter electricity exactly the way the family already provisions for December. When a question asks you to "comment on the budget", that is the expected shape: the arithmetic is the easy half, and the judgement about how much room the household really has is the half that earns the marks.

**Thandi:** This one tripped a lot of us: our break-even came out at 194,59 koeksisters and we rounded to 195, but someone else rounded to 194 because "the digit after the comma was a five, so you look at the next digit" — and we honestly could not agree on the rule.

**Tutor:** The disagreement exists because you were both reaching for the digit rule, and break-even does not use it. Rounding in Mathematical Literacy follows the context, not the decimal. The ordinary digit rule applies when you are reporting a measurement. Break-even is not a measurement — it is a threshold that must actually be reached, so test both sides and let the money decide. At 194 koeksisters, income is 194 times R6,50, which is R1 261,00, while total cost is the R720 of fixed costs plus 194 times R2,80, which is R543,20 — R1 263,20 in all. She is still R2,20 behind. At 195, income is R1 267,50 against costs of R1 266,00, so she is R1,50 ahead. Only 195 satisfies "income at least covers cost", so 195 is the answer, whatever the decimals were doing. The same context logic runs both ways through this subject: round UP for anything you must have enough of — break-even sales, tins of paint, taxis for a trip — and round DOWN for how many whole items fit or how long a supply lasts. Whenever you round, write one short sentence naming the direction and the reason. That sentence is regularly worth a mark on its own.

**Thandi:** Last one, the practical one: when the money runs short, what gets cut first? Our group had four different orders and a small argument.

**Tutor:** There is a defensible order, and defending it is precisely what such a question tests. Start with what can actually move. Fixed costs — rent, school fees, the funeral policy, the phone contract — are contractual: within this month they do not respond to using less, and escaping them means renegotiating, relocating or breaking an agreement, all of which take longer than a month and often cost more than they save. So the variable items are the only fast lever, and within them you cut in order of distance from necessity. Data and airtime before food. Small comforts before transport — because transport carries people to work and school, and cutting it can cut income. Groceries get trimmed by buying differently before anyone eats less. The December provision can be eased temporarily, but know what that is: borrowing from a month that will still arrive. In our example, R80 off data, R100 off groceries and R50 off the December provision recovers R230 and turns a R129 deficit into a R101 surplus without one missed contractual payment. And close your answer by looking at the other side of the page: the household should also try to raise income, because more income is the only fix that does not shrink the family's life.
