**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! First one, and it comes up every year: why can't I just take 15 percent off a VAT-inclusive price to get the price before VAT? Adding 15 percent is how the VAT got there, so surely taking 15 percent off removes it.

**Tutor:** Because adding VAT was never an addition of a fixed amount — it was a MULTIPLICATION by 1,15, and a multiplication is undone by division, not by subtraction. Follow the sizes and the error becomes visible. Take the standard-rated part of the basket, R172,50 including VAT. The shop's own price was R150,00, and SARS took 15 percent of that R150,00, which is R22,50. Now try the wrong method: 15 percent of R172,50 is R25,88, and taking that off leaves R146,62 — you have removed more tax than was ever charged, because you took the percentage of the bigger number. The right method divides: R172,50 ÷ 1,15 gives exactly R150,00, and the VAT is the difference, R22,50. Test it in reverse to be sure — R150,00 × 1,15 is R172,50, back where you started. So the rule to carry into the exam is: multiply by 1,15 to put VAT on, divide by 1,15 to take VAT off. And there is a bonus shortcut for finding the VAT directly inside an inclusive price: divide the inclusive amount by 23 and multiply by 3, because 15 over 115 simplifies to 3 over 23. R172,50 ÷ 23 is R7,50, times 3 is R22,50. Same answer, and a neat check on your first one.

**Thandi:** Next: our whole basket came to R374,50 and the VAT was only R22,50. That is barely six percent. Did the shop undercharge the tax, or did we make a mistake?

**Tutor:** Neither — you have just met zero-rating, and you have measured it correctly. South Africa charges VAT at 15 percent on most goods, but a specific list of basic foodstuffs and fuels is zero-rated, meaning the rate applied to them is zero percent. Brown bread, maize meal, rice, samp, dried beans and lentils, milk, eggs, fresh fruit and vegetables, vegetable oil, tinned pilchards and illuminating paraffin all sit on that list, and the reason is social: those items make up a far larger share of a poor household's spending than of a rich one's, so taxing them would hit hardest where it hurts most. In your basket, R202,00 was zero-rated and only R172,50 was standard-rated. The tax you paid, R22,50, is fifteen percent of the taxed portion — but it is only six percent of the whole basket, which is exactly the policy working as designed. The examinable habit follows directly: before you calculate VAT on any till slip, sort the items into taxed and zero-rated, add the taxed ones separately, and only then divide by 1,15. Learners who add every line first and then take 15 percent will overstate the VAT badly — in this case by more than double.

**Thandi:** This one caused an argument. If a shop takes 20 percent off and then another 10 percent off, why does the order not matter, and why is the answer still not 30 percent off?

**Tutor:** Two good questions, and they have opposite answers. Take the second one first. The discounts do not add because each one is calculated on what the previous one left. Twenty percent off R640,00 leaves R512,00. The ten percent then comes off R512,00, not off R640,00, so it removes R51,20 rather than R64,00 — and the shopper pays R460,80. Multiply the two multipliers, 0,80 × 0,90, and you get 0,72: seventy-two percent of the marked price, which is 28 percent off, not 30. The missing R12,80 is the difference between ten percent of R640 and ten percent of R512. Now for the order. Multiplication does not care about sequence: 0,90 × 0,80 is also 0,72, so ten percent first and twenty percent second lands on exactly the same R460,80. That is worth knowing, because examiners sometimes swap the order to see whether you are following a memorised recipe or actually understanding the multipliers. What DOES change the answer is mixing a discount with something calculated on a different base — a delivery fee added before or after a discount, for instance, gives genuinely different totals depending on order. So read the wording of the question with care: the words tell you what each percentage is being taken of, and that is the only thing that matters.

**Thandi:** Last one, and it is about the fridge. If waiting six months earns R195 of interest, why do you say waiting is a loss? The family ends up with more money than they started with.

**Tutor:** They end up with more money and a worse outcome, and separating those two things is the point of the whole session. Compare like with like. Buying during the sale costs R6 120,00 — that is R7 200 × 0,85 — and leaves R380,00 in the account, plus a fridge that keeps food from spoiling for the next six months. Waiting grows the savings by 6 500 × 0,06 × 0,5, which is R195,00, to R6 695,00 — but the sale has ended and the fridge is back at its marked price of R7 200,00, which is R505,00 more than they now have. So the R195 is real, and it is simply far too small to matter next to the R1 080,00 discount they let go: netting the two, waiting is R885,00 worse off. The general lesson is one that runs through the whole of Finance in this subject: an interest rate of 6 percent per year is a slow, steady, straight-line gain, while a 15 percent discount is an immediate one-off gain on a much bigger base. Never compare percentages to percentages — convert both to rand amounts on the actual sums of money involved, then compare the rands. And write the recommendation as a sentence, because a marker cannot award the reasoning mark for a number sitting alone on the page.
