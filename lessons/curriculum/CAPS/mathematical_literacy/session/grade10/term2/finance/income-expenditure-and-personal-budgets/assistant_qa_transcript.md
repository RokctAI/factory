**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! First: our group put the pension contribution in the expenses column, because it IS money the family doesn't get to spend. The teacher marked it wrong. Why?

**Tutor:** Because it has already been counted, and counting it twice makes the household look poorer than it is by exactly that amount. Follow the money once. The employer calculates R9 500 as gross, then removes R760 for tax, R95 for the unemployment fund and R475 for the pension — R1 330 in total — and pays those over on the employee's behalf. What is deposited into the bank account is R8 170. That deposited figure is what enters the budget as income, and the R1 330 is already reflected in it, by being absent. If you now also write R475 in the expenses column, you have subtracted the pension from money that never contained the pension. Your bottom line drops by R475 for no real-world reason. A useful picture: deductions happen upstream of your bowl, and expenses happen downstream. You budget with what reaches the bowl. The one place deductions legitimately reappear is a question that asks about total cost to the employee or the value of the whole package — but never inside a household budget that starts from take-home pay.

**Thandi:** Next: someone said the family's budget is fine because they have R681 left over. But then their electricity went up and it all fell apart. Was the budget wrong?

**Tutor:** The budget was correct and thin, and those are different things. R681 out of R10 200 is a margin of about six and a half percent — the household is spending roughly ninety-three cents of every rand that arrives. That is a plan with no shock absorber. A single winter, where prepaid electricity climbs a few hundred rand, or one month where the sewing work does not come, wipes it out. So a good answer does not stop at "surplus of R681". It says the surplus exists but is small relative to income, names the specific risks visible in the numbers — a variable income stream of R1 130 that is not guaranteed, and electricity that rises in winter — and suggests what to do: build the surplus into an emergency provision rather than absorbing it into spending, and provision for winter electricity in the same way the family already provisions for December. That is what examiners mean when they ask you to "comment on the budget". The arithmetic is the easy half; the judgement about how much room the household actually has is the half that earns the marks.

**Thandi:** This one confused a lot of us: for break-even we got 133,33 vetkoek and rounded to 133, because that's what rounding rules say. Why is that wrong when it followed the rule?

**Tutor:** Because rounding in Mathematical Literacy follows the context, not the digit. The ordinary rule — look at the next digit, round down below five — applies when you are reporting a measurement. Break-even is not a measurement; it is a threshold you must reach. Test both sides and it settles itself. At 133 vetkoek, income is 133 times R8,00, which is R1 064,00, while total cost is the R600 fixed plus 133 times R3,50, which is R465,50, giving R1 065,50 — she is still R1,50 down. At 134, income is R1 072,00 against a total cost of R1 069,00, so she is R3,00 up. Only 134 satisfies "income at least covers costs", so 134 is the answer. The same context logic runs through the whole subject in both directions: round UP for anything you must have enough of — tins of paint, taxis for a school trip, break-even sales — and round DOWN for how many whole things fit or how long a supply lasts. When a rounding question appears, say which direction you chose and why. That sentence is often worth a mark on its own.

**Thandi:** Last one, and it is the practical one: if the money runs short, what actually gets cut first? Everyone in our group had a different order and we couldn't agree.

**Tutor:** There is a defensible order, and being able to justify it is exactly what the question is testing. First, understand what you can move. Fixed costs — rent, school fees, the funeral policy, the phone contract — are contractual, so within this month they cannot be reduced by deciding to use less; changing them means renegotiating, moving, or breaking an agreement, all of which take longer than a month and often cost more than they save. That leaves the variable items as the only fast lever, and inside them you cut in order of how far each rand is from a necessity. Airtime and data before food. Small comforts before transport, because transport is what gets people to work and school and cutting it can cost income. Groceries can be trimmed by buying differently rather than eating less. Provisions for December can be reduced temporarily, but understand what you are doing — you are borrowing from a future month that will still arrive. In our example, R120 off airtime, R200 off groceries and R150 off the December provision recovers R470 and turns a R449 deficit into a R21 surplus without a single missed contractual payment. And say the last part out loud in your answer: the household should also look at the income side, because raising income is the only fix that does not shrink the household's life.
