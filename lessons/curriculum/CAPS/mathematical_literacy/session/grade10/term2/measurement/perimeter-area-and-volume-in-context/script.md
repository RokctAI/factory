# Part 1 — Expert

A household in Seshego is doing three jobs this month: fencing a vegetable garden against the goats, tiling and painting a back room, and working out how long the new water tank will last when the taps run dry. Every one of those jobs is a measurement question with money attached — and every one of them is lost at the same two places: the unit conversion at the start, and the rounding decision at the end. This session builds perimeter, area and volume in the contexts CAPS uses them, and installs the habits that hold the marks together.

## Subtopic: Convert First — the Step That Decides the Mark

Measurement questions arrive with mixed units on purpose. A plan gives millimetres, a price list gives square metres, a tank is described in metres and the household thinks in litres. Convert everything to ONE unit before a single formula is touched.

The length conversions you already know: 1 km is 1 000 m, 1 m is 100 cm, 1 cm is 10 mm. So 3,5 m is 350 cm, and 2 400 mm is 2,4 m. Multiply when moving to a smaller unit; divide when moving to a bigger one.

Area conversions are where candidates fall, because the factor is squared. A square metre is a square 100 cm by 100 cm, so 1 m² is 10 000 cm² — not 100. A hectare is 10 000 m². Volume factors are cubed: 1 m³ is 1 000 000 cm³.

Then the conversion this topic lives on: 1 m³ holds exactly 1 000 litres, and 1 cm³ holds exactly 1 millilitre. That single line turns every tank, drum and reservoir question into an answer a household can use, because nobody buys water by the cubic metre in their head.

Two habits protect the marks. Write the unit on every line of working, not only on the answer — a number without a unit earns nothing. And do not round on the way: carry the full value through and round once, at the end, to whatever the context demands — money to the cent, paint to whole tins, days to whole days.

Take on this section's questions now — convert before you calculate, and remember that the area factor is squared and the volume factor cubed.

## Subtopic: Fencing the Garden — Perimeter as a Shopping List

PERIMETER is the distance all the way around a shape, measured in units of length. For a rectangle, perimeter = 2 × (length + breadth).

The vegetable garden is 12 m long and 8 m wide. Substitute — and write the substitution line, because it carries a mark: perimeter = 2 × (12 + 8) = 2 × 20 = 40 m. The single most common error in the topic is stopping at 12 + 8 = 20 and buying half a fence.

Now turn 40 metres into a shopping list, because that is what the question actually wants. A 2 m gate takes up part of the boundary, so the fencing needed is 40 − 2 = 38 m. Wire mesh is sold in 30 m rolls at R899,00 a roll. Divide: 38 ÷ 30 = 1,27 rolls. You cannot buy 0,27 of a roll, so round UP: 2 rolls, costing 2 × R899,00 = R1 798,00, with 22 m of mesh left over — a real waste worth mentioning in an answer about cost-effectiveness.

Poles stand every 2 m around the full 40 m boundary. Because the boundary closes on itself, the number of poles equals the number of gaps: 40 ÷ 2 = 20 poles at R65,00 each, which is R1 300,00. Total materials: R1 798,00 + R1 300,00 = R3 098,00.

Two examinable subtleties. Rounding goes UP because materials must cover the job — half a roll short is a hole in the fence. And a closed boundary needs as many poles as gaps, while a straight run needs one more; read whether the shape closes before you count.

This section's questions are in front of you now: double the sum of length and breadth, subtract openings, then round the material upward and say what it costs.

## Subtopic: Tiles and Paint — Area, Coverage and Waste

AREA is the surface a shape covers, measured in square units. For a rectangle, area = length × breadth. For a triangle, area = ½ × base × perpendicular height. For a circle, area = π × radius², with π taken as 3,142 unless the paper says otherwise.

The back room floor is 4,2 m by 3,5 m. Area = 4,2 × 3,5 = 14,7 m². Tiles come in boxes covering 1,5 m² at R249,90 a box. Before dividing, add a WASTE ALLOWANCE, because tiles are cut at the edges and some break: the standard instruction is 10 percent. So 14,7 × 1,10 = 16,17 m². Boxes needed: 16,17 ÷ 1,5 = 10,78 → 11 boxes. Cost: 11 × R249,90 = R2 748,90.

Now the walls, which need a different area entirely. Wall area is the perimeter of the room multiplied by the wall height: perimeter = 2 × (4,2 + 3,5) = 15,4 m, and the walls are 2,7 m high, so 15,4 × 2,7 = 41,58 m².

Openings do not get painted. The door is 0,9 m by 2,1 m, which is 1,89 m². The window is 1,5 m by 1,2 m, which is 1,8 m². Paintable area: 41,58 − 1,89 − 1,8 = 37,89 m².

The paint tin says it covers 8 m² per litre, and the job needs two coats, so the area to cover is 37,89 × 2 = 75,78 m². Litres required: 75,78 ÷ 8 = 9,47 ℓ. Paint is sold in 5 ℓ tins at R429,99, so 9,47 ℓ means two tins — 10 ℓ — costing R859,98, with about half a litre spare for touch-ups.

Notice the discipline in both calculations: find the area, adjust it for waste or openings, divide by the coverage rate, then round up to whole units of what the shop sells. Coverage rates are given in the question; the marks are for using them.

Work through this section's questions now, and show the subtraction of every door and window — markers award that line separately.

## Subtopic: The Water Tank — Volume, Litres and How Long It Lasts

VOLUME is the space inside a solid, measured in cubic units. For a rectangular prism, volume = length × breadth × height. For a cylinder, volume = π × radius² × height — the circle's area, pushed upward through the height.

The tank is a cylinder 1,4 m across and 1,6 m tall. Diameter is 1,4 m, so the radius is half of that: 0,7 m. Halving the diameter is the step most often forgotten, and it makes the answer four times too large when skipped.

Substitute in full: volume = 3,142 × 0,7² × 1,6. Work in order — the square first: 0,7² = 0,49. Then 3,142 × 0,49 = 1,53958. Then × 1,6 = 2,463 m³.

Convert to litres, because a household plans in litres: 2,463 m³ × 1 000 = 2 463 litres.

Now the decision the family actually faces. Five people use about 90 ℓ each per day for drinking, cooking, washing and the toilet: 5 × 90 = 450 ℓ a day. Days of supply: 2 463 ÷ 450 = 5,47 days.

Here the rounding reverses, and this is the examinable judgement. Materials round UP; supplies and complete units round DOWN. The family gets 5 full days, because the sixth day runs dry before it ends. Answer in a sentence: "A full 2 463 ℓ tank supplies a household of five using 450 ℓ a day for 5 complete days."

One more conversion this context rewards. Rain harvested from a roof is a volume too. A roof of 60 m² catching 12 mm of rain collects 60 × 0,012 = 0,72 m³, because 12 mm is 0,012 m — that is 720 litres from one modest shower, nearly a third of the tank. Convert the millimetres to metres before multiplying, or the answer arrives a thousand times too big.

The last questions of this part are with you now — halve the diameter, substitute the full formula, convert cubic metres to litres, and decide whether the context wants you to round up or down.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same three jobs from the yard: a fence to walk around, a floor and walls to cover, and a tank to fill — with a tape measure, a price list and no formula sheet in sight.

## Subtopic: Walking Around It, Covering It, Filling It

Three questions, three completely different kinds of answer, and if you can tell them apart you are most of the way there.

"How far around?" is perimeter. Walk the boundary of the garden with the wire in your hand: 12 m up, 8 m across, 12 m back, 8 m across. That is 40 m of walking and 40 m of wire — each side walked once, long, short, long, short, which is why the quick way is to add one long and one short and double it. The number-one mistake in the country is answering 20, and buying exactly half the fence.

"How much to cover?" is area, and it is measured in squares. Picture the floor of the back room tiled with metre-square tiles: 4,2 tiles along one wall, 3,5 along the other, so 4,2 × 3,5 = 14,7 squares. Length times breadth, and the answer wears a little 2 on top because you are counting squares.

"How much to fill?" is volume — squares stacked into a box, so the answer wears a little 3. And the friendly bit for South Africans: one cubic metre is exactly 1 000 litres. Learn that one line and every tank question becomes a water question.

One warning before we go on. Because area counts squares, a square metre is not 100 square centimetres — it is a 100 by 100 block of them, so 10 000. Same trap in volume: a cubic metre is a million cubic centimetres. When the shape gets more dimensions, so does the conversion.

Some questions on telling the three apart are with you right now — for each one, decide first whether it is a walk around, a cover, or a fill.

## Subtopic: Buying Enough Paint Without Buying Too Much

The fence first, because it is a shopping list, not a maths problem. You need 40 m of boundary. A 2 m gate takes its own space, so the wire has to cover 38 m. The shop sells 30 m rolls at R899. One roll is not enough. So you buy two — R1 798 — and you take 22 m home unused. That is real money, and a good answer says so.

Poles every 2 m around a 40 m boundary: 40 ÷ 2 is 20. And because the fence goes all the way round and meets itself, 20 gaps need exactly 20 poles. At R65 each that is R1 300, and the whole job costs R3 098.

Now the room. The floor is 14,7 m². Tiles come 1,5 m² to a box. But tiles get cut at the edges and some crack, so add ten percent before you divide: 14,7 × 1,10 is 16,17 m². Divide by 1,5 and you get 10,78 boxes. Eleven boxes, at R249,90 each — R2 748,90.

The walls are a different sum, and this catches people. Do not use the floor area. Walk the room with your hand on the wall — that is the perimeter, 15,4 m — and the walls are 2,7 m high, so the wall surface is 15,4 × 2,7 = 41,58 m². Then take out what you do not paint: the door, 0,9 by 2,1, which is 1,89 m², and the window, 1,5 by 1,2, which is 1,8 m². That leaves 37,89 m² of actual wall.

Two coats means painting it twice: 75,78 m². The tin says 8 m² a litre, so 75,78 ÷ 8 is 9,47 litres. Five-litre tins at R429,99 — two tins, R859,98, with a bit left for touch-ups. And here is the rule that never changes when you are buying materials: whatever the decimal, go UP. You cannot buy 0,47 of a tin, and coming home half a tin short means a second trip and a wall that does not match.

Your questions for this section are ready now. Take out the doors and windows before you divide, and round the shopping up every time.

## Subtopic: How Many Days Does the Tank Give You

The tank is a drum: a circle pushed upward. So work out the circle first, then push it up.

It is 1,4 m across. That across-measurement is the diameter, and the formula wants the radius — half of it — which is 0,7 m. Halve it. Everyone forgets, and forgetting makes the answer four times too big, which is the kind of mistake that gives a family a tank they do not have.

Circle area: 3,142 × 0,7 × 0,7. Square first, so 0,49, times 3,142 is about 1,54 m². Now push it up through the 1,6 m height: 1,54 × 1,6 is 2,463 m³. And one cubic metre is a thousand litres, so the tank holds about 2 463 litres.

Now the question the family really asked. Five people, about 90 litres each a day — for drinking, cooking, washing and flushing — is 450 litres a day. How many days? 2 463 ÷ 450 is 5,47.

And here is the twist worth remembering, because it is the opposite of the paint. You do not round 5,47 up to 6. The sixth day runs dry halfway through, so the honest answer is 5 full days. Materials round up, because you must have enough. Days and whole items round down, because you cannot have part of one. Ask yourself which kind of rounding the situation wants — the marker is watching for exactly that judgement.

One more, because it is beautiful. A roof of 60 m² in a shower that drops 12 mm of rain collects 60 × 0,012 — and 12 mm is 0,012 m, so that is 0,72 m³, which is 720 litres. Nearly a third of the tank, from one ordinary Highveld afternoon.

The final questions of the lesson are with you now. Halve the diameter, keep the units on every line, turn cubic metres into litres, and finish with a sentence a real family could act on — how many days, how many tins, how many rands.
