**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! First one, and it caught nearly everybody: why is one square metre ten thousand square centimetres and not one hundred? A hundred centimetres make a metre, so a hundred feels right.

**Tutor:** Your instinct is right about length and wrong about area, and the difference is exactly what the little raised two is telling you. Build the square in your head. A square metre is a square whose sides are each one metre — which is one hundred centimetres — so it is a square one hundred centimetres by one hundred centimetres. To count how many one-centimetre squares fit inside it, multiply: one hundred rows of one hundred squares each, which is ten thousand. The conversion factor gets used twice because there are two dimensions, so the length factor of one hundred becomes one hundred squared. The same logic runs one step further for volume: a cubic metre is one hundred by one hundred by one hundred centimetre-cubes, which is one million cubic centimetres. So the rule is simple to state and easy to check: for lengths use the factor once, for areas square it, for volumes cube it. And when a question mixes units — a plan in millimetres and a tile price per square metre — convert the lengths to metres FIRST and then find the area, because converting lengths is the direction people get right.

**Thandi:** Next: how do I know the difference between the floor area and the wall area? In the tiling and painting question they were completely different numbers and I used the floor for both.

**Tutor:** That is the most expensive confusion in the topic, and there is a physical way to keep them apart. For the floor, imagine lying flat and looking down: you are covering one rectangle, so it is length times breadth — 4,2 times 3,5, which is 14,7 square metres. For the walls, stand up and put your hand on the wall and walk all the way round the room without lifting it. The distance your feet travel is the PERIMETER, 2 times 4,2 plus 3,5, which is 15,4 metres. Now your hand also has to go up and down as high as the wall, 2,7 metres. Unroll all four walls and lay them flat and you get one long strip 15,4 metres long and 2,7 metres high, which is 41,58 square metres. Perimeter times height — that is wall area, and it has nothing to do with the floor. Then subtract what you do not paint: the door, 0,9 by 2,1, which is 1,89 square metres, and the window, 1,5 by 1,2, which is 1,8, leaving 37,89 square metres. Markers award a separate mark for that subtraction, so always write it as its own line.

**Thandi:** This one started an argument in class. Twenty poles for a forty metre fence with a pole every two metres — someone said it should be twenty-one, because of the fence-post problem. Who is right?

**Tutor:** Both of you, in different situations, and knowing which situation you are in is the examinable part. Think about a STRAIGHT run of fence, say twenty metres with a pole every two metres. Ten gaps, but you need a pole at the start and a pole at the end, so eleven poles — gaps plus one. That is the fence-post problem your classmate is remembering, and it is real. Now think about our garden. The fence goes all the way round and comes back to where it started, so the boundary CLOSES. The last pole and the first pole are the same pole, so there is no extra one to add: forty metres with a pole every two metres gives twenty gaps and exactly twenty poles. The test is simply whether the shape closes on itself. A rectangular garden, a circular kraal, a fenced sports field — closed, so poles equal gaps. One side of a plot, a driveway edge, a washing line between two walls — open, so poles equal gaps plus one. Sketch the shape mentally, ask whether the last point meets the first, and count accordingly.

**Thandi:** Last one: the rounding still worries me. In the same paper I might have to round up for tins and down for days. Is there a rule, or do I just have to feel it?

**Tutor:** There is a rule, and it is not about the decimal at all — it is about what the number is counting. Ask one question: must I have ENOUGH, or must it be COMPLETE? If the answer is a quantity of material you have to buy so the job can be finished, you must have enough, so you round UP no matter how small the decimal — 9,47 litres of paint means two five-litre tins, 10,78 boxes of tiles means eleven boxes, and 1,27 rolls of mesh means two rolls. If the answer counts whole things that only exist entire — days of water, complete loaves from a bag of flour, full bus loads of passengers — then a part does not count, and you round DOWN: 5,47 days of water is five days, because the sixth day runs dry before it ends. And a third case worth naming: money always rounds to the cent, two decimal places, at the very end and never in the middle of a calculation. So the habit is to finish the arithmetic, then stop and ask what the number is counting before you write the final line. That pause is worth a mark in almost every measurement question you will meet.
