# Part 1 — Expert

A school in Katlehong runs a fun day, and three questions arrive within an hour. A learner wants to know her chance of pulling a red sweet from the tuck-shop jar. A stall wants to know what prize it can afford to offer on a two-coin game without losing money. And a clinic nurse, surveying who came in by taxi, wants to know whether age and transport are connected. All three are probability questions, and CAPS answers them with the same small toolkit: a scale from 0 to 1, three ways of writing the same number, a table, and a tree. This session builds all four, and ends with the question that makes probability worth learning — is the game fair?

## Subtopic: The Scale from 0 to 1 and Three Ways to Say It

PROBABILITY is a measure of how likely an event is, and every probability in existence lies between 0 and 1 inclusive. Zero means IMPOSSIBLE — the event cannot happen. One means CERTAIN — it must happen. A half means an EVEN CHANCE. Anything below a half is UNLIKELY; anything above it is LIKELY. No probability is ever negative, and none is ever greater than 1 — an answer outside that range is a signal to check your working, not a result.

The vocabulary must be exact. An OUTCOME is a single possible result of one action: rolling a 4, or drawing a green sweet. An EVENT is the outcome or set of outcomes you are interested in: "rolling an even number" is an event containing three outcomes. The SAMPLE SPACE is the complete list of possible outcomes: for one die, the numbers 1 to 6.

The same probability can be written three ways, and questions ask for all three. Take the tuck-shop jar: 12 red sweets, 8 green and 5 yellow, which is 25 sweets in total. As a FRACTION, the probability of drawing red is 12 out of 25 — written 12/25. As a DECIMAL, divide: 12 ÷ 25 = 0,48. As a PERCENTAGE, multiply the decimal by 100: 48 percent. Same fact, three costumes, and the instruction word in the question tells you which one to wear.

Then COMPLEMENTARY events, which save time in almost every paper. The probability of NOT red is everything left over: 8 green plus 5 yellow is 13 out of 25, which is 0,52 or 52 percent. Notice that 0,48 and 0,52 add to exactly 1, and that is not a coincidence — an event and its complement together cover the whole sample space. So the probability of NOT an event equals 1 minus the probability of the event. When "at least one" or "not" appears in a question, reach for the complement.

Take on this section's questions now: place a probability on the 0-to-1 scale, convert between fraction, decimal and percentage, and use the complement rather than counting the long way.

## Subtopic: Theoretical Probability and Relative Frequency

CAPS asks for two different probabilities, and confusing them costs marks.

THEORETICAL PROBABILITY is worked out by reasoning, before anything happens. It is the number of favourable outcomes divided by the total number of possible outcomes, and it assumes every outcome is equally likely. For a fair die, the probability of an even number is 3 out of 6, which is 0,5 or 50 percent. For the sweet jar, the probability of yellow is 5 out of 25, which is 0,2 or 20 percent.

RELATIVE FREQUENCY — also called experimental probability — is worked out afterwards, from what actually happened. It is the number of times the event occurred divided by the number of trials. A learner standing at the Katlehong taxi rank counts 200 taxis and finds that 128 are heading for town: the relative frequency is 128 ÷ 200 = 0,64, or 64 percent. There is no theory here at all; the number comes from observation, and it is the only kind of probability available when the outcomes are not equally likely.

Now the relationship between them, which examiners ask about directly. Toss a fair coin 50 times and you may well get 28 heads — a relative frequency of 0,56, not the theoretical 0,5. That does not mean the coin is loaded. Toss it 5 000 times and the relative frequency will sit far closer to 0,5. The rule to state: as the number of trials increases, the relative frequency tends towards the theoretical probability. Small samples wander; large samples settle.

And the misconception that survives every warning: after three heads in a row, tails is NOT "due". The coin has no memory. Each toss is an INDEPENDENT event, and the probability of a head on the next toss is 0,5 exactly as it was on the first. What large samples promise is a stable long-run proportion, not a correction of past results.

This section's questions are in front of you now — say which of the two probabilities a question is asking for, and be ready to explain why a small sample can disagree with theory without anything being wrong.

## Subtopic: Two-Way Tables — Probability from Real Data

A TWO-WAY TABLE sorts one group of people or objects by two characteristics at once, and it is the neatest source of probability questions in the subject because the numbers are real.

The clinic surveyed 200 patients, recording age group and how they travelled. Of those under 30, 46 walked and 74 came by taxi, giving 120. Of those 30 and over, 34 walked and 46 came by taxi, giving 80. The column totals: 46 plus 34 is 80 who walked, and 74 plus 46 is 120 who came by taxi. Everything checks against the grand total of 200 — and checking the margins before you use a table is a habit worth building, because a table that does not add up will produce answers that cannot be right.

Now read probabilities off it. The probability that a randomly chosen patient came by taxi is 120 out of 200, which is 0,6 or 60 percent — take the row or column total and divide by the grand total. The probability that a patient is BOTH under 30 AND came by taxi is the single cell where those two meet: 74 out of 200, which is 0,37 or 37 percent.

Then the reading that carries the most marks, because it changes the denominator. "Of the patients under 30, what proportion came by taxi?" Here the group has narrowed — we are no longer choosing from 200 people but from the 120 who are under 30. The answer is 74 out of 120, which is 0,617, or about 61,7 percent. Compare that with the older group: 46 out of 80, which is 0,575, or 57,5 percent. The younger patients are somewhat more likely to take a taxi, but the difference is modest — and saying so, with both figures, is what a full answer looks like.

The examinable discipline is simple to state and easy to forget: always ask what the whole group is before you write the denominator. "Out of all patients" means 200. "Out of the under-30s" means 120. The numerator is usually obvious; the denominator is where the marks live.

Work through this section's questions now, checking the table's totals first and naming the group each denominator comes from.

## Subtopic: Tree Diagrams and Fair Games

A TREE DIAGRAM lays out a two-stage or three-stage activity, with a branch for every outcome at each stage. It matters because it makes the complete sample space visible, and the sample space is where most errors hide.

Take the stall's game: toss two coins. Stage one branches into heads and tails. From each of those, stage two branches into heads and tails again. Follow every path to its end and there are four equally likely outcomes: heads-heads, heads-tails, tails-heads and tails-tails. Note carefully that heads-tails and tails-heads are DIFFERENT paths, even though both give "one of each" — treating them as one outcome is the classic error, and it turns a correct probability of 0,5 for "one of each" into a wrong 0,333.

So the probability of two heads is 1 out of 4, which is 0,25 or 25 percent. And the branch rule that makes trees powerful: multiply ALONG the branches. The probability of head then head is 0,5 × 0,5 = 0,25 — the same answer, obtained without listing anything, which is what you need when the stages multiply. Draw a second sweet from the jar after replacing the first, and the probability of red then red is 0,48 × 0,48 = 0,2304, about 23 percent.

Now the fairness question, which is what the topic is for. The stall charges R5,00 to play and pays R15,00 for two heads. Reason over 100 plays. Money in: 100 × R5,00 = R500,00. Expected wins: 25 percent of 100 plays is 25 wins, so money out is 25 × R15,00 = R375,00. The stall keeps R125,00 per 100 plays. The game is UNFAIR to the player — not because anything is hidden, but because the prize is too small for the odds.

Make it fair. A FAIR GAME is one in which, in the long run, neither side expects to gain: money in must equal money out. The 25 expected winners must share the R500,00, so the prize must be 500 ÷ 25 = R20,00. At a R20,00 prize the game breaks even; below that the stall profits; above it the stall loses. That single calculation explains every raffle, lottery and casino game a learner will meet, and it answers the question honestly: games of chance are priced so the house keeps a margin.

The last questions of this part are with you now — build the full sample space before calculating, multiply along the branches, and judge fairness by comparing money in with money out over many plays.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same ideas from the fun day itself, with a jar of sweets on one table, two coins on another, and a clipboard at the clinic door.

## Subtopic: From Never to Always — Where Your Words Sit

Every probability in the world lives on one short line, from 0 to 1. Nothing sits outside it.

At 0 sits IMPOSSIBLE — snow in Durban in January, a 7 on an ordinary die. At 1 sits CERTAIN — the sun rising, a taxi eventually filling up. Halfway sits the EVEN CHANCE — a tossed coin. Below halfway is unlikely; above it is likely. If your answer ever comes out negative, or bigger than 1, you have not discovered something interesting — you have made a mistake, and that is genuinely useful to know, because it is a free check on every answer you write.

Now the jar on the tuck-shop table: 12 red sweets, 8 green, 5 yellow. That is 25 sweets. Reach in without looking. What is the chance of red? Twelve of the 25 are red, so 12 out of 25. That is the fraction. Divide 12 by 25 and you get 0,48 — that is the decimal. Multiply by 100 and you get 48 percent. Three ways of saying one thing, like calling the same person by their name, their nickname and their surname.

And a shortcut that saves you every time. What is the chance of NOT red? You could count the greens and yellows — 13 out of 25 — or you could notice that red and not-red must together cover everything, so they add to 1. So not-red is 1 minus 0,48, which is 0,52. Whenever a question says "not" or "at least one", ask what is left over instead of counting the long way.

Some questions on the scale are with you right now — put each event in its place from never to always, and give the answer in the form the question asks for.

## Subtopic: Counting Instead of Guessing

There are two honest ways to answer "how likely is it?", and they come from different places.

The first is thinking. Before you touch anything, count what could happen and count what you want: 5 yellow sweets out of 25 means a 1 in 5 chance, 0,2, twenty percent. No experiment needed. That is theory, and it only works when every outcome is equally likely — every sweet the same size, the coin not bent.

The second is counting what actually happened. A learner stands at the taxi rank and tallies 200 taxis; 128 go to town. So 128 out of 200 — 0,64, sixty-four percent. Nothing was assumed here; it was observed. And that is the only method available when the outcomes are not equally likely, because nobody can reason their way to how many taxis go where.

Now the two together, and here is what confuses people. Toss a coin 50 times and get 28 heads — that is 56 percent, not 50. Is the coin cheating? No. Small numbers of tries wander around. Toss it 5 000 times and you will land very near half. The more you try, the closer the counting gets to the thinking.

But — and this is the one to burn in — a coin has no memory. Three heads in a row does not make tails "due". The coin does not know what it did last time. The next toss is 50-50, exactly like the first one. People lose real money on this belief every week.

Then the clipboard at the clinic. Of 200 patients, 120 came by taxi, so the chance a random patient took a taxi is 120 out of 200 — 60 percent. But look closer: of the 120 patients under 30, 74 came by taxi, which is 74 out of 120 — about 62 percent — while of the 80 older patients, 46 took a taxi, which is 46 out of 80, about 57 percent. The trick is noticing what changed: when the question narrows the group, the bottom number changes with it. Always ask "out of whom?" before you write the fraction.

Your questions for this section are ready now. For each one, say out loud which group the answer is being taken out of before you write anything down.

## Subtopic: Is the Game Fair?

Two coins, R5 to play, R15 if you get two heads. Sounds generous. Let us find out.

First, list what can happen — and be careful, because this is where everyone slips. Coin one can be H or T. For each of those, coin two can be H or T. So: HH, HT, TH, TT. Four things, all equally likely. And yes, HT and TH really are different — the first coin and the second coin are different coins, even if the result looks the same in your hand. Draw it as a little tree, two branches splitting into two, and you cannot lose track.

So two heads is 1 out of 4 — a 25 percent chance. There is also a quick way that skips the drawing: to get heads AND then heads, multiply the chances along the path — a half times a half is a quarter. Multiply along the branches. That trick keeps working when the stages pile up.

Now the money, which is the real question. Imagine 100 people play. The stall collects 100 × R5, which is R500. About 25 of them win, and each takes R15, so the stall pays out 25 × R15 — R375. The stall walks away with R125 out of every R500. The game is not rigged, nothing is hidden — the prize is simply too small for the odds.

So what would a FAIR game look like? Fair means neither side expects to come out ahead in the long run: the money in must equal the money out. R500 shared among the 25 expected winners is 500 ÷ 25 — R20. At a R20 prize, the stall breaks even. Anything less and the stall profits; anything more and it loses.

That one sum explains raffles, lotteries and every game of chance you will ever be offered. Ask what it costs, ask what fraction win, ask what the prize is — then compare money in with money out. Once you can do that, nobody can sell you a bad bet, and that is a life skill hiding inside an exam topic.

The final questions of the lesson are with you now. List the whole sample space before you calculate, multiply along the branches, and finish with a sentence saying whether the game is fair and what prize would make it so.
