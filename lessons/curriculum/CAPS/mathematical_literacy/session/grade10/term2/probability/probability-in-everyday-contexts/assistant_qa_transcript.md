**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! First, and it started a real argument: why are heads-tails and tails-heads counted as two different outcomes? In your hand it looks exactly the same — one head and one tail.

**Tutor:** Because the coins are two separate objects, and probability counts ways things can happen, not what the result looks like afterwards. Make the coins distinguishable and the argument dissolves. Give one a red dot and leave the other plain. Now toss them. There is a genuine, separate way for the world to turn out where the red coin is heads and the plain coin is tails, and another genuine way where the red is tails and the plain is heads. Those are two different arrangements of reality, and painting a dot on a coin cannot change how likely anything is. So the four outcomes are red-head-plain-head, red-head-plain-tail, red-tail-plain-head and red-tail-plain-tail, all equally likely. Now count. Two heads is one way out of four, which is 0,25. One of each is TWO ways out of four, which is 0,5 — twice as likely as two heads. Learners who merge the mixed results get three outcomes and claim a third for each, which makes two heads look more likely than it is and would have cost the stall real money. The safe habit is to build a tree: branch on the first coin, then branch each of those on the second, and count the paths at the ends. Trees never merge outcomes by accident, which is exactly why the syllabus insists on them.

**Thandi:** Next: the coin has come up heads five times in a row. Everyone at the table says tails is due. Is there really no sense in which that is true?

**Tutor:** None at all for the next toss, and the reason is worth stating precisely, because this belief costs people money every week. The coin is an object with two sides. It has no memory, no record and no sense of obligation. The probability of heads on the next toss is 0,5, exactly as it was on the first toss and on every toss since. Statisticians call this INDEPENDENCE: the outcome of one trial has no influence on the next. Now here is the subtle part, because there IS a true idea hiding underneath the false one. Over a very large number of tosses, the proportion of heads does settle near 0,5 — that is the law you met as relative frequency approaching theoretical probability. But it settles by DILUTION, not by correction. If you are five heads ahead after five tosses and you then toss ten thousand more times, those five extra heads become a rounding error against ten thousand — the proportion drifts back towards a half without any single toss ever being nudged. Nothing pushes tails to catch up. So the correct exam sentence is: each toss is independent, so the probability of a head remains 0,5 regardless of previous results. Write that and you have the mark; write "tails is due" and you have lost it.

**Thandi:** This one keeps catching me in tests. In the clinic table I get the top number right and the bottom number wrong. How do I know what to divide by?

**Tutor:** You have diagnosed yourself perfectly, and there is a one-question cure: before writing the fraction, ask "out of whom?" The wording of the question always answers it, and there are two patterns worth recognising. Pattern one asks about the whole survey — "what is the probability that a randomly chosen patient came by taxi?" Nothing narrows the group, so the denominator is the grand total, 200, and the answer is 120 out of 200, which is 0,6 or 60 percent. Pattern two narrows the group first — "of the patients under 30, what proportion came by taxi?" or "given that a patient is under 30". The phrase "of the" or "given that" is your signal that the group has shrunk, so the denominator becomes the row total for that group, 120, and the answer is 74 out of 120, which is about 61,7 percent. Same cell on top, different bottom, very different meaning. A third pattern uses a single cell over the grand total — "the probability that a patient is both under 30 AND came by taxi" — which is 74 out of 200, or 37 percent. Three questions, three answers, one cell. So underline the words that define the group before you touch the numbers, and check the table's margins add to the grand total first, because a table that does not balance will never give an answer that does.

**Thandi:** Last one: how can a game be unfair if nobody is cheating? The stall runs it honestly, the coins are ordinary coins, and everybody can see the rules.

**Tutor:** That is exactly the insight the topic is built to deliver, and you have already half stated it: unfairness in mathematics is about the PRICE, not about honesty. A game is FAIR when neither side expects to gain in the long run — money in equals money out. So do the arithmetic on the stall's game, which is completely open. Over 100 plays it takes in 100 times R5,00, which is R500,00. The probability of two heads is 0,25, so about 25 of those 100 plays win, and each winner takes R15,00, which is R375,00 paid out. The stall keeps R125,00 of every R500,00 — a quarter of everything staked — without a single dishonest act. The prize is simply too small for the odds. Now solve for fairness: the R500,00 must go entirely to the 25 expected winners, so the prize would need to be 500 divided by 25, which is R20,00. At R20,00 nobody wins in the long run. Anything below and the stall profits, anything above and it loses. That single method transfers everywhere — raffles, lotteries, competitions on a chip packet. Ask three things: what does it cost, what fraction win, and what is the prize. Then compare the money coming in with the money going out. Every commercial game of chance is deliberately priced on the stall's side of that comparison, and now you can measure by how much.
