**Thandi:** Time for the questions that come up a lot in analysis! First: percentage change — I always mix up what goes on the bottom of the fraction. Is there a way to make it stick?

**Tutor:** Anchor it to the word change: a change is measured from where you STARTED, so the starting value — the original, the earlier one — is the denominator, every time. June to July: usage went from 500 to 560, so the change of 60 sits over the original 500, giving 12 percent. Put the 560 underneath and you get 10,7 percent — a real number, but the answer to a different question, namely what fraction of July does the difference represent. The exam wants the change relative to where things began. Two habits lock it in. First, write the timeline before the fraction: from 500, to 560, change 60 — the word after from is your denominator. Second, sense-check with a famous case: if a price doubles from 100 to 200, the change should be 100 percent, and only dividing by the original 100 gives that; dividing by the new 200 gives 50 percent, which instantly feels wrong for a doubling. Run that doubling test in your head whenever you hesitate, and the denominator sorts itself out.

***

**Thandi:** Next: the question asked whether fixing the leak was worth the plumber's fee, and I calculated everything but still lost the last mark. What was missing?

**Tutor:** The verdict. A worth-it question is a decision question, and the mark scheme reserves its final point for the decision stated with its evidence. Your calculations carry the journey: the repair saves 9 kilolitres a month, which at R32 per kilolitre is R288; the plumber charged R650; two months of savings give R576, still short, and three months give R864, past the fee — so the repair pays for itself during the third month, and thereafter saves R3 456 a year. But the marker then wants the sentence that closes the loop: yes, the repair was worth it, because the fee is recovered within three months and every month after is pure saving. Notice the shape — decision, then because, then numbers. It is the same discipline as the account-comparison questions in Finance: arithmetic without a conclusion is a journey without an arrival. Whenever a question contains the words should, worth, better or advise, budget the last line of your answer for the verdict sentence before you even start calculating.

***

**Thandi:** Third: conclusions and predictions — the memo accepted my friend's prediction but called mine an overreach. Both used the same graph. What is the line between them?

**Tutor:** The line is whether the sentence claims more certainty than the data owns. A conclusion may speak firmly, but only about what the picture actually shows: July used 560 units, the most of any month — solid, because it is read straight off the graph. A prediction speaks about next year, which no graph can show, so it must wear a hedge: next July will LIKELY peak NEAR 560 units, IF the household's winter pattern continues. Your friend's sentence probably carried those seatbelts; an overreach sounds like next July will use 560 units — a fact-shaped claim about the unknown. There is a matching error on the conclusion side: smuggling in judgements the data never measured, like the family wastes electricity — the graph records usage, not waste, so that sentence cannot be supported no matter how reasonable it feels. The working rule: for conclusions, ask could a stranger verify this sentence using only the graph; for predictions, check that the sentence contains a softener and a condition. Likely, approximately, if the pattern holds — those small words are not weakness; they are precision about uncertainty, and the memo pays for them.

***

**Thandi:** Last one: the misleading-graph question. I can see the bars look wrong, but my explanations come out vague. What is the exact sentence the markers want?

**Tutor:** They want the mechanism, not just the accusation — name what the axis does and what the eye wrongly concludes. The template has three parts. Part one, the fault: the vertical axis starts at 250 instead of nought. Part two, the consequence: each bar therefore shows only the portion of its value above 250 — February keeps a stump of 50 while July keeps a tower of 310 — so the picture displays only the difference between the months, not the amounts themselves. Part three, the honest comparison: the true values are 300 and 560 units, so July is less than double February, not the six-fold explosion the bars suggest. Fault, consequence, truth — three sentences, usually three marks. And keep the accusation precise: the DATA is not false, the SCALE is misleading; markers distinguish those, and so do arguments in real life. If the question then asks how to fix the graph, the answer is one line: redraw it with the vertical axis starting at nought, so equal distances again mean equal amounts.
