# Part 1 — Expert

Drawing a graph earns marks; analysing one earns more, because analysis is where data handling meets real decisions. This lesson works through one household's municipal year in three pictures. A line graph of monthly electricity use: from a summer low of 300 units in February, climbing through 500 units in June to a winter peak of 560 in July, then falling back. A pair of bars comparing water use before and after fixing a leaking toilet: 28 kilolitres down to 19. And a pie chart splitting the household's R2 400 municipal bill into electricity, water, refuse and sewerage. From these three pictures we will read values, compute ranges and percentage changes, turn shares into rand, and finish with the analyst's final duty: honest conclusions, careful predictions, and a sharp eye for the axis trick that makes ordinary graphs tell lies.

## Subtopic: Reading Values and Ranges from a Graph

Analysis begins with disciplined reading — no interpretation yet, just extracting numbers accurately, because every later calculation inherits any reading error.

To read a value from the electricity line graph: find the month on the horizontal axis, rise vertically to the plotted point, then travel horizontally to the vertical axis and read the number against the scale. July: up to the highest point, across to 560 units. February: 300 units, the year's floor. If a point falls between gridlines, read proportionally: halfway between 400 and 450 is 425 — and exam memos allow small tolerances for between-line readings, but only small ones.

Maximum, minimum and range come next, and they are pure reading. The maximum is the highest point — July's 560. The minimum is the lowest — February's 300. The range is their difference: 560 minus 300 gives 260 units. Range measures how much the household's usage swings across the year — a big range here signals seasonal behaviour, winter heating on top of the everyday load.

Two reading disciplines protect everything downstream. First, always read the axis scale before reading any point: a gridline step might be 20 units, or 50 — assume and you inherit an error in every answer. Second, quote units with every value: 560 units, 28 kilolitres, R2 400. In data questions the unit is part of the answer, and a memo will dock the mark for 560 standing alone.

The questions for this section are with you now. Pure extraction: values, the peak, the floor, and the range between them.

## Subtopic: Trends, Comparisons and Percentage Change

Now the graph starts talking. A trend is the overall direction of the data, stated with evidence. The electricity line rises from summer into winter — 300 in February to 560 in July — then falls toward spring. The full sentence pattern for trend marks: usage rises through autumn into a winter peak, from 300 units in February to 560 in July, then declines. Direction plus numbers: that pairing is what examiners call describing, and it always outscores a bare word like increasing.

Comparisons quantify differences. June against July: 560 minus 500 gives 60 more units in July. And the sharper tool — percentage change — measures the jump relative to where it started: change over original, times 100. From June to July: 60 over 500 gives 0,12, times 100 — a 12 percent rise. The original — the earlier value — is always the denominator. Dividing by the new 560 gives about 10,7 percent, a different and wrong answer; the reference point for change is where you started, not where you landed.

The water bars make the same tool tell a better story. Before the plumber: 28 kilolitres a month. After: 19. The saving: 9 kilolitres, and as a percentage of the before value: 9 over 28 gives 0,3214 — about 32,1 percent less water. One repaired toilet cut usage by nearly a third, and the graph proves it.

Why percentages beat raw differences in comparisons: 9 kilolitres means little until you know it came off 28 — off a base of 200 it would be trivial. Percentage change carries its own context, which is exactly why exam questions ask for it.

Work through this section's questions: trends with evidence, differences, and percentage changes with the original safely in the denominator.

## Subtopic: From Graph to Rand — Calculating with What You Read

Municipal graphs matter because they price real money, and the exam always walks the bridge from picture to rand.

Electricity first. Prepaid electricity costs R2,50 per unit. July's 560 units cost 560 times 2,50 — take 560 times 2 as 1 120, plus 560 halves which is 280 — R1 400 for the peak month. February's 300 units: 300 times 2,50 gives R750. The seasonal swing in rand: 1 400 minus 750 — R650 between the cheapest and dearest month, and suddenly the line graph is a budgeting document.

The water repair, priced. Saving 9 kilolitres a month at R32 per kilolitre: 9 times 32 gives R288 saved every month. Across a year: 288 times 12 — R3 456. If the plumber charged R650 for the repair, the job pays for itself in the third month: two months save 576, not yet 650; three months save 864, well past it. This is the analysis exams love most — data, turned into money, turned into a decision that the numbers defend.

The pie chart completes the tour. The whole bill is R2 400. Electricity holds 50 percent of the circle: 0,50 times 2 400 gives R1 200. Water holds 25 percent: R600. Refuse 15 percent: 0,15 times 2 400 gives R360. Sewerage 10 percent: R240. Audit: 1 200 plus 600 plus 360 plus 240 returns exactly 2 400, and the shares total 100 percent. Every pie calculation is share times whole, and the audit line at the end catches both your slips and the question's deliberate misprints.

Notice the shape of all three calculations: read the number off the graph, multiply by a rate or a share, then let the answer say something — about a budget, a repair, a bill. Reading, arithmetic, meaning: the three-step rhythm of every analysis question.

This section's questions are in front of you — each one walks that bridge from picture to rand.

## Subtopic: Conclusions, Predictions and the Axis That Lies

The final skills separate analysis from arithmetic: saying what the data supports, no more, and catching graphs designed to mislead.

A conclusion must stand on the numbers. Supported: winter months use the most electricity — July's 560 units against February's 300. Unsupported: the household wastes electricity — nothing in the graph measures waste. The discipline: every conclusion sentence carries its evidence with it, and claims nothing the picture cannot show.

A prediction extends a pattern, and honest predictions come with seatbelts. Next July will likely peak again near 560 units, IF the household's winter habits stay the same. Exams reward the hedge — likely, approximately, if the pattern continues — because data supports probability, not certainty. A prediction stated as fact is an overreach; a refusal to predict wastes the pattern. The middle path: number, plus condition.

Now the lie. A newspaper shows the household's February-versus-July usage as bars — but its vertical axis starts at 250 instead of nought. February's bar shows 300 minus 250: 50 tall. July's shows 560 minus 250: 310 tall. The bars LOOK like a six-fold explosion; the truth — 560 against 300 — is less than double. Nothing on the graph is false; the axis simply amputated the part where both bars stood equal, leaving only their difference on display. This trick — the truncated axis — is the most common graphical deception in real media, and the exam's favourite spot-the-problem question. The tell: a number axis that starts anywhere but nought. The fix: read the actual values and recompute the comparison yourself — which you now do by reflex.

The analyst's checklist, four lines. Read accurately, units attached. Compute with the original as the base of every percentage change. Conclude only what the numbers show, evidence in the sentence. And trust no graph before glancing at where its axis starts.

The closing questions of this part are with you now — conclusions to sort into supported and unsupported, one prediction to hedge, and one lying axis to expose.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Same three pictures, same house — now read the way you would actually read them at the kitchen table, with the bill in one hand.

## Subtopic: The Graph Is a Story About Your House

A municipal graph is not homework — it is your own house talking. The electricity line drifts along around 300 units all summer, starts climbing in autumn, and spikes to 560 in July. You do not need a textbook to know why: winter. The heater, the geyser working harder on cold water, hot suppers, early dark evenings with every light on. The line is a diary of the family's cold months.

Reading it is a finger exercise. Want July? Put a finger on July at the bottom, slide up to the dot, then slide left to the numbers: 560. Want the swing across the year? Highest dot minus lowest dot: 560 minus 300 gives 260 units of difference between the family's coldest and warmest behaviour. That swing is the number to remember, because it is the part of the bill you can actually fight — nobody cancels winter, but blankets are cheaper than heaters.

The water bars tell a prouder story. Before: 28 kilolitres a month, with a toilet quietly leaking day and night. After the fix: 19. One repair, nine kilolitres a month, gone. The bars stand side by side like a before-and-after photo, and the shorter one is the plumber's signature.

So the first move of every analysis question is friendly: ask what story is this picture telling about the house? Cold months. A fixed leak. A bill split four ways. Name the story first, and the calculations that follow will all make sense as details OF that story.

The questions for this piece are with you now — read with your finger, and say each number's story out loud.

## Subtopic: Numbers First, Opinions Second

At the kitchen table, someone always has an opinion: we use TOO much electricity. The analyst's habit — and the exam's — is to make the numbers speak before anyone's feelings do.

Is July really so bad? Read, then multiply: 560 units at R2,50 each is R1 400. February was 300 units — R750. So winter costs this family about R650 a month more than summer. Now THAT is a fact worth an opinion: budget an extra R650 for each winter month, or find which winter habit to trim.

Was the plumber worth it? Numbers first: 9 kilolitres saved monthly, at R32 a kilolitre — R288 a month. The repair cost R650. By month three the savings pass the price — 864 against 650 — and every month after is pure profit: R3 456 a year. Verdict, delivered by arithmetic: the plumber was a bargain.

Where does the bill actually go? The pie says: half to electricity — that is R1 200 of the R2 400. A quarter to water: R600. Refuse R360, sewerage R240. When the family argues about saving money, the pie points at the big slice — a 10 percent cut of electricity saves R120 a month, while 10 percent off refuse saves R36. Aim at big slices; that is what shares are FOR.

Notice the pattern in all three: read the number, do one multiplication, and only then judge. Opinion built on arithmetic earns full marks — and wins kitchen-table arguments.

Your questions for this section are up. Read first, multiply second, judge last.

## Subtopic: When a Picture Exaggerates

One last skill, and it may be the most valuable thing in this whole topic: catching a graph that is technically true and completely misleading.

The trick looks innocent. A graph compares February's 300 units with July's 560 — but its number axis starts at 250 instead of nought. What is left of February above 250? A stump of 50. Of July? A tower of 310. The tower stands six times the stump, and your eye screams that winter usage EXPLODED. The real numbers say July is not even double February.

Where did the lie live? Not in the data — 300 and 560 are honest. The lie lives in the missing bottom of the picture: the part where both months stood equal, floor to 250, was cut off, so ONLY the difference got drawn. It is a passport photo cropped to the frown — nothing fake was added, but everything calm was removed.

Why does anyone draw graphs this way? Sometimes to sell a story: a newspaper wants a crisis, an advert wants its product to tower over rivals, a report wants a tiny improvement to look heroic. Sometimes just carelessness. Your defence never changes and takes five seconds: look where the number axis starts. If it is not nought, stop trusting your eyes, read the actual values off the axis, and redo the comparison in your head — 560 against 300, less than double, no explosion.

The exam versions ask: why does this graph create a misleading impression, and what does an accurate comparison show? Now you have both sentences ready: the axis starts at 250, so the bars display only the differences above that line, exaggerating the gap; the true values, 300 and 560, differ by less than a factor of two.

Read the last questions of the lesson now. Check every axis's starting point before believing any picture — the habit costs five seconds and it is the difference between reading the news and being read BY it.
