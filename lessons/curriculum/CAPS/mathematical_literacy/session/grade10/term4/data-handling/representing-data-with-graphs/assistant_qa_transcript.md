**Thandi:** Time for the questions that come up a lot with graphs! First: bar graph or histogram — I know one has gaps, but in the exam I freeze. Is there a test I can apply in five seconds?

**Tutor:** Ask one question of the horizontal axis: is it a number line, or a list of names? If the axis carries named groups — flavours, taxi routes, provinces, sports — nothing exists between the labels, so the bars stand apart with gaps: a bar graph. If the axis carries intervals of numbers — marks from nought to 49, ages, masses, waiting times — the intervals tile a continuous number line, 19 followed immediately by 20, so the bars must stand shoulder to shoulder: a histogram. The gaps and the touching are not style choices; each one is a factual claim. A gap says nothing can live here, which is true between cola and orange and false between 19 and 20 marks. That is also how you answer the two-mark explanation question — why do these bars touch — in one sentence: because the data is grouped along a continuous number line, and a gap would claim a stretch of values that no one could occupy. Five seconds: names apart, numbers together.

***

**Thandi:** Next: how do I choose a scale that will not cost me marks? I never know whether to count in fives, fifties or hundreds.

**Tutor:** Let the biggest value choose for you, with three constraints. Start at nought — a number axis that starts anywhere else distorts every comparison. Use equal steps, because the whole meaning of a graph is that equal distances stand for equal amounts. And pick the step so the biggest value fits in roughly ten to twelve jumps: for flavour counts peaking at 45, steps of 5 reach 50 comfortably; for takings peaking at R510, steps of 50 reach 600; steps of 5 there would demand over a hundred gridlines, and steps of 500 would flatten the whole week into one stripe. Then write the numbers on the axis before drawing any bar, and plot by counting steps, not by eyeballing. Two more habits close the common leaks: label the axis with its unit — takings in rand — because a bare axis costs a mark, and after drawing, read each bar back against the table. Most scale marks are lost not to bad choices but to unlabelled or uneven axes drawn in a hurry.

***

**Thandi:** Third: the pie chart says cola is 45 percent. The question asks how many drinks that is — but last week's paper asked for the angle instead. Which calculation is which?

**Tutor:** Both start from the same idea — the slice is a fraction of the whole — and they differ only in what the whole is. When the question asks for an amount, the whole is the total number of items: 45 percent of 100 drinks is 45 drinks, and if a bigger week sells 240 drinks in the same proportions, cola is 0,45 times 240, which is 108 drinks. When the question asks for the angle, the whole is the circle itself: 360 degrees, so cola's slice spans 0,45 times 360, which is 162 degrees. Same fraction, two different wholes — drinks for amounts, degrees for angles. And both versions carry a built-in audit that markers love to test: the percentages of all slices must total 100, and the angles must total 360. Our four flavours: 162 plus 108 plus 54 plus 36 is exactly 360. If a printed pie's numbers fail that audit, the question is probably asking you to find the error — and now you can, by adding the shares before doing anything else.

***

**Thandi:** Last one: interpretation questions say describe the trend and suggest a reason. My description was right but my reason got no marks. What counts as a good reason?

**Tutor:** A good reason is one the data itself makes plausible, tied to the exact feature you described. Structure the answer in three layers. Facts first, with numbers: takings fell to R150 on Wednesday, the week's lowest point, against a high of R510 on Friday. Trend second, with evidence: apart from Wednesday, the week rises steadily, Monday's 380 to Friday's 510. Reason third — and here is where marks are lost — the reason must connect the context to the dip: school closed early for sports, so the stall missed its lunch-rush customers. That earns marks because it explains lower sales on that specific day. Reasons that fail are either fantasies the graph cannot support or vague mumbles like something happened. You are not being asked to know the true cause — the word suggest invites any sensible possibility — but sensible means: if your reason were true, the graph would look exactly like it does. Test your reason against that sentence before writing it, and the interpretation marks follow.
