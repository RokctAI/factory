# Part 1 — Expert

Term 4 data handling asks you to build the pictures yourself: single bar graphs, histograms, line and broken-line graphs — and to read pie charts, which Grade 10 interprets but never draws. The data all comes from one week at Amara's tuckshop. Flavour counts of the 100 cooldrinks sold: cola 45, orange 30, grape 15, apple 10. The daily takings, Monday to Friday: R380, R420, R150, R460, R510 — with a story hiding in Wednesday. And the term's maths marks for her class of 30, grouped in tens. One dataset per graph type, because the deepest skill in this topic is matching the data's shape to the right picture — and then drawing that picture so carefully that it cannot be misread.

## Subtopic: Choosing the Right Graph for the Data

Data arrives in three shapes, and each shape has its own graph.

Shape one: categories with counts. The flavours — cola 45, orange 30, grape 15, apple 10 — are separate named groups with nothing in between: no flavour lives halfway between cola and orange. Categorical data takes a single bar graph: one bar per category, bars the same width, gaps between them, heights showing the counts.

Shape two: numerical data grouped into intervals. The 30 maths marks, sorted into nought to 9, 10 to 19, 20 to 29, 30 to 39, 40 to 49 — intervals on a continuous number line, touching each other with no space between where one ends and the next begins. Grouped numerical data takes a histogram: bars that TOUCH, because the intervals themselves touch.

Shape three: a quantity followed over time. The daily takings — Monday R380 through Friday R510 — are one thing measured again and again as time passes. Time data takes a line graph: points plotted above each day, joined in time order. When the values jump about rather than flowing smoothly, the joined zigzag is called a broken-line graph.

And the pie chart? It shows shares of a whole — how the 100 drinks split between flavours — as slices of a circle. Grade 10 reads pie charts and calculates from them, but never constructs them: interpretation only.

The choosing rule in one breath: named groups, bar graph. Grouped numbers, histogram. Change over time, line graph. Shares of a whole, pie chart — read, not drawn.

The questions for this section are with you now. Each gives a dataset and asks for the graph that fits its shape — the decision that comes before any drawing.

## Subtopic: Building a Bar Graph That Cannot Be Misread

A bar graph is a small machine with five parts, and exam markers check each one.

Part one: the title — what, where, when. Cooldrink sales by flavour at Amara's tuckshop, one week. A graph without a title is a picture of nothing.

Part two: the category axis — the horizontal one — with the four flavour names evenly spaced, each label under its own bar.

Part three: the number axis — vertical — and here lives the first big decision: the scale. The largest count is 45, so run the axis from nought to 50, marked in steps of 5. Two laws govern it: the scale must start at nought, and its steps must be EQUAL — nought, 5, 10, 15 and onward, never nought, 5, 20, 45. Unequal steps stretch some values and squash others, and the picture lies. Label the axis with what it measures: number of drinks sold.

Part four: the bars. Equal width, equal gaps, one per category. Cola rises to 45 — nine steps of 5. Orange to 30, grape to 15, apple to 10. The gaps matter: they announce that the categories are separate islands, which is exactly what makes this a bar graph and not a histogram.

Part five: the check. Read each bar back off your own axis and confirm it lands on the data. Drawing is transcription, and transcription gets audited.

Exam questions test this list two ways: draw the graph from the table — marks for title, axes, labels, even scale, correct heights — or find the error in a drawn graph: a missing label, a scale jumping unevenly, a bar of the wrong height. Know the five parts and both question types are yours.

Work through this section's questions before the histogram, where one of these five parts changes in a way that catches half the class.

## Subtopic: The Histogram — When the Bars Must Touch

The 30 maths marks, grouped: nought to 9, two learners. Ten to 19, five. Twenty to 29, twelve. Thirty to 39, eight. Forty to 49, three. Check the total: 2 plus 5 plus 12 plus 8 plus 3 gives 30 — nobody lost, nobody counted twice. That check is a mark in the exam and a habit for life.

The histogram looks like a bar graph with one loud difference: NO gaps. The bars stand shoulder to shoulder, each one covering its interval on the horizontal axis, because the axis here is a number line, and the intervals tile it completely — 9 is followed by 10, 19 by 20; there is no empty ground between intervals for a gap to represent. A gap in a histogram would claim some stretch of marks that no learner could possibly have, which is false. Gaps in a bar graph tell the truth about separate categories; touching bars in a histogram tell the truth about a continuous number line. The gap question — why do these bars touch — is a standard two-mark explanation, and now you can give it in one sentence.

Build it like the bar graph otherwise: title, labelled axes, vertical scale from nought to just past the biggest frequency — twelve — in equal steps, and heights transcribed from the table: 2, 5, 12, 8, 3.

Read the finished shape, because exams ask: where do the marks cluster? The tallest bar is 20 to 29, holding twelve of the thirty learners. The shape rises to that peak and falls away — most of the class sits in the middle interval, with tails of very low and very high marks. The interval 20 to 29 is also where the modal class lives — the interval with the highest frequency.

This section's questions are in front of you: totals, touching bars, modal class, and one deliberately faulty histogram to catch.

## Subtopic: Line Graphs, and Reading the Pie

The week's takings, plotted: Monday R380, Tuesday R420, Wednesday R150, Thursday R460, Friday R510. Days along the horizontal in calendar order; rand up the vertical, nought to 600 in steps of 50. Plot the five points, join them in order with straight segments — a broken line rising, plunging on Wednesday, then climbing to Friday's peak.

Now read the story, because a line graph's shape is a story. The general trend across the week rises: Friday's 510 stands well above Monday's 380. But Wednesday breaks it — takings crash to 150, then recover fully on Thursday. The graph cannot say why; it can only point. The context can: Wednesday was sports afternoon, school closed early, the stall lost its lunch rush. Exams love this pairing — describe the trend, then suggest a reason for the exception — and full marks needs both the numbers and the plausible reason.

The pie chart closes the toolkit. The flavour data as a circle: the whole circle is all 100 drinks; each slice is a flavour's share. Cola's 45 out of 100 is 45 percent of the circle. Two calculations cover every pie question Grade 10 asks. From share to amount: grape holds 15 percent of the circle, and 15 percent of 100 drinks is 15 drinks — and if next week sells 240 drinks in the same proportions, cola's share is 45 percent of 240, which is 108 drinks. From share to angle, for checking a drawn pie: the full circle is 360 degrees, so cola's slice spans 45 percent of 360 — 0,45 times 360 gives 162 degrees. All four slices: 162 plus 108 plus 54 plus 36 — for orange 30 percent, grape 15, apple 10 — total 360 exactly. When shares are given, they must total 100 percent, and angles must total 360: the pie's built-in audit.

The closing questions of this part are with you now — plotting order, the Wednesday story, and pie shares turned into drinks and degrees.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Same tuckshop week — now with the graphs as three simple pictures: piles, buckets and a journey.

## Subtopic: Piles, Buckets and Journeys

Every dataset you will ever meet in Grade 10 is one of three animals, and once you can name the animal, the graph chooses itself.

Animal one: piles. Sort the week's cooldrinks by flavour and you get four separate piles on the counter — 45 colas, 30 oranges, 15 grapes, 10 apples. Separate piles, nothing in between them — there is no drink halfway between cola and orange. Piles get a bar graph: one bar per pile, standing apart with gaps, because the piles themselves stand apart.

Animal two: buckets. The class's maths marks run along a number line from nought to 49, and we scoop them into buckets: nought to 9, 10 to 19, and so on. The buckets stand rim to rim — a mark of 19 and a mark of 20 fall into neighbouring buckets with no space between. Buckets get a histogram: bars that touch, because the buckets touch.

Animal three: journeys. The daily takings are one traveller — the till — checked every day: 380, 420, 150, 460, 510. A journey gets a line graph: footsteps plotted in order and joined, so the eye can walk the same road.

And the pie? The pie is not a fourth animal — it is a way of looking at the piles: squash all four piles into one round tart and ask what fraction of the tart each flavour owns. You will be given the tart already sliced; your job is only to read it.

Piles, buckets, journeys. The questions for this piece are with you now — name the animal first, and the graph is the easy half.

## Subtopic: The Ruler Rules of a Good Graph

A graph is a promise: equal distances mean equal amounts. Every drawing rule you will be marked on protects that one promise.

Rule one: the number axis starts at nought and climbs in equal steps. Nought, 50, 100, 150 — every step worth the same. The moment steps go nought, 50, 300, the picture breaks its promise: a small rise looks huge or a huge rise looks small. Choose the step size by looking at your biggest value — Friday's 510 — and picking a step that gets just past it in a reasonable number of jumps: steps of 50 up to 600.

Rule two: label everything. Title on top — takings per day at the tuckshop for the week. Words on each axis — day of the week; takings in rand. A graph with naked axes is a maths sentence with no nouns; the marker cannot even tell what was measured, and the marks fall off one by one.

Rule three: bars equal in width, and gap rules obeyed — gaps between pile-bars, no gaps between bucket-bars. Width plays no role in meaning; only HEIGHT speaks. Fat and thin bars whisper false importance.

Rule four: transcribe, then audit. Every bar height and every plotted point is copied from the table — so after drawing, read your own graph backwards and check each value against the table: does the cola bar sit on 45? Does Wednesday's point sit on 150? One minute of checking has saved more graph marks than any other habit.

These four rules are exactly the checklist markers use — title, labels, even scale, correct heights. Draw with the checklist and the marks are mechanical.

Your questions for this section are up: each shows a graph breaking one ruler rule — name the broken rule.

## Subtopic: The Wednesday Dip

Plot the week and look at what the line does: up from 380 to 420, cliff-dive to 150, leap back to 460, on up to 510. That zigzag drop-and-recovery is the most exam-rich shape in all of data handling, so read it slowly, in three layers.

Layer one: the facts. Lowest point Wednesday at R150; highest Friday at R510. The week's range: 510 minus 150 gives R360. Total for the week: 380 plus 420 plus 150 plus 460 plus 510 — 1 920 rand.

Layer two: the trend. Ignore Wednesday for a moment and the line climbs steadily all week — Monday 380 to Friday 510. A trend is the line's overall direction, described with its evidence: rising, because Friday stands 130 above Monday.

Layer three: the exception, and its story. Wednesday does not fit the trend. The graph flags it; the context explains it — early closing for sports, no lunch-rush customers. When an exam asks suggest a possible reason, it wants something the data makes plausible, tied to the dip: school closed early, so fewer buyers. It does not want a fantasy the graph cannot support.

One warning while reading line graphs: the line between plotted points is a guide for the eye, not new information. The segment joining Tuesday to Wednesday does not mean takings slid smoothly through R285 at midnight — there were no sales between days at all. Values live at the plotted points; the line just helps you walk from one to the next.

Facts, trend, exception-with-reason: answer in those three layers and the interpretation questions empty their marks into your hands.

Last questions of the lesson are with you now — read the dip, name the range, defend the trend, and keep your reasons anchored to the data.
