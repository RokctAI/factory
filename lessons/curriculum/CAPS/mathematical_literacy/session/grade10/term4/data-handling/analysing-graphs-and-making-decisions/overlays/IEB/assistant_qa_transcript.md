**Thandi:** Time for the questions that trip everyone up in analysis! First one: percentage change — I never remember which number belongs on the bottom of the fraction. How do I make it stick?

**Tutor:** Tie it to the word change: a change is measured from where you STARTED, so the starting value — the original, earlier one — sits in the denominator, every single time. May to June: usage moved from 500 to 575, so the change of 75 goes over the original 500, giving 15 percent. Put 575 underneath and you get about 13,0 percent — a real number, but it answers a different question, namely what fraction of June the difference represents. Assessments want the change relative to where things began. Two habits lock it in. First, write the timeline before the fraction: from 500, to 575, change 75 — the number after the word from is your denominator. Second, sense-check with the famous case: if a price doubles from 100 to 200, the change should be 100 percent, and only dividing by the original 100 gives that; dividing by the new 200 gives 50 percent, which instantly feels wrong for a doubling. Run that doubling test in your head whenever you hesitate, and the denominator sorts itself out.

***

**Thandi:** Next: the question asked whether replacing the leaking tap was worth the plumber's fee, and I did every calculation but still dropped the last mark. What was missing?

**Tutor:** The verdict. A worth-it question is a decision question, and the final mark is reserved for the decision stated with its evidence. Your calculations carry the journey: the repair saves 7 kilolitres a month, which at R35 per kilolitre is R245; the plumber charged R580; two months of savings give R490, still short, and three months give R735, past the fee — so the repair pays for itself during the third month, and thereafter saves R2 940 a year. But the marker then wants the sentence that closes the loop: yes, the repair was worth it, because the fee is recovered within three months and every month after is pure saving. Notice the shape — decision, then because, then numbers. It is the same discipline as the account-comparison questions in Finance: arithmetic without a conclusion is a journey without an arrival. Whenever a question contains the words should, worth, better or advise, budget the last line of your answer for the verdict sentence before you even begin calculating.

***

**Thandi:** Third: conclusions and predictions — my friend's prediction was accepted and mine was called an overreach, and we used the same graph. Where exactly is the line between them?

**Tutor:** The line is whether the sentence claims more certainty than the data owns. A conclusion may speak firmly, but only about what the picture actually shows: June used 575 units, the most of any month — solid, because it is read straight off the graph. A prediction speaks about next year, which no graph can show, so it must wear a hedge: next June will PROBABLY peak NEAR 575 units, IF the household's winter pattern continues. Your friend's sentence almost certainly carried those seatbelts; an overreach sounds like next June will use 575 units — a fact-shaped claim about the unknown. There is a matching mistake on the conclusion side: smuggling in judgements the data never measured, like the family is careless with electricity — the graph records usage, not carelessness, so that sentence cannot be supported however reasonable it feels. The working rule: for conclusions, ask whether a stranger could verify the sentence using only the graph; for predictions, check that the sentence contains a softener and a condition. Probably, roughly, if the pattern holds — those small words are not weakness; they are precision about uncertainty, and markers pay for them.

***

**Thandi:** Last one: the misleading-graph question. I can see the bars look wrong, but my explanation always comes out vague. What is the exact sentence markers want?

**Tutor:** They want the mechanism, not just the accusation — name what the axis does and what the eye wrongly concludes. The template has three parts. Part one, the fault: the vertical axis starts at 300 instead of nought. Part two, the consequence: each bar therefore shows only the portion of its value above 300 — January keeps a stub of 20 while June keeps a column of 275 — so the picture displays only the difference between the months, not the amounts themselves. Part three, the honest comparison: the true values are 320 and 575 units, so June is less than double January, not the fourteen-fold explosion the bars suggest. Fault, consequence, truth — three sentences, usually three marks. And keep the accusation precise: the DATA is not false, the SCALE is misleading; markers distinguish those, and so do arguments in real life. If the question then asks how to repair the graph, the answer is one line: redraw it with the vertical axis starting at nought, so equal distances once again mean equal amounts.
