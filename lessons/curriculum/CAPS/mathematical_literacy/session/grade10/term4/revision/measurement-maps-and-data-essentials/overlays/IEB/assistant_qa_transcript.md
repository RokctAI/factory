**Thandi:** Questions from the class before the finals! First: perimeter, area and volume — I know the formulas, but I keep using the wrong one. How do I choose fast?

**Tutor:** Let the question's spending choose for you, because each measurement buys a different kind of thing. Perimeter is the edge — anything sold by the running metre points there: fencing, skirting boards, ribbon around a box, the border of a flowerbed. Area is the surface — anything that covers points there: paving, paint, tiles, grass, fabric. Volume is the inside — anything that fills: water, concrete, sand, the capacity of a tank or a box. So underline the object being bought and ask which of the three it is — an edge thing, a cover thing or a fill thing. The garden makes it concrete: fence at R90 per metre needs the 33 metres around; paving at so much per square metre needs the 67,5 square metres across; the tank's water needs the 4,2 cubic metres inside. One more tell: the units in the price are a confession. Per metre confesses perimeter, per square metre confesses area, per cubic metre or per litre confesses volume. The price tag has already chosen the formula — you just have to read its units.

**Thandi:** Next: scale questions. I multiply when I should divide, and divide when I should multiply. Is there a way to keep the direction straight?

**Tutor:** Anchor to which world your starting number lives in — the paper world or the real world. The scale 1 to 100 says the real world is 100 times bigger than the paper. Starting on paper and wanting reality? You are growing, so multiply: 62 millimetres becomes 6 200 millimetres, which is 6,2 metres. Starting in reality and wanting the drawing? You are shrinking, so divide: a 2,4-metre wall, 2 400 millimetres, drawn at 1 to 100, becomes 24 millimetres. Before calculating, write one small sentence: paper to real, multiply — or real to paper, divide. Then run the sanity check at the end, which catches every direction error: the real length must be the BIG number, the paper length the small one. If your real wall came out at 0,62 millimetres, the direction was wrong and the check caught it before the marker did. Maps behave identically with bigger factors — 8 centimetres at 1 to 25 000 grows to 200 000 centimetres, and the final conversion to 2 kilometres tames the giant. Growing multiplies, shrinking divides, and reality is always the big number.

**Thandi:** Third: mean versus median. The question asked which one better represents the data, and I never know what the marker wants me to say.

**Tutor:** The marker wants three things chained together: the comparison, the mechanism and the verdict. Start with the numbers — for 11, 14, 14, 16, 20, the mean is 15 and the median 14, close together, because the data is even-tempered. Now add the outlier, the 55-bucket household. Recompute: the total jumps from 75 to 130 across 6 values, so the mean leaps to about 21,7 — while the median moves only to the average of 14 and 16, settling at 15. That contrast is the mechanism: the mean is built from every value, so one extreme drags it toward itself; the median is built from position, the middle of the ordered queue, so an extreme at the end barely shifts it. Then the verdict sentence the marks are reserved for: the median better represents a typical household, because the mean of 21,7 describes almost nobody — five of the six houses use far less. Structure your answer exactly that way — both values computed, the dragging explained, the choice stated with its reason — and the standard two or three marks are all yours.

**Thandi:** Last one: probability from data. The question said estimate the probability — how is that different from probability with coins and dice?

**Tutor:** Coins and dice give theoretical probability: symmetry does the counting, so a fair coin's chance of heads is a half before anyone flips. Taxis, weather and survey data have no symmetry to lean on, so probability is estimated from observation — relative frequency: the fraction of trials in which the thing happened. Nine green taxis out of 30 observed gives 9 over 30 — 0,3 — and that estimate is the best available claim about the next taxi: a 30 percent chance, equally writable as three tenths. Three expectations follow. First, express it in whatever forms the question names — fraction, decimal, percentage — and convert cleanly between them. Second, place it in words on the scale from 0 to 1: 0,3 sits below the even chance of 0,5, so a green taxi is unlikely but far from impossible. Third, respect its nature as an estimate: more observations steady it, and a different corner on a different day may give a different fraction. The phrase to write is estimated from the data — it tells the marker you know the difference between counting symmetry and counting the street.
