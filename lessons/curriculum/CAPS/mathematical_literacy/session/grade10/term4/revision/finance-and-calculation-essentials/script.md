# Part 1 — Expert

This session is the money-and-numbers revision sweep for the final examination — the whole year's finance and calculation work, reorganised around one household and one small business. Naledi's family budget, the till slips from Mrs Dlamini's spaza shop, a bank statement, and a prepaid electricity tariff: every number the paper can ask about lives in one of those four documents. Nothing here is new; everything here is the exam-day version — percentages, ratio and rate first, then VAT, discount and interest, then budgets and profit, then banking and tariffs, each compressed to the moves that earn the marks.

## Subtopic: Percentages, Ratio and Rate — the Calculation Toolkit

Every finance question stands on three calculation tools, so the revision starts by sharpening them.

Tool one: percentage of an amount. A shop offers 25 percent off school shoes. Percent means per hundred, so 25 percent is 0,25, and 25 percent of R480 is 0,25 times 480 — R120 off. The percentage change tool runs the other way: a taxi fare rises from R15 to R18. Change over original, times 100: the change is 3, over the original 15, giving 0,2 — a 20 percent increase. The original — the earlier value — is always the denominator; divide by the new 18 and you answer a different question and lose the mark.

Tool two: ratio. Juice concentrate mixes with water in the ratio 1 to 4. That is 5 parts in total, so 2 litres of mixed juice needs 2 over 5 — 0,4 litres of concentrate and 1,6 litres of water. The discipline: total the parts first, then share; a ratio compares part to part, and the whole is the sum.

Tool three: rate, and its sharpest exam form, unit price. Rice comes as 2 kilograms for R45,90 or 5 kilograms for R109,50. Divide each price by its size: R22,95 per kilogram against R21,90 per kilogram — the big bag wins by R1,05 on every kilogram. Best-buy questions are decided per unit, never by the shelf price, and the memo wants the division shown for both options plus the verdict sentence naming the winner.

The habits that carry the whole paper: write the operation before reaching for the calculator, keep the units in the answer, and round money to two decimal places only at the end.

This section's questions are with you now — one percentage change with the original safely underneath, one ratio shared, one best buy defended.

## Subtopic: VAT, Discount and Interest

Three ways money grows or shrinks, one multiplication each.

VAT first — 15 percent, added to almost everything sold. A desk costs R240 before VAT. The VAT is 0,15 times 240 — R36 — so the till price is R276. The faster route: multiply once by 1,15, because the customer pays 100 percent plus 15 percent. And the reverse gear, the one the exam loves: a kettle's shelf price of R299 already includes VAT. The shelf price is 115 percent of the true price, so divide by 1,15: R260 before VAT, which makes the VAT itself R39. Dividing by 1,15 undoes VAT; subtracting 15 percent of R299 does not, because that takes 15 percent of the wrong number.

Discount is the same machine in reverse: 25 percent off a R799 jacket is 0,25 times 799 — R199,75 off — leaving R599,25, or in one move, 799 times 0,75. Discount comes off before VAT questions say otherwise; read the order the question sets.

Simple interest grows a fixed amount every year, always calculated on the original deposit. R2 000 saved at 8 percent per year for 3 years: one year's interest is 0,08 times 2 000 — R160 — so three years earn 160 times 3, which is R480, and the account closes on R2 480. The word simple is the signal: same interest every year, straight-line growth, and a table with one row per year earns the method marks even when the arithmetic slips.

The recognition drill: the words including VAT send you dividing by 1,15; the word discount multiplies by what is LEFT; the words per annum start a year-by-year table.

Take this section's questions now — one VAT added, one VAT reversed, one interest table built.

## Subtopic: Budgets, Income, Expenditure and Profit

A budget is a plan written as two lists — money in and money out — and the exam tests whether you can build it, read it and judge it.

Naledi's household: wages of R6 200 and R600 from weekend sewing — income R6 800. Expenses: rent R2 500, food R1 200, transport R900, electricity R650 and school costs R800 — total R6 050. Income minus expenses: a surplus of R750. Surplus means income wins; deficit means expenses win, and the sign of that one subtraction is usually a full mark. The follow-up the paper loves: food rises by 10 percent — R120 more — does the budget survive? New total R6 170, surplus R630: yes, and the sentence stating yes with the new numbers is where the final mark sits.

Fixed against variable is the other budget classification: rent stays the same every month — fixed; electricity moves with usage — variable. Advice questions want cuts aimed at variable items, because fixed ones do not bend.

Now the spaza shop, where the same subtraction wears business clothes. Mrs Dlamini buys chips at R6 a packet — the cost price — and sells at R8,50 — the selling price. Profit per packet: R2,50. Forty packets sold: 2,50 times 40 — R100 profit. If a fridge breakdown forces her to sell milk below cost, each sale makes a loss, and the exam expects the word loss, not a minus sign left to speak for itself.

Financial documents tie the topic together: on a till slip, find the VAT line and check the addition; on an account statement, distinguish the opening balance, the payments and the closing balance. Every document question is really a reading question with one calculation hiding inside.

Work through this section's questions — one budget balanced, one price shock tested, one profit totalled.

## Subtopic: Banking, Tariffs and the Graphs That Price Them

Money kept and money metered — the year's last two document types.

Bank charges first. A withdrawal at the counter costs a fixed R2 plus 1,2 percent of the amount. Withdraw R500: 2 plus 0,012 times 500 — 2 plus 6 — R8. Withdraw R1 500: 2 plus 18 — R20. The structure is always fixed part plus variable part, and comparison questions ask which option is cheaper FOR A GIVEN AMOUNT — the answer can switch as the amount grows, so calculate both options at the amount the question names, then write the verdict. Statements repeat the skill: find the fee lines, add them, and subtract them from the balance.

Tariffs are prices per unit of something metered. Prepaid electricity at R2,50 per unit: 300 units cost 750 rand. Add a fixed monthly charge of R50 and the month costs R800. That two-part structure — fixed charge plus rate times usage — is the same formula as the bank fee, and the exam writes it three ways: as a sentence, as a formula, and as a graph.

The graph version is the year's patterns work earning its keep. Cost on the vertical axis, units on the horizontal. A tariff with a fixed charge starts at R50, not at nought — the starting value is the fixed charge, and the slope is the price per unit: every extra unit lifts the line by R2,50. Two tariffs on one grid cross where they cost the same; left of the crossing one is cheaper, right of it the other. Reading that crossing point and saying who should choose what is the highest-value question in the section.

The four-line checklist for any money document: name the fixed part, name the rate, calculate at the usage given, end with a sentence that answers the actual question.

The final questions of this part are with you now — one fee compared, one tariff priced, one crossing read.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same revision at the kitchen table — the shop, the statement and the meter box, read the way a family actually reads them.

## Subtopic: Three Questions Every Price Must Answer

Every money decision in a real month comes down to three questions, and each one is a single calculation.

Question one: what does it really cost? A price tag says R299 including VAT. The tag hides the true price the shop charged before the government's 15 percent. Divide by 1,15 and the kettle is R260, wearing R39 of tax. Whenever a question says including, the honest number is underneath, and division by 1,15 digs it out.

Question two: is the special actually special? A jacket at 25 percent off R799 — is that a good deal? Multiply by 0,75: R599,25. Now the trick shops play: compare that against the OTHER store's everyday price, not against the old tag. And in the food aisle the same question is settled per unit: the 5 kilogram rice at R109,50 works out to R21,90 a kilogram; the 2 kilogram bag at R45,90 costs R22,95 a kilogram. The big bag is the real special — R1,05 cheaper on every single kilogram — and no bright yellow sticker changes that arithmetic.

Question three: what will it cost by the time it is paid off? R2 000 borrowed or saved at 8 percent simple interest does not stay R2 000. Each year adds R160; after three years, R480 has grown on top, and the total is R2 480. Interest is just a percentage with a clock attached.

Really cost, really special, really paid — divide by 1,15, price per unit, interest per year. Three questions, three one-line calculations, and most of Paper 1's finance marks.

Your questions for this section are ready — dig out one true price, expose one fake special, run one clock.

## Subtopic: The Budget Is a Seesaw

Picture the family budget as a seesaw: income sits on one side, expenses on the other, and every money question just asks which way it tips.

Load the seesaw. Income side: R6 200 in wages, R600 from sewing — R6 800. Expense side: rent R2 500, food R1 200, transport R900, electricity R650, school R800 — R6 050. The seesaw tips toward income by R750. That R750 surplus is the family's breathing room, and every exam question about this budget is secretly asking what happens to the breathing room.

Food prices jump 10 percent? That adds R120 to the expense side; the tip shrinks to R630. Still breathing. A new expense of R800 for a phone contract? Now expenses reach R6 850 and the seesaw tips the wrong way — a deficit of R50 — and something must give. Which something? Look for the seats that can slide: rent is bolted down, fixed; electricity and food can shift, variable. Advice always targets the sliding seats.

Mrs Dlamini's shop sits on the same seesaw with different labels. Money in: forty packets of chips at R8,50 — R340. Money out: forty packets bought at R6 — R240. Tips toward income by R100, and that tip is called profit. When the tip goes the other way, it is called a loss, and the shop's job — like the family's — is keeping the seesaw leaning the right way.

One picture, every budget question: list both sides, total both sides, say which way it tips and by how much. The by-how-much number, with its name — surplus, deficit, profit, loss — is the answer.

Here are this section's questions — load the seesaw, tip it, name the tip.

## Subtopic: The Traps That Eat Marks, and the Last Check

Revision is mostly about the traps, because in November the arithmetic is familiar but the traps are fresh. Four of them harvest the most finance marks.

Trap one: taking 15 percent off an including-VAT price to find the price before VAT. R299 minus 15 percent of 299 gives R254,15 — wrong, because the R299 is 115 percent, not 100. Antidote: divide by 1,15, get R260, and check by multiplying back — 260 times 1,15 lands exactly on 299.

Trap two: percentage change over the wrong base. Fare from R15 to R18: the change of 3 sits over the ORIGINAL 15 — 20 percent — never over the new 18. Antidote: the word from names the denominator.

Trap three: comparing shelf prices instead of unit prices. R45,90 looks cheaper than R109,50, but per kilogram the big bag wins. Antidote: divide both, then judge.

Trap four: arithmetic without a verdict. A worth-it, which-option or does-the-budget-survive question reserves its last mark for the sentence: the 5 kilogram bag is the better buy because it costs R21,90 per kilogram against R22,95. Decision, because, numbers — that shape, every time.

And the last check, the habit that turns near-marks into full marks: reverse your answer. Found a price before VAT? Multiply by 1,15 and hit the tag. Balanced a budget? Add the surplus back to the expenses and land on the income. Split a ratio? Add the parts and recover the whole. Every finance answer carries its own proof, and the proof is one line.

The final questions of the lesson are with you now — four traps, four antidotes, and every answer reversed before it leaves your desk.
