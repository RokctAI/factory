# Part 1 — Expert

This session is the space-and-information revision sweep for the final examination — the year's measurement, maps and data work, rebuilt around one project: the Khumalo family upgrading their backyard in Soweto. A garden to fence and pave, a water tank to size, a floor plan for the new room, a map to the building supplier, and the survey data the street committee collected about water usage. Nothing here is new; everything here is the exam-day version — formulas substituted cleanly, scales converted both ways, and data read, summarised and defended.

## Subtopic: Perimeter, Area, Volume and the Formula Sheet

Measurement questions all start the same way: choose the formula, substitute with units attached, calculate, and answer in a sentence.

The garden is a rectangle, 8,5 metres by 6 metres. Perimeter is the distance around — 2 times, 8,5 plus 6 — 2 times 14,5, giving 29 metres. Area is the space inside — length times width, 8,5 times 6, giving 51 square metres. The units police themselves: perimeter in metres, area in square metres, and writing 51 m for an area drops the mark. Now the money layer the exam always adds: fencing costs R85 per metre, so the fence costs 29 times 85 — R2 465. Perimeter prices the edge; area prices the surface — paving, paint, grass — and choosing between them is the real test. The word around signals perimeter; the word cover signals area.

Volume fills the space: the water tank is a rectangular prism, 2 metres long, 1,5 wide, 1,2 high. Volume is length times width times height: 2 times 1,5 times 1,2 — 3,6 cubic metres. And the conversion that turns geometry into plumbing: one cubic metre holds 1 000 litres, so the tank holds 3 600 litres.

Substitution is the umbrella skill. A formula is a recipe: write it, replace each letter with its number in brackets-free spoken working, then calculate following the order of operations. The memo awards the substitution line itself — formula, values in, answer with unit — three little marks that careless workers skip straight past.

This section's questions are with you now — one edge priced, one surface covered, one tank filled.

## Subtopic: Conversions and the Units That Carry Marks

Every measurement answer travels through units, and conversion errors are the cheapest marks lost in the whole paper.

The metric ladder for length: millimetres to centimetres to metres to kilometres, each step a factor of 10, 100 or 1 000. Going up the ladder — small units to big — divide; going down — big to small — multiply. So 4 500 millimetres is 4,5 metres, and 3,2 kilometres is 3 200 metres. Mass runs grams to kilograms by 1 000; volume runs millilitres to litres by 1 000, and 2 500 millilitres is 2,5 litres.

The two conversions the exam loves most. First, cubic metres to litres: multiply by 1 000 — the tank's 3,6 cubic metres is 3 600 litres, and that number now talks to the municipal bill, priced per kilolitre, where one kilolitre IS one cubic metre. Second, the sneaky one inside area problems: measurements given in mixed units. A path 750 centimetres long and 1,2 metres wide must speak one language before multiplying — 7,5 metres times 1,2 metres gives 9 square metres. Multiply mixed units and the answer is nonsense with a confident face.

Temperature completes the set: degrees Celsius in weather questions, read off thermometers and graphs, with differences calculated by subtraction — a day swinging from 9 degrees to 24 degrees swung by 15.

The working habit: convert FIRST, calculate SECOND. Write both the original and the converted value, because the conversion line is itself a mark, and an examiner who sees 750 centimetres become 7,5 metres pays for the thinking even if the multiplication later stumbles.

Take this section's questions now — ladders climbed both directions, one mixed-unit trap defused.

## Subtopic: Scale, Floor Plans and Maps

Scale is one idea wearing two costumes: the number scale, like 1 to 100, and the bar scale, a printed ruler. Both say the same thing — every unit on paper equals so many in reality.

The floor plan of the Khumalos' new room is drawn at 1 to 100. A wall measures 45 millimetres on the plan, so the real wall is 45 times 100 — 4 500 millimetres — 4,5 metres. That is the entire mechanism: measure on paper, multiply by the scale, convert to sensible units. In reverse, a 3-metre wall drawn at 1 to 100 becomes 3 000 divided by 100 — 30 millimetres on paper. Plans add their own vocabulary: the symbols for doors and windows, north arrows, and the discipline of checking WHICH wall a dimension label belongs to before quoting it.

The street map to the building supplier uses 1 to 50 000. The route measures 6 centimetres: 6 times 50 000 is 300 000 centimetres — 3 kilometres. Scale calculations always produce absurd-looking numbers mid-journey; the conversion at the end tames them, and the memo wants both steps visible.

Elevation plans complete the plan family: the floor plan looks straight down, showing the layout of walls and rooms; an elevation looks from the side, showing heights, windows and the roofline. Exam questions ask which view answers which question — how wide is the room is floor-plan work; how high is the window sill is elevation work.

Assembly diagrams and packaging round off the year's representations: an exploded diagram shows parts and order without words, and a packaging question asks how many small boxes fill a big one — volume division with a physical-fit check, because 3,5 boxes cannot be packed; only 3 whole ones can.

Work through this section's questions — one plan measured, one map distanced, one view chosen.

## Subtopic: Data and Probability — from Collection to Conclusion

The data cycle in one breath: pose a question, collect, organise, summarise, represent, interpret. The street committee asked 40 households about weekly water usage — that is a survey of a sample, and its first product is a tally table becoming a frequency table.

Summarising compresses the table into four numbers. Take five households' buckets per day: 12, 15, 15, 18, 20. The mean: total 80, divided by 5 — 16. The median: the middle of the ordered list — 15. The mode: the most frequent — 15. The range: 20 minus 12 — 8, a measure of spread, not of centre. The exam's favourite twist: one extreme value. Add a household using 60 buckets and the mean leaps while the median barely moves — so the median represents a typical household better when the data holds an outlier, and explaining that choice in a sentence is a standard two-mark question.

Representing turns numbers into pictures — bar graphs for categories, histograms for grouped numbers, pie charts for shares of a whole, line graphs for change over time — and interpretation reads them back: identify the tallest bar, compute a share of the total, describe a trend with its numbers attached.

Probability joins the toolkit as data about the future. The scale runs from 0 — impossible — through 0,5 — even chance — to 1 — certain, written as fractions, decimals or percentages. Of 40 taxis passing the corner, 8 were red, so the relative frequency of red is 8 over 40 — 0,2, a 20 percent chance the next taxi is red. Relative frequency is probability estimated from data, and more data steadies the estimate.

The final questions of this part are with you now — one summary computed, one outlier judged, one chance expressed three ways.

## Subtopic: The Tape Measure Pays the Bills

Every measurement question is secretly a shopping question, and the tape measure decides what you pay.

Walk the garden's edge with the tape: 8,5 metres, then 6, then 8,5 again, then 6 — 29 metres around. That 29 is not geometry for its own sake; it is the fencing order, and at R85 a metre the order costs R2 465. Now stand in the middle and look down: 8,5 times 6 — 51 square metres of ground. That 51 is the paving order, the grass order, the anything-that-covers order. Edge numbers buy edge things; surface numbers buy surface things — and the classic exam wrong-answer is buying fence by the square metre.

The tank is the third kind of number: how much fits inside. Two metres by 1,5 by 1,2 — 3,6 cubic metres of space, and since each cubic metre swallows 1 000 litres, the tank holds 3 600 litres of rain. Around, across, inside — perimeter, area, volume — and every question tells you which one it wants by what money it spends.

One habit keeps all three honest: make the units match before multiplying. A path 750 centimetres by 1,2 metres must become 7,5 metres by 1,2 before the multiplication — 9 square metres. Mixed units multiply into nonsense, and nonsense priced at R120 per square metre is expensive nonsense.

The questions for this piece are with you now — walk the edge, cover the surface, fill the space, and let the units check your work.

## Subtopic: Paper Worlds — Plans and Maps as Shrunken Reality

A plan is the real world photocopied smaller by an exact amount, and the scale tells you the shrink factor.

The room plan says 1 to 100: everything real got shrunk 100 times to fit on the page. So reading it is just un-shrinking: a wall of 45 millimetres on paper stretches back to 4 500 millimetres — 4,5 metres of actual brick. Going the other way, planning a 3-metre cupboard wall means shrinking: 3 000 millimetres becomes 30 on the page. Multiply to grow, divide to shrink — and always ask which direction the question is travelling.

The map works identically, just with a bigger shrink: 1 to 50 000. The route to the supplier measures 6 centimetres of paper; un-shrink it — 300 000 centimetres — then speak it like a human: 3 kilometres. The giant middle number is normal; the final conversion is what makes it useful.

Two views, one building: the floor plan is the view a bird gets — walls, doors, room sizes. The elevation is the view a neighbour gets — heights, windows, the roof. Want to know if the couch fits: bird. Want to know if the window catches the afternoon sun: neighbour. Exam questions hand you both drawings and quietly test whether you look down or across.

And the flat-pack diagram is a plan for time instead of space: parts drawn floating in order, numbers for sequence, arrows for movement. Count the parts against the list before starting — the diagram answers questions the same way a map does, by careful looking before any calculating.

Your questions for this section are up — un-shrink one wall, un-shrink one journey, and pick the right view twice.

## Subtopic: Reading the Street's Numbers Honestly

The street committee's water survey turns the whole data cycle into one neighbourhood story — and the story is only as good as the honesty of its summary.

Five households reported daily bucket counts: 12, 15, 15, 18, 20. The mean — 80 shared equally among 5 — says 16 buckets is the balanced share. The median — the middle household — says 15. The mode — the popular answer — is also 15. The range — 8 — says the street's habits differ by up to eight buckets. Four numbers, four different sentences about the same street.

Then the mansion with the swimming pool joins the survey: 60 buckets. The mean rockets — one extreme resident drags the average toward himself — but the median hardly flinches, because the middle of the queue is still an ordinary house. When someone quotes an average, the street-smart question is: who is IN that average? If an outlier lurks, the median tells the truer story of a typical home, and the exam pays two marks for saying so with the numbers shown.

Probability is the same honesty aimed forward. Eight of 40 passing taxis were red, so the street's data says the chance of the next one being red is 8 over 40 — 0,2 — 20 percent. Not a promise; an estimate, and one that steadies as more taxis pass. Impossible sits at 0, certain at 1, and everything real lives between them.

The last habit of the whole session: every summary you quote, quote with its evidence — the mean IS 16 because the total IS 80 over 5 households. Numbers with their working attached cannot be argued with, in the exam hall or at the street meeting.

Read the last questions of the lesson now — summarise once, defend the median once, and estimate one honest chance.
