**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! The first one is a practical panic: the graph in my test had two lines and no labels on them at all. How am I supposed to know which line is Option A and which is Option B?

**Tutor:** By asking each line what it costs when nothing has been used yet — the answer is written into where it starts. Option A carries no fixed charge, so a household that used zero kilowatt-hours owes zero rand; its line must begin on the horizontal axis, at the origin. Option B charges R150 before a single light goes on, so a household that used nothing still owes R150; its line must begin at R150 up the vertical axis. The starting height on the vertical axis is the vertical intercept, and on any money graph the intercept is the fixed charge. So you match each option's fixed fee to the height at which a line begins, and the lines identify themselves. If both options happened to have the same fixed charge, you would fall back on the second identifier: the steeper line is the one charging more per unit. Between those two features — where it starts and how fast it climbs — every tariff line on every graph you will meet can be named without a label.

**Thandi:** Next question, and it is an honest one. In maths class, gradient was just a number you calculated. What is it actually doing here on an electricity bill?

**Tutor:** Here the number has a job and a unit. The gradient of a cost line is the price of one more unit — how many rands your bill climbs when you use one extra kilowatt-hour. On Option A it is R2,00 per kilowatt-hour; on Option B it is R1,50 per kilowatt-hour. That is why A is the steeper of the two: each step to the right costs more money on A than on B. You can measure it off the line itself without knowing the tariff. Between 100 and 200 kilowatt-hours on Option A, the cost goes from R200 to R400, so the bill rose R200 while 100 units passed; R200 divided by 100 units is R2,00 per unit. Rise divided by run. And when an examiner asks you to explain what the steeper gradient means, the marks are in the rands, not the adjective. Say: Option A charges more per unit, R2,00 against R1,50, so its cost rises faster as usage increases. Naming the number and its unit is what turns a description into an explanation.

**Thandi:** This one comes from a learner who is annoyed. She wrote "Option B is cheaper" for the 450 kilowatt-hour question, got two marks out of three, and cannot see what was missing.

**Tutor:** She was missing the evidence. "Option B is cheaper" is a claim; a comparison question in Mathematical Literacy almost always carries a mark for the values and a mark for the explicit comparison. The full answer runs like this: on Option A, 450 units at R2,00 each comes to R900. On Option B, the fixed R150 plus 450 units at R1,50 each — that is R150 plus R675 — comes to R825. Option B is therefore cheaper by R75. Three moves: claim, calculate, compare. Notice that the difference of R75 has its own meaning too, which is why markers want it — it is the actual money the household keeps each month, and it is what makes the answer useful rather than merely correct. A good habit is to finish every comparison sentence with the words "by R-something". If you cannot fill in that blank, you have not finished the question.

**Thandi:** Last one, and it is a what-if. What if the exam gives me the two tariffs in words with no graph at all — can I still find the break-even, or am I stuck?

**Tutor:** You can find it, and often faster than reading it off a graph. Think about what the fixed charge is buying. Option B asks R150 up front, and in exchange it sells every unit 50 cents cheaper than Option A does — R1,50 instead of R2,00. So the question becomes: how many units of electricity does it take for those 50-cent savings to add up to R150? R150 divided by R0,50 per unit is 300 units. That is the break-even, and it is the same 300 kilowatt-hours the crossing point shows. Verify it in rands: Option A gives 300 times R2,00, which is R600; Option B gives R150 plus 300 times R1,50, which is R150 plus R450, also R600. Equal, so break-even confirmed. The same reasoning works for cellphone contracts and gym deals: divide the fixed fee by the saving per use, and you have the usage at which the deals swap places.
