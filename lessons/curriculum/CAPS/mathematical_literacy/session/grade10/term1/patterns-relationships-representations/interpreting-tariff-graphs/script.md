# Part 1 — Expert

Mathematical Literacy does not treat a graph as a picture; it treats it as an answer machine for a real money decision. This session builds that reading skill on a case every South African household faces: two electricity tariff options drawn on one set of axes. Option A charges R2,00 per kilowatt-hour with no fixed fee; Option B charges a fixed R150 per month and then R1,50 per kilowatt-hour. From those two lines the session extracts the fixed charge from the vertical intercept, the price per unit from the gradient, and the break-even usage from the crossing — verified by calculation — before answering the examination's question: which option is cheaper for a household using 450 kilowatt-hours a month, and by how many rands.

## Subtopic: Meeting the Graph — Axes, Lines and the Story They Tell

Here is our situation. The municipality offers two ways to pay for electricity. Option A has no fixed monthly charge — you simply pay R2,00 for every kilowatt-hour, every unit, you use. Option B charges a fixed R150 per month before you switch on a single light, but then each unit costs only R1,50.

The graph shows both options on the same axes. The horizontal axis carries electricity usage in kilowatt-hours, running from 0 to 600. The vertical axis carries the monthly cost in rands. Always read the axes first — titles, units and scale — before you look at any line. A graph without its axes is a shape; with them, it is information.

Now identify each line by thinking about where it must start. Option A: if you use nothing, you pay nothing — its line starts at the origin, at zero. Option B: if you use nothing, you still pay the R150 fixed charge — its line starts at R150 on the vertical axis. That starting value is called the vertical intercept, and in money graphs the intercept is always the fixed cost. Read the intercept, know the fixed charge — no calculation needed.

Both lines rise as usage grows — more units, more money — but they rise at different speeds, and they cross somewhere in the middle of the graph. That crossing point is where today's real decision lives.

The questions for this section are with you now. Look for the two things a line declares before it climbs anywhere: the height at which it starts on the vertical axis, and what that starting value means in rands for a household that used no electricity at all.

## Subtopic: Gradient — What "Steeper" Means in Rands

Look at the two lines climbing. Option A's line is steeper than Option B's. In everyday language, steeper just means "goes up faster". In graph language, the steepness is the gradient, and in this context it has an exact meaning: the gradient is the price per unit — the number of rands the cost climbs for each extra kilowatt-hour used.

Option A climbs R2,00 for every unit along the horizontal axis: gradient 2 rands per kilowatt-hour. Option B climbs R1,50 per unit: gradient 1,5 rands per kilowatt-hour. A is steeper because each unit costs more on that option — that is the full explanation the exam wants: "the steeper line means Option A charges more per kilowatt-hour — its cost rises by R2,00 for every extra unit, compared with R1,50 for Option B."

You can measure a gradient straight off any line, even without the tariff information: pick two easy points on the line, read how much the cost rises between them, and divide by how many units passed. On Option A's line, between 100 and 200 kilowatt-hours the cost climbs from R200 to R400 — a rise of R200 over 100 units — R2,00 per unit. Rise over run, exactly as you plot points, just in reverse.

So each line carries its tariff openly: intercept shows the fixed charge, gradient shows the price per unit. Option A: intercept 0, gradient 2. Option B: intercept 150, gradient 1,5. The whole pricing structure, readable at a glance — that is why companies, banks and municipalities present options as graphs.

Take this section's questions now. Every one of them expects a gradient stated with its units — rands per kilowatt-hour, not a bare number — so keep the rise-over-run arithmetic from the 100-to-200 unit stretch close at hand.

## Subtopic: The Break-Even Point

Now to the crossing. The two lines intersect at one point — trace it down to the horizontal axis: 300 kilowatt-hours. Trace it across to the vertical axis: R600. This is the break-even point: the usage at which both options cost exactly the same — 300 units for R600, whichever option you chose.

Confirm it with arithmetic, because a read-off answer should always be verified when the numbers allow. Option A at 300 units: 300 times R2,00 is R600. Option B at 300 units: R150 plus 300 times R1,50 — that is R150 plus R450 — R600. Both R600. The graph and the calculation agree; the break-even is confirmed.

Why does break-even matter? Because it splits the graph into two decision zones. To the LEFT of 300 units — low usage — Option A's line runs below Option B's: A is cheaper. That makes sense: light users should not pay a R150 entrance fee for a discount they barely use. To the RIGHT of 300 units — high usage — Option B's line dips below A's: B is cheaper, because the saving of 50 cents on every unit eventually outweighs the fixed charge.

This zone-reading is the skill: below the crossing, the lower line names the cheaper option; the crossing itself is the point of indifference; beyond it, the other line takes over. Every "which cellphone contract? which gym membership? which bank account?" question in this subject is this same picture wearing different clothes.

This section's questions are in front of you. Answer them with the break-even established both ways — read off the axes and confirmed by calculation — and be ready to name the cheaper option on each side of 300 kilowatt-hours, with the reason attached.

## Subtopic: Answering the 450 kWh Question — and the Exam Method

The exam question: which option is cheaper for a household using 450 kilowatt-hours per month?

Method one — read the graph. Find 450 on the horizontal axis, run your finger straight up. You strike Option B's line first — lower cost — and Option A's line higher up. At 450 units, B's line is clearly below A's. Option B is cheaper. And notice the shortcut reasoning: 450 is to the right of the break-even at 300, and beyond break-even the fixed-charge option wins. Zone logic gives the answer even before your finger moves.

Method two — verify with calculation, exam-style. Option A: 450 times R2,00 equals R900. Option B: R150 plus 450 times R1,50 — R150 plus R675 — equals R825. B costs R825 against A's R900: Option B is cheaper by R75. State the saving; comparisons earn full marks when they carry the difference, not just the winner's name.

Now capture the whole method, because this is what Paper 1's finance questions and the integration questions on both papers keep asking. One: read the axes — variables, units, scale. Two: identify each line by its intercept — the fixed cost. Three: read gradients as prices per unit — steeper means more expensive per unit. Four: find the intersection — the break-even — and verify it by calculation. Five: split the graph into zones and name the winner on each side. Six: for any specific usage, read vertically, compare heights, and confirm with arithmetic including the rand difference.

One last habit separates strong candidates: sense-check every reading against life. Does it make sense that a heavy user prefers the fixed-charge option? Yes — bulk users benefit from cheaper units. Does it make sense that at zero usage A costs nothing and B costs R150? Yes — that is exactly what "no fixed charge" and "fixed charge" mean. When the graph, the arithmetic and common sense all shake hands, your answer is safe.

The final questions of this part are with you now. Each comparison in them wants the same three things in the same order: both rand totals, the name of the cheaper option, and the difference in rands between them.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same two tariffs from the kitchen table where the electricity bill actually lands — same R150, same R2,00 and R1,50, told the way you would explain it to somebody who has to choose tonight.

## Subtopic: Two Ways to Pay — the Day Pass and the Membership

Think about a gym in the mall. It offers you two deals. Deal one: no joining fee at all, but you pay R2,00 every time you walk through the door. Deal two: you pay R150 the moment you sign up, before you have lifted anything, and after that each visit costs only R1,50.

Electricity is sold exactly like that. Option A is the day-pass deal: nothing to join, R2,00 for every unit of electricity you burn. Option B is the membership deal: R150 goes off your money at the start of the month whether you switch on a light or not, and after that each unit is R1,50.

Now picture how each deal looks when you draw the money against the units used. Start at the very beginning of the month, at zero units. On Option A you owe nothing yet, so its line starts down on the floor, at R0. On Option B you already owe R150 before anything happens, so its line starts up in the air, at the R150 mark. That starting height is the joining fee, plain and simple. If you ever have to work out which line belongs to which deal, ask what each one costs at zero — the answer is the height where it starts.

From there both lines climb, because both deals charge you for what you use. Neither line ever comes down, because your bill never gets smaller by using more electricity. The whole rest of this lesson is just watching how fast each one climbs.

That starting height has a proper name in the exam: the vertical intercept. And what it stands for in a money question is always the same thing — the fixed charge.

Nice work — the questions for this piece are with you now. They are all about the beginning of the month: what each deal costs before you have used a single unit, and which line starts where.

## Subtopic: How Fast Your Money Leaves

Now the climbing. Imagine two taps filling the same bucket. One tap runs faster than the other. Both fill the bucket; one just empties your pocket quicker.

On Option A, every unit of electricity you use adds R2,00 to your bill. On Option B, every unit adds only R1,50. So when you draw them, A's line climbs more sharply for every step you take to the right — A is the faster tap. That sharpness has a name, the gradient, but forget the name for a second and hold on to what it actually is: the price of one unit. On a money graph, how steep a line is means how much one more unit costs you. Nothing more mysterious than that.

You can read the price straight off the line even if nobody tells you the tariff. Take two easy spots on Option A's line: at 100 units the bill is R200, and at 200 units the bill is R400. So going from 100 to 200 units — that is 100 more units — the bill went up by R200. R200 for 100 units means R2,00 for one unit. You just read a price off a picture: how much it went up, divided by how many units went past.

Do the same on Option B and you get R1,50 a unit. So the two lines are honest about everything: where they start tells you the joining fee, and how steeply they climb tells you the price per unit. Between those two numbers you know the whole deal.

And when an exam asks what the steeper line means, do not say "it goes up faster" and stop there. Say it in rands: Option A costs R2,00 per kilowatt-hour against Option B's R1,50, so A's bill rises faster for every extra unit used.

Your questions on this section are up now — read them slowly, because the marks sit in saying the steepness in rands per unit rather than just calling one line steeper.

## Subtopic: Where the Lines Swap Places — and the 450-Unit Answer

Here is the good part. Option B makes you pay R150 up front — but it then sells you every unit 50 cents cheaper than Option A does. So the R150 is not a punishment; it is something you are buying with. The question is simply: how much electricity must you use before those 50-cent savings add up to the R150 you handed over?

Work it out the way you would work out anything at a shop: R150 to make back, saving 50 cents each unit, so R150 divided by 50 cents is 300 units. At 300 units you have saved exactly what you paid, and the two deals cost you the very same money. Check it in rands. On A: 300 units times R2,00 is R600. On B: R150 plus 300 times R1,50, which is R150 plus R450 — also R600. Dead level at R600. That is where the two lines cross on the picture, and the exam calls it the break-even point.

So the month splits into two halves. Use less than 300 units and Option A wins — you never make back that R150, so why pay it. Use more than 300 units and Option B wins — every unit past 300 is pure 50-cent savings in your pocket. Light user, no membership. Heavy user, take the membership.

Now the question the exam actually asked: your household uses 450 units. Which deal is cheaper? You already know the answer before you calculate, because 450 is past 300, so B wins. But say it in rands, because that is where the marks are. Option A: 450 times R2,00 is R900. Option B: R150 plus 450 times R1,50, which is R150 plus R675 — R825. Option B is cheaper by R75.

And look how neatly the story checks out: at 450 units you saved 50 cents on each of 450 units, which is R225, and you paid R150 for the privilege. R225 minus R150 is R75 — exactly the saving. The numbers and the reasoning agree, and when those two shake hands you can write the answer with confidence.

Last questions of the lesson are with you now. When you meet a comparison, give them all three pieces — what A costs, what B costs, and who wins by how many rands — and this whole family of questions, cellphone contracts and gym deals included, belongs to you.
