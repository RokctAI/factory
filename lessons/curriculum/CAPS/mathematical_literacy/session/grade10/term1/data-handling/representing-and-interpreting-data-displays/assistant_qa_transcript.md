**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! First: a bar graph and a histogram look identical to me except for the little gaps. Does it really cost marks if I draw the bars touching when they should have gaps?

**Tutor:** It does, and the reason is worth more than the mark. The gaps are not decoration; they are a statement about what kind of data you are showing. A bar graph shows categories — walk, taxi, bus, car — and there is nothing between "taxi" and "bus", no halfway mode of transport, so the space between the bars honestly represents that emptiness. You could also swap those two bars around and nothing about the information would change, which is another way of seeing that the horizontal axis is a list, not a scale. A histogram shows measurements grouped into bands — R0 up to R50, R50 up to R100 — and there is nothing between those bands, because R50 belongs to the second one and every rand in between has a home. The bars touch because the intervals touch, and you may not rearrange them, because the axis is a number line running in order. So when a marker sees touching bars over category names, the message received is that you have not understood which kind of data you were given. Look along the bottom axis before you draw the first bar: words mean gaps, numbers in order mean touching.

**Thandi:** Next, and this caused a fight: someone said a pie chart is always the clearest graph because you can see the shares immediately. Is that true?

**Tutor:** It is true for one narrow job and misleading for everything else. A pie chart answers exactly one question well: how is a single whole divided? For the household earning R12 000 a month, seeing that housing takes a clean quarter and food nearly a third is instant understanding, and no other display gives it that fast. Now the weaknesses. Human eyes are poor at comparing angles: a 22 percent slice and a 25 percent slice look the same, whereas two bars of those heights are clearly different, so pie charts lose precision exactly where bar graphs keep it. Pie charts cannot show change over time — you would need a separate pie for every month, and comparing them is unreliable unless every total is identical, which household incomes never are. They cannot show more than one group at a time. And they collapse entirely with many small categories, because a dozen thin slivers cannot be read or labelled. Add the trap that a pie chart carries no amounts at all — without the R12 000 written on the page, no slice converts to rands. So use one when the question is about shares of one whole, and reach for a bar graph when the question is about comparing sizes, or a line graph when it is about change.

**Thandi:** This one worries me for the exam: how do I answer "comment on the trend" without just writing "it goes up"? That has to be worth more than one mark.

**Tutor:** It is worth more, and there is a structure that collects the marks. Describe in four layers. Layer one, the overall direction: use rose overall from January to June. Layer two, the shape of that rise, because trends are rarely uniform: it was broadly flat from January to March, then climbed steeply from April onward. Layer three, the named extremes with their values: the lowest was 240 units in February, the highest 480 units in June. Layer four, the steepest section, which is not the same as the highest point and is the part most candidates miss: from April to May use rose 110 units, more than any other month-to-month change, including May to June's 60. Then quantify the whole movement — 480 minus 250 is an extra 230 units, which is 230 divided by 250, a 92 percent increase on January. Finally, give the reason the context suggests, because Mathematical Literacy always wants the story: the climb matches the arrival of winter, with heating and longer dark evenings. Direction, shape, extremes, steepest section, a calculated figure, and a plausible reason. Write those and a one-line "it goes up" turns into a full-mark paragraph.

**Thandi:** Last one, and it is a bit cheeky: if a truncated axis is so misleading, why do real newspapers and even science textbooks do it? Is it always dishonest?

**Tutor:** Not always, and this is where you get to be genuinely thoughtful rather than just suspicious. There are cases where starting the axis at zero hides the information. Body temperature over a week sits between 36 and 38 degrees; drawn from zero, the line is flat and useless, and the two degrees that matter clinically are invisible. Rainfall, exchange rates and blood pressure often have the same problem: the interesting variation is small compared with the size of the numbers. So a truncated axis is a legitimate tool when the small variation IS the subject and the graph makes the truncation obvious — a clearly labelled axis, and often a break symbol showing where the scale jumps. What makes it dishonest is the combination of three things: the truncation is not signalled, the display is a BAR graph, where the eye reads the whole bar as the quantity, and the accompanying words claim a size of change the numbers do not support. That is our newspaper: bars from 400, no break shown, and a headline saying "quadruples" for what is really about a 14,3 percent rise. So in an exam, do not simply write "the axis does not start at zero" as if that were automatically a crime. Write what it does to the reader and what the honest figure is. That is the answer of someone who understands graphs rather than someone reciting a rule.
