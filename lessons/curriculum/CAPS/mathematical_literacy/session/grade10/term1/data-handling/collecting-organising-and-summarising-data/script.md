# Part 1 — Expert

The sister-in-charge at a Mamelodi clinic asks a Grade 10 group to find out how long patients actually wait before being seen. She has money for one extra chair, one extra nurse for the morning shift, or nothing at all — and she will decide on the evidence the class brings her. This session runs the DATA CYCLE end to end: posing a question worth asking, collecting and classifying the data, organising it into a tally and a frequency table, summarising it with the mean, median, mode and range — then choosing which summary actually answers the sister's question.

## Subtopic: The Data Cycle and the Question Worth Asking

The data cycle has a fixed order, and examiners expect its stages named: pose a question; collect the data; organise and classify it; summarise and represent it; analyse and interpret; and report an answer. Skip a stage and the conclusion is unsupported.

Stage one carries the most weight. "Is the clinic slow?" is not a usable question — nobody can measure "slow". "How many minutes does a patient wait between arriving and being seen by a nurse, on a weekday morning?" is usable: it names what is measured, in what unit, on whom and when.

Classify the data before you collect it, because the classification decides everything afterwards. NUMERICAL data are counts or measurements: DISCRETE if they can only take separate whole values, like the number of patients in a queue; CONTINUOUS if they fall anywhere on a scale, like a waiting time of 47,5 minutes. CATEGORICAL data are labels, not numbers: the reason for the visit, the network on a person's phone, whether a patient had an appointment.

Then the source. PRIMARY data are collected by you, for your question — the class at the door with a clipboard. SECONDARY data already exist, gathered by someone else, like the clinic's register: cheap, but shaped by another purpose.

Finally, guard against BIAS. Recording only the patients who arrive at eight o'clock, or only those who complain, produces a sample that does not represent the population — every patient the clinic serves. A leading question — "Do you agree the waiting time is unacceptable?" — pushes the answer it wants. So the class takes 15 patients spread across a Tuesday morning, reading arrival and consultation times off the register rather than asking people to guess.

The questions for this section are with you now: name the stages in order, sort numerical from categorical, and say which sampling choices would have wrecked the study.

## Subtopic: Tallies, Frequency Tables and Class Intervals

Fifteen waiting times come back, in the order recorded, in minutes: 45, 120, 25, 50, 35, 45, 20, 90, 30, 55, 45, 25, 60, 140, 40. Raw data in collection order tell you almost nothing, so the first act of organising is to ORDER them: 20, 25, 25, 30, 35, 40, 45, 45, 45, 50, 55, 60, 90, 120, 140.

A TALLY converts the list into counts: work through the data once, striking a mark for each value in its group and crossing the fifth mark through the previous four so the marks read in fives. The FREQUENCY is the total of the marks in that row.

Because waiting time is continuous, the values are grouped into CLASS INTERVALS of equal width that cover every value and never overlap: 0 to 29 minutes, 30 to 59, 60 to 89, 90 to 119, and 120 to 149. Now count. Zero to 29 holds 20, 25 and 25 — frequency 3. Thirty to 59 holds 30, 35, 40, 45, 45, 45, 50 and 55 — frequency 8. Sixty to 89 holds only 60, and 90 to 119 only 90 — frequency 1 each. And 120 to 149 holds 120 and 140 — frequency 2.

Check the table before you use it: 3 plus 8 plus 1 plus 1 plus 2 equals 15, the number of patients recorded. A frequency column that does not total the sample size means a value was counted twice or lost.

Two traps live here. Overlapping intervals — writing 0 to 30 and 30 to 60 — leave a patient waiting exactly 30 minutes with two homes and no rule for choosing. And once data are grouped, the individual values are gone: the table remembers that eight patients waited between 30 and 59 minutes, but not that three of them waited exactly 45. Keep the ordered list; you will need it for the median.

Work through this section's questions now, and pay attention to interval boundaries — that is where the marks are quietly won and lost.

## Subtopic: Mean, Median, Mode and Range

Four summaries, four definitions, four calculations. Three of them are measures of CENTRE — a single value standing for the whole group — and the fourth measures SPREAD.

The MEAN is the total divided by the number of values. Add the fifteen waiting times: 20 plus 25 plus 25 plus 30 plus 35 plus 40 plus 45 plus 45 plus 45 plus 50 plus 55 plus 60 plus 90 plus 120 plus 140 gives 825 minutes. Divide by 15 patients: the mean is 55 minutes. In words, if every patient had waited the same length of time, each would have waited 55 minutes.

The MEDIAN is the middle value of the ORDERED list — and ordering first is not optional. With 15 values, the middle position is found from the number of values plus one, divided by two: 16 divided by 2 gives position 8. Count to the eighth value in the ordered list: 20, 25, 25, 30, 35, 40, 45, and the eighth is 45. The median is 45 minutes. When the count is EVEN, the middle position falls between two values, and the median is the mean of those two.

The MODE is the value that appears most often. Forty-five appears three times, more than any other, so the mode is 45 minutes. The mode is the only measure of centre that also works for CATEGORICAL data — you cannot average "headache, injury, chronic medication", but you can name the most common reason for a visit.

The RANGE is the largest value minus the smallest: 140 minus 20 gives 120 minutes. It measures how stretched the data are, not where they sit.

Three habits protect these marks. Show the substitution — write 825 divided by 15, not just 55. Keep the unit — the answer is 55 minutes, not 55. And round only at the end, to a sensible degree: a waiting time reported as 54,6667 minutes is arithmetic showing off, not information.

Take on this section's questions now: compute all four summaries, and be ready to state the median rule for an even number of values.

## Subtopic: Which Summary Answers Which Question

The class has four numbers: mean 55 minutes, median 45, mode 45, range 120. They disagree, and the disagreement is the finding.

The mean sits ten minutes above the median because three patients waited 90, 120 and 140 minutes. Those OUTLIERS pull the mean upward; the median barely notices them, because it only cares which value sits in the middle. So when data contain a few extreme values, the MEDIAN describes the typical experience better than the mean. That is exactly why income is reported as a median: a handful of very large incomes drags the mean above what most people earn.

Match the summary to the question. If the sister asks what a patient can typically expect, quote the median: 45 minutes. If the provincial office asks how much nursing time the morning consumes in total, use the mean, because mean times number of patients rebuilds the total — 55 times 15 gives back 825 minutes. If the pharmacy asks which stock item to keep deepest, that is a mode question. And if the complaint is about unpredictability, the range answers it: 120 minutes between the luckiest and the unluckiest patient on one ordinary morning. The range has a weakness worth stating, though — it is computed from only two values, so one unusual patient can triple it.

Now report properly — in a sentence that answers what was asked. "On a Tuesday morning the median wait was 45 minutes, but waits ranged from 20 to 140 minutes, with three patients waiting well over an hour. The clinic's problem is not the typical wait but the long tail, so extra capacity is best placed where those long waits arise rather than adding a chair to the queue." Numbers, then interpretation, then a recommendation the sister can act on. That is a full-mark answer.

The final questions of this part are in front of you now: justify a choice of summary in words, and explain what an outlier does to the mean but not to the median.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same clinic study from the kitchen table — asking a question you can actually answer, sorting the answers into piles, and finding the number that speaks for the whole family.

## Subtopic: Asking a Question the Numbers Can Answer

Say you want to know whether your household spends too much on airtime. "Do we spend too much?" gets you an argument, not an answer, because nobody can measure "too much".

Now sharpen it: "How many rands did each person in this house spend on airtime and data last month?" That one you can answer, because it says exactly WHAT you are counting (rands), on WHOM (each person here) and WHEN (last month). Sharpening the question is where most projects go wrong.

Next, know what kind of answers will come back. Some are numbers you can add up — rands spent, minutes waited, learners in a class. Others are just labels — the network someone uses, the reason they went to the clinic. You can add rands; you cannot add "Telkom plus Vodacom".

Then, who do you ask? If you only ask the two cousins with the newest phones, of course the answer comes out high — you picked the people whose answers you already guessed. Same at the clinic: stand at the door only at eight in the morning and you miss everyone who came at eleven. Spread who you ask across the whole group, and never let the question lean — "Don't you agree the queue is terrible?" is fishing, not asking.

And two ways to get the numbers: collect them yourself with a clipboard, or use numbers someone already wrote down, like the clinic's own book. Yours fit your question perfectly but cost you a morning; theirs are free but were written for someone else's purpose.

Questions on all of that are with you right now — start each one by asking whether the question could actually be answered with a number.

## Subtopic: Sorting the Washing

You never fold washing straight out of the basket. You sort it into piles first — shirts here, socks there — and only then can you see what you have. Data are the same.

The class came back with fifteen waiting times, jumbled: 45, 120, 25, 50, 35, 45, 20, 90, 30, 55, 45, 25, 60, 140, 40. Useless in that order. So do the first thing you would do with washing — put them in order, smallest to biggest: 20, 25, 25, 30, 35, 40, 45, 45, 45, 50, 55, 60, 90, 120, 140. Already the story shows: a crowd of shortish waits, then three that ran away.

Now the piles. Under half an hour: 20, 25, 25 — 3 people. Half an hour to just under an hour: 30, 35, 40, 45, 45, 45, 50, 55 — 8 people, the big pile. Then one at 60, one at 90, and two more at 120 and 140. As you sort, mark each one with a stroke, and let every fifth stroke go across the four before it so the marks count themselves in fives. That is a tally, and the number at the end of each row is the frequency.

Two rules keep the piles honest. The piles must not overlap — if one pile is "0 to 30" and the next is "30 to 60", where does a 30-minute wait go? Nowhere and both places at once. And add your frequencies at the end: 3 plus 8 plus 1 plus 1 plus 2 must give 15, because 15 people walked in. If it does not, a sock is missing.

Small questions on sorting and counting are here now. Order first, then pile, then add the piles back up.

## Subtopic: The Middle, the Most, the Average and the Stretch

Four ways to say "what were the waits like?", and each says something different.

The AVERAGE is the sharing-out number: pile all the minutes together and share them equally. Total the fifteen waits and you get 825 minutes; share those among 15 people and each gets 55. So the average wait was 55 minutes — what everyone would have waited if the waiting were shared fairly.

The MIDDLE is whoever stands in the centre of the queue when everyone lines up shortest to longest. Fifteen people, so the eighth is the middle one — seven ahead, seven behind. Count along: 20, 25, 25, 30, 35, 40, 45, and there is the eighth — 45 minutes. With an even number of people nobody stands exactly in the middle, so you take the two middle ones and split the difference.

The MOST-OFTEN is the wait that shows up more than any other: 45 appears three times. This is the only one of the three that works for words instead of numbers — you cannot average "headache, injury, chronic medication", but you can say which came up most.

The STRETCH is the gap between the longest and the shortest: 140 take away 20 is 120 minutes. Two hours between the luckiest patient and the unluckiest, on one ordinary morning.

Now the part that matters. Why is the average 55 but the middle only 45? Because three people waited 90, 120 and 140 minutes, and those long waits drag the sharing-out number up — the same way one uncle with a big salary lifts the "average income" of a family where everyone else is struggling. The middle number does not get dragged, so it tells you what a normal patient can expect. Your paper calls these the mean, the median, the mode and the range — and it wants you to say WHICH one you used and WHY.

Your last questions are with you now. Say the number, put the minutes or the rands on it, and finish with one plain sentence about what the clinic should actually do — because a number without a decision is only half the work.
