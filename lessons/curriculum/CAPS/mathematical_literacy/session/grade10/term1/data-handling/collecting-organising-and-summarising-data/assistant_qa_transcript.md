**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! First one: our group got a mean of 55 minutes and a median of 45. Which one do we write down as "the answer"? It feels like one of them must be wrong.

**Tutor:** Neither is wrong — they are answers to different questions, and the skill being tested is knowing which question is in front of you. The mean shares the total equally: 825 minutes of waiting spread over 15 patients gives 55 minutes each. That makes the mean the right tool whenever you care about the TOTAL, because mean multiplied by the number of values rebuilds it — 55 times 15 returns your 825 minutes of nursing time consumed. The median ignores totals entirely and reports position: line the fifteen patients up shortest wait to longest, and the eighth one waited 45 minutes. That makes the median the right tool whenever you care about the TYPICAL experience, because it is not moved by a few extreme values. Here three patients waited 90, 120 and 140 minutes; they add a lot to the total, so they lift the mean, but they only shift the median by one position. So write both, then say which one you are relying on and why. "The median wait was 45 minutes; the mean of 55 is higher because three unusually long waits inflate the total." That sentence earns marks on its own.

**Thandi:** Next: why can't we just group the waiting times into intervals of nought to thirty, thirty to sixty, sixty to ninety? It reads so much more neatly than nought to twenty-nine.

**Tutor:** It reads neatly and it is unusable, because the boundaries belong to two intervals at once. A patient who waited exactly thirty minutes fits "nought to thirty" and also fits "thirty to sixty", and nothing in your table says which. Two people organising the same data would then produce two different frequency tables, and the whole point of organising is that anybody following your rules lands on your counts. So intervals must cover every possible value and overlap nowhere. Two ways to do that. For data recorded in whole minutes, use nought to twenty-nine, thirty to fifty-nine, sixty to eighty-nine, and so on — every whole number has exactly one home. For data that can take any value, including decimals, use the "from up to but not including" style: thirty up to but not including sixty. Either convention is accepted; mixing them is not. And whichever you choose, finish by adding the frequency column and checking it equals the number of values you collected. If your five intervals hold three, eight, one, one and two, that totals fifteen, and fifteen patients walked in — so nothing was lost and nothing counted twice.

**Thandi:** This one confused everyone: our teacher said the mode is sometimes useless and sometimes the only thing that works. How can both be true?

**Tutor:** Both are true because the mode's usefulness depends entirely on the kind of data. The mode is the value that occurs most often, and it is the ONLY measure of centre available for categorical data — labels rather than numbers. You cannot add "headache, injury, chronic medication" and divide by three; there is no mean reason for a clinic visit, and no middle reason. But you can say which reason came up most often, and that is exactly what a pharmacy needs before deciding which stock to keep deepest, or a shop needs before deciding which bread size to order most of. Now the useless case. For continuous measurements — waiting times, masses, distances — repeats are often accidental. If our fifteen waits had all been different, there would be no mode at all; if two values had tied, there would be two modes and no single answer. Our data happened to give a clear mode of 45 minutes only because times were recorded to the nearest five minutes. So the rule is: for categories, reach for the mode first. For measurements, treat it as extra information rather than the headline, and lead with the median or the mean. Naming that reasoning in your answer is exactly the analysis the examiner is looking for.

**Thandi:** Last one, and it is really about the project: how many patients do we actually need, and does it matter WHO we ask if we are just going to average everything anyway?

**Tutor:** It matters more than the size does, and that catches most learners out. A sample is a smaller group standing in for the whole population, and it can only stand in honestly if every part of the population had a fair chance of being in it. Suppose you record only the patients who arrive at eight in the morning. You will capture the early rush and miss everyone who comes at eleven, and your median will describe a queue that only exists for one hour of the day. Suppose instead you record only the people who complain at the desk. You have handpicked the longest waits, and your data are guaranteed to look terrible no matter what the clinic does. Averaging cannot repair either problem; the arithmetic faithfully summarises whatever you fed it, including the bias. So spread your collection across the whole morning, and take patients as they come rather than choosing them. On size: fifteen is enough for a school investigation and small enough that one unusual patient noticeably moves the mean — which is precisely why you also report the median and the range. More values give a steadier picture, but a small fair sample beats a large crooked one every time. Say all of that in your report; explaining how you guarded against bias is worth real marks.
