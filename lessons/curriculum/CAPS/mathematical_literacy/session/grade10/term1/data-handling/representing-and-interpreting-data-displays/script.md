# Part 1 — Expert

A community newspaper in Polokwane runs four pictures on one page: a bar graph of how learners get to school, a histogram of what they spend on data, a pie chart of a household's monthly income, and a line graph of one family's electricity use through the winter. Four displays, four different jobs — and one of them, before the page is finished, will be quietly redrawn to make a small change look enormous. This session builds the four displays, the rule for choosing between them, and the checklist that catches a misleading graph before it fools you.

## Subtopic: Bar Graphs and Histograms — Cousins, Not Twins

A BAR GRAPH displays CATEGORICAL data — labels, not measurements. Each bar stands for one category, the bars are separated by GAPS, and the order of the bars can be rearranged without changing the meaning.

The transport survey covered 300 learners: 90 walk, 135 take a taxi, 45 take a bus, and 30 come by car. The bars sit at heights 90, 135, 45 and 30 on a vertical axis labelled "Number of learners", above a horizontal axis labelled "Mode of transport". Check the total: 90 plus 135 plus 45 plus 30 gives 300.

Read it properly. The tallest bar names the MODAL category — taxi. Converting to a percentage makes it quotable: 135 out of 300 is 135 divided by 300, which is 0,45, or 45 percent of learners travelling by taxi. Walking accounts for 90 over 300, which is 30 percent.

A HISTOGRAM looks similar and is not the same thing. It displays CONTINUOUS numerical data grouped into class intervals, so the bars TOUCH — because the intervals themselves touch — and the order cannot be rearranged, since the horizontal axis is a number line.

The data-spend survey of 60 learners gives: R0 up to R50, eight learners; R50 up to R100, fifteen; R100 up to R150, twenty-one; R150 up to R200, ten; R200 up to R250, six. Total: 8 plus 15 plus 21 plus 10 plus 6 gives 60. The tallest bar identifies the MODAL CLASS — R100 up to R150 — and note the wording: with grouped data you may name the modal class, never a single modal value, because the individual amounts are no longer in the picture.

One more reading skill: combining intervals. How many learners spend less than R100? Eight plus fifteen gives 23 learners, which is 23 over 60, or about 38,3 percent.

The first questions of the session are with you now — decide bar graph or histogram by asking whether the horizontal axis holds labels or numbers.

## Subtopic: Pie Charts — Reading Shares, Not Amounts

A PIE CHART shows how a whole is divided into parts. Each SECTOR is a fraction of the circle, and because a full revolution is 360 degrees, the size of any sector follows from its percentage: percentage divided by 100, times 360 — which is the same as multiplying the percentage by 3,6.

A household with a monthly income of R12 000 divides it: housing 25 percent, food 30 percent, transport 15 percent, electricity and water 10 percent, school costs 8 percent, savings 7 percent, and airtime and data 5 percent. The percentages total 100, as they must.

Convert to angles. Housing: 25 times 3,6 gives 90 degrees — a clean quarter. Food: 30 times 3,6 gives 108 degrees. Transport: 15 times 3,6 gives 54 degrees. Electricity and water: 10 times 3,6 gives 36 degrees. School: 8 times 3,6 gives 28,8 degrees. Savings: 7 times 3,6 gives 25,2 degrees. Airtime and data: 5 times 3,6 gives 18 degrees. Add them: 90 plus 108 plus 54 plus 36 plus 28,8 plus 25,2 plus 18 gives exactly 360 degrees. That check is compulsory.

Now go the other way, because examiners ask it more often. A sector measures 54 degrees; what does it cost the household? Its share is 54 over 360, which is 0,15 — so 15 percent of R12 000, which is R1 800 on transport. Food at 108 degrees is 108 over 360, or 0,30 — R3 600.

Two limitations must be stated. First, a pie chart shows PROPORTIONS only: without the total of R12 000 written somewhere, no sector can be converted to rands. Second, two pie charts cannot be compared directly unless their totals are equal — a 30 percent food slice of R12 000 is R3 600, while a 30 percent food slice of R6 000 is only R1 800, and the two slices look identical.

Turn to this section's questions now: convert both ways, and always confirm that your angles total a full revolution.

## Subtopic: Line and Broken-Line Graphs — Change Over Time

A LINE GRAPH puts time on the horizontal axis and shows how a quantity changes across it. Where the quantity itself is continuous — temperature through a day — a solid line is genuinely meaningful at every point. Where readings exist only at separate moments — a monthly meter reading — the points are joined by a BROKEN-LINE graph, and the joining lines show the trend rather than claiming real values in between.

A household in Mbombela buys prepaid electricity each month: January 250 units, February 240, March 260, April 310, May 420, June 480.

Read the four things a line graph is asked for. The MAXIMUM: 480 units in June. The MINIMUM: 240 units in February. The TREND: broadly flat from January to March, then rising steeply from April into winter as heating and early darkness take hold. And the STEEPEST SECTION, which is where change is fastest — April to May rises by 420 minus 310, which is 110 units, against May to June's 480 minus 420, only 60 units. April to May is therefore the steepest climb, even though June is the highest point.

Then quantify. Total units bought over the six months: 250 plus 240 plus 260 plus 310 plus 420 plus 480 gives 1 960 units. The increase from January to June is 480 minus 250, which is 230 units — as a percentage of January, 230 divided by 250 gives 0,92, a 92 percent increase. And at a tariff of R2,80 per unit, June's electricity cost 480 times R2,80, which is R1 344,00.

That last step is the point of the whole display: the graph shows the shape, the arithmetic prices it, and the household now knows to budget almost double in June.

The questions for this section are ready — describe the trend in words first, then support the description with a calculated figure.

## Subtopic: Choosing a Display — and Catching a Misleading One

Choose by asking what the data ARE and what the question wants. Categories to compare — bar graph. Continuous measurements grouped into intervals — histogram. Parts of one whole, where the shares matter more than the amounts — pie chart. A quantity tracked over time — line or broken-line graph.

Every honest display carries the same five things: a title, a label on each axis, the unit of measurement, a scale that is evenly spaced, and a source. Missing any one of them is a legitimate criticism to raise in an answer.

Now the newspaper's trick. The electricity figures for May and June — 420 and 480 units — are redrawn on a vertical axis that starts at 400 instead of 0. June's bar now rises four times as high above the axis as May's, and the headline reads "Winter electricity use quadruples". The real increase is 60 units on 420, which is 60 divided by 420, about 14,3 percent. Nothing quadrupled. A TRUNCATED VERTICAL AXIS is the single most common way of misleading with a graph, and naming it earns the mark.

The rest of the checklist. Uneven spacing on an axis, so equal gaps stand for unequal amounts. Three-dimensional effects, where a tilted pie makes the front slice look bigger than an equal slice at the back. Pictograms where an image is doubled in height AND width, so a doubling looks like a quadrupling in area. A time window chosen to end at a convenient month. Percentages quoted without the total, so "a 100 percent increase" hides a rise from two learners to four. And any axis with no label at all, which is not a graph so much as a shape.

Answer the way a marker rewards: identify the feature, explain the distortion it creates, and give the honest figure. "The vertical axis begins at 400 rather than 0, which exaggerates the difference between the bars. The actual increase from 420 to 480 units is about 14,3 percent, not a quadrupling." Feature, effect, correct number.

The final questions of this part are in front of you now: choose the right display with a reason, and criticise a graph by naming the feature and supplying the honest figure.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same four pictures from the kitchen table — because a graph is only a way of showing numbers so a person can see them without counting.

## Subtopic: Bars With Gaps, Bars That Touch

Think of bars as piles. You are counting how the learners at your school get here, so you make a pile for walkers, a pile for taxi riders, a pile for the bus, a pile for cars. Ninety walk, 135 take a taxi, 45 take the bus, 30 come by car — and adding those gives 300, everybody counted.

Draw each pile as a bar, and leave a GAP between them. The gaps matter: walkers and taxi riders are different kinds of people, not different amounts of the same thing, so the bars must not touch. You could swap walkers and bus riders around and the picture would still be true — which is exactly how you know these are separate categories.

The tallest bar tells you what most people do: taxi. Want it as a percentage? 135 out of 300 — that is 135 shared over 300, which is 0,45, so 45 out of every 100 learners, 45 percent.

Now the cousin that looks the same and is not. Say you ask 60 learners what they spend on data in a month, and instead of names you get rands. Rands run in a line — R49 and R51 are next-door neighbours, not different tribes. So you group them into bands: under R50, R50 to R100, R100 to R150, and so on, and the bars TOUCH, because the bands touch. Eight, fifteen, twenty-one, ten and six learners — adding to 60. The busiest band is R100 to R150.

The one-second test: look along the bottom. Words down there means gaps. Numbers running in order down there means the bars touch.

Some quick questions on the two kinds of bars are here for you now — check the bottom axis first, every time.

## Subtopic: Slices of the Same Plate

A pie chart is a plate of pap. There is only ever ONE plateful, and the question is how it gets divided.

A family brings in R12 000 a month. A quarter of the plate goes to the roof over their heads — that is R3 000. The biggest slice, nearly a third, is food: 30 out of every 100 rands, which is R3 600. Transport takes 15 percent, R1 800. Lights and water, 10 percent, R1 200. School things, 8 percent, R960. Savings, 7 percent, R840. Airtime and data, 5 percent, R600. Add the rands: they come back to R12 000, and add the percentages: they come back to 100. If either total misses, a slice was measured wrong.

Turning a slice into an angle is one small step. A full plate turned all the way round is 360 degrees, and 100 percent is the whole plate, so each 1 percent is 3,6 degrees. Food at 30 percent is 30 lots of 3,6 — 108 degrees. Housing at 25 percent is 90 degrees, a perfect quarter, which you can see with your eyes. All the angles together must land on 360.

And here is the catch that costs marks. A pie chart never tells you rands on its own — only shares. If your neighbour's food slice is also 30 percent but she brings in R6 000, her slice looks the same size as this family's and buys half as much food. Same picture, half the groceries. So before you convert any slice into money, find the total written somewhere on the page.

Questions on slices are with you right now. Percentage times 3,6 for the angle; percentage of the total for the rands.

## Subtopic: The Winter Line, and the Graph That Lies

Last picture: a line that walks across the months. A family in Mbombela buys prepaid electricity — 250 units in January, 240 in February, 260 in March, then 310, then 420, then 480 in June. Plot each month as a dot and join the dots, and the line does something you can feel: flat through summer, then climbing hard into winter.

Read it like a road. The lowest point is February, 240 units. The highest is June, 480. The steepest climb is April to May, where the line jumps 110 units in one month — steeper than May to June's 60. And the money follows: at R2,80 a unit, June cost 480 times R2,80 — R1 344,00. From January to June, use went from 250 to 480 units, an extra 230, which is nearly double. That is a household that must start putting money aside in March, not June.

Now the ugly part, and it happens in real newspapers. Take just May and June — 420 and 480 — and draw the bars on an axis that starts at 400 instead of 0. Suddenly May is a stub and June towers over it, and someone writes "electricity use quadruples". It did not. It rose 60 units on 420, which is about 14 out of every 100 — around 14 percent. The picture lied by cutting the bottom off the axis.

So before you trust any graph, run the same check you would run on a taxi fare: where does the scale START, are the gaps on the axis even, is there a label on every axis and a unit, and is the total given? A tilted three-dimensional pie makes the front slice look fatter. A picture drawn twice as tall AND twice as wide looks four times as big. All of it is the same trick — making your eyes do a calculation the numbers do not support.

Your last questions are with you now. Name the feature that misleads, say what it does to the reader, then give the honest number — those three moves together are the full answer.
