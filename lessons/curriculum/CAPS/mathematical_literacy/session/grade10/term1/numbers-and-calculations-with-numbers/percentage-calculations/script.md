# Part 1 — Expert

Percentage calculations are the most-used tool in the whole of Mathematical Literacy: they run through VAT, discounts, interest, inflation and tariffs, and they carry marks in Question 2 of every Paper 1 from now until matric. This session builds the method in one continuous line — percentage as a decimal, the single multiplier, two changes chained on the same item, the one overall percentage that the whole chain amounts to, and finally the chain run backwards to recover a price before VAT. The case study looks simple and hides a famous surprise: a jacket priced at R850 is discounted by 15 percent, then 15 percent VAT is added to the discounted price. What is the final price — and what single percentage change from the original does the whole journey amount to?

## Subtopic: What a Percentage Really Is — and the Decimal Trick

A percentage is a fraction with a fixed denominator of 100. Fifteen percent means fifteen out of every hundred — 15 over 100 — which as a decimal is 0,15. Every percentage calculation becomes easy the moment you translate the percentage into its decimal form: divide by 100, then multiply by whatever amount you are working with.

So 15 percent OF R850 is 0,15 times 850. Compute it: 0,1 times 850 is 85; 0,05 times 850 is 42,50; together, R127,50. That is the size of our discount — the amount the shop takes off.

Now the upgrade that separates strong candidates from calculator-fumblers: the multiplier method. Instead of finding the percentage amount and then adding or subtracting it, capture the whole change in ONE multiplication.

A 15 percent decrease leaves you with 85 percent of the original — 100 minus 15. So multiply by 0,85 in one step. A 15 percent increase gives you 115 percent of the original — multiply by 1,15. Decrease: one minus the decimal. Increase: one plus the decimal.

Check it against the long way. Long way: 850 minus 127,50 gives 722,50. Multiplier way: 850 times 0,85 — 850 times 0,8 is 680, plus 850 times 0,05 which is 42,50 — gives 722,50. Same answer, half the steps, and — as you are about to see — the multiplier method is the only comfortable way to handle chains of changes.

The questions for this section are with you now. Attend to three things as you answer them: the decimal form of a percentage, the multiplier that performs a decrease, and the multiplier that performs an increase. Everything that follows in this session is built on those three.

## Subtopic: The Discount Step, Then the VAT Step

Back to the jacket, working the chain properly. Rule number one for chained percentages: each step works on the RESULT of the previous step, never on the original. The question says VAT is added to the discounted price — read those words carefully, they define the chain.

Step one, the discount. The marked price is R850, and the discount is 15 percent. Using the multiplier: R850 times 0,85 equals R722,50. That is the till price before tax — the shop's reduced price.

Step two, the VAT. VAT — value-added tax — in South Africa is 15 percent, and it is ADDED to the price. Increase multiplier: 1,15. Apply it to the discounted price, not the original: R722,50 times 1,15.

Compute carefully: 722,50 times 1 is 722,50. 722,50 times 0,15: one tenth is 72,25; half of that is 36,125; together 108,375. Add: 722,50 plus 108,375 gives 830,875. Money rounds to two decimals: R830,88.

The final price of the jacket is R830,88.

Pause on the classic error before we celebrate. Many learners compute 15 percent of the ORIGINAL R850 for both steps: minus R127,50, plus R127,50 — and declare the final price R850, "because they cancel". They do not cancel. The discount acted on R850, but the VAT acted on the smaller R722,50 — so the VAT added back less than the discount removed. Percentages of different amounts are different amounts of money, even when the percentage rate looks identical. This single insight will protect you in every interest, inflation and mark-up question for the next three years.

Work through this section's questions before we go on. They test the two steps in the order the wording demands, the base each step acts on, and the reason the two fifteens leave R830,88 rather than R850 on the till slip.

## Subtopic: The Single Percentage Change — One Multiplier for the Whole Journey

Now the second half of the exam question: what single percentage change takes you from the original R850 to the final R830,88?

Elegant method first: multiply the multipliers. The whole journey was: times 0,85, then times 1,15. So the combined effect is 0,85 times 1,15. Multiply them: 0,85 times 1 is 0,85; 0,85 times 0,15 is 0,1275; total 0,9775.

Read the combined multiplier the same way you built the individual ones. A multiplier of 0,9775 means the final price is 97,75 percent of the original. Since 100 minus 97,75 is 2,25 — the whole journey is a 2,25 percent DECREASE.

Confirm with the direct method, because two roads to the same answer is verification. Percentage change equals the change divided by the ORIGINAL, times 100. The change: 850 minus 830,875 is 19,125. Divide by the original: 19,125 over 850 equals 0,0225. Times 100: 2,25 percent decrease. Both methods agree.

Notice the headline result: minus 15 percent followed by plus 15 percent is NOT zero — it is minus 2,25 percent. Down-then-up by the same rate always lands you slightly below where you started, because the increase works on a smaller base. Shops know this; now you do too.

And the direct percentage-change formula deserves its own spotlight, because it answers every "by what percentage did it change?" question: new minus original, divided by original, times 100. The original is always the denominator — the reference point. Divide by the new value by mistake and the answer shifts; the reference for change is always where you started.

This section's questions are in front of you. Keep both roads open as you answer — the combined multiplier and the direct formula — and check every percentage-change calculation for the one thing markers look at first: that R850, the original, is sitting in the denominator.

## Subtopic: Reverse Percentages — and the Full Toolkit

One more weapon completes the kit: working backwards. Suppose a different question: a price tag reads R830,88 INCLUDING 15 percent VAT — what was the price before VAT?

The trap answer subtracts 15 percent: 830,88 times 0,85 gives about R706,25. Wrong. Why? Because the 15 percent VAT was calculated on the BEFORE-VAT price, not on the final price — subtracting 15 percent of the wrong base repeats the error we exposed earlier, just in reverse.

The correct move: the final price IS the before-VAT price times 1,15. So run the multiplication backwards — divide. R830,88 divided by 1,15 equals R722,50 (allowing the cent of rounding). There is our discounted price again, recovered perfectly. To undo a multiplier, divide by it: that is the whole theory of reverse percentages.

Assemble the complete toolkit, your five moves for any percentage question. Move one: percentage OF an amount — decimal times amount. Move two: increase or decrease — one single multiplier, 1 plus or 1 minus the decimal. Move three: chained changes — multiply the multipliers, each step acting on the previous result. Move four: percentage change — difference over ORIGINAL, times 100. Move five: reverse percentage — divide by the multiplier to recover the earlier price.

Verify the whole jacket problem end to end, as you should in the exam: 850 times 0,85 is 722,50; times 1,15 is 830,875, rounding to R830,88; combined multiplier 0,9775 confirms a 2,25 percent overall decrease; and 830,875 divided by 1,15 lands back on 722,50. Every arrow runs forwards and backwards without contradiction. That closed loop is what mastery feels like — and in Finance questions from tariffs to inflation to interest, you will run this loop again and again.

The closing questions of this part are with you now. They cover the reverse step, the trap of subtracting fifteen percent from a VAT-inclusive price, and the five moves of the toolkit — for each question, name the move it belongs to before you calculate anything.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same jacket from the till at your local shopping centre — same R850, same two fifteens, same final answer, told the way you would work it out standing in the queue.

## Subtopic: Taking Off and Putting On — the One-Button Way

Picture a price tag as a full plate of food. R850 is the whole plate. And "percent" only ever means "out of a hundred", so think of the plate as being cut into a hundred little pieces. That is all a percentage is: how many of the hundred pieces we are talking about.

The shop says fifteen percent off. Fifteen pieces come off the plate. So how many pieces are still on the plate? A hundred take away fifteen — eighty-five. There is the whole idea, and it is not clever, it is just counting: take fifteen off and eighty-five stays.

Most people now do two jobs. Job one: work out the fifteen pieces, 0,15 times R850, which is R127,50. Job two: take it away, R850 minus R127,50, which is R722,50. That is honest and correct, and it is twice the work.

The one-button way does a single job: keep eighty-five percent. Eighty-five percent written as a decimal is 0,85, so R850 times 0,85 gives R722,50 straight out. Same rand amount, one press instead of two, and — this is the part that matters later — nothing left over on your screen to lose track of.

Going up works the same way. Your R100 airtime voucher comes with a ten percent bonus. You keep your whole hundred percent and gain ten more, so you land on a hundred and ten percent: R100 times 1,10 is R110 of airtime. Down, you land under one. Up, you land over one.

That number you multiply by — the 0,85, the 1,10, the 1,15 — has a name. It is called the multiplier, and every single thing in this lesson is that one number doing different jobs.

Right — the questions for this piece are with you now, and they are all one move: turn the change into a single number, then multiply. Say the number out loud before you press anything.

## Subtopic: Two Fifteens That Refuse to Cancel

Here is the part that catches almost everybody, including grown-ups with bank accounts.

Two friends each ask you for a quarter of your chips. The first one takes a quarter of a full packet — a big handful. The second one asks for the same fraction, a quarter, but by now the packet is smaller, so she gets a smaller handful. Same fraction, less food. Nothing unfair happened; the packet just changed size in between.

The jacket is exactly that. Fifteen percent of R850 is R127,50 — that is the discount, taken off the full packet. Then VAT arrives and asks for fifteen percent, but the packet is now R722,50, so fifteen percent of it is only R108,38. Same fifteen percent, R19,13 less money. Add it on: R722,50 plus R108,38 is R830,88.

So you go down by R127,50 and come back up by only R108,38. Of course you do not land where you started. You land R19,13 below R850, at R830,88, and the shop is not cheating you — the arithmetic simply cannot do anything else.

Now put a percentage on that whole trip. You went down and then up, so do both moves in one go: 0,85 times 1,15 gives 0,9775. That says the final price is 97,75 percent of what it was — just under the whole hundred. And a hundred take away 97,75 leaves 2,25, so the jacket ended 2,25 percent cheaper than it started.

Check it the plain way and see the same number appear. The price dropped by R19,13 out of a starting R850. Nineteen comma one two five divided by 850 is 0,0225, and times a hundred is 2,25 percent. Both roads, one answer. That plain way has a name too — percentage change: the difference, divided by where you started, times a hundred. Where you started, always, on the bottom.

Your questions on this bit are up now. Take the one about which price the VAT bites slowly, because if you can answer that one, you have got the whole lesson.

## Subtopic: Winding the Till Backwards

Last piece, and it is the one that pays in real life. Your till slip says R830,88 including VAT. The shop owner wants to know: what was that jacket worth before VAT?

The tempting move is to take fifteen percent off: R830,88 times 0,85 gives R706,25. It feels fair. It is wrong by R16,25, and here is why. The VAT that got charged was fifteen percent of the smaller price, R722,50 — it came to R108,38. But taking fifteen percent off the final price removes fifteen percent of the bigger number, R124,63, which is more VAT than was ever charged. You are handing back money that was never taken.

The honest move: think about how the VAT got on there in the first place. It got there by multiplying by 1,15. And the only thing that undoes a multiplication is a division. So divide: R830,88 divided by 1,15 gives R722,50 — the price before VAT, exactly where we were before the tax was added.

Think of it like a turnstile at the stadium. You got in through that gate, so you get out through that same gate, walking backwards. You do not climb the fence and hope. Multiply in, divide out.

Prove it to yourself with the till slip's own numbers: R722,50 before VAT, VAT of R108,38 on top, total R830,88. Every shop slip in the country is printed on that arithmetic, and now you can check yours.

So the whole lesson, five short moves, in plain words. Finding a percentage of something: turn the percent into a decimal and multiply. Making something bigger or smaller: use one number, over one for up, under one for down. Two changes one after the other: multiply the two numbers together, because the second change works on what the first one left. Asking how much something changed by: difference over where you started, times a hundred. Going backwards: divide by the number that got you there.

And here come the last questions of the lesson. If you can undo a multiplier by dividing, you can read the VAT off any till slip in South Africa — go and show them.
