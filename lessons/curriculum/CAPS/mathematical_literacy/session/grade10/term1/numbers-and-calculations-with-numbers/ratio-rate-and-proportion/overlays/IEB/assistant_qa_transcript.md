**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! First one, and plenty of us fell for it: when a question says share R3 600 in the ratio 4 : 3 : 2, why can't we just divide R3 600 by 4, then by 3, then by 2? That feels like sharing.

**Tutor:** It feels like sharing, but it hands the whole pile out three separate times. Divide R3 600 by 4 and you get R900; by 3, R1 200; by 2, R1 800. Add those supposed shares and you reach R3 900 — three hundred rand the stall never earned. The ratio does not say how many people divide each amount; it says how many equal PARTS of one pile each person takes. So the pile is cut once, into the total number of parts: 4 plus 3 plus 2 gives 9. One part is R3 600 divided by 9, which is R400. Now distribute — 4 parts is R1 600, 3 parts is R1 200, 2 parts is R800 — and those rebuild exactly R3 600, because one pile was cut once and fully handed out. Keep that picture: the colon numbers are slices, and the count of all the slices is what you divide by. And always re-add your answers at the end; if they do not reconstruct the original amount, something slipped, and you still have time to catch it.

**Thandi:** Next: I keep muddling ratio and rate. Both compare two things, both involve dividing. What is the actual difference, and does it really matter in a test?

**Tutor:** It matters, and the difference is a single word: units. A ratio compares quantities of the SAME kind — rands with rands, grams with grams, learners with learners — so the units cancel in the division and the answer carries no unit at all. That is why it is written with a bare colon, like 4 : 3 : 2, nothing attached. A rate compares quantities of DIFFERENT kinds — rands against kilograms, kilometres against litres — so the units cannot cancel and must both stay in the answer. That is why we say R25,00 per kilogram, or 14 kilometres per litre, or R16,80 per kilolitre. The word "per" is the giveaway, and it always means "for every one". The practical consequence: before simplifying a ratio, both quantities must be in the SAME unit, so 400 g to 2 kg becomes 400 g to 2 000 g, which is 1 : 5. A rate never needs that conversion, because its two quantities are supposed to differ. In a test, a ratio answer with a unit glued on is wrong, and a rate answer stripped of its units is incomplete. Same arithmetic, opposite finishing move.

**Thandi:** This one started a proper debate: six workers take 20 days, so eight workers take 15. Somebody objected that by this logic a thousand workers finish the hall in half an hour, which is ridiculous. Is the mathematics wrong?

**Tutor:** The mathematics is right and the objection is also right, and holding both at once is precisely what this subject trains. The calculation says the job has a fixed size — 6 workers times 20 days is 120 worker-days — so 8 workers clear it in 120 divided by 8, which is 15 days, and 120 workers would clear it in a single day. But inverse proportion quietly assumes every extra worker is as productive as the first, and reality breaks that assumption: a hundred tilers cannot kneel on the same floor, they queue for adhesive and cutters, and somebody has to direct traffic. The model describes a sensible range well and the extremes badly. So in a test you run the calculation exactly as the inverse rule demands, and then, if asked to comment, you say plainly that the relationship only holds while every worker has the space and tools to stay productive. That comment earns credit. Never let the criticism stop the calculation, and never let the calculation silence the criticism.

**Thandi:** Last one, from someone who feels a bit cheated: she proved the 10 kg bag was the better buy, and her mother bought the 2 kg bag anyway. Was her mathematics wasted?

**Tutor:** Not wasted — it answered a different question from the one her mother was living. The mathematics settles "which bag gives more rice per rand?", and firmly: R56,99 over 2 kilograms is about R28,50 a kilogram, R249,99 over 10 kilograms is R25,00 a kilogram, so the big bag wins by roughly R3,50 a kilogram. Her mother was answering "what can I pay for today, and what will still be good when we finally eat it?" A better unit price means nothing if the R249,99 is not in the purse this week, and a bulk bag that draws damp or weevils before it is finished is not cheaper — it is dearer, because you paid for rice that got thrown away. This is the heart of Mathematical Literacy: the number is necessary but not sufficient. Write the unit rates, state which is cheaper per kilogram, then add one honest sentence about affordability, storage or spoilage. That sentence is not padding — it carries credit, and it is also what makes the mathematics genuinely useful at home.
