# Part 1 — Expert

Three friends wash cars in Tembisa and must split R2 400 fairly. A shopper in the maize-meal aisle must choose between a 2,5 kg bag and a 10 kg bag. A builder must turn a heap of mix into the right amounts of cement, sand and stone. All three are the same mathematics in different clothes: RATIO, RATE and PROPORTION. This session builds four moves — sharing in a ratio, calculating a unit rate, telling direct from indirect proportion, and comparing two prices honestly.

## Subtopic: Ratio — Sharing Without Argument

A RATIO compares quantities of the SAME kind, measured in the SAME unit. It is written with a colon — 5 : 3 : 2 — and it carries no unit of its own, because the units cancel when like is compared with like.

Two rules examiners test immediately. First, ORDER matters: if the ratio of adults to children is 3 : 7, then writing 7 : 3 is a different statement and loses the mark. Second, the quantities must be in the same unit BEFORE you simplify. A recipe calling for 250 g of butter to 1 kg of flour is not 250 : 1. Convert first: 250 g : 1 000 g, and now divide both sides by 250 to get 1 : 4.

Now the sharing method, the single most examined ratio skill. Lerato, Sipho and Mpho worked different hours at the car wash, so they split the day's R2 400 in the ratio 5 : 3 : 2.

Step one: add the parts. 5 plus 3 plus 2 gives 10 parts. Step two: find the value of ONE part. R2 400 divided by 10 equals R240 per part. Step three: multiply each person's parts by that value. Lerato gets 5 times R240, which is R1 200; Sipho gets 3 times R240, which is R720; Mpho gets 2 times R240, which is R480. Step four — never skip it — check the total: R1 200 plus R720 plus R480 equals R2 400. The shares reconstruct the whole, so the work is sound.

The same three steps do building work. A concrete mix is specified as cement to sand to stone in the ratio 1 : 3 : 5, and the job needs 27 wheelbarrows of mix. Parts total 1 plus 3 plus 5, which is 9. One part is 27 divided by 9, which is 3 wheelbarrows. So the mix is 3 wheelbarrows of cement, 9 of sand and 15 of stone — and 3 plus 9 plus 15 confirms 27.

The first set of questions for this session is with you now. Watch for the two things that cost marks: the order of the ratio, and converting to a common unit before simplifying.

## Subtopic: Rate — A Comparison That Carries Its Units

A RATE compares quantities of DIFFERENT kinds, and unlike a ratio it keeps its units. Rands per kilogram, kilometres per litre, cents per kilowatt-hour, litres per minute, rands per hour — the word "per" is the signal, and "per" always means "for every one".

A UNIT RATE is a rate expressed for exactly one of the second quantity, and it is obtained by dividing.

Worked example one, fuel consumption. A bakkie travels 480 km on 40 litres of petrol. Consumption equals distance divided by fuel: 480 divided by 40 gives 12 km per litre. Now the rate does real work — the driver plans a 300 km trip, so fuel needed is 300 divided by 12, which is 25 litres; at R23,40 per litre the trip costs 25 times R23,40, which is R585,00.

Worked example two, speed, and the trap hidden inside it. A taxi covers 63 km in 45 minutes. Speed equals distance divided by time — but the answer is wanted in kilometres per HOUR, so the time must be in hours first. 45 minutes is 45 over 60 of an hour, which is 0,75 of an hour. Speed equals 63 divided by 0,75, which is 84 km per hour. A candidate who divides 63 by 45 gets 1,4 and writes "1,4 km/h", which is a taxi being overtaken by a walking pensioner. Units first, then arithmetic.

Worked example three, a tariff rate. A municipality charges R14,50 per kilolitre of water, so a household using 18 kℓ pays 18 times R14,50, which is R261,00. The rate is the price of one unit; the cost is the rate times the number of units — the engine of every tariff question in the subject.

Take on this section's questions now, and check every answer twice: once for the number, and once for the unit attached to it — an unlabelled rate is an incomplete answer.

## Subtopic: Direct and Indirect Proportion

Two quantities are in DIRECT PROPORTION when multiplying one multiplies the other by the same factor — double the loaves, double the cost. The test is that dividing one by the other always gives the SAME constant.

A bakery sells 6 loaves for R114,00. Divide to get the constant: 114 divided by 6 equals R19,00 per loaf — that is the unit rate again, and it is the constant of proportionality. Eleven loaves therefore cost 11 times R19,00, which is R209,00. This is the UNITARY METHOD: go down to one, then up to the number you want.

Two quantities are in INDIRECT PROPORTION — also called inverse proportion — when multiplying one DIVIDES the other by the same factor. More of this means less of that, and the test is that MULTIPLYING the pair always gives the same constant.

Four painters take 15 days to paint a school block. Multiply the pair: 4 times 15 equals 60 painter-days — that is the fixed size of the job. With 6 painters, the days needed are 60 divided by 6, which is 10 days. Notice the shape of the answer: the painters went up by a factor of one and a half, and the days came down by a factor of one and a half.

A second inverse case, straight out of a school trip. A bus is hired for a fixed R900. Shared among 15 learners, each pays 900 divided by 15, which is R60,00. Shared among 20, each pays 900 divided by 20, which is R45,00. Multiply each pair: 15 times 60 is 900, and 20 times 45 is 900 — the constant confirms the relationship is inverse.

Deciding which one you are looking at is worth marks on its own. Ask what happens to the second quantity when the first increases. Up together — divide to find the constant. One up while the other goes down — multiply to find the constant.

This section's questions are ready for you — identify the type of proportion first, in words, before you touch the calculator.

## Subtopic: Best Buy — Making the Comparison Fair

Two bags of maize meal sit on the shelf. A 2,5 kg bag costs R42,99. A 10 kg bag costs R159,99. The big bag has the bigger price tag, and the small bag has the smaller one — and neither of those facts answers the question, because the bags hold different amounts. The only honest comparison is a UNIT RATE: the price of ONE kilogram in each case.

Small bag: R42,99 divided by 2,5 kg. That is R17,196 per kilogram, which rounds to R17,20 per kg. Big bag: R159,99 divided by 10 kg, which is R15,999 per kilogram, or R16,00 per kg. The 10 kg bag costs about R1,20 less per kilogram, so it is the better buy — and buying 10 kg in the big bag rather than four small bags saves roughly R12,00.

Now the exam discipline that turns a correct calculation into full marks. Show the SUBSTITUTION — write R42,99 divided by 2,5 kg on the page, not just the answer, because the method carries marks even when the arithmetic slips. Keep the UNITS on every line, because "17,20" is a number while "R17,20 per kg" is an answer. Round only at the END; if you round R17,196 to R17 before comparing, you throw away the very cents the comparison depends on. And answer the question that was ASKED, in a sentence: "The 10 kg bag is the better buy at R16,00 per kg compared with R17,20 per kg."

Then add the judgement that Mathematical Literacy always wants alongside the number. Better value per kilogram is not automatically the better decision: a pensioner with R50,00 in her purse cannot buy the 10 kg bag at any price per kilogram, and a family without dry storage may watch the big bag spoil. Cheaper per unit assumes you can afford the outlay and use the goods before they go off. State the mathematics, then state the condition — that pairing is exactly what the examiner rewards.

The final questions of this part are with you now: compare per unit, keep the units on the page, and finish with a sentence that names the better buy and says why.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same three ideas from the kitchen table — dividing food fairly, knowing the price of one, and working out whether more hands means more days or fewer.

## Subtopic: Cutting the Loaf Into Shares

Picture a loaf of bread and three cousins who did not do equal work. One swept, one washed, one just carried a bucket once. Nobody wants equal slices; they want fair slices. So they agree: five slices' worth to the first, three to the second, two to the third.

Count the slices you would need altogether: five plus three plus two is ten. Now cut the loaf into ten equal slices — and the trick of the whole topic is right there. Whatever you are sharing, cut it into the TOTAL number of parts first, then hand out the parts.

Money works the same way. The car wash made R2 400 that Saturday. Ten parts, so one part is R2 400 shared into ten equal piles — R240 a pile. Now deal them out: five piles is R1 200, three piles is R720, two piles is R480. Add the three amounts back up and you should land exactly on R2 400 — R1 200 plus R720 is R1 920, plus R480 is R2 400. That check takes five seconds and it has saved more marks than any other habit in this topic.

One warning from the kitchen: you cannot compare a cup to a bucket until they sit in the same container. If a recipe says 250 grams of butter to one kilogram of flour, do not write 250 to 1. Turn the kilogram into 1 000 grams first, and the comparison reads 250 to 1 000 — one to four, one part butter for every four parts flour.

That way of comparing — same kind of thing, same unit, written with a colon — is what your paper calls a RATIO. Small questions on it are with you right now, so start each one by adding the parts.

## Subtopic: What One Costs, What One Does

Now the second idea, and it is the one your granny uses in every shop without ever calling it mathematics. She wants to know: what does ONE cost?

That little word "per" means "for every one". Rands per kilogram. Kilometres per litre. Rands per hour. And you get to "one" by dividing.

The bakkie went 480 kilometres and drank 40 litres. Cut the kilometres into 40 equal shares — one for each litre — and each litre carried the bakkie 12 kilometres. Now you know the bakkie. Planning 300 kilometres? That is 300 divided by 12, which is 25 litres in the tank. At R23,40 a litre, that is 25 lots of R23,40 — R585,00 for the trip. You just budgeted a journey with one division and one multiplication.

Careful with time, because minutes love to trip people. A taxi does 63 kilometres in 45 minutes, and someone asks for the speed in kilometres per HOUR. You cannot divide by 45, because 45 is minutes and the answer must be per hour. Forty-five minutes is three-quarters of an hour — 0,75. So 63 divided by 0,75 gives 84 kilometres per hour. Sensible for a taxi. Whenever the answer says "per hour", get your time into hours before you press divide.

And the shop version: water at R14,50 per kilolitre means every single kilolitre costs R14,50, so 18 of them cost 18 lots of R14,50 — R261,00. Price of one, times how many. That is the whole rule.

Those "per" numbers are RATES, and here come your questions on them. Keep the words attached — rands per kilogram, kilometres per hour — because a naked number is only half an answer.

## Subtopic: More Hands, Fewer Days — and Finding the Real Price

Two shapes of relationship, and everyday life shows you both before breakfast.

The first shape: more bread, more money. Six loaves cost R114,00, so one loaf costs R114 shared into six — R19,00. Eleven loaves then cost eleven lots of R19,00, which is R209,00. Up together, in step. Go down to one first, then up to what you need — that path never fails, and your paper calls this DIRECT proportion.

The second shape is the opposite, and it feels wrong until you picture it. Four painters take 15 days to finish a school block. Bring in more painters and the job takes FEWER days. So how many days for six painters? Ask how big the job really is: 4 painters for 15 days is 60 painter-days of work. Six painters chew through it in 60 divided by 6 — 10 days. More hands, fewer days: that is INDIRECT proportion, and you find its constant by MULTIPLYING the pair instead of dividing.

You have lived the money version. A bus costs R900 whether it is full or half-empty. Fifteen learners going means R900 split fifteen ways — R60 each. Twenty going means R45 each. More people, smaller share. Every stokvel, every taxi, every group gift works on this rule.

Last piece: the shelf. A 2,5 kg bag of maize meal is R42,99 and a 10 kg bag is R159,99, and your eyes are useless here because the bags are different sizes. Do what granny does — get both down to one kilogram. R42,99 shared over 2,5 kilograms is about R17,20 a kilogram. R159,99 shared over 10 kilograms is R16,00 a kilogram. The big bag wins by about R1,20 every kilogram, which is around R12 saved on ten kilograms.

Then the grown-up part, where you sound like someone who actually shops. Cheaper per kilogram is only better if you HAVE the R159,99 today, and if the meal will be eaten before the weevils find it. Say the number, then say the condition, in a proper sentence — that answer is complete, and complete answers are what get ticked.

Your last questions are here now. Price of one, then compare; and remember to round only at the very end, because the cents you throw away early are exactly the cents the answer turns on.
