**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! First one, and half of us got it wrong in the test: when a question says share R2 400 in the ratio 5 : 3 : 2, why can't we just divide R2 400 by 5, then by 3, then by 2? That feels like sharing.

**Tutor:** It feels like sharing, but it shares the money three separate times instead of once. Dividing R2 400 by 5 gives R480, by 3 gives R800, and by 2 gives R1 200 — add those and you get R2 480, which is more money than the friends ever earned. The ratio is not telling you how many people share each amount; it is telling you how many equal PARTS each person takes out of one pile. So the pile must be cut into parts first, and the number of parts is the sum: 5 plus 3 plus 2 gives 10. One part is R2 400 divided by 10, which is R240. Now hand them out — 5 parts is R1 200, 3 parts is R720, 2 parts is R480 — and those add back to exactly R2 400, because you cut the pile once and distributed all of it. Hold that picture: the colon numbers are slices, and the total number of slices is what you divide by. And always add your three answers at the end. If they do not rebuild the original amount, something went wrong, and you still have time to find it.

**Thandi:** Next: I keep mixing up ratio and rate. They both compare two things and they both involve dividing. What is the actual difference, and does it matter in the exam?

**Tutor:** It matters, and the difference is one word: units. A ratio compares quantities of the SAME kind — rands to rands, kilograms to kilograms, learners to learners — so when you divide, the units cancel and the answer carries no unit at all. That is why we write it with a colon, like 5 : 3 : 2, with nothing attached. A rate compares quantities of DIFFERENT kinds — rands against kilograms, kilometres against litres, rands against kilolitres — so the units cannot cancel, and the answer must carry them both. That is why we say R16,00 per kilogram, or 12 kilometres per litre, or R14,50 per kilolitre. The word "per" is the giveaway, and it always means "for every one". The practical consequence is this: before you simplify a ratio, both quantities must be in the SAME unit, so 250 g to 1 kg becomes 250 g to 1 000 g, which is 1 : 4. But a rate never needs that conversion, because the two quantities are supposed to be different. In an exam, a ratio answer with a unit stuck on it is wrong, and a rate answer with the units stripped off is incomplete. Same arithmetic, opposite finishing move.

**Thandi:** This one caused an argument: four painters take 15 days, so six painters take 10 days. Somebody said that is nonsense, because if you keep adding painters you eventually get a job done in zero days. Is the mathematics actually wrong?

**Tutor:** The mathematics is right and the objection is right, and holding both at once is exactly what Mathematical Literacy asks of you. The calculation says the job has a fixed size — 4 painters times 15 days gives 60 painter-days of work — so 6 painters clear it in 60 divided by 6, which is 10 days, and 60 painters would clear it in one day. But inverse proportion assumes every extra worker is as productive as the first, and that assumption breaks in the real world: sixty painters cannot all reach the same wall, they queue for ladders and paint, someone must supervise them, and the job stops getting faster. The model is a good description over a sensible range and a bad one at the extremes. So in an exam you do the calculation exactly as the inverse-proportion rule requires, and then, if the question asks you to comment, you say plainly that the relationship holds only while there is enough space and equipment for each worker to be fully productive. Markers reward that comment. Never let the criticism stop you from doing the calculation, and never let the calculation stop you from making the criticism.

**Thandi:** Last one, from someone who feels cheated: she calculated the 10 kg bag as the better buy, but her mother bought the small bag anyway. Was her mathematics wasted?

**Tutor:** Not wasted at all — it just answered a different question from the one her mother was solving. The mathematics answers "which bag gives more meal per rand?", and the answer is firm: R42,99 over 2,5 kilograms is about R17,20 a kilogram, R159,99 over 10 kilograms is R16,00 a kilogram, so the big bag wins by roughly R1,20 a kilogram. Her mother was solving "what can I buy today with the money in my purse, and what will still be good by the time we eat it?" A better price per kilogram is worthless if the R159,99 is not there this month, and a bulk bag that draws damp or weevils before it is finished is not cheaper — it is more expensive, because you paid for meal you threw away. This is the heart of the subject: the number is necessary but not sufficient. Write the unit rates, state which is cheaper per kilogram, and then add one honest sentence about affordability, storage or spoilage. That sentence is not softness — it is a mark, and it is also what makes the mathematics genuinely useful at home.
