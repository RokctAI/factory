# Part 1 — Expert

This session identifies the participants in the circular flow of a closed economy and explains the role that each plays. The anchor is a short list: in a closed economy there are three participants, households, businesses and the government; an open economy adds a fourth, the foreign sector. The governing principle is that every participant is both a buyer and a seller, and that the spending of one participant is always the income of another. That is why the flow of income in an economy is circular.

## Subtopic: Open and Closed Economies and the Three Participants

An economy can be studied as closed or open. A closed economy is one that does not trade with other countries: there are no imports, no exports and no flows of money across the border. No real country is completely closed, but the model of a closed economy is a useful simplification, because it lets us see clearly how the domestic participants depend on each other before adding the complications of foreign trade. An open economy is one that trades with the rest of the world, and South Africa, which exports minerals, vehicles and fruit and imports oil, machinery and electronics, is an open economy. In Grade 9 the circular flow is drawn for a closed economy; the foreign sector is added in Grade 10.

The participants, also called economic agents or sectors, are the groups whose decisions together make up the economy. In a closed economy there are three. Households are the consumers and the owners of the factors of production. Businesses, which economists also call firms, are the producers of goods and services. The government is the public sector, including national, provincial and local government, which collects taxes and provides public goods and services. Some textbooks also show financial institutions, such as banks, separately, because they link savers and borrowers; in the Grade 9 model they are treated as part of the business sector and the financial market.

In a simple two-sector model, only households and businesses take part. Households supply factors of production to businesses and receive income in the form of rent, wages, interest and profit. Households then spend that income buying goods and services from businesses. Adding the government makes a three-sector model, which is the model of the closed economy used in this topic.

Each participant has two roles. Households are sellers in the factor market and buyers in the goods market. Businesses are buyers in the factor market and sellers in the goods market. The government is a buyer in both markets and also collects taxes and pays out grants. Understanding these dual roles is the key to drawing and explaining the circular flow diagram in the next lesson.

## Subtopic: Households: Owners of the Factors and Consumers

A household is a person or a group of people living together who make joint decisions about income and spending. A family living in a house, a student living alone in a flat and three friends sharing a flat are all households.

Households have two main economic roles. First, they own the factors of production. Every factor ultimately belongs to a household: a person's labour, land and buildings owned by families, savings deposited in banks, and the entrepreneurial ability of individuals. Even large companies are owned by households through shares, held directly or through pension funds. Households supply these factors to businesses and the government through the factor market and in return receive income: wages and salaries for labour, rent for natural resources, interest for capital and profit for entrepreneurship.

Second, households are consumers. They use their income to buy goods and services from businesses in the goods and services market: food, clothing, transport, electricity, airtime, school uniforms and entertainment. This spending is called consumption, and it is the largest single part of total spending in most economies, including South Africa's, where household consumption makes up roughly 60 percent of total spending.

Households also have a relationship with the government. They pay taxes: income tax on wages and salaries, value-added tax, VAT, on most goods and services they buy, at the standard rate of 15 percent in South Africa, and other taxes such as fuel levies. In return they receive public goods and services such as roads, schools, clinics and policing, and many households receive transfer payments, such as the old age grant and the child support grant. A transfer payment is a payment by the government for which no good, service or factor is given in return.

Finally, households do not spend all of their income. The part that is not spent on consumption or paid in tax is saved, usually through banks, insurance policies or pension funds. Those savings flow through the financial market to businesses and the government that want to borrow.

## Subtopic: Businesses: Producers and Employers

A business, or firm, is an organisation that combines factors of production to produce goods and services, usually in order to make a profit. Businesses range from a hawker selling fruit at a taxi rank to large corporations such as Sasol, Vodacom or Pick n Pay.

Businesses also have two main roles. First, they are producers and sellers. They produce goods and services and sell them to households, to other businesses and to the government in the goods and services market. The money they receive from sales is their revenue. Second, they are buyers in the factor market. To produce, they must hire labour, rent premises, borrow capital and rely on entrepreneurial skill, all of which they obtain from households. The payments they make, wages, rent, interest and profit, become household income.

Businesses also buy capital goods, such as machines, buildings and vehicles, from other businesses. Spending on new capital goods is called investment. Investment matters greatly for the economy, because new machines and factories increase the economy's capacity to produce in future and create jobs. Businesses often borrow from banks to finance investment, which links them to the financial market.

Businesses interact with the government in both directions. They pay taxes, including company income tax, which in South Africa is charged at 27 percent of taxable income, and they collect VAT from customers on behalf of SARS. They must follow government rules, such as labour laws, health and safety regulations and the national minimum wage. In return, they benefit from roads, ports, electricity, a legal system and an educated workforce, and some businesses sell goods and services to the government, for example a construction company building a school or a stationery supplier delivering textbooks.

Economists usually group businesses into the primary, secondary and tertiary sectors, which you will study in Term 2. In the circular flow, though, all businesses are treated as one participant.

## Subtopic: Government: Taxes, Spending and Services

The government is the public sector. In South Africa it has three spheres: national government, provincial government in the nine provinces, and local government in the municipalities. State-owned enterprises such as Eskom are part of the wider public sector.

The government plays several roles in the circular flow. First, it collects taxes from households and businesses. The South African Revenue Service, SARS, collects personal income tax, company income tax and VAT, which are the three largest sources of revenue, as well as fuel levies and customs duties. Municipalities collect property rates and charges for water and electricity.

Second, the government buys goods and services from businesses in the goods and services market: textbooks, medicines, police vehicles, computers and construction work on roads, schools and hospitals.

Third, the government buys factors of production in the factor market. It is one of the largest employers in South Africa, employing teachers, nurses, police officers, soldiers and public servants, and paying them salaries. These salaries are income for households.

Fourth, the government provides public goods and services, such as roads, street lighting, defence, policing, public schools and public hospitals, usually free or at low cost.

Fifth, the government makes transfer payments to households, mainly social grants such as the old age grant, the child support grant and the disability grant, and in some cases subsidies to businesses. Social grants reach around 19 million South Africans, apart from temporary relief grants, which makes them one of the government's largest spending items.

Finally, the government regulates the economy by passing laws, setting the minimum wage, protecting consumers and promoting competition.

A simple calculation shows how these flows balance for a household. Suppose a household earns a monthly salary of R18 000 from a provincial department. It pays R2 000 in income tax, leaving R16 000. It spends R14 500 on goods and services, from groceries to taxi fares, and saves the remaining R1 500 in a bank account. In figures: 2 000 + 14 500 + 1 500 = 18 000, so every rand of income is either taxed, spent or saved. The R14 500 becomes revenue for businesses, the R2 000 becomes government revenue, and the R1 500 becomes funds that banks can lend. Nothing disappears; it simply moves to another participant.

Notice how the government both takes money out of the flow and puts money back in. Taxes are withdrawn from households and businesses; government spending on goods, services, salaries and grants returns money to them. If the government spends more than it collects in taxes, it has a budget deficit and must borrow the difference through the financial market.

The error museum for this lesson holds four mistakes. First, that a closed economy has no government; a closed economy has no foreign trade, but it does have a government. Second, that only businesses pay taxes; households pay income tax and VAT too. Third, that social grants are payments for goods or services; they are transfer payments with nothing given in return. Fourth, that households only buy; they are also the sellers of every factor of production.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same ideas again, through the travels of one coin, three players and some short stories, in plain words with the same facts.

## Subtopic: A Day in the Life of a Rand

Follow one rand coin through a day.

In the morning the coin is in the till of a factory that makes school shoes. At the end of the month the factory pays its workers, and the coin goes into the salary of a machine operator. The factory has bought labour from a household. Now the coin is household income.

At lunch time the machine operator buys a loaf of bread at the corner shop. The coin moves from the household to a business. The household has bought a good. Now the coin is business revenue.

The shop owner pays VAT on the sale to SARS. A few cents of our coin move from the business to the government. That is a tax.

The government uses its tax money to pay a teacher's salary and to pay a grandmother her old age grant. Our coin moves from the government back to a household.

The grandmother uses the grant to buy school shoes for her grandchild, made by the very factory where our coin started. The coin is back in the factory's till.

See what happened? The coin went round in a circle. Every time somebody spent it, it became somebody else's income. That is why we call it the circular flow. In this lesson we keep the story inside one country, with no buying from or selling to other countries. That is called a closed economy, and it has three players: households, businesses and the government.

## Subtopic: Three Players, Three Jobs

Here are the three players and their jobs.

Households are families and people living together. They have two jobs. They own the things needed to make goods: their work, their land, their savings and their business ideas. They sell or rent these to businesses and the government, and they get paid: wages, rent, interest and profit. Then they spend that money buying goods and services. So households sell in one market and buy in another.

Businesses are the makers and sellers: the shoe factory, the corner shop, the taxi owner, the bank. They also have two jobs. They make and sell goods and services. And they buy what they need to make them: they hire workers, rent buildings and borrow money. So businesses buy in one market and sell in another, the opposite of households.

The government is the third player. It collects taxes from households and businesses. It spends that money on teachers' and nurses' salaries, on building roads and schools, and on buying things like textbooks and police cars. It gives grants to people who need help, like pensioners and children. And it makes the rules, like the minimum wage.

A grant is special. When the government pays a teacher, the teacher gives work in return. When the government pays a grant, nothing is given back. That kind of payment is called a transfer payment.

Here is one way to picture them. Draw three boxes on a page: households on the left, businesses on the right and government at the top. Every payment in the economy is an arrow from one box to another. Wages go from the business box to the household box. Shopping goes from the household box to the business box. Taxes go up into the government box from both sides. Salaries, purchases and grants come back down from the government box. When you can draw every arrow, you understand the economy.

Three players. Households own and buy. Businesses make and sell. Government taxes, spends and makes rules.

## Subtopic: Spotting the Participant in a Story

Let's practise. In each little story, spot who is paying, who is receiving, and what for.

A nurse at a provincial hospital receives her monthly salary. The government is paying a household, for labour. That is the government buying in the factor market.

A family buys groceries at a supermarket. A household is paying a business, for goods. That is the goods and services market.

A construction company is paid to build a new clinic. The government is paying a business, for a service. The government is buying in the goods and services market.

A pensioner receives the old age grant. The government is paying a household, for nothing in return. That is a transfer payment.

A shoe factory pays company tax. A business is paying the government. That is a tax.

A taxi owner pays the bank interest on the loan for his minibus. A business is paying a financial institution, and the interest ends up with the households who saved.

Here is the trick that makes every story easy. Ask three questions. Who pays? Who receives? What do they get in return: a good, a service, a factor, or nothing? Answer those three, and you can place any payment in the circular flow. If the answer to the third question is nothing, you have found a transfer payment. If it is work, land, savings or a business idea, you are in the factor market. If it is a good or a service, you are in the goods and services market.
