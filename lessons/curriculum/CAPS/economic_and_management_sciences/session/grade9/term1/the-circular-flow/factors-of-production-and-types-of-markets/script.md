# Part 1 — Expert

This session sets out the building blocks of the circular flow: the factors of production that every economy uses to produce goods and services, the reward that each factor earns, and the markets in which goods, services, factors and money are exchanged. The anchor is a four-row table, natural resources earn rent, labour earns wages and salaries, capital earns interest, and entrepreneurship earns profit, and a three-part classification of markets: goods and services markets, factor markets and financial markets. The governing principle is that every rand of income in an economy is a reward paid to the owner of a factor of production.

## Subtopic: The Four Factors of Production

Factors of production are the resources used to produce goods and services. Economists group them into four categories.

Natural resources, often called land, include everything provided by nature that is used in production: the land itself, minerals such as gold, platinum and coal, water, forests, fish in the sea, sunlight and wind. A farm in the Free State, the platinum under the ground in Rustenburg and the wind that turns turbines near Jeffreys Bay are all natural resources. Natural resources are limited in quantity and some, such as minerals, are non-renewable: once mined, they are gone.

Labour is the human effort, physical and mental, used in production. A bricklayer, a nurse, a teacher, a software developer and a mine worker all supply labour. The quality of labour depends on education, training, skills and health, which economists call human capital. A country with a well-educated, healthy workforce can produce more from the same number of workers.

Capital refers to man-made resources that are used to produce other goods and services. Examples are machinery, tools, factory buildings, vehicles used for business, computers and infrastructure such as roads, ports and power stations. A taxi owned by a taxi operator is capital, but a car used only for family trips is a consumer good. Note carefully that in economics capital does not mean money; money is used to buy capital goods, but the money itself does not produce anything. In accounting, by contrast, capital means the owner's investment in the business, which is a different idea that you will meet in the financial literacy topics.

Entrepreneurship is the factor that brings the other three together. An entrepreneur identifies an opportunity, organises natural resources, labour and capital to produce a good or service, makes the key decisions and, crucially, takes the risk that the business may fail and the money invested be lost. Without entrepreneurship, the other factors would lie idle. The founder of a car-wash business who rents a site, buys pressure washers and employs three people is acting as an entrepreneur.

Because all four factors are scarce, every economy faces the basic economic problem. The combination of factors used to produce a good is the answer to the question how to produce. Producing more with fewer factors is called productivity, and rising productivity is the main source of rising living standards over time.

## Subtopic: Rewards for the Factors of Production

Each factor of production earns a reward, a payment for its use, and these rewards are the incomes of households. Natural resources earn rent. When a farmer leases land from its owner, or a business rents a shop, the payment is rent. Labour earns wages or salaries. Wages are usually paid per hour, day or week to workers whose hours vary; salaries are usually a fixed monthly amount. Capital earns interest. When households save money in a bank, the bank lends it to businesses to buy capital goods, and the savers receive interest; businesses pay interest on money borrowed to buy machines and buildings. Entrepreneurship earns profit, which is what is left over after all the other factors have been paid. Profit is the reward for taking risk, and it can be negative: a business that makes a loss gives its owner a negative reward.

The relationship can be summarised as: natural resources and rent, labour and wages or salaries, capital and interest, entrepreneurship and profit. These four rewards together make up the income of the economy.

Consider a simple example. A small bakery has sales of R50 000 in a month. It pays rent of R8 000 for its premises, wages of R22 000 to its bakers, interest of R2 000 on a loan used to buy its ovens, and R12 000 for flour, oil and other ingredients bought from other businesses. The profit is the amount left after all these costs: 50 000 - 8 000 - 22 000 - 2 000 - 12 000 = 6 000, so the owner earns a profit of R6 000 as the reward for entrepreneurship. Notice that the payment for ingredients is not a factor reward; it is a payment to other businesses, which in turn use it to pay their own rent, wages, interest and profit.

Who owns the factors? In a market or mixed economy, households own the factors of production. Even when a company owns machines, the company itself is owned by households through shares. This is why, in the circular flow, households supply factors of production and businesses pay rewards to households. Government also earns income, mainly through taxes, which you will see in the next lessons.

## Subtopic: What a Market Is and the Main Types of Markets

A market is any arrangement that brings buyers and sellers together so that they can exchange goods, services, factors of production or money at a price. A market does not have to be a physical place. A street vendor's stall in Durban's Warwick Junction is a market; so is an online shopping website, a WhatsApp group where people sell second-hand goods, or a telephone call between a mine and a buyer in China. What makes it a market is the interaction of buyers and sellers, which determines the price and quantity traded.

Economists classify markets according to what is traded. There are three main types.

The goods and services market, also called the product market or consumer goods market, is where final goods and services are bought and sold. Households buy food, clothing, airtime, taxi rides and haircuts from businesses. This market includes markets for consumer goods, which are used up by households, and markets for capital goods, such as machinery bought by businesses. In the goods market, businesses are the sellers and households are mainly the buyers.

The factor market, also called the factor of production market, is where the factors of production are bought and sold, or hired. The labour market is the most important part: households sell their labour to businesses and the government in exchange for wages and salaries. There are also markets for natural resources, such as land and mineral rights, and for capital goods. In the factor market, households are the sellers and businesses are the buyers, the reverse of the goods market.

The financial market is where money is borrowed and lent, and where financial assets such as shares and bonds are traded. Households save, and through banks and other financial institutions their savings are channelled to businesses and government that want to borrow. The financial market is usually divided into the money market, for short-term borrowing and lending of up to a year, and the capital market, for long-term borrowing and lending, such as home loans, bonds and shares. The Johannesburg Stock Exchange, the JSE, is South Africa's main market for shares. The foreign exchange market, where currencies such as the rand and the dollar are traded, is also a financial market.

## Subtopic: Markets by Place, Reach and Time

Markets can also be described by where they happen, how far they reach and how they operate.

By place, a market can be physical or virtual. A physical market has a location where buyers and sellers meet, such as the Johannesburg Fresh Produce Market, a flea market or a shopping mall. A virtual market operates through technology, without buyers and sellers meeting in person: online shopping, app-based ride-hailing, online job advertisements and internet banking are examples. Many businesses now operate in both.

By reach, a market can be local, national or international. A local market serves a small area, such as a spaza shop serving one street or a hairdresser serving one suburb. A national market serves the whole country, such as a supermarket chain with stores in every province. An international or global market crosses borders, such as the market for platinum, crude oil or smartphones. The global economy studied earlier in this term consists of international markets.

Markets can also be classified as formal or informal. In the formal market, businesses are registered, pay tax and follow labour laws. In the informal market, businesses such as many street vendors, spaza shops and home-based traders are not registered and do not pay tax on their income. South Africa's informal sector provides work for millions of people and is an important part of many communities.

Finally, markets are distinguished by the number of sellers and the degree of competition. A market with many sellers of similar products, such as fruit vendors outside a taxi rank, is highly competitive. A market with only one seller, such as the supply of electricity in many areas, is a monopoly. These market structures are studied in detail in Grade 11 Economics.

The error museum holds four mistakes. First, that capital means money; in economics capital means man-made goods used in production, such as machines. Second, that profit is the reward for labour; profit is the reward for entrepreneurship, and labour earns wages and salaries. Third, that a market must be a physical place; online and telephone markets are markets too. Fourth, that households are buyers in the factor market; households are the sellers of factors and businesses are the buyers.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same ideas again, through a batch of vetkoek, a pay day and a walk around town, in plain words with the same facts.

## Subtopic: Making a Batch of Vetkoek

Imagine you want to start selling vetkoek outside the school gate. What do you need?

You need a place to stand and fry, and you need things that come from nature: wheat for the flour, sunflowers for the oil, water for the dough. That is the first factor, natural resources, sometimes called land.

You need people to knead the dough, fry the vetkoek and serve the customers. That might be you and your cousin. That is the second factor, labour.

You need a pot, a gas burner, a table and a cooler box. These are things people have made that help you make something else. That is the third factor, capital. Careful here: in economics, capital is the pot and the burner, not the money in your pocket. The money just buys the pot.

And you need someone who has the idea, puts it all together, decides how many to make and what to charge, and takes the risk. If nobody buys, the money spent on flour is lost. That person is the entrepreneur, and that is the fourth factor, entrepreneurship.

Here is a quick test for any item. Ask: did nature make it, or did people make it? If nature made it, like water, soil, sunlight or gold, it is a natural resource. If people made it so that it can help make something else, like a pot, a sewing machine or a delivery bakkie, it is capital. Is it a person's work? Then it is labour. Is it the idea, the organising and the risk? Then it is entrepreneurship.

The same thing can change box depending on how it is used. A car that takes your family to church on Sunday is not capital; it is something your family enjoys. The same model of car used as a metered taxi, earning money every day, is capital, because it is being used to produce a service. So always ask what the thing is being used for.

Every business in the world, from a vetkoek stand to a car factory, uses these same four factors. A mine uses the platinum in the ground, the miners, the drilling machines and the people who started and run the company. Only the size is different.

## Subtopic: Who Gets Paid What

Each factor gets its own kind of pay. Here is an easy way to remember it.

The person who owns the land or the building gets rent. If you set up your stall on someone's property, you pay them rent.

The workers get wages or salaries. Your cousin who fries with you gets a wage.

The person who lends money so you can buy the burner gets interest. If your aunt lends you R500 and you pay back R550, the R50 extra is interest.

And the entrepreneur gets whatever is left: profit. Profit is the reward for taking the risk. It can be big, small or even a loss.

Here is a bakery. It sells bread for R50 000 in a month. It pays R8 000 rent, R22 000 wages, R2 000 interest on its oven loan, and R12 000 for flour and other ingredients. Take everything away from 50 000: 50 000 - 8 000 - 22 000 - 2 000 - 12 000 = 6 000. So the owner's profit is R6 000.

Four factors, four rewards: land gets rent, labour gets wages, capital gets interest, the entrepreneur gets profit. Say it like a chant: rent, wages, interest, profit.

## Subtopic: Where Things Are Bought and Sold

A market is anywhere buyers and sellers meet to swap things for money. It does not need a roof or stalls. If you sell your old sneakers on a WhatsApp group, that is a market.

There are three big kinds. When your family buys bread, airtime and taxi rides, they are in the goods and services market. Businesses sell, families buy.

When your mother goes to work and gets paid a salary, she is selling her labour. That is the factor market. Now families sell and businesses buy. It is the other way round.

When your family saves money in the bank, or borrows to buy a house, that is the financial market. Short-term borrowing, up to a year, is the money market. Long-term borrowing, like a home loan, and buying shares on the JSE, is the capital market.

Markets also come in sizes. The spaza shop on your street is a local market. A supermarket with stores in every province is a national market. Platinum sold to Japan is an international market.

And some markets you can walk into, like the flea market or a mall. Others live on your phone, like online shopping. Those are virtual markets.

Here is a way to check which market you are looking at. Ask what is being swapped for the money. If it is bread, a haircut or a taxi ride, it is the goods and services market. If it is someone's work, the use of land or a building, it is the factor market. If it is money itself, being saved, lent or borrowed, it is the financial market.

So: goods market, families buy. Factor market, families sell. Financial market, saving and borrowing.
