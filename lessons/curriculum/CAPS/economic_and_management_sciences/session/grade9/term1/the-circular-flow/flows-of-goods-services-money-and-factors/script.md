# Part 1 — Expert

This session draws and explains the circular flow diagram of a closed economy: the flows of goods and services, money and factors of production between households, businesses and the government, through the goods and services market and the factor market. The anchor is a rule about direction: real flows of goods, services and factors move in one direction, and money flows move in exactly the opposite direction. The governing principle is that total spending in the economy equals total income, because every rand spent by one participant is received as income by another.

## Subtopic: Real Flows and Money Flows

The circular flow model shows two kinds of flows between participants. Real flows, sometimes called physical flows, are flows of actual goods, services and factors of production. When a worker gives eight hours of labour to a business, that labour is a real flow from the household to the business. When the business sells a pair of jeans to a household, the jeans are a real flow from the business to the household. When a teacher works for the provincial education department, labour flows from the household to the government; when a public clinic treats a patient, a service flows from the government to the household.

Money flows, also called monetary flows or nominal flows, are the payments made in exchange for the real flows. When labour flows from a household to a business, wages flow from the business to the household. When jeans flow from the business to the household, the price of the jeans flows from the household to the business. Taxes and grants are also money flows, although they have no matching real flow in the opposite direction.

The essential rule is that for every exchange in a market, the real flow and the money flow move in opposite directions. Factors of production flow from households to businesses, while factor income, rent, wages, interest and profit, flows from businesses to households. Goods and services flow from businesses to households, while consumption spending flows from households to businesses. If you draw both flows on a diagram, the real flows go round in one direction and the money flows go round in the other.

The money flows are the ones economists measure. Real flows are made up of countless different things, hours of labour, loaves of bread, haircuts, tonnes of coal, that cannot simply be added together. Money gives them a common unit: the rand. When economists calculate the total value of production in South Africa, they measure the money value of the flows, which is the basis of gross domestic product, GDP, studied in Grade 10 and Grade 11.

## Subtopic: Drawing the Two-Sector Circular Flow Diagram

The simplest version of the diagram has two participants, households and businesses, and two markets, the factor market and the goods and services market. Draw it in four steps.

Step one: draw a box for households on the left and a box for businesses on the right. Step two: draw a box for the goods and services market at the top, between them, and a box for the factor market at the bottom, between them. Step three: draw the real flows. In the factor market, an arrow from households to the factor market labelled factors of production, and an arrow from the factor market to businesses with the same label. In the goods market, an arrow from businesses to the goods and services market labelled goods and services, and an arrow from the goods market to households with the same label. Step four: draw the money flows in the opposite direction. In the factor market, an arrow from businesses to the factor market labelled factor payments, and from the factor market to households labelled income: rent, wages, interest and profit. In the goods market, an arrow from households to the goods and services market labelled consumption spending, and from the goods market to businesses labelled revenue.

Read the finished diagram as a story. Households sell factors in the factor market and earn income. They spend that income in the goods market. Businesses receive that spending as revenue and use it to pay for the factors they need, which becomes household income again. The money goes round clockwise or anticlockwise, depending on how the diagram is drawn, and the real flows go the other way.

In this simplest model, if households spend all their income, the amount businesses receive equals the amount they paid out, and the flow continues at the same level. Suppose households receive total income of R1 000 million in a period and spend all of it. Businesses then receive revenue of R1 000 million, which they pay out again as R1 000 million of factor income. Income equals spending equals production, and the flow is steady.

## Subtopic: Adding the Government to the Diagram

The closed economy of the Grade 9 syllabus has three participants, so the government must be added. Draw the government as a third box, usually in the centre between the two markets or below the factor market. Then add its flows.

The government takes part in both markets. In the factor market it buys labour and other factors from households: teachers, nurses, police officers and public servants supply labour, a real flow from households to the factor market to the government, and the government pays salaries, a money flow in the opposite direction. In the goods and services market it buys goods and services from businesses, such as medicines, textbooks and road construction: goods and services flow from businesses through the goods market to the government, and government spending flows in the opposite direction to businesses.

The government also provides public goods and services to households and businesses, such as roads, policing, defence and public schooling. These are real flows from the government to households and businesses, usually without a direct price.

Finally, there are money flows with no market in between. Taxes flow from households and from businesses directly to the government: personal income tax and VAT from households, company tax from businesses. Transfer payments, such as social grants, flow from the government to households, and subsidies may flow from the government to businesses. These flows are drawn as direct arrows between the boxes, because nothing is bought or sold.

It helps to trace a single policy through the three-sector diagram. Suppose the government decides to build ten new clinics. It pays construction businesses, a money flow from the government through the goods and services market to businesses, and receives buildings in return, a real flow in the opposite direction. The construction businesses hire bricklayers, electricians and plumbers, so labour flows from households to businesses through the factor market and wages flow back. The clinics then employ nurses, so labour flows from households to the government and salaries flow back. Patients receive health care, a real flow of public services from the government to households. And the money to pay for it all came from taxes, the direct flows from households and businesses to the government. One decision touches every arrow on the diagram.

A well-labelled three-sector diagram therefore shows: factors of production and factor income in the factor market; goods and services and spending in the goods market; government purchases of factors and goods; taxes from households and businesses to government; and grants and public services from government to households. In an examination, every arrow must have a label and a direction, and real and money flows must be distinguishable, for example by using solid lines for money and broken lines for goods, services and factors, with a key.

## Subtopic: Leakages, Injections and Keeping the Flow Steady

In the simple model all income was spent, but in reality households do not spend all of their income. Some is paid in taxes and some is saved. Money that is taken out of the circular flow of income and spending is called a leakage, or withdrawal. In a closed economy the two leakages are savings and taxes. In an open economy, imports are a third leakage.

Money that is added to the circular flow from outside the household-business loop is called an injection. In a closed economy the two injections are investment, spending by businesses on new capital goods, and government spending. In an open economy, exports are a third injection.

The size of the flow depends on the balance between leakages and injections. Consider a closed economy in which households receive income of R1 000 million. They pay R200 million in taxes, save R100 million and spend the rest on consumption: 1 000 - 200 - 100 = 700, so consumption is R700 million. Leakages are taxes plus savings: 200 + 100 = 300, or R300 million. Now suppose businesses invest R100 million, financed through the banks from household savings, and the government spends R200 million of its tax revenue. Injections are 100 + 200 = 300, or R300 million. Total spending on production is consumption plus investment plus government spending: 700 + 100 + 200 = 1 000, or R1 000 million, exactly equal to income. Because leakages equal injections, the flow stays at the same level.

If injections are greater than leakages, for example if the government spends more or businesses invest more, total spending rises above income, businesses sell more, employ more people, and the flow grows. If leakages are greater than injections, for example if households suddenly save much more or businesses cut investment, spending falls below income, businesses sell less and cut jobs, and the flow shrinks. This is how economists explain recessions and recoveries, a central idea of Grade 11 and Grade 12 Economics.

The error museum holds four mistakes. First, drawing real flows and money flows in the same direction; they are always opposite. Second, showing taxes passing through a market; taxes are direct flows to the government, with no good or service sold. Third, calling savings an injection; savings are a leakage, and investment is the matching injection. Fourth, leaving arrows unlabelled; an arrow without a label earns no marks.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same ideas again, through two rivers, a drawing done one line at a time, and a leaky pipe, in plain words with the same facts.

## Subtopic: Two Rivers Flowing in Opposite Directions

Picture two rivers running around a big circle, side by side, but flowing in opposite directions.

The first river carries things: work, land, goods and services. When your father goes to work at a factory, his work flows from your home to the factory. When the factory makes school shoes and the shop sells you a pair, the shoes flow from the business to your home. That is the river of real things.

The second river carries money, and it flows the other way. Your father's work goes to the factory, so his wages come from the factory to your home. The shoes come to your home, so your money goes from your home to the business.

Every time something real moves one way, money moves the other way to pay for it. Work out, wages in. Shoes in, money out. That is the most important rule of the circular flow: real things and money always move in opposite directions.

There are a few special payments that do not have anything real moving the other way. Taxes go from families and businesses to the government, and nothing is bought with them directly. Grants go from the government to families, and nothing is given back. Those are one-way money arrows.

Why do economists count the money river instead of the real river? Because you cannot add up hours of work, loaves of bread and haircuts. They are different things. But you can add up rand. So the money river tells us how big the economy is.

## Subtopic: Building the Diagram Step by Step

Let's draw the diagram one piece at a time, so it never looks scary.

Step one. Draw a box on the left and write households. Draw a box on the right and write businesses.

Step two. Draw a box at the top between them and write goods and services market. Draw a box at the bottom and write factor market.

Step three. Draw the things river. At the bottom, an arrow from households, through the factor market, to businesses: factors of production. At the top, an arrow from businesses, through the goods market, to households: goods and services.

Step four. Draw the money river the opposite way. At the bottom, from businesses, through the factor market, to households: rent, wages, interest and profit. At the top, from households, through the goods market, to businesses: spending.

Step five. Put the government box in the middle. Draw arrows for taxes going in from households and from businesses. Draw arrows for salaries and grants going out to households, and for spending going out to businesses through the goods market.

Use a solid line for money and a dotted line for real things, and draw a small key in the corner. Label every single arrow. A beautiful arrow with no label gets no marks.

Now read it like a story. Families sell their work and get paid. They spend their pay at businesses. Businesses use the money to pay families again. And the government takes some in taxes and puts it back as salaries, purchases and grants.

## Subtopic: Leaks and Taps in the Pipe

Now think of the money river as water in a pipe going round in a loop. If nothing gets in and nothing gets out, the water just keeps going round at the same speed.

But real pipes have leaks. In a closed economy there are two leaks. Families save some of their money instead of spending it. And families and businesses pay taxes. Saving and taxes take money out of the spending loop. We call them leakages.

The pipe also has taps that add water. Businesses borrow the savings from the bank and spend them on new machines and buildings. That is investment. And the government spends the tax money on salaries, roads and schools. That is government spending. These add money back into the loop. We call them injections.

Here are some numbers. Families earn R1 000 million. They pay R200 million in tax, save R100 million, and spend R700 million. Leaks: 200 + 100 = 300. Now businesses invest R100 million and the government spends R200 million. Taps: 100 + 200 = 300. Leaks equal taps, so the water level stays the same. Total spending is 700 + 100 + 200 = 1 000, the same as income.

If the taps add more than the leaks take out, the flow gets bigger: more sales, more jobs. If the leaks are bigger than the taps, the flow shrinks: fewer sales, job losses. That is a recession.

So remember: savings and taxes leak out; investment and government spending flow in.
