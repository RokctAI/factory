### subtopic_1 — The Four Factors of Production
**Assistant:** Why do economists insist that money is not capital, when businesses talk about raising capital all the time?

**Tutor:** Because economists are asking what actually produces goods and services, and money on its own produces nothing. Picture a pile of banknotes on a table. It cannot dig a hole, bake bread or carry passengers. What it can do is buy things that do produce: a delivery van, an oven, a computer, a factory building. Those man-made aids to production are what economists call capital, or capital goods. Money is a claim on resources, a means of exchange; capital goods are the resources themselves. When a business says it is raising capital, it means it is raising money in order to buy capital goods, so in everyday speech the word slides from the money to what the money buys. In economics we keep the two apart because it matters for understanding growth. A country does not become richer by printing more money; if it did, every government would simply print. It becomes richer by building up its stock of real capital, its roads, ports, power stations, machines and computers, and by improving the skills of its labour, so that each worker can produce more. That is why investment, spending on new capital goods, is so important in Grade 10 and later. You will also meet a third meaning in the financial literacy part of this course: in accounting, capital is the owner's contribution to the business, recorded as owner's equity. So in each question, check which subject you are in. In economics, capital is the machine. In accounting, capital is the owner's investment.

### subtopic_2 — Rewards for the Factors of Production
**Assistant:** Why is profit called a residual reward, and why can it be negative when wages cannot?

**Tutor:** Profit is called a residual because it is what remains after all the other factors and suppliers have been paid. Think about the order in which money flows out of a business. Workers are promised a wage in a contract, and the business must pay it whether sales are good or bad. A landlord is promised rent in a lease. A bank is promised interest in a loan agreement. Suppliers of ingredients are paid for what they deliver. These are contractual payments, fixed in advance. The entrepreneur is the only participant who has no contract guaranteeing a payment. The entrepreneur receives whatever is left over, the residual. In the bakery example, sales of R50 000 less rent of R8 000, wages of R22 000, interest of R2 000 and ingredients of R12 000 leave R6 000 profit. Now suppose a competitor opens next door and sales fall to R40 000. The contractual payments do not change, so the calculation becomes 40 000 - 8 000 - 22 000 - 2 000 - 12 000 = -4 000, a loss of R4 000. The workers, landlord and bank are still paid; the entrepreneur loses. That is exactly why profit is the reward for risk. The entrepreneur accepts the chance of a loss in return for the chance of a large profit if the business succeeds. If entrepreneurs could never earn more than a safe wage, few people would take that risk, and many of the businesses that create jobs and new products would never be started. So profit is not simply greed; it is the price an economy pays for people to take risks.

### subtopic_3 — What a Market Is and the Main Types of Markets
**Assistant:** In the factor market households are sellers, but in the goods market they are buyers. Why the switch?

**Tutor:** Because households play two different roles in the economy, as owners of factors of production and as consumers, and each role puts them on a different side of a different market. Start with ownership. Households own the factors of production: their own labour, any land or buildings they own, the savings they lend, and the entrepreneurial ability of their members. Businesses own nothing in the end except what households have provided or what households own through shares. So when a business needs a worker, it must buy, or more precisely hire, labour from a household. The household is the seller of labour and the business is the buyer. The price in that market is the wage. The same applies to renting land or borrowing savings. Now consumption. Households need food, clothing, transport and services. Businesses produce them. So in the goods and services market the roles reverse: businesses sell and households buy, and the price is the price of the product. Put the two together and you can see that money goes round in a circle. Businesses pay wages, rent and interest to households in the factor market; households spend that income buying goods and services from businesses in the goods market; and the businesses use that revenue to pay wages again. That loop is the circular flow of income, which is the next part of this topic. The switch of roles is not a contradiction; it is the reason the flow is circular. When you draw the diagram, label the factor market with the household as seller and the goods market with the household as buyer, and the arrows will make sense.

### subtopic_4 — Markets by Place, Reach and Time
**Assistant:** Is a spaza shop part of the formal or the informal market, and why does that matter?

**Tutor:** It depends on how the particular spaza shop is run. Many spaza shops operate informally: they are run from a home, they are not registered as businesses, they do not keep formal accounting records and they do not pay income tax or register for VAT. Others are registered, have a trading licence from the municipality, keep books and pay tax, which puts them in the formal market. The distinction matters for several reasons. For the economy as a whole, the informal sector provides a livelihood for millions of South Africans who cannot find formal jobs, so it reduces poverty and keeps money circulating in townships and rural areas. For the government, informal businesses do not contribute income tax, and the people working in them are not protected by labour laws, minimum wages or unemployment insurance. For the business owner, informality has costs too: it is difficult to get a bank loan without records, it is harder to supply larger customers who need tax invoices, and the business may be vulnerable to fines or closure. Government programmes therefore try to help informal businesses formalise, for example by simplifying registration and offering a simpler turnover tax for small businesses. In the financial literacy part of this course you will learn the bookkeeping that a formal business keeps, starting with the cash journals. So when you classify a market, ask whether the business is registered, keeps records and pays tax. If it does, it is formal; if not, it is informal. Both are real markets where buyers and sellers meet.
