# Part 1 — Expert

This session describes the global economy: how the economies of the world are linked through trade, investment, technology and people, and where South Africa fits into that network. The anchor ideas are globalisation, international trade, the balance of trade and the exchange rate, and the key institutions that govern world trade. The governing principle is that countries trade because they differ in the resources they have and the things they can produce cheaply, so specialisation and trade allow all of them to consume more than they could on their own, although the gains are not shared equally.

## Subtopic: Globalisation and Why Countries Trade

The global economy is the economy of the whole world, seen as one interconnected system. Globalisation is the process by which the economies, cultures and societies of different countries become more closely connected and dependent on each other. It happens through four main flows: the flow of goods and services across borders, called international trade; the flow of money and investment, for example when a foreign company builds a factory in South Africa; the flow of technology and information, through the internet and telecommunications; and the flow of people, as workers, students and tourists move between countries.

Globalisation is not new. Trade routes such as the Silk Road linked Asia and Europe for centuries, and the arrival of the Dutch East India Company at the Cape in 1652 was itself part of a global trade network. But the pace has increased enormously since the second half of the twentieth century, driven by cheaper shipping in standardised containers, cheaper air travel, the internet, the lowering of trade barriers through international agreements, and the opening of formerly planned economies such as China, from 1978, and the countries of the former Soviet Union, after 1991.

Why do countries trade? First, resources are unevenly distributed. South Africa has large reserves of platinum, manganese, chrome and coal, but must import most of its crude oil. Second, climate differs: South Africa can grow citrus fruit and grapes in the Western Cape when it is winter in Europe. Third, countries differ in skills and technology: some specialise in aircraft, others in software. Fourth, some goods are produced more cheaply on a very large scale, so it is cheaper for one country to make them for many countries than for every country to make its own.

The central economic idea is specialisation. If each country concentrates on producing what it can produce relatively efficiently and trades for the rest, total world output rises. Economists call this comparative advantage, an idea associated with the British economist David Ricardo in 1817. A country does not need to be the best at something to benefit from trade; it only needs to give up less to produce it than other countries do. You will study comparative advantage more formally in Grade 10 and beyond.

## Subtopic: Imports, Exports, the Balance of Trade and Exchange Rates

Exports are goods and services produced in one country and sold to buyers in another country. When a South African mine sells platinum to Japan, or a hotel in Cape Town accommodates a tourist from Germany, South Africa is exporting. Imports are goods and services bought from other countries: a cellphone made in China, crude oil from the Middle East, a streaming subscription from an American company. Exports bring money into the country, and imports send money out.

South Africa's major exports include platinum group metals, gold, coal, iron ore, manganese, motor vehicles and parts, and agricultural products such as citrus fruit and wine. Its major imports include crude oil and petroleum products, machinery, electronic equipment such as phones and computers, and vehicles and parts. China is South Africa's largest single trading partner, and the European Union, taken as a group, is also a major partner, as are the United States, other African countries and India.

The balance of trade is the difference between the value of a country's exports of goods and the value of its imports of goods over a period. If exports are greater than imports, there is a trade surplus; if imports are greater than exports, there is a trade deficit. Consider a small economy that exports goods worth R150 million in a month and imports goods worth R120 million. The balance of trade is R150 million minus R120 million, that is 150 - 120 = 30, a surplus of R30 million. If instead it exported R95 million and imported R110 million, the balance would be 95 - 110 = -15, a deficit of R15 million.

To trade, countries must exchange currencies. A South African importer buying goods from the United States must pay in US dollars, so it buys dollars with rand. The exchange rate is the price of one currency in terms of another. If the exchange rate is R18 per US dollar, a laptop priced at 500 dollars costs 500 × 18 = 9 000 rand. If the rand weakens, so that a dollar costs R20, the same laptop costs 500 × 20 = 10 000 rand. A weaker rand therefore makes imports more expensive for South Africans, but it makes South African exports cheaper for foreign buyers, which helps exporters such as fruit farmers and mines. A stronger rand does the opposite: imports become cheaper and exports more expensive. Exchange rates change every day as the demand for and supply of each currency change.

## Subtopic: International Organisations and Trade Agreements

Because trade crosses borders, countries create organisations and agreements to set rules and reduce barriers. A trade barrier is any measure that restricts imports, such as a tariff, which is a tax on imported goods, or a quota, which is a limit on the quantity that may be imported.

The World Trade Organization, the WTO, was established in 1995 and is based in Geneva. It sets the rules for trade between its member countries, negotiates the reduction of tariffs, and settles trade disputes between members. South Africa is a founding member. The International Monetary Fund, the IMF, and the World Bank were both created in 1944. The IMF helps to keep the international monetary system stable and lends to countries in financial difficulty; the World Bank lends money for development projects in poorer countries, such as dams, roads and schools.

On the African continent, the Southern African Customs Union, SACU, which includes South Africa, Botswana, Lesotho, Namibia and Eswatini, was founded in 1910 and is the oldest customs union in the world: goods move between its members without tariffs, and they charge a common tariff on goods from outside. The Southern African Development Community, SADC, has sixteen member states and works towards regional integration and development. The African Continental Free Trade Area, AfCFTA, under which trading began in 2021, aims to create a single market across the continent by removing most tariffs between African countries.

South Africa is also a member of BRICS, a group originally of Brazil, Russia, India and China, which invited South Africa to join in 2010, with South Africa attending its first summit as a member in 2011; the group has since expanded to include more countries. BRICS cooperates on trade and investment and established the New Development Bank. South Africa is also the only African member of the G20, the group of the world's largest economies.

Multinational corporations, also called MNCs, are businesses that operate in more than one country. Car manufacturers such as BMW, Toyota, Volkswagen and Mercedes-Benz have assembly plants in South Africa that export vehicles around the world, and South African companies such as MTN, Shoprite and Naspers operate in many other countries. MNCs are among the main drivers of globalisation because they move capital, technology and jobs across borders.

## Subtopic: Benefits and Costs of Globalisation for South Africa

Globalisation brings clear benefits. South African businesses gain access to much larger markets than the domestic one, so exporters can grow and create jobs; the motor industry in the Eastern Cape and Gauteng exists largely because it exports. Consumers have a wider choice of goods, often at lower prices, because imports compete with local products. Foreign direct investment brings capital, technology and skills, and creates employment. Ideas and technology spread quickly: South Africans use the same smartphones and software as people in wealthy countries. Tourism earns foreign currency and creates jobs.

Globalisation also has costs. Local industries that cannot compete with cheaper imports may shrink or close; South Africa's clothing and textile industry lost tens of thousands of jobs as cheap imports increased. Economies become exposed to shocks elsewhere: the global financial crisis of 2008 and the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020 both caused recessions and job losses in South Africa. Dependence on exporting raw materials such as minerals leaves the economy exposed to falling world prices. Profits of multinational companies may flow out of the country to foreign shareholders. Globalisation can widen inequality, because skilled workers and owners of capital gain more than unskilled workers. Some argue it also threatens local cultures and contributes to environmental damage through increased transport and production.

For this reason South Africa follows a mixed approach. It is open to trade, but it uses tariffs to protect some industries, such as clothing and poultry, and it supports local production through programmes that encourage buying South African goods.

The error museum holds four mistakes. First, that a weaker rand makes imports cheaper; it makes them more expensive, because more rand are needed to buy each dollar. Second, that a trade deficit means a country is bankrupt; it means that it imported more goods than it exported in that period, which is common and can be financed in other ways. Third, that globalisation only means trade in goods; it also includes investment, technology and people. Fourth, that a country must be best at producing something to export it; specialisation according to comparative advantage benefits trading partners even when one is more efficient at everything.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same ideas again, through a cellphone, a trip to the border and a village market, in plain words with the same facts.

## Subtopic: The Journey of a Cellphone

Pick up a cellphone and ask: where did this come from? The design was probably done in one country, the United States or South Korea. The tiny chips were made in another, perhaps Taiwan. Some of the metals inside, like platinum-group metals and others, may have been mined in Africa, maybe even here in South Africa. The phone was put together in a factory in China or Vietnam. It travelled across the ocean in a big metal container on a ship to the port of Durban. Then a truck took it to a shop in your town. And the apps on it come from companies all over the world.

That one phone touched maybe ten countries. That is globalisation: the countries of the world being joined up through trade, money, technology and people.

Why don't we just make everything here? Because every country is good at different things. South Africa has lots of platinum, coal and sunshine for growing oranges. Other countries are better at making computer chips. If each country does what it is good at and swaps, everybody ends up with more. It is like a class project: if the learner who draws well does the poster and the learner who types fast does the typing, the project is finished faster than if everyone did a bit of everything. That is specialisation.

When we sell to other countries, like platinum to Japan or oranges to Europe, that is an export. Money comes in. When we buy from other countries, like the phone from China or petrol made from imported oil, that is an import. Money goes out.

Keep the journey of the phone in mind and try the questions for this part.

## Subtopic: Rands and Dollars at the Border

Here is a problem. A shop in Johannesburg wants to buy sneakers from America. The American factory does not want rand. It wants dollars. So the shop must first swap its rand for dollars at a bank. The exchange rate is the price of a dollar in rand.

Say one dollar costs R18. A pair of sneakers costs 50 dollars. So the shop pays 50 × 18 = 900 rand.

Now the rand gets weaker, and one dollar costs R20. The same sneakers now cost 50 × 20 = 1 000 rand. Nothing changed in America. The sneakers just got more expensive for us because our rand buys less. A weaker rand means imports cost more.

But turn it around. An orange farmer in Citrusdal sells a box of oranges for R180. When a dollar cost R18, an American buyer paid 10 dollars. When a dollar costs R20, the American pays only 9 dollars for the same box. Our oranges just got cheaper for them, so they buy more. A weaker rand helps exporters.

Now the balance of trade. Add up everything a country sold to other countries, its exports, and everything it bought, its imports. Exports R150 million, imports R120 million: we sold more than we bought, a surplus of R30 million. Exports R95 million, imports R110 million: we bought more than we sold, a deficit of R15 million.

So: weak rand, imports up in price, exports cheaper for others. Strong rand, the opposite.

## Subtopic: Winners and Losers in the Global Village

Think of the whole world as one big village market. Being part of it brings good things. Our car factories in the Eastern Cape sell cars to people all over the world, which means jobs here. You can buy a phone, sneakers and medicine from anywhere, often for less. Foreign companies build factories and hotels here and hire people. Tourists come to see Table Mountain and the Kruger Park and spend their money.

But a big market also has losers. If clothes made overseas are much cheaper, our own clothing factories struggle and some close, and workers lose their jobs. That really happened here. And when something goes wrong far away, like the money crisis in 2008 or COVID-19 in 2020, we feel it too: people stop buying our goods, and jobs are lost.

There are rule-makers for this village. The World Trade Organization, the WTO, makes rules for trade between countries and settles fights. Closer to home, SACU lets goods move between South Africa, Botswana, Lesotho, Namibia and Eswatini with no extra tax, and the new African free trade area, the AfCFTA, is trying to do the same for the whole continent. South Africa is also in BRICS, a club with Brazil, Russia, India and China and others.

Sometimes a country protects its own factories by putting a tax on imports. That tax is called a tariff. It makes imports more expensive so local products have a better chance.

So globalisation is not all good or all bad. It is a big market with winners and losers, and countries try to get more of the good and less of the bad.
