### subtopic_1 — The Basic Economic Problem and the Three Questions
**Assistant:** If a country became very rich, would it still have the basic economic problem of scarcity?

**Tutor:** Yes, and this is one of the most important ideas in the whole subject. Scarcity does not mean that people are poor. It means that resources are limited compared with the wants people have, and wants grow as income grows. A very rich country still has only so much land, so many hours of labour, so much clean water and so much electricity. Its people simply want different and more expensive things: faster trains, better hospitals, longer holidays, more research into disease. The government of a rich country still has a budget and must choose between, say, a new airport and more teachers. A rich family still has twenty-four hours in a day and must choose whether to spend them working or resting. Every one of those choices carries an opportunity cost, the next-best alternative given up. That is why the three basic questions, what to produce, how to produce and for whom, are asked in every economy in history: in a hunter-gatherer band, in the Soviet Union, in the United States and in South Africa today. What differs is not whether the questions are asked, but who answers them. In a planned economy a state authority answers them, in a market economy millions of buyers and sellers answer them through prices, and in a mixed economy both do. So when you meet a question on economic systems, begin with scarcity and the three questions, and then ask who decides.

### subtopic_2 — The Planned Economy and Its Origins
**Assistant:** Why did the planned economies of the Soviet Union and Eastern Europe so often suffer from shortages?

**Tutor:** The main reason is information. In a market economy, prices carry information automatically: when people want more of something, they bid the price up, and producers see a profit opportunity and make more. In a planned economy, prices are fixed by the state, so they do not rise when a good becomes scarce. A central planning office has to estimate in advance how many shoes, how much bread and how many tractors hundreds of millions of people will want, in which sizes and in which towns, for the next five years. No office, however clever, can gather and process all of that information accurately. When the plan guessed wrong, goods piled up in warehouses that nobody wanted while other goods ran out, and because the price could not rise, the shortage showed up as long queues instead. A second reason is incentives. State factory managers were rewarded for meeting the quantity targets in the plan, not for pleasing customers, so they had little reason to improve quality or to innovate. A factory told to produce a certain mass of nails might make a few enormous, useless nails. Workers earned similar wages whatever their effort. The Soviet Union did achieve fast industrialisation, near-full employment, free education and health care, and those were real gains. But by the 1980s its economy had fallen far behind market economies, and in 1991 it collapsed. China kept its communist government but introduced markets from 1978, and its economy grew very quickly afterwards.

### subtopic_3 — The Market Economy and Its Origins
**Assistant:** What did Adam Smith mean by the invisible hand, and does it mean government is not needed?

**Tutor:** Adam Smith, in The Wealth of Nations in 1776, observed that we get our dinner not from the kindness of the butcher, the brewer or the baker, but from their concern for their own interest. Each of them wants to earn a living. To do that, they must offer something that customers want at a price customers will pay, and they must do it better or more cheaply than their competitors, or the customers will go elsewhere. So, without anyone planning it, the pursuit of private gain leads to goods being produced that people want, in the quantities they want, by the cheapest methods. It is as if an invisible hand were coordinating everybody. That is the invisible hand, and it is the core argument for the market economy. But Smith never said that government is unnecessary. He argued that the state must provide defence, a system of justice to enforce contracts and protect property, and certain public works that no private business would find profitable, such as roads and bridges. He was also suspicious of business owners, warning that when people in the same trade meet, the conversation often ends in a conspiracy to raise prices. That is exactly the behaviour that South Africa's Competition Commission exists to stop. So the invisible hand works well only when there is competition and a legal framework. Where those are missing, or where people have no income to take to the market, markets alone do not produce fair results, and that is the argument for the mixed economy.

### subtopic_4 — The Mixed Economy and South Africa's System
**Assistant:** How can we tell that South Africa has a mixed economy and not a market economy?

**Tutor:** Look for both kinds of decision-making side by side, and you will find them everywhere. The market part is easy to see. Most goods and services in South Africa are produced by privately owned businesses, from a spaza shop in a township to companies listed on the Johannesburg Stock Exchange, and their prices are set by demand and supply. A learner choosing between two brands of bread in a supermarket is taking part in the market. Now look for the state. The state owns enterprises in key industries: Eskom generates most of the electricity, Transnet runs the ports and freight railways, and the state also owns the national broadcaster, the SABC. The state provides public goods and services that the market would not supply to everyone, such as public schools, clinics, public hospitals, the police, roads and social grants to millions of people, paid for with taxes collected by SARS. And the state regulates the market: it sets a national minimum wage, the Competition Commission fines firms that fix prices, the National Credit Regulator controls lending, and the National Energy Regulator approves electricity tariffs. If South Africa were a pure market economy, none of those would exist. If it were a planned economy, the state would own the supermarkets and set the price of bread. Because we have a large private sector together with significant state ownership, provision and regulation, South Africa sits in the middle of the line, as a mixed economy. In Grade 10 you will extend this by comparing how much the state should do, which is one of the central debates in South African economic policy.
