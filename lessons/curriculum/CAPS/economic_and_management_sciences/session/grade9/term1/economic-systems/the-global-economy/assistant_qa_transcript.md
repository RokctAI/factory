### subtopic_1 — Globalisation and Why Countries Trade
**Assistant:** If South Africa could make everything itself, why would it still be better off trading?

**Tutor:** Because making everything yourself means giving up a great deal to produce the things you are relatively bad at. Every resource has an opportunity cost. Suppose South Africa used its land, workers and capital to manufacture its own computer chips. It would need enormous factories, highly specialised engineers and very expensive machines, and because the domestic market is small, it could not produce on the vast scale that makes chips cheap. The resources used for that would have to be taken away from activities in which South Africa is relatively efficient, such as mining platinum, growing citrus in a Mediterranean climate or assembling vehicles in plants that already exist. The result would be expensive chips and less of everything else. If instead South Africa specialises in what it gives up least to produce, and sells those goods abroad, it can use the earnings to buy chips from countries that make them cheaply. Both sides end up with more than if each tried to make everything. This is the principle of comparative advantage, set out by David Ricardo in 1817, and it is the core reason that economists generally favour trade. Think of your own household: your family probably could grow its own vegetables, sew its own clothes and repair its own car, but most families find it better to specialise in their jobs and buy those things. A country is the same. The gains are real, but they are not shared evenly, and some workers in industries that face imports lose out, which is why governments sometimes protect industries or help workers retrain.

### subtopic_2 — Imports, Exports, the Balance of Trade and Exchange Rates
**Assistant:** Why does a weaker rand help a fruit farmer but hurt a family buying a laptop?

**Tutor:** Because they stand on opposite sides of the exchange. The farmer sells abroad and is paid in foreign currency; the family buys goods priced in foreign currency. Take an exchange rate that moves from R18 to R20 per US dollar, which means the rand has weakened: each dollar now costs more rand. The family wants a laptop priced at 500 dollars. Before the change it costs 500 × 18 = 9 000 rand; after the change it costs 500 × 20 = 10 000 rand. The laptop has not changed at all, but the family must pay R1 000 more, because its rand buys fewer dollars. Imported goods such as electronics, medicines and, importantly, crude oil all become more expensive, which is why a weaker rand often leads to higher petrol prices and higher inflation. Now the farmer. Suppose a box of citrus is sold for 10 dollars. Before the change, the farmer receives 10 × 18 = 180 rand; after it, 10 × 20 = 200 rand. The same box earns R20 more. Alternatively, the farmer can lower the dollar price to win more customers and still earn as much in rand. So a weaker rand makes South African exports more competitive and boosts export industries such as agriculture, mining and tourism, because foreign tourists find South Africa cheaper. A stronger rand reverses all of this. That is why there is no simple answer to whether a strong rand is good: it depends on whether you are mainly an importer or an exporter.

### subtopic_3 — International Organisations and Trade Agreements
**Assistant:** What is the difference between a free trade area and a customs union like SACU?

**Tutor:** Both remove tariffs on trade between their members, but they treat goods from outside differently. In a free trade area, members remove tariffs on goods traded among themselves, but each member keeps its own tariffs on imports from non-members. So two members of a free trade area might charge very different tariffs on, say, cars imported from Asia. Because of that, free trade areas need rules of origin, rules that decide whether a product really comes from a member country, so that outside goods cannot simply enter through the member with the lowest tariff and then move freely. The African Continental Free Trade Area works on this model. A customs union goes one step further. Its members remove tariffs among themselves and also agree on a common external tariff: every member charges the same tariff on goods from outside. The Southern African Customs Union, which links South Africa, Botswana, Lesotho, Namibia and Eswatini, is a customs union and was founded in 1910, making it the oldest in the world. Because goods can enter at any member's border and then move freely, SACU collects the customs duties into a common pool and shares the revenue between the members according to a formula. For the smaller members, that shared revenue is an important part of their government income. In Grade 10 and later you will meet further stages of integration, such as a common market, which also allows workers and capital to move freely, and an economic union, which may share a currency, as the euro area does.

### subtopic_4 — Benefits and Costs of Globalisation for South Africa
**Assistant:** Should South Africa use tariffs to protect its clothing factories from cheap imports?

**Tutor:** This is a genuine debate, so the strongest answer weighs both sides. The case for tariffs is that clothing factories employ many people with relatively few formal skills, often women in areas such as KwaZulu-Natal and the Western Cape where other jobs are scarce. When very cheap imports flooded in, tens of thousands of these jobs were lost. A tariff, a tax on imported clothing, raises the price of imports and gives local factories time and space to compete, protecting jobs and communities. Some argue that a young or recovering industry needs protection until it becomes efficient. The case against tariffs is that they raise prices for consumers, and clothing is a basic need, so the burden falls heavily on poor households. They reduce competition, so protected factories may have little incentive to become more efficient. And trading partners may retaliate with tariffs on South African exports, hurting other industries that employ people. A balanced conclusion recognises that South Africa actually uses a combination: it charges tariffs on clothing imports, but it also tries to make local factories competitive through support programmes, and it encourages retailers to buy locally. The best long-term protection for jobs is productivity: factories that can produce quickly for local retailers, with designs suited to local customers, can compete even against cheaper imports. Whatever conclusion you reach, the marks are earned by explaining the effects on producers, workers, consumers and trading partners.
