# Part 1 — Expert

This session sets out the characteristics, advantages and disadvantages of the three economic systems: the planned economy, the market economy and the mixed economy. The anchor is a comparison table with the same rows for every system: ownership of resources, who decides, how prices are set, the motive for producing, and the role of the state. The governing principle is that every system involves a trade-off between efficiency and choice on one side and equality and security on the other. In examinations, an advantage or disadvantage must always be stated as a full sentence that gives a reason, not as a single word.

## Subtopic: Characteristics of the Planned Economy

The planned economy, also called the command or centrally planned economy, has five defining characteristics. First, the state owns most of the factors of production: land, mines, factories, banks and transport. Second, a central planning authority makes the economic decisions, setting production targets for each industry in long-range plans. Third, prices and wages are fixed by the state rather than by demand and supply. Fourth, the motive for production is meeting social needs and the targets in the plan, not private profit. Fifth, the state provides most services, such as education, health care and housing, often free or heavily subsidised.

The advantages follow from these characteristics. Because the state distributes goods and sets wages, there is less inequality of income and wealth. Basic needs such as food, housing, health care and education can be provided for everyone. Because the state assigns jobs, unemployment is officially low. Resources can be directed quickly towards national priorities, for example heavy industry or defence, as the Soviet Union did in its five-year plans from 1928. And there is little duplication or wasteful competition, such as two companies building parallel railway lines.

The disadvantages are just as clear. Planners cannot gather enough information to know what millions of people want, so there are shortages of some goods and surpluses of others, and shortages show up as long queues because prices cannot rise. Because there is no profit motive, there is little incentive for managers to work efficiently, improve quality or invent new products. Consumers have little choice: they buy what the plan provides. Individual economic freedom is limited, because people cannot start their own businesses or always choose their jobs. A large bureaucracy is needed to run the plan, and it is costly and slow. These weaknesses are a major reason why the Soviet economy fell behind and why the Soviet Union collapsed in 1991.

## Subtopic: Characteristics of the Market Economy

The market economy, also called the free-market or capitalist economy, has the opposite characteristics. First, the factors of production are privately owned by individuals and businesses, and property rights are protected by law. Second, decisions are made by consumers and producers acting in their own interest: consumers decide what to buy and businesses decide what to produce. Third, prices are determined by demand and supply in the market; this is the price mechanism. Fourth, the motive for production is profit. Fifth, there is competition between businesses for customers. Sixth, the state plays a limited role, mainly protecting property, enforcing contracts and providing defence, police and courts.

The advantages of the market economy include the following. Competition forces businesses to use resources efficiently and keep costs low, because inefficient firms lose customers and close. The profit motive encourages innovation, so new and better products and methods are developed continually; most modern technology, from mobile phones to vaccines, has been developed by private firms. Consumers have a wide variety of choice. Consumer sovereignty means that consumers decide what is produced by spending their money. People have economic freedom to own property, start businesses and choose their work. Prices adjust automatically to shortages and surpluses, so no large bureaucracy is needed.

The disadvantages are significant. Income and wealth become very unequally distributed, because those who own capital or scarce skills earn far more than others. Goods are produced for those who can pay, so luxury goods may be produced while poor people lack basic necessities. Merit goods such as education and health care are under-supplied, and public goods such as street lighting and national defence would not be provided at all, because nobody can be made to pay for them. Firms may grow into monopolies that reduce competition and raise prices. Private firms may cause pollution and other harm to the environment when it is cheaper for them. And market economies experience business cycles, with periods of recession and unemployment, such as the Great Depression of the 1930s and the global financial crisis of 2008.

## Subtopic: Characteristics of the Mixed Economy

The mixed economy combines the characteristics of both. First, there is both private and public ownership of the factors of production: most businesses are private, but the state owns some enterprises, usually in strategic industries such as electricity, water, transport and ports. Second, most decisions are made through markets, but the state plans and intervenes where markets fail. Third, most prices are set by demand and supply, but the state controls or regulates some prices, such as electricity tariffs, fuel prices and the minimum wage. Fourth, both the profit motive and the social welfare motive operate. Fifth, the state provides public goods, merit goods and a social safety net, funded by taxation.

The advantages of the mixed economy are that it aims to combine the strengths of both systems. Private enterprise and competition provide efficiency, innovation and choice. The state reduces inequality through progressive taxes, in which higher incomes are taxed at higher rates, and through social grants. It provides public goods and merit goods that markets under-supply. It protects consumers and workers through laws, and it can act to reduce unemployment during a recession.

The disadvantages are that the state sector can be inefficient and may need repeated rescue from taxpayers; South Africa's state-owned enterprises such as Eskom have needed large government support. High taxes may discourage work and investment. Government regulation creates red tape that can slow down business. And there is permanent debate about the right balance: too much state involvement brings the problems of planning, too little brings the problems of the pure market.

South Africa's economy shows all these features. The private sector produces most goods and services; the state owns Eskom, Transnet and others; the state pays social grants to millions of people and provides free education in no-fee schools; and fuel prices and the minimum wage are set or regulated by the state.

## Subtopic: Comparing the Systems and Writing the Answer

The most efficient way to learn this content is a comparison table with five rows and three columns. Row one, ownership: planned, the state; market, private; mixed, both. Row two, decision-making: planned, the central planning authority; market, consumers and producers in markets; mixed, mainly markets with state intervention. Row three, prices: planned, set by the state; market, demand and supply; mixed, mainly demand and supply with some state control. Row four, motive: planned, social needs and plan targets; market, profit; mixed, profit and social welfare. Row five, role of the state: planned, total; market, minimal; mixed, significant but limited.

When a question asks for an advantage or disadvantage, the answer must link a characteristic to a consequence. A weak answer is a single word, such as choice. A strong answer is a full sentence that explains: in a market economy consumers have a wide choice of goods, because competing businesses try to attract customers with different products. Another strong answer: in a planned economy there may be shortages, because prices are fixed and cannot rise to signal that a good is scarce.

A useful check is that the advantages of one system tend to be the disadvantages of the other. The market's strength is efficiency and choice, which is the planned economy's weakness. The planned economy's strength is equality and security, which is the market's weakness. The mixed economy tries to get both and risks getting the weaknesses of both.

Some questions ask you to evaluate or discuss rather than simply list. An evaluation needs three parts: the case for, the case against, and a reasoned conclusion. Suppose the question is whether South Africa should move closer to a market economy. The case for: more competition could lower prices, private investment could improve services such as electricity generation, and the state would spend less rescuing loss-making enterprises. The case against: more people could be left without basic services, inequality, already very high, could rise further, and private firms might neglect poor and rural areas where profits are low. A conclusion might be that South Africa benefits most from a mixed economy in which the private sector is encouraged to grow and compete, while the state concentrates on public goods, merit goods and a social safety net. There is no single correct conclusion; the marks are for supporting it with reasons drawn from the characteristics.

It also helps to recognise that the three systems are models. A model simplifies reality so that it can be studied. No real country matches a model exactly. Even in the Soviet Union there were small private plots and informal markets, and even in the United States the state runs schools, the army and pensions. When a case study describes a country, look for the dominant features and decide where on the line from planned to market it falls, rather than expecting a perfect fit.

The error museum holds four mistakes. First, writing that a market economy has no inequality because everyone is free; freedom to compete produces unequal results. Second, writing that a planned economy has no unemployment, full stop; it has low official unemployment, but people may be employed in unproductive work. Third, writing that public goods would be supplied in a pure market economy; no firm can charge everyone who benefits from street lighting, so it would not be supplied. Fourth, listing advantages as one-word answers with no reason.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same ideas again, through a loaf of bread, a seesaw and a sorting game, in plain words with the same facts.

## Subtopic: The Bread Queue and the Bread Aisle

Think about how you get a loaf of bread in two different kinds of country.

In a planned economy, a government office decided last year how many loaves the state bakery would bake. The price is printed on the wrapper by the state and it is cheap, so almost everyone can afford it. But if the planners guessed wrong, the shop runs out by ten in the morning. The price cannot go up, so instead the people queue. And there is one kind of bread. If you wanted brown, or rolls, too bad.

In a market economy, you walk into a supermarket and find a long aisle of bread: white, brown, seed loaf, rolls, five different brands, all competing for your money. Bakeries compete, so they try to make better bread and keep costs down. If many people want seed loaf, the price rises a little and bakers bake more of it. Shelves are seldom empty. But the cheapest loaf may still be too expensive for a family with no income, and nobody in the market is responsible for them.

In a mixed economy, like South Africa, you get the aisle full of choice, but the state also pays social grants, so that poor families can afford bread, and the Competition Commission watches to make sure the big bakeries do not secretly agree to push prices up. In fact, the Competition Commission caught exactly that in South Africa: after an investigation that began in 2007, big bread companies were fined for fixing bread prices.

So: the planned economy is fair but brings queues and no choice. The market economy brings choice and full shelves, but not everyone can afford them. The mixed economy tries to have both.

Keep the queue and the aisle in mind and try the questions for this part.

## Subtopic: Fair or Free? The Trade-off

Imagine a seesaw. On one side sits freedom and choice and efficiency. On the other side sits fairness and equality and security. When you push one side down, the other side goes up.

A market economy pushes the freedom side down hard. You are free to start a business, choose your job and buy what you like, and competition makes businesses efficient and creative. But the fairness side goes up in the air: some people become very rich and others are left with very little.

A planned economy pushes the fairness side down. Everyone gets basic food, housing, school and a job, and the gap between rich and poor is small. But now freedom goes up in the air: you cannot start your own business, there is little choice, and nobody has much reason to work harder or invent something new.

A mixed economy tries to keep the seesaw closer to balanced. Businesses compete and make profits, which keeps freedom and choice. The government taxes higher incomes at higher rates and uses the money for grants, schools and clinics, which adds fairness. The problem is that it is hard to balance a seesaw. Too much tax and red tape, and businesses stop growing. Too little help, and many people are left behind. That is why countries argue about it all the time, and why South Africans argue about it too.

So when you write about advantages and disadvantages, remember the seesaw. The good things about one system are usually the weak points of the other.

## Subtopic: Sorting Statements into Boxes

Draw three boxes on your page: planned, market, mixed. Now sort these statements.

The state sets the price of every good. That goes in planned.

A business closes because customers prefer a competitor's product. That goes in market. Competition rewards efficient businesses and punishes inefficient ones.

The state owns the electricity company but most shops are private. That goes in mixed.

People queue for hours because the shops have run out. Planned, because fixed prices cannot rise to show the shortage.

A rich few own most of the wealth and there is no help for the poor. Market, a pure market with no state safety net.

Higher incomes are taxed at a higher rate and the money pays for social grants. Mixed.

Now the most important exam habit. When you give an advantage or a disadvantage, do not just write one word like choice or equality. Write a sentence with a because in it. For example: a market economy gives consumers more choice, because businesses compete for customers by offering different products. Or: a planned economy can have shortages, because prices are fixed and cannot rise to show that a good is scarce.

A sentence with a because in it earns the marks. Practise one for each box.

One last trick. If you get stuck, go back to the seesaw. Is the statement about freedom, choice and efficiency? Then it is probably a strength of the market and a weakness of the planned economy. Is it about fairness, equality and security? Then it is probably a strength of the planned economy and a weakness of the market. Is it about getting a bit of both, with taxes and rules? Then it belongs in mixed.
