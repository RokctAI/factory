# Part 1 — Expert

This session describes the three major economic systems, the planned economy, the market economy and the mixed economy, and where each one came from. The anchor idea is the basic economic problem: human wants are unlimited, but the resources available to satisfy them are scarce, so every society must choose. An economic system is the set of rules and institutions a country uses to make those choices. Each system answers the same three questions in a different way, and each grew out of a particular moment in history. This is the opening topic of Grade 10 Economics, so the vocabulary used here is the vocabulary used there.

## Subtopic: The Basic Economic Problem and the Three Questions

Economics begins with scarcity. Human wants have no end: once a household has food and shelter it wants a fridge, then a car, then a holiday, then a better school for the children. The resources used to produce goods and services, which economists call the factors of production, are limited. There is only so much land, so much water, so many workers with certain skills, so many machines and buildings, and so many people willing to take the risk of starting a business. Because resources are scarce relative to wants, every society must make choices, and every choice has an opportunity cost: the next-best alternative that is given up. If the government spends R1 billion building a new hospital, the opportunity cost is whatever else that R1 billion could have built, perhaps forty new schools.

Every society, rich or poor, ancient or modern, must answer three basic economic questions. The first is what to produce: which goods and services, and in what quantities. Should a country's land grow maize or be used for a shopping mall? Should factories make cars or tractors? The second is how to produce: which combination of resources and which methods to use. Should roads be built by hundreds of workers with spades or by a few workers with heavy machines? The third is for whom to produce: how the goods and services are shared out among the people. Does everyone receive the same, or do people receive according to what they can afford, or according to what they need?

An economic system is the way a society organises itself to answer these three questions. Economists describe three main systems. In a planned economy, also called a command or centrally planned economy, the state decides. In a market economy, also called a free-market or capitalist economy, individuals and private businesses decide through buying and selling in markets. In a mixed economy, both the market and the state play a role. Before these modern systems existed, most societies used a fourth way, a traditional economy, in which custom and inherited roles answered the questions: a son farmed because his father farmed, and harvests were shared according to long-standing rules. Traces of traditional economies remain today in subsistence farming and communal land in rural areas.

The key comparison is always the same: who owns the factors of production, and who makes the decisions? Keep that pair of questions in mind for each system.

## Subtopic: The Planned Economy and Its Origins

In a planned economy the state owns most of the factors of production: the land, the mines, the factories, the banks and the transport system. A central planning authority, a government department, decides what will be produced, how it will be produced and who will receive it. Prices are set by the state rather than by buyers and sellers, and production targets are written into long-range plans, often five-year plans.

The ideas behind the planned economy came from the German philosopher Karl Marx, who, with Friedrich Engels, published The Communist Manifesto in 1848. Marx was writing during the Industrial Revolution in Europe, when factory workers laboured very long hours for low wages in dangerous conditions while factory owners grew rich. He argued that this was unfair and unstable, and that workers, whom he called the proletariat, should collectively own the means of production so that wealth would be shared. His ideas are called socialism and, in their most complete form, communism.

The first large planned economy was created in Russia after the revolution of 1917, which led to the Soviet Union. From 1928 the Soviet government ran the economy through a series of five-year plans that set targets for steel, coal, electricity and grain. The Soviet Union industrialised very quickly, but consumers often faced shortages and long queues. After the Second World War, planned economies spread to Eastern Europe, China, Cuba and North Korea. The Soviet Union collapsed in 1991, and most of these countries moved towards market systems. China kept a communist government but introduced market reforms from 1978. Today North Korea and Cuba remain among the closest examples of planned economies, and even Cuba has allowed some private businesses.

In planned economies the state also provides most services directly: education, health care and housing are provided by the state, often free or at low cost. The aim is equality, so incomes are kept close together, and unemployment is officially low because the state assigns people to jobs.

## Subtopic: The Market Economy and Its Origins

In a market economy the factors of production are privately owned, by individuals and businesses. The three questions are answered by millions of separate decisions made in markets. What to produce is decided by consumers: if people want more smartphones and fewer radios, they spend their money on smartphones, the price and profit rise, and businesses produce more of them. How to produce is decided by businesses competing to keep costs low, so they choose the cheapest efficient methods. For whom to produce is decided by income: goods go to those who are willing and able to pay the market price.

The price mechanism, the interaction of demand and supply, coordinates all of this without a central planner. Prices act as signals. A rising price tells producers that a good is scarce and worth producing; a falling price tells them to produce less. This is the price theory studied in Term 2.

The ideas behind the market economy are linked to the Scottish economist Adam Smith, who published The Wealth of Nations in 1776. Smith argued that when individuals pursue their own interest in a free market, they are led, as if by an invisible hand, to serve the interest of society as a whole: a baker bakes good bread not out of kindness but because good bread sells and earns a profit. Smith argued for limited government interference, an approach called laissez-faire, which is French for let it be. During the 1800s Britain and the United States moved closest to this model, and the private ownership of capital that it rests on is why market economies are also called capitalist economies.

In a pure market economy the government's role would be limited to defence, the police and courts, and protecting property rights and contracts. No country has a pure market economy, because markets on their own do not provide some goods well, such as street lighting and national defence, and they can leave people without income or health care.

## Subtopic: The Mixed Economy and South Africa's System

A mixed economy combines private ownership and markets with state ownership and planning. Most goods and services are produced by private businesses and sold in markets, but the state also owns some enterprises, provides public goods and services, and regulates markets through laws and taxes. Every country in the world today is mixed to some degree; what differs is the mix.

The mixed economy grew out of the Great Depression of the 1930s, when markets in the United States and Europe collapsed, banks failed and unemployment rose to about a quarter of workers in the United States. The British economist John Maynard Keynes argued in 1936 that governments should step in and spend money to support employment when private spending falls. After the Second World War, most Western countries built welfare states, with public health care, pensions and unemployment insurance, alongside private enterprise.

South Africa has a mixed economy. Most businesses are privately owned, from spaza shops and taxi associations to banks such as Standard Bank and retailers such as Shoprite. The state owns state-owned enterprises, also called SOEs or parastatals, such as Eskom, which generates most of the country's electricity, Transnet, which runs ports and freight railways, and the South African Post Office. The state provides public goods and services such as schools, public hospitals, clinics, roads, the South African Police Service and social grants, paid for by taxes collected by the South African Revenue Service. It regulates markets through bodies such as the Competition Commission, which acts against price fixing, and the National Credit Regulator. It also sets a national minimum wage.

The Constitution of 1996 supports this mix: it protects the right to own property while also guaranteeing rights of access to housing, health care, food, water and social security, which the state must progressively realise.

One reliable way to describe any economy is as a point on a line. At one end is the pure planned economy, with total state control. At the other end is the pure market economy, with no state involvement. Real economies sit somewhere in between: North Korea near the planned end, the United States and Hong Kong closer to the market end, and South Africa in the middle, with a large private sector and a significant state.

The error museum for this lesson holds four common mistakes. First, that a mixed economy means half the businesses are owned by the state; in fact the mix can lean either way, and in South Africa the private sector is much larger. Second, that in a market economy there is no government at all; even the freest economies need courts and police. Third, that the planned economy was invented by the Soviet Union; the ideas came from Marx in 1848, and the Soviet Union only put them into practice from 1917. Fourth, that the three economic questions only apply to rich countries; every society faces scarcity and must answer them.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same ideas again, through a tuck shop, a village and a simple line, in plain words with the same facts.

## Subtopic: Who Decides? The School Tuck Shop Test

Imagine your school tuck shop. There is only so much money to buy stock, only so much space on the shelf and only two learners helping at break. But everybody wants something different: chips, vetkoek, juice, sweets, fruit. That is scarcity. The shop cannot have everything, so somebody must choose.

The tuck shop has to answer three questions. What should we sell? How should we make or get it, buy vetkoek from a mother who bakes, or fry them ourselves? And who gets it, the first learners in the queue, those who can pay, or everyone a fair share?

Now ask the big question: who decides? If the principal decides everything, what to sell, how much it costs, and that each learner gets one item, that is like a planned economy. The authority decides. If the tuck shop simply sells whatever learners buy most, and raises the price of vetkoek when they sell out early, that is like a market economy. Buyers decide with their money. If learners mostly decide with their money, but the principal bans fizzy drinks, keeps prices fair and gives free fruit to learners who cannot pay, that is a mixed economy. Both decide.

Every country in the world faces this same tuck shop problem, just much bigger. And the thing you give up when you choose is called the opportunity cost. If you spend your R10 on chips, the opportunity cost is the juice you could have bought instead.

Keep that picture in mind and try the questions for this part: scarcity, the three questions, and who decides in each system.

## Subtopic: Three Ways to Run a Village

Picture three villages, each with the same land, the same people and the same river.

In the first village, the council owns all the land and all the tools. Every year the council writes a plan: this field grows maize, that field grows beans, these ten people work in the fields, those five build houses. At harvest the council shares the food equally. Nobody goes hungry, but if the council plans badly there may be too many beans and not enough maize, and nobody can do anything about it. That is a planned economy. The idea came from Karl Marx in 1848, who was angry at how poorly factory workers were treated, and it was first tried on a big scale in the Soviet Union after 1917.

In the second village, each family owns its own field. Families grow whatever they think will sell best at the market. If tomatoes sell for a good price, more families plant tomatoes next year. Families who work hard and choose well grow rich; families who are unlucky or sick may struggle. There is plenty of choice at the market, but nothing looks after the poor. That is a market economy, and the thinker linked to it is Adam Smith, who wrote in 1776 that people chasing their own gain end up serving everybody, as if guided by an invisible hand.

In the third village, families own their fields and sell at the market, but the council collects a small tax from everyone. With the tax it builds a school, a clinic and a road, and it gives food to old people who can no longer farm. It also makes a rule that nobody may cheat with false scales. That is a mixed economy, and it is how most countries work today. It grew strong after the 1930s, when markets crashed and governments stepped in to create jobs.

So: council decides, market decides, or both. That is the whole idea of the three systems.

## Subtopic: Where South Africa Sits on the Line

Draw a long line on the board. On the far left write planned: the state decides everything. On the far right write market: buyers and sellers decide everything. No real country sits at either end. Every country is somewhere on the line.

North Korea and Cuba sit far to the left. The United States sits well to the right. South Africa sits somewhere in the middle. Why? Look around you. Most shops, taxis, farms, banks and factories are owned by private people and companies, so there is a lot of market. But Eskom, which makes most of our electricity, and Transnet, which runs the ports and the goods trains, are owned by the state. The state builds public schools, runs clinics and hospitals, pays the police and pays social grants. It collects taxes through SARS to pay for all of that. It sets a minimum wage so that workers are not paid too little. And the Competition Commission stops big companies from secretly agreeing to push prices up.

So South Africa is a mixed economy. Remember that mixed does not mean fifty-fifty. Our private sector is much bigger than our state sector.

Here is a quick way to check any example. Ask two questions. Who owns it? Who decides the price? If the state owns it and sets the price, it is the planned part. If a private owner owns it and buyers and sellers set the price, it is the market part. Next time you buy airtime, pay a taxi fare or switch on a light, ask which part of the economy you are using.
