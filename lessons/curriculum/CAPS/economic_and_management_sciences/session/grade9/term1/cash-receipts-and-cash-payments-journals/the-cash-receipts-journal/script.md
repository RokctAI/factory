# Part 1 — Expert

This session sets out how to complete the cash receipts journal, the CRJ, of a trading business. The anchor is the column layout of the CRJ: document, day, details, folio, analysis of receipts, bank, sales, cost of sales and the sundry accounts columns. The governing principle is that every amount received is entered in the bank column and also in the column of the account that is credited, so that the totals cross-cast: the bank total equals the sales total plus the sundry total. The cost of sales column is a memorandum column that does not form part of that check. The example used is a hardware store, Ridgeview Hardware, for the month of March, with a mark-up of 50 percent on cost.

## Subtopic: The Purpose and Format of the Cash Receipts Journal

A journal is a book of first entry: the first place a transaction is recorded from its source document, in date order, before it is posted to the ledger. Instead of writing every transaction into the ledger accounts directly, a business groups similar transactions in subsidiary journals. All money received is recorded in the cash receipts journal, and all money paid in the cash payments journal. This saves time, because similar transactions can be totalled and posted to the ledger as one figure at the end of the month, and it divides the work, because different clerks can keep different journals.

The CRJ of a trading business in Grade 9 has the following columns, from left to right. Document: the number of the source document, such as the duplicate receipt number or CRR for the cash register roll. Day: the day of the month; the month itself is written once at the top. Details: the name of the person or business from whom the money was received, or cash sales. Folio, Fol: used when posting to the debtors ledger later in the year; for now it is left open. Analysis of receipts: used when several receipts are deposited together on one deposit slip, to list the separate amounts that make up one bank deposit. Bank: the amount deposited into the bank. Sales: the selling price of goods sold for cash. Cost of sales: the cost price of those goods. Sundry accounts: three columns, amount, folio and details, for receipts that do not have a column of their own, such as capital, rent income, a loan or interest income.

The heading of the journal names the business, the journal and the month, for example: Cash Receipts Journal of Ridgeview Hardware, March, CRJ 1. The journal number, CRJ 1, is used as a folio reference when amounts are posted to the ledger.

The source documents for the CRJ are the duplicate receipt for money received from a person, the cash register roll for cash sales, and the bank statement for amounts received electronically that the business has not yet recorded, such as interest on its bank balance or an EFT from a customer.

Think of the CRJ as the debit side of the bank account written out in detail. Every entry in the CRJ increases the bank balance. The column in which the amount also appears tells you which account will be credited.

## Subtopic: Recording Cash Sales and Cost of Sales

Ridgeview Hardware marks up its goods by 50 percent on cost. On 3 March, cash sales according to the cash register roll were R12 000. The entry is: document CRR, day 3, details cash sales, bank R12 000, sales R12 000, and cost of sales. The cost of sales must be calculated from the selling price: 12 000 × 100 ÷ 150 = 8 000, so R8 000 is entered in the cost of sales column.

On 15 March cash sales were R9 000. Cost of sales: 9 000 × 100 ÷ 150 = 6 000. On 25 March cash sales were R6 600. Cost of sales: 6 600 × 100 ÷ 150 = 4 400.

Why is cost of sales recorded in the CRJ at all, if no money is received for it? Because the business uses a perpetual inventory system: every time goods are sold, the trading stock account must be reduced by the cost of the goods sold, and the cost of sales account increased. The CRJ is a convenient place to record this, alongside the sale that caused it. At the end of the month, the total of the cost of sales column is posted as a debit to the cost of sales account and a credit to the trading stock account. No money is involved, which is why the cost of sales column is excluded from the cross-cast of the bank column.

Sometimes a question gives the cost price and the mark-up instead, and asks you to find the selling price: goods costing R4 400 at a mark-up of 50 percent sell for 4 400 × 1,5 = 6 600. Sometimes it gives the selling price and the gross profit; then cost of sales is selling price minus gross profit. Read the question carefully, write out the calculation, and check it forwards: cost plus mark-up must equal the selling price.

The analysis of receipts column deserves a word here. Suppose that on one day the store received R700 rent from the tenant and R2 000 of cash sales, and deposited both together on one deposit slip of R2 700. Each receipt is entered on its own line with its amount in the analysis of receipts column, R700 and R2 000, and their total, R2 700, is entered once in the bank column, because that is the amount on the deposit slip and on the bank statement. The analysis column lets the bank column agree with the actual deposits. In this lesson, each receipt is deposited separately, so the analysis column is left empty and each amount goes straight into the bank column.

Every cash sale entry therefore has the same figure in the bank column and the sales column, and a smaller figure in the cost of sales column. If a cash sales line has a cost of sales figure that is larger than the sales figure, something has gone wrong, unless the goods were deliberately sold at a loss.

## Subtopic: Recording Other Receipts in the Sundry Accounts Column

Receipts other than cash sales are recorded in the bank column and in the sundry accounts column, with the name of the account to be credited written in the sundry details column.

On 1 March the owner deposited R80 000 as capital. Duplicate receipt 001. Details: the owner's name, or simply the owner. Bank R80 000. Sundry accounts amount R80 000, details Capital.

On 10 March, the business received rent of R2 500 from a tenant who rents a small office at the back of the store. Receipt 002. Bank R2 500. Sundry R2 500, details Rent income.

On 20 March, the business received a loan of R30 000 from its bank, deposited directly into its account. The source document is the bank statement, or a loan agreement with the bank's deposit advice. Bank R30 000. Sundry R30 000, details Loan: the name of the bank.

On 31 March, the bank statement showed interest of R150 credited on the business's positive balance. Bank R150. Sundry R150, details Interest income.

The sundry column is necessary because a business has many different types of receipt, and it would be impractical to have a separate column for each. Columns are created only for transactions that occur frequently, such as cash sales; the rest go into sundry. Later in Grade 9, when the business sells on credit, a debtors control column will be added for receipts from debtors.

Each amount in the sundry column is posted individually to the ledger, because each one belongs to a different account. Only the totals of the special columns, such as bank, sales and cost of sales, are posted as one figure. That is why the sundry section has its own folio column, filled in during posting, and why the sundry total is not posted.

## Subtopic: Totalling, Cross-casting and Checking the CRJ

At the end of the month, rule off the journal and total each column.

Bank: 80 000 + 12 000 + 2 500 + 9 000 + 30 000 + 6 600 + 150 = 140 250, or R140 250.

Sales: 12 000 + 9 000 + 6 600 = 27 600, or R27 600.

Cost of sales: 8 000 + 6 000 + 4 400 = 18 400, or R18 400.

Sundry accounts: 80 000 + 2 500 + 30 000 + 150 = 112 650, or R112 650.

Now cross-cast. Cross-casting means checking that the totals of the analysis columns add up to the total of the bank column, because every rand in the bank column must also appear in exactly one other column. Sales plus sundry: 27 600 + 112 650 = 140 250, which equals the bank total. The journal cross-casts correctly. The cost of sales column is left out of the cross-cast, because it is not money received.

If the totals do not cross-cast, there is an error: an amount was entered in the bank column but not in an analysis column, an amount was entered in the wrong column, or a total was added incorrectly. Find and correct it before posting.

The totals are written in the totals line, double-underlined, and the date of the totals line is the last day of the month. In the next lesson on the general ledger, the totals will be posted: bank to the debit of the bank account, sales to the credit of the sales account, cost of sales to the debit of cost of sales and credit of trading stock, and each sundry amount to the credit of its own account.

The error museum holds four mistakes. First, including cost of sales in the cross-cast; it is a memorandum column with no money received. Second, entering capital or a loan in the sales column; sales means only the sale of goods. Third, calculating cost of sales by taking 50 percent off the selling price; at a mark-up of 50 percent, divide by 150 and multiply by 100. Fourth, recording payments in the CRJ; money paid out belongs in the cash payments journal.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same ideas again, through a notebook for money coming in, a line-by-line walk through the columns, and a final check, in plain words with the same facts.

## Subtopic: A Notebook Just for Money Coming In

Imagine you run a hardware store. Money comes in every day: customers paying for nails and paint, a tenant paying rent for the back office, the bank lending you money. You need to write it all down somewhere.

So you keep a special notebook only for money coming in. That is the cash receipts journal, the CRJ. Money going out has its own notebook, the cash payments journal. Never mix them.

The notebook has columns, like a table. On the left: which piece of paper proves it, the day, and who paid you. Then the bank column: every rand that comes in is written here, because it all goes into the bank. Then, next to it, a column that says what kind of money it was. If it was from selling goods, it goes in the sales column. If it was anything else, capital, rent, a loan, interest, it goes in a column called sundry, which just means other, and you write what it was.

There is one extra column that is a bit strange: cost of sales. When you sell paint for R150, the paint cost you R100. The CRJ lets you write down that R100 as well, so you know how much stock left the shop. No money came in for that R100, so it is just a note, but a very important note.

Why use a notebook like this instead of writing every transaction into the accounts straight away? Because at the end of the month you can add up each column and carry one total to the accounts, instead of dozens of separate amounts. It saves time and makes mistakes easier to find.

## Subtopic: Filling In the Columns Line by Line

Let's fill in Ridgeview Hardware's notebook for March. Its mark-up is 50 percent on cost.

Day 1. The owner puts in R80 000. Proof: receipt 001. Bank: 80 000. It is not a sale, so sundry: 80 000, Capital.

Day 3. The till roll says cash sales of R12 000. Proof: CRR. Bank: 12 000. Sales: 12 000. Now the cost: 12 000 divided by 150, times 100, is 8 000. Cost of sales: 8 000.

Day 10. A tenant pays rent of R2 500. Receipt 002. Bank: 2 500. Sundry: 2 500, Rent income.

Day 15. Cash sales R9 000. Bank and sales: 9 000. Cost: 9 000 ÷ 150 × 100 = 6 000.

Day 20. The bank lends the shop R30 000. Bank: 30 000. Sundry: 30 000, Loan.

Day 25. Cash sales R6 600. Bank and sales: 6 600. Cost: 6 600 ÷ 150 × 100 = 4 400.

Day 31. The bank statement shows R150 interest earned. Bank: 150. Sundry: 150, Interest income.

Notice the pattern. Every line has a bank amount. Sales lines also have a sales amount and a cost of sales amount. All other lines have a sundry amount with a name.

## Subtopic: Do the Totals Agree?

At the end of the month, draw a line and add up each column.

Bank: 80 000 + 12 000 + 2 500 + 9 000 + 30 000 + 6 600 + 150 = 140 250.

Sales: 12 000 + 9 000 + 6 600 = 27 600.

Cost of sales: 8 000 + 6 000 + 4 400 = 18 400.

Sundry: 80 000 + 2 500 + 30 000 + 150 = 112 650.

Now the magic check, called cross-casting. Every rand in the bank column also sits in either sales or sundry. So sales plus sundry must equal bank. 27 600 + 112 650 = 140 250. It matches the bank total. The numbers never lie.

Leave cost of sales out of this check. It was only a note about stock, not money coming in.

If the totals do not agree, look for a line where you wrote the bank amount but forgot to put it in sales or sundry, or where you put it in two places. Fix it, add again, and only then carry the totals forward to the ledger.

A good habit is to check each line before you move on, not only the totals at the end. Ask: did I put this amount in the bank column? Did I put it in exactly one other money column, sales or sundry? If it was a sale, did I work out the cost of sales? Three small checks per line save a long search later.

Underline the totals twice. They are the numbers that will travel to the ledger in the next lesson.
