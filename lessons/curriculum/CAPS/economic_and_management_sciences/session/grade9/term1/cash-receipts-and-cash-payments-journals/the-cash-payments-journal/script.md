# Part 1 — Expert

This session sets out how to complete the cash payments journal, the CPJ, of a trading business, the companion of the cash receipts journal. The anchor is the column layout: document, day, name of payee, folio, bank, trading stock, wages and sundry accounts. The governing principle is the same as for the CRJ: every amount paid is entered in the bank column and in exactly one analysis column, so that the bank total equals the sum of the analysis totals. The example continues with Ridgeview Hardware for March, whose CRJ showed total receipts of R140 250.

## Subtopic: The Purpose and Format of the Cash Payments Journal

The cash payments journal is the book of first entry for all money paid out of the business bank account, whether by electronic funds transfer, debit order, card or, in older records, cheque. Like the CRJ, it is kept in date order, totalled at the end of the month and posted to the general ledger.

Its columns, from left to right, are: Document, the number of the source document, for example EFT 01 or the cheque number; Day; Name of payee, the person or business that received the payment; Folio, for posting to the creditors ledger later in the year; Bank, the amount paid; Trading stock, the cost of goods bought for resale; Wages, wages paid to employees; and Sundry accounts, with amount, folio and details columns, for every other kind of payment. Some businesses add other columns for payments that occur often, such as stationery or creditors control; in Grade 9 the standard layout has trading stock, wages and sundry. The heading reads, for example: Cash Payments Journal of Ridgeview Hardware, March, CPJ 1.

The source documents for the CPJ are the EFT payment confirmation, or proof of payment, generated by internet banking; the cheque counterfoil in older records; and the bank statement, for amounts the bank deducts directly, such as bank charges, debit orders and interest on overdrafts, which the business only discovers when it receives the statement. When trading stock is bought for cash, the cash invoice or till slip from the supplier is also kept, as evidence of what was bought.

Think of the CPJ as the credit side of the bank account written out in detail. Every entry reduces the bank balance. The column in which the amount also appears tells you which account will be debited.

Strong internal control is especially important for payments, because this is where money leaves the business. Common controls include requiring two people to authorise an EFT, paying only against an approved invoice, never paying cash for large amounts, and comparing the CPJ with the bank statement every month.

## Subtopic: Recording Purchases of Trading Stock and Wages

The two most frequent payments in a trading business are for trading stock and for wages, which is why each has its own column.

On 2 March, Ridgeview Hardware bought trading stock from a wholesaler and paid R20 000 by EFT. Document EFT 01. Name of payee: the wholesaler. Bank R20 000. Trading stock R20 000. The trading stock column is debited because the business now has more stock; the purchase is recorded at cost price, the amount paid to the supplier. No mark-up is involved when buying.

On 18 March, it bought further stock for R9 000 by EFT 05. Bank R9 000, trading stock R9 000.

On 12 March it paid wages of R6 000 by EFT 04, and on 26 March a further R6 000 by EFT 07. In each case: bank R6 000, wages R6 000. The payee details may read wages or the names of the employees.

Note what does not go in the trading stock column. Equipment bought for use in the business, such as a new shelving unit or a computer, is not trading stock, because it is not for resale. Stationery used in the office is not trading stock. Only goods the business intends to sell to customers are trading stock. A useful test: would this item appear on a customer's till slip? If yes, it is trading stock. If no, it belongs in sundry.

Also note what does not go in the wages column. Money the owner takes for personal use is never wages, even if the owner works in the business every day, because the owner is not an employee. It is drawings and goes in sundry. In a sole trader business, the owner's reward is the profit, and anything the owner takes out during the year is drawings against that profit.

Every amount in the trading stock column increases the trading stock asset, and every amount in the wages column increases the wages expense. At month end, their totals will be posted as one debit each.

## Subtopic: Recording Other Payments in the Sundry Accounts Column

All other payments go into the sundry accounts column, with the name of the account debited in the details column.

On 4 March, Ridgeview paid rent for the month of R5 000 by EFT 02 to its landlord. Bank R5 000. Sundry R5 000, details Rent expense. The account name rent expense distinguishes it from the rent income received from the tenant, which was recorded in the CRJ.

On 8 March, it bought a display shelving system, equipment, for R7 500 by EFT 03. Bank R7 500. Sundry R7 500, details Equipment.

On 22 March, the owner transferred R2 000 to a personal account for private use, EFT 06. Bank R2 000. Sundry R2 000, details Drawings.

On 29 March, it paid the telephone account of R850 by EFT 08. Bank R850. Sundry R850, details Telephone.

On 31 March, the bank statement showed bank charges of R240 deducted from the account. The source document is the bank statement, abbreviated B/S. Bank R240. Sundry R240, details Bank charges.

Other examples of sundry payments in a trading business are water and electricity, advertising, stationery, insurance, repairs, the repayment of a loan, and interest on a loan. When a loan is repaid, the details read Loan followed by the lender's name; this reduces a liability. When interest is paid on a loan, the details read Interest on loan; this is an expense.

Consider how a loan repayment with interest is recorded, because it is a common examination item. Suppose that, in a later month, Ridgeview pays the bank R3 300 by EFT, made up of R3 000 to reduce the loan and R300 interest on the loan. These are two different accounts, so they are entered on two lines with one document number, or on one line in the bank column with two sundry amounts: sundry R3 000, details Loan, and sundry R300, details Interest on loan. The bank column shows the single amount paid, R3 300, because that is what left the account: 3 000 + 300 = 3 300. The loan account, a liability, decreases by R3 000, and the interest on loan account, an expense, increases by R300. Only the interest reduces the profit; the capital repayment simply reduces what is owed.

As in the CRJ, each sundry amount is posted individually to the debit side of its own account in the general ledger, and the sundry folio column records where it was posted.

## Subtopic: Totalling, Cross-casting and the Bank Balance

At the end of March, the CPJ is ruled off and totalled.

Bank: 20 000 + 5 000 + 7 500 + 6 000 + 9 000 + 2 000 + 6 000 + 850 + 240 = 56 590, or R56 590.

Trading stock: 20 000 + 9 000 = 29 000, or R29 000.

Wages: 6 000 + 6 000 = 12 000, or R12 000.

Sundry: 5 000 + 7 500 + 2 000 + 850 + 240 = 15 590, or R15 590.

Cross-cast: trading stock plus wages plus sundry must equal bank. 29 000 + 12 000 + 15 590 = 56 590. It agrees with the bank total, so every payment has been analysed into exactly one column. In the CPJ there is no memorandum column, so every analysis column is included in the cross-cast.

Now the two cash journals can be combined to find the bank balance. Ridgeview's total receipts in the CRJ were R140 250 and its total payments in the CPJ were R56 590. The balance in the bank at the end of March is 140 250 - 56 590 = 83 660, or R83 660. A positive balance is called a favourable balance; it is an asset. If payments had exceeded receipts, the bank balance would be unfavourable, an overdraft, which is a liability.

That bank balance should agree with the bank statement, provided that all transactions have been recorded and processed by the bank. Any difference, for example an EFT not yet cleared, is investigated through a bank reconciliation, which you will learn in Grade 10.

We can also see what happened to trading stock during March. The business bought stock costing R29 000 and the CRJ showed that stock costing R18 400 was sold. The stock that should be on hand at the end of the month is 29 000 - 18 400 = 10 600, or R10 600. A physical count of the shelves should confirm this figure.

The error museum holds four mistakes. First, putting equipment in the trading stock column; equipment is not for resale, so it goes in sundry. Second, recording the owner's withdrawals as wages; the owner is not an employee, so it is drawings. Third, forgetting bank charges until the end; they come from the bank statement and must be recorded. Fourth, calling the rent payment rent income; payments are expenses, so the details read rent expense.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same ideas again, through a notebook for money going out, a home for every payment, and the bank balance, in plain words with the same facts.

## Subtopic: A Notebook Just for Money Going Out

Last lesson we had a notebook for money coming in, the CRJ. Today the shop gets its second notebook, only for money going out. That is the cash payments journal, the CPJ.

Every time Ridgeview Hardware pays someone, whether by an EFT on the computer, by a debit order, or because the bank took a fee, it goes in the CPJ. Each line says: which piece of paper proves it, the day, who was paid, and the amount in the bank column. Every single payment has a bank amount, because all the money comes out of the bank.

Then, next to the bank amount, there is a column to say what the money was for. The shop pays for two things very often: stock to sell, and wages for its workers. So those get their own columns, trading stock and wages. Everything else, rent, equipment, telephone, bank charges, the owner taking money for herself, goes in the sundry column, with its name written next to it.

The proof for most payments today is the EFT proof of payment that the banking app or website gives you. For fees the bank takes by itself, the proof is the bank statement, because that is where the shop first sees them.

Here is a way to remember the difference between the two notebooks. In the CRJ, the question is: where did this money come from? From sales, or from something else, sundry. In the CPJ, the question is: what did this money buy? Stock, workers' time, or something else, sundry. Same shape of notebook, opposite direction of money.

Because money leaves the business here, shops are very careful. Often two people must approve a payment before it goes out. That stops mistakes and stops anyone from quietly paying themselves.

## Subtopic: Every Payment Needs a Home

Let's give each of Ridgeview's March payments a home.

Day 2. Stock bought from the wholesaler for R20 000. Bank 20 000, trading stock 20 000. Is it something a customer will buy? Yes, so it is trading stock.

Day 4. Rent for the shop, R5 000. Bank 5 000, sundry 5 000, Rent expense.

Day 8. New shelves for the shop, R7 500. Will a customer buy the shelves? No, the shop keeps them. So they are equipment: sundry 7 500, Equipment.

Day 12. Wages R6 000. Bank 6 000, wages 6 000.

Day 18. More stock, R9 000. Bank and trading stock 9 000.

Day 22. The owner sends R2 000 to her own account. Is she an employee? No. So it is not wages. It is drawings: sundry 2 000, Drawings.

Day 26. Wages again, R6 000.

Day 29. Telephone account R850: sundry, Telephone.

Day 31. The bank statement shows R240 bank charges: sundry, Bank charges.

Two questions find the right home. Will a customer buy it? Then trading stock. Is it pay for an employee? Then wages. Anything else goes in sundry with its name.

## Subtopic: Money In Minus Money Out

Add up the columns.

Bank: 20 000 + 5 000 + 7 500 + 6 000 + 9 000 + 2 000 + 6 000 + 850 + 240 = 56 590.

Trading stock: 20 000 + 9 000 = 29 000. Wages: 6 000 + 6 000 = 12 000. Sundry: 5 000 + 7 500 + 2 000 + 850 + 240 = 15 590.

Check: 29 000 + 12 000 + 15 590 = 56 590. It matches the bank total. This time there is no note column to leave out; every column is in the check.

Now put the two notebooks together. Money in for March, from the CRJ: R140 250. Money out, from the CPJ: R56 590. Money left in the bank: 140 250 - 56 590 = 83 660. Ridgeview has R83 660 in the bank at the end of March.

And the stock? The shop bought stock that cost R29 000, and sold stock that cost R18 400. So there should be 29 000 - 18 400 = 10 600 worth of stock left on the shelves.

What if the check does not match? Say your three columns add up to 56 350 but the bank column says 56 590. The difference is 56 590 - 56 350 = 240. Is there a payment of R240? Yes, the bank charges. You probably wrote it in the bank column but forgot to put it in sundry. Finding the difference first, and then looking for a payment of exactly that size, is the fastest way to find the mistake.

Those two numbers, the bank balance and the stock left, are exactly what the ledger will show in the next lesson. The notebooks feed the ledger, and the ledger tells the whole story.
