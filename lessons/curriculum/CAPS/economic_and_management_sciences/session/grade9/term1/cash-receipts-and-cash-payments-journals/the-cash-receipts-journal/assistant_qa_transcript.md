### subtopic_1 — The Purpose and Format of the Cash Receipts Journal
**Assistant:** Why record receipts in a journal first instead of writing them straight into the ledger accounts?

**Tutor:** There are three good reasons: efficiency, order and division of work. Efficiency first. A trading business may have dozens of cash sales entries in a month. If each were posted directly to the ledger, the bank account and the sales account would each need dozens of lines. By recording the receipts in the CRJ, the business can total each column at the end of the month and post one figure for bank, one for sales and one for cost of sales. That saves a great deal of writing and reduces the opportunities for copying errors. Second, order. The journal records every transaction in date order with its source document number, so it gives a complete, chronological record of all money received. If someone later asks what happened on 15 March, the CRJ answers it immediately, and the document number leads to the evidence. The ledger, by contrast, is organised by account, so the details of a single transaction are split between two accounts. Third, division of work and control. In a larger business, one clerk keeps the CRJ, another keeps the CPJ, and a senior bookkeeper posts to the ledger and checks the totals. Separating these tasks makes it harder for one person to make or hide an error or a theft, which is an important internal control. The journal is also where the first check happens: cross-casting proves that every amount in the bank column has been analysed into exactly one other column. Only when the journal is correct are the totals posted to the ledger. That is why journals are called books of first entry, and the ledger is the book of final entry.

### subtopic_2 — Recording Cash Sales and Cost of Sales
**Assistant:** Why is the cost of sales column not included when we cross-cast the CRJ?

**Tutor:** Because cross-casting checks that the money received has been fully analysed, and cost of sales is not money received. Think about what the bank column represents: every rand deposited into the business bank account. Each of those rands came from somewhere, either from selling goods or from another source, so each one is also entered once in the sales column or once in the sundry column. That is why the total of bank must equal the total of sales plus the total of sundry. It is a test that no amount has been missed or double-counted. The cost of sales column is different. It records a second, separate event that happens at the moment of the sale: goods leave the shelf, so the trading stock asset falls and the cost of sales expense rises. No money changes hands in that event. The business is simply recording that it no longer has the goods, valued at what it paid for them. Because the amount is not part of the money received, adding it to the cross-cast would make the totals disagree. In Ridgeview's journal, sales of R27 600 and sundry of R112 650 add to R140 250, the bank total. If cost of sales of R18 400 were added, the analysis columns would total R158 650, which does not match, even though nothing is wrong. So cost of sales is called a memorandum column: it is a note carried in the CRJ for convenience, because that is where the sales are recorded, and its total is posted separately, as a debit to cost of sales and a credit to trading stock.

### subtopic_3 — Recording Other Receipts in the Sundry Accounts Column
**Assistant:** Why does each sundry amount get posted separately while the sales total is posted as one figure?

**Tutor:** Because all the amounts in the sales column belong to one account, while the amounts in the sundry column belong to different accounts. Every figure in the sales column is income from selling goods, so they can be added together and credited to the sales account in one line. The same applies to bank and cost of sales. The sundry column is a mixture. In Ridgeview's March journal it contains R80 000 for capital, R2 500 for rent income, R30 000 for a loan and R150 for interest income. Those are four different accounts: capital is owner's equity, the loan is a liability, and rent income and interest income are two separate income accounts. Their total, R112 650, has no meaning as a single figure; there is no account called sundry to which it could be posted. So each amount is posted on its own to the credit side of its own account: R80 000 to capital, R2 500 to rent income, R30 000 to the loan account and R150 to interest income. The total of the sundry column is still calculated, but only so that the journal can be cross-cast. When posting, the folio column in the sundry section is filled in with the folio of the ledger account, B1 for capital, for example. That folio shows that the amount has been posted and where it went, creating an audit trail in both directions. If a receipt occurs often enough, such as receipts from debtors once credit sales begin, the business creates a separate column for it, so that it too can be posted as a single total.

### subtopic_4 — Totalling, Cross-casting and Checking the CRJ
**Assistant:** If the CRJ cross-casts correctly, does that prove every entry is right?

**Tutor:** No, and it is important to know what the check can and cannot find. Cross-casting proves that every amount entered in the bank column has also been entered in exactly one analysis column, sales or sundry, and that the columns have been added correctly. It will catch an amount written in bank but forgotten in sales, an amount entered twice, and most addition errors. But some errors leave the totals in agreement. If a cash sale of R9 000 were recorded in both bank and sales as R900, the journal would still cross-cast, because the same wrong figure appears in both columns. If capital were wrongly entered in the sales column instead of the sundry column, the journal would still cross-cast, because the money is still analysed into one column, just the wrong one; the error would only show up as overstated sales. If a whole receipt were left out, the journal would still cross-cast. And if the cost of sales were miscalculated, the cross-cast would not detect it at all, because cost of sales is not part of the check. So cross-casting is a necessary check but not a complete one. Other controls catch the rest. The bank column is compared with the bank statement, which is called a bank reconciliation in Grade 10. Each entry is checked against its source document, and the document numbers are checked for gaps. Cost of sales can be verified by recalculating from the mark-up. And the trial balance later checks that the ledger balances. Accountants rely on several independent checks for this reason: each one catches errors the others miss.
