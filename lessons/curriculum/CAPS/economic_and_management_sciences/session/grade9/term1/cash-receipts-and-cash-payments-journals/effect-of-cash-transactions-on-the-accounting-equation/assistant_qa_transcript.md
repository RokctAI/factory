### subtopic_1 — The Accounting Equation and Its Three Elements
**Assistant:** Why must the accounting equation always balance, even after hundreds of transactions?

**Tutor:** Because the two sides describe the same thing from two directions. The left side lists the resources of the business: its assets. The right side lists where those resources came from: the owner, through capital and retained profit, and outsiders, through liabilities. There is no third source. Every rand of assets the business has was either provided by the owner, earned by the business and therefore belonging to the owner, or borrowed from someone who expects it back. So the total of assets must equal the total of owner's equity plus liabilities at every moment. Now think about what a transaction does. It always has two sides, which is why we call the system double entry. If the business receives a loan, it gains an asset, money, and gains a liability, the debt. If it buys equipment for cash, it gains one asset and loses another. If it pays wages, it loses an asset, money, and the owner's equity falls because an expense was incurred. In every case the two effects keep the equation equal. A transaction cannot change one side alone, because that would mean a resource appeared from nowhere or disappeared without trace. That is why the equation is so useful as a check. After hundreds of transactions, if the totals do not agree, at least one recording is wrong: a figure was entered once instead of twice, a plus became a minus, or an amount was copied incorrectly. In Grade 9 you will see the same principle at work in the general ledger, where every debit has an equal credit, and in the trial balance, where the total of the debit balances must equal the total of the credit balances.

### subtopic_2 — Analysing Cash Transactions Step by Step
**Assistant:** When the business buys equipment for cash, why does the total of assets stay the same?

**Tutor:** Because the business has simply exchanged one asset for another of equal value. Before the purchase it had R8 000 in the bank. After the purchase it has R8 000 less in the bank and equipment worth R8 000. Its total resources are unchanged; only their form has changed, from money into a machine. So in the equation, you record assets plus R8 000 for Equipment and assets minus R8 000 for Bank. The net effect on assets is zero, and nothing on the owner's equity or liabilities side is affected, so the equation still balances. This is an important point to understand, because learners often feel that spending money must make the business poorer. Spending money on an asset does not make it poorer, because it receives something of the same value that it keeps. Spending money on an expense does make the business poorer, because the resource is used up: the rent paid for this month is gone and cannot be sold again. That is why paying rent reduces owner's equity, while buying equipment does not. You can test this with the question: does the business still have something to show for the money? If yes, it is an asset exchange. If no, it is an expense, or drawings if the owner took the benefit. Over time, equipment does wear out, and in Grade 10 you will learn to record that wearing out as an expense called depreciation. In Grade 9, record the purchase as a plus and a minus on the asset side, give the reason for each, and check that total assets have not moved.

### subtopic_3 — Income, Expenses and Drawings in the Equation
**Assistant:** Drawings and expenses both reduce owner's equity, so why is it wrong to call drawings an expense?

**Tutor:** Because they are reductions for completely different reasons, and the difference affects the profit of the business. An expense is a cost incurred by the business in order to earn income. Rent is paid so the shop has premises to trade from; wages are paid so that staff can serve customers; electricity is paid so the lights and till work. These costs are part of running the business, and they are subtracted from income to calculate net profit. Drawings are assets taken out by the owner for personal use: cash for groceries, trading stock taken home, or money paid from the business account for the owner's private car. They do nothing to help the business earn income. They are simply the owner taking back part of what belongs to the owner. If drawings were treated as an expense, the profit of the business would be understated. Suppose the shop has income of R20 000, expenses of R12 000 and the owner draws R3 000. The correct net profit is 20 000 - 12 000 = 8 000. If the drawings were wrongly treated as an expense, the profit would appear to be 20 000 - 15 000 = 5 000, which misrepresents how well the business performed. Owner's equity ends up the same either way, but the information is wrong, and anyone judging the business, a bank considering a loan or SARS assessing tax, would be misled. So in the equation, drawings reduce owner's equity with the reason drawings, and in the ledger drawings have their own account, separate from the expense accounts, and it is closed off to capital rather than to profit.

### subtopic_4 — Checking That the Equation Balances
**Assistant:** What is the quickest way to find an error when my equation does not balance?

**Tutor:** Work row by row, because each row should balance on its own. In the table of transactions, every row shows the effect on assets, owner's equity and liabilities. For each row, the change in assets must equal the change in owner's equity plus the change in liabilities. If a row does not balance, the error is in that row. Common culprits are a missing second effect, for example recording the loan as assets plus R20 000 but forgetting liabilities plus R20 000, or a wrong sign, such as recording a rent payment as owner's equity plus instead of minus. If every row balances but the totals still disagree, the error is in your adding up. Recalculate each total in a column and compare. A useful check is the difference between the two sides. If the difference is exactly an amount that appears in the transactions, you have probably omitted or double-counted that transaction. If the difference is twice an amount, you probably used the wrong sign: a figure that should have been subtracted was added, which moves the total by double the amount. If the difference divides exactly by 9, you may have swapped two digits, writing 54 500 as 45 500, for example; the difference, 9 000, is divisible by 9. These checks come from long experience of auditors and bookkeepers, and they work equally well in the trial balance later this term. Finally, prevent errors by working neatly: one transaction per row, the two accounts named, the plus or minus sign and amount in each column, and the reason in words.
