### subtopic_1 — The Purpose and Format of the Cash Payments Journal
**Assistant:** Why are bank charges recorded from the bank statement rather than from a document the business creates?

**Tutor:** Because the business does not initiate bank charges; the bank deducts them directly from the account, so the first evidence the business receives is the bank statement. For most payments, the business itself creates the source document: it makes an EFT and receives a proof of payment, or in older records it writes a cheque and keeps the counterfoil. That document is recorded in the CPJ on the day of payment. Bank charges, monthly account fees, transaction fees, cash deposit fees and interest on an overdraft are different. The bank calculates and deducts them according to the account agreement, and they appear on the statement. Debit orders work similarly: the business has authorised a supplier, such as an insurer, to collect a fixed amount each month, and the deduction appears on the statement. So when the statement arrives, the bookkeeper compares it with the CPJ and CRJ, line by line. Any amount that the bank has processed but the business has not recorded, such as bank charges, debit orders or interest earned, is entered in the journals using the bank statement as the source document, usually on the last day of the month. The document column shows B/S. This is a first step towards the bank reconciliation you will learn in Grade 10, where the business reconciles its own bank balance with the balance on the statement. It also matters for internal control: comparing the statement with the journals each month detects unauthorised debit orders, fraudulent payments and errors by the bank or the business. A business that never reads its bank statement can lose money for months without noticing.

### subtopic_2 — Recording Purchases of Trading Stock and Wages
**Assistant:** Why can the money a sole trader owner takes never be recorded as wages, even if she works full-time in the shop?

**Tutor:** Because wages are payments to employees, and the owner of a sole trader is not an employee of her own business. Employment is a relationship between two parties: an employer and an employee, with a contract, agreed hours and a wage. A sole trader business is not a separate legal person from its owner; the owner and the business are, in law, the same person, even though in accounting we keep their records separate under the business entity concept. She cannot employ herself. Her reward for working in and owning the business is the profit, which belongs entirely to her. When she transfers money from the business to her personal account during the year, she is taking out some of the resources she owns, in advance of or in addition to the profit. That is drawings, which reduces owner's equity but is not an expense. If drawings were wrongly recorded as wages, the expenses of the business would be overstated, the net profit understated, and anyone reading the accounts would get a false picture of how well the business performs. It would also misstate the tax position, because SARS taxes the owner of a sole trader on the profit of the business as part of her personal income, and drawings are not deductible. Things are different in a company, which is a separate legal person: a director who works for the company can be an employee and receive a salary as an expense of the company. You will meet that distinction in Grade 10. In Grade 9, the rule is simple: money the owner takes is drawings, in the sundry column, every time.

### subtopic_3 — Recording Other Payments in the Sundry Accounts Column
**Assistant:** Why does buying shelving go in sundry as equipment, but buying shelving for a furniture shop goes in trading stock?

**Tutor:** Because the classification depends on why the business acquires the item, not on what the item is. Trading stock is defined as goods bought for the purpose of resale. Equipment is an asset bought to be used in running the business over several years. A hardware store that buys a shelving system to display its paint and tools intends to keep and use those shelves; it will never sell them to a customer. So for the hardware store, the shelves are equipment, a non-current asset, recorded in the sundry column with the details equipment, and they will appear in the equipment account in the ledger. A furniture shop that buys the same shelving units to sell to customers intends to resell them at a mark-up. For that shop, they are trading stock, recorded in the trading stock column, and when sold, their cost becomes cost of sales. The same reasoning applies to many items. A computer bought by a stationery shop for its office is equipment; a computer bought by a computer shop for its display floor is trading stock. A delivery bakkie bought by a hardware store is a vehicle; the same bakkie bought by a car dealer is trading stock. Even stationery depends on the business: paper used in the office of a hardware store is an expense, stationery, while paper bought by a stationery shop to sell is trading stock. In an examination, read the description of the business at the top of the question carefully, because it tells you what that business sells. Then ask the test question: is this business going to sell this item to a customer?

### subtopic_4 — Totalling, Cross-casting and the Bank Balance
**Assistant:** How can a business have money in the bank but still have a problem with cash flow?

**Tutor:** Because the bank balance is a snapshot of one moment, and what matters for survival is whether money will be available when payments fall due. Ridgeview Hardware ends March with R83 660 in the bank, which looks healthy. But look at where it came from. R80 000 was the owner's capital and R30 000 was a loan, which must be repaid with interest. Those are not earnings from trading; they are funds the business was given or borrowed to get started. Its cash sales for the month were R27 600, while it paid R29 000 for stock, R12 000 for wages and R15 590 for other items, including R7 500 for equipment that will not need to be bought again soon. In its first month a business usually spends heavily to set itself up, so it pays out more on trading activities than it receives. If, month after month, the business pays more for stock, wages, rent and expenses than it collects from sales, the bank balance will fall steadily, and when the loan repayments start it could run out of cash, even if it is technically making a profit. Cash flow problems are made worse by credit sales, which you will meet in Term 2: goods are sold, but the money arrives weeks later. That is why accountants distinguish between profit, which measures income earned minus expenses incurred, and cash, which measures money actually received and paid. Both matter. A business can be profitable and still fail because it runs out of cash to pay its suppliers and staff. In Grade 10 and later you will prepare cash budgets that forecast receipts and payments month by month, precisely to prevent this.
