# Part 1 — Expert

This session revises the Grade 8 work on the accounting equation and the effect of cash transactions on it, as preparation for recording the transactions of a trading business in Grade 9. The anchor is the accounting equation itself: assets equal owner's equity plus liabilities, written A = OE + L. The governing principle is double entry: every transaction affects at least two items, and after every transaction the equation must still balance. Every figure in this lesson is traceable, and every total is checked.

## Subtopic: The Accounting Equation and Its Three Elements

Every business has resources, and every resource has been provided by someone. The accounting equation expresses exactly that: the total of what the business owns equals the total of the claims against it. Written in full, assets equal owner's equity plus liabilities, A = OE + L.

Assets are the possessions of the business, resources it owns or controls that have value and will bring future benefits. Examples are land and buildings, vehicles, equipment, trading stock, money in the bank and the cash float in the till. Assets are divided into non-current assets, which the business intends to keep for longer than a year, such as vehicles and equipment, and current assets, which are cash or will be turned into cash within a year, such as trading stock, bank and cash.

Owner's equity is the owner's claim on the business. It starts with capital, the money or other assets that the owner contributes. It increases when the business earns profit, through income, and it decreases when the business incurs expenses or when the owner takes drawings for personal use.

Liabilities are the debts of the business: amounts it owes to outsiders who are not the owner. A loan from a bank is a liability. Later this year you will meet creditors, suppliers to whom the business owes money for goods bought on credit, which are also liabilities.

The equation is a statement of balance. On the left are the resources. On the right are the sources of those resources: the owner's contribution and accumulated profit, and what was borrowed from outsiders. Because every rand of assets must have come from one of those two sources, the two sides are always equal. That is why the equation can be used to check the accuracy of every recording: if it does not balance after a transaction, an error has been made.

The business is treated as a separate entity from its owner. This is the business entity concept. When the owner puts money into the business, the business receives an asset and owes the owner that amount as capital. When the owner takes money out for personal use, it is drawings, not an expense of the business.

## Subtopic: Analysing Cash Transactions Step by Step

To analyse any transaction, follow the same four steps in order. Step one: identify the two accounts affected. Step two: decide which element each account belongs to, assets, owner's equity or liabilities. Step three: decide whether each one increases or decreases and by how much. Step four: state the reason, and check that the equation still balances.

Consider a new business, a stationery shop, and its first transactions.

Transaction one: the owner deposits R50 000 into the business bank account as capital. The accounts are Bank and Capital. Bank is an asset; it increases by R50 000 because cash was received. Capital is owner's equity; it increases by R50 000 because the owner contributed capital. Assets plus R50 000, owner's equity plus R50 000. Both sides rise equally; the equation balances.

Transaction two: the business obtains a loan of R20 000 from a bank, which is deposited in its bank account. The accounts are Bank and Loan. Bank, an asset, increases by R20 000, because money was received. Loan, a liability, increases by R20 000, because the business now owes the bank. Assets plus R20 000, liabilities plus R20 000.

Transaction three: the business buys equipment for R8 000 and pays by electronic funds transfer, EFT. The accounts are Equipment and Bank. Equipment, an asset, increases by R8 000, because equipment was bought. Bank, an asset, decreases by R8 000, because money was paid. Assets plus R8 000 and minus R8 000: the total of assets does not change, and nothing on the right side changes. This is an exchange of one asset for another, and the equation still balances.

Transaction four: the business repays R5 000 of the loan by EFT. The accounts are Loan and Bank. Loan, a liability, decreases by R5 000, because part of the debt was repaid. Bank, an asset, decreases by R5 000, because money was paid. Assets minus R5 000, liabilities minus R5 000.

Notice the pattern. A transaction can increase both sides, decrease both sides, or increase and decrease items on the same side. What it can never do is change one side only. The equation always balances. That is the numbers telling the truth, and it is the foundation of double entry.

Each transaction is supported by a source document. Money received is supported by a duplicate receipt or a cash register roll for cash sales, or by the bank statement for an EFT received. Money paid is supported by a cheque counterfoil or, more commonly now, by an EFT payment confirmation or the bank statement. A deposit slip shows money deposited into the bank.

## Subtopic: Income, Expenses and Drawings in the Equation

Income, expenses and drawings all affect owner's equity, because they change what the owner can claim.

Income increases owner's equity. When the business receives income, such as rent income from subletting part of its premises, or interest on a bank balance, the business is richer and so is the owner. Transaction five: the business receives rent income of R1 500 in cash and deposits it. Bank, an asset, increases by R1 500, because money was received. Owner's equity increases by R1 500, because income was earned. Assets plus R1 500, owner's equity plus R1 500.

Expenses decrease owner's equity. When the business pays an expense, such as rent, wages, telephone or water and electricity, it uses up resources to earn income, and the owner's claim falls. Transaction six: the business pays rent of R3 000 by EFT. Bank, an asset, decreases by R3 000, because money was paid. Owner's equity decreases by R3 000, because an expense was incurred. Assets minus R3 000, owner's equity minus R3 000.

Drawings decrease owner's equity, but they are not an expense. Drawings are assets taken by the owner for personal use: cash, trading stock or other assets. Transaction seven: the owner withdraws R1 000 cash for personal use. Bank, an asset, decreases by R1 000, because money was paid out. Owner's equity decreases by R1 000, because of drawings by the owner. The reason must say drawings, not expense. An expense is incurred to earn income for the business; drawings are for the owner's private benefit and earn the business nothing.

Why does income go to owner's equity and not somewhere else? Because profit belongs to the owner. At the end of the financial year, income minus expenses gives the net profit, which is added to the owner's capital. During the year, accountants record income and expenses in separate accounts, called nominal accounts, so that the profit can be calculated; but in the accounting equation, their effect is shown directly on owner's equity.

For a trading business there is one more transaction type, which you will study in detail next: the sale of goods. When goods that cost R600 are sold for R900 cash, Bank increases by R900, Trading stock decreases by R600 because the goods have left the business at cost, and owner's equity increases by the difference, 900 - 600 = 300, the profit on the sale.

## Subtopic: Checking That the Equation Balances

After a series of transactions, the equation can be checked by adding up each element. Take the seven transactions of the stationery shop.

The bank account received R50 000 capital, R20 000 loan and R1 500 rent income, and paid R8 000 for equipment, R5 000 loan repayment, R3 000 rent and R1 000 drawings. The bank balance is 50 000 + 20 000 + 1 500 - 8 000 - 5 000 - 3 000 - 1 000 = 54 500. Equipment is R8 000. Total assets are 54 500 + 8 000 = 62 500, or R62 500.

Owner's equity began with R50 000 capital, increased by R1 500 income, and decreased by R3 000 rent expense and R1 000 drawings: 50 000 + 1 500 - 3 000 - 1 000 = 47 500, or R47 500.

The loan was R20 000, less R5 000 repaid: 20 000 - 5 000 = 15 000, or R15 000 of liabilities.

Owner's equity plus liabilities is 47 500 + 15 000 = 62 500, which equals total assets of R62 500. The equation balances: A = OE + L becomes 62 500 = 47 500 + 15 000.

The examination usually presents this as a table with columns for the transaction number, the account debited or the asset affected, and the effect on assets, owner's equity and liabilities, plus a column for the reason. Use a plus or minus sign and the amount in each column, and write 0 where an element is not affected. In each row, the change in assets must equal the change in owner's equity plus the change in liabilities. Check every row as you go.

The error museum holds four mistakes. First, recording drawings as an expense; drawings reduce owner's equity, but the reason is drawings, not expense. Second, recording a loan as income; a loan must be repaid, so it is a liability. Third, changing only one side for a purchase of equipment; buying an asset for cash is plus and minus on the asset side. Fourth, treating the owner's personal purchases as business transactions; the business entity concept keeps them separate.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same ideas again, through a simple question, a plus-minus game and a final count, in plain words with the same facts.

## Subtopic: What the Business Has and Who Paid for It

Imagine a new shop that sells stationery. On the left side of a page, write everything the shop has: money in the bank, shelves, a till, pens and paper to sell. Those are assets.

Now ask: who paid for all of that? Some came from the owner, who put in her own money. That is owner's equity. Some came from the bank, which lent money that must be paid back. That is a liability, a debt.

So the page has two sides. Left: what the business has. Right: who paid for it. And the two sides must be equal, because every single thing the business has was paid for by someone. Assets equal owner's equity plus liabilities. A = OE + L.

There is one more idea that makes everything easier. The shop and the owner are two different people in accounting. If the owner puts R50 000 into the shop, the shop now has R50 000, and it owes her R50 000 as capital. If she takes R1 000 home for groceries, that is not a shop expense. It is called drawings, because she drew money out for herself.

Owner's equity goes up when the owner puts money in and when the shop earns income. It goes down when the shop pays expenses and when the owner takes drawings. Liabilities go up when the shop borrows and down when it pays back.

## Subtopic: The Plus-Minus Game

Here is a game. For every transaction, find two things that change, and say plus or minus for each.

The owner puts in R50 000. Bank goes plus R50 000. Owner's equity goes plus R50 000. Both sides up.

The bank lends R20 000. Bank goes plus R20 000. Liabilities go plus R20 000. Both sides up.

The shop buys equipment for R8 000 and pays from the bank. Equipment plus R8 000. Bank minus R8 000. Both on the assets side, so the total does not change. The shop swapped money for a machine.

The shop pays back R5 000 of the loan. Bank minus R5 000. Liabilities minus R5 000. Both sides down.

The shop receives R1 500 rent from someone using its back room. Bank plus R1 500. Owner's equity plus R1 500, because income makes the owner richer.

The shop pays R3 000 rent for its premises. Bank minus R3 000. Owner's equity minus R3 000, because an expense makes the owner poorer.

The owner takes R1 000 home. Bank minus R1 000. Owner's equity minus R1 000. And the reason is drawings.

Now sell something. Pens that cost the shop R600 are sold for R900 cash. Bank plus R900. Trading stock minus R600, because the pens are gone. Owner's equity plus R300, because that is the profit.

The rule of the game: after every move, the left side must change by the same amount as the right side.

## Subtopic: Adding Up Both Sides

At the end of the game, add up each side to check.

Start with the bank. Money in: 50 000, then 20 000, then 1 500. Money out: 8 000, 5 000, 3 000 and 1 000. So the bank has 50 000 + 20 000 + 1 500 - 8 000 - 5 000 - 3 000 - 1 000 = 54 500. Add the equipment of R8 000, and the shop has assets of 54 500 + 8 000 = 62 500.

Now owner's equity. The owner put in 50 000. Add the rent income of 1 500. Take away the rent expense of 3 000 and the drawings of 1 000. So 50 000 + 1 500 - 3 000 - 1 000 = 47 500.

Now liabilities. The loan was 20 000 and 5 000 was paid back: 20 000 - 5 000 = 15 000.

Right side: 47 500 + 15 000 = 62 500. Left side: 62 500. They match. The numbers never lie.

Notice something about the numbers. The bank balance of R54 500 is not the same as owner's equity of R47 500. That surprises many learners. The bank holds money from the owner and money from the loan, and some of it has been turned into equipment. So you cannot look at the bank balance and know what the owner owns. You need the whole equation.

If your two sides do not match, do not panic. Go back row by row and check that each transaction changed both sides by the same amount. Usually one plus should have been a minus, or one transaction was put on only one side. Fix that row and add up again.
