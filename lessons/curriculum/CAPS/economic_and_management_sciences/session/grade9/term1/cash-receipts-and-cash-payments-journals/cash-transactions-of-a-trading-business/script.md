# Part 1 — Expert

This session introduces the trading business, a business that buys goods and sells them again at a profit, and the cash transactions it records. The anchor ideas are cost price, mark-up, selling price, cost of sales and gross profit, and the rule that every cash sale in a trading business has two effects: the sale at selling price and the outflow of trading stock at cost price. The governing principle is that trading stock is an asset until it is sold, and only when it is sold does its cost become an expense, cost of sales. Every figure is calculated and checked.

## Subtopic: What Makes a Trading Business Different

In Grade 8 you recorded the transactions of a service business, such as a hairdresser or a plumber, which earns income by providing a service. In Grade 9 the focus moves to a trading business, which earns income by buying goods and reselling them. A spaza shop, a supermarket, a clothing store, a hardware store and a cellphone shop are trading businesses. The goods they buy in order to sell are called trading stock.

Trading stock is an asset, a current asset, because it belongs to the business and will be turned into cash when it is sold. When a trading business buys stock, it is not yet an expense; the business has simply exchanged money for goods of the same value. When the stock is sold, two things happen. The business receives income from sales at the selling price, and the stock leaves the business at the price it was bought for, its cost price. The cost price of the goods sold is an expense called cost of sales. The difference between sales and cost of sales is gross profit.

In Grade 9 the business uses a perpetual inventory system. This means that the trading stock account is updated with every purchase and every sale, so that at any moment the business knows the cost of stock it should have on hand, and cost of sales is recorded at the time of each sale. Modern tills and point-of-sale software in South African shops work in exactly this way: when a cashier scans a barcode, the system records the sale and reduces the stock.

A trading business also has other income and expenses, just like a service business. It may earn rent income or interest income, and it pays expenses such as wages, rent, water and electricity, telephone, advertising, stationery and bank charges. Net profit is gross profit plus other income minus these operating expenses.

The owner of a sole trader runs the business alone, provides the capital, takes the risk, receives all the profit and is personally responsible for its debts. Most small trading businesses in South Africa, from spaza shops to hardware stores, start as sole traders. In Grade 10 you will meet partnerships and companies.

## Subtopic: Cost Price, Mark-up, Selling Price and Gross Profit

The cost price is the price the business pays its supplier for the goods. The selling price is the price the customer pays. The business adds a mark-up to the cost price to arrive at the selling price. Mark-up is usually expressed as a percentage of the cost price.

Selling price equals cost price plus mark-up. If a shop buys a school bag for R600 and marks it up by 50 percent, the mark-up is 600 × 0,5 = 300, so the selling price is 600 + 300 = 900, or R900.

When the shop sells that bag for R900 cash, sales are R900, cost of sales is R600 and gross profit is 900 - 600 = 300, or R300. Gross profit on a single item equals its mark-up.

A second example: a hardware store marks up its goods by 40 percent on cost. Goods costing R2 500 are sold for 2 500 × 1,4 = 3 500, or R3 500. The gross profit is 3 500 - 2 500 = 1 000, or R1 000.

Often the selling price is known and the cost price must be found. If goods are sold for R1 200 at a mark-up of 50 percent on cost, then the selling price is 150 percent of the cost price, because cost is 100 percent and mark-up is 50 percent. Cost price equals 1 200 × 100 ÷ 150 = 800, or R800. Check: 800 + 400 = 1 200. A common error is to take 50 percent off the selling price, giving R600, which is wrong, because the mark-up is calculated on cost, not on selling price.

Similarly, if the total sales for a day are R7 000 at a mark-up of 40 percent on cost, the cost of sales is 7 000 × 100 ÷ 140 = 5 000, or R5 000, and the gross profit is 7 000 - 5 000 = 2 000, or R2 000. You will use this calculation repeatedly when completing the cash receipts journal, where every cash sale has a cost of sales figure next to it.

The mark-up percentage itself can be calculated as gross profit divided by cost price, multiplied by 100. If an item costs R250 and sells for R325, the mark-up is (325 - 250) ÷ 250 × 100 = 30, or 30 percent.

## Subtopic: Cash Transactions and Their Source Documents

A cash transaction is one in which payment is made or received immediately, by cash, card, cheque or electronic funds transfer. In Grade 9 all such transactions are recorded in two cash journals: the cash receipts journal, the CRJ, for money received, and the cash payments journal, the CPJ, for money paid. Every entry in a journal must be supported by a source document, the original evidence of the transaction.

For money received, the main source documents are: the cash register roll, CRR, the till slip roll that records all cash sales for the day, which is used to record total daily cash sales as one entry; the duplicate receipt, the copy of the receipt issued by the business when it receives money for anything other than cash sales, such as capital, rent income or a loan; and the bank statement, which shows electronic transfers received.

For money paid, the main source documents are: the cheque counterfoil, the stub kept when a cheque is written, although cheques have been phased out by South African banks since 2021; the EFT payment confirmation or proof of payment generated by internet banking; and the bank statement, which shows debit orders and bank charges. When a business buys trading stock for cash, it also receives a cash invoice or till slip from the supplier, which shows what was bought.

The deposit slip shows the money paid into the bank. In Grade 9 the business deposits all cash received daily, so the cash receipts journal and the bank column agree.

Each source document carries a number, which is entered in the document column of the journal. This creates an audit trail: anyone checking the books can trace every entry back to its evidence. This is part of internal control, the procedures a business uses to protect its assets and to make sure the records are accurate. For example, receipts are pre-numbered so that a missing number can be investigated, and the person who handles cash is not the person who records it.

Typical cash transactions of a trading business include: the owner contributing capital, cash sales of goods, receiving rent income, buying trading stock for cash, paying wages and other expenses, buying equipment, paying for stationery and advertising, and the owner taking cash or trading stock for personal use.

## Subtopic: Trading Transactions in the Accounting Equation

The trading transactions affect the accounting equation in specific ways. Analyse each in the four steps: accounts, elements, increase or decrease, reason.

Purchase of trading stock for cash. A shop buys stock for R10 000 and pays by EFT. Trading stock, an asset, increases by R10 000, because stock was bought. Bank, an asset, decreases by R10 000, because money was paid. Owner's equity is not affected, because stock is an asset until sold.

Cash sale of trading stock. The shop sells goods costing R600 for R900 cash. There are two parts. First, Bank, an asset, increases by R900, and owner's equity increases by R900 because of income from sales. Second, Trading stock, an asset, decreases by R600, because stock left the business, and owner's equity decreases by R600, because of the expense cost of sales. The net effect is assets plus R300 and owner's equity plus R300, which is the gross profit. In examination answers you may be asked to show the two parts separately, sales and cost of sales, or the net effect, so read the instruction carefully.

Owner takes trading stock for personal use. The owner takes goods that cost R400 home. Trading stock, an asset, decreases by R400. Owner's equity decreases by R400, because of drawings. Drawings of stock are always recorded at cost price, not at selling price, because no sale has taken place and the business has not earned any profit.

Payment of wages. The shop pays wages of R4 500 by EFT. Bank decreases by R4 500; owner's equity decreases by R4 500, an expense.

Buying equipment for cash, a new till for R5 000: Equipment increases by R5 000, Bank decreases by R5 000; owner's equity is not affected, because equipment is an asset bought for use, not trading stock bought for resale. The difference matters: the same item, a till, is equipment in a stationery shop but trading stock in a shop that sells tills.

The error museum holds four mistakes. First, recording the purchase of trading stock as an expense; stock is an asset until it is sold. Second, recording a cash sale with only one effect; every sale has a selling-price effect and a cost-price effect. Third, recording drawings of stock at selling price; drawings of stock are at cost. Fourth, calculating cost price by taking the mark-up percentage off the selling price; mark-up is on cost, so divide by 100 plus the mark-up percentage.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same ideas again, through a little shop, a shopkeeper's sums and the two halves of every sale, in plain words with the same facts.

## Subtopic: Buy Low, Sell Higher

A hairdresser sells a service: a haircut. A spaza shop does something different. It buys goods, like bread, airtime, sweets and paraffin, and sells them again for a bit more. That is a trading business. The goods it buys to sell again are called trading stock.

Here is the important idea. When the spaza owner buys a box of chips from the wholesaler, the shop is not poorer. It swapped money for chips, and the chips are still worth the same money. The chips sit on the shelf as an asset, trading stock.

Only when a packet of chips is sold does something change. The customer pays the selling price, so money comes in: that is sales. And the packet leaves the shelf, so stock goes out at the price the shop paid for it: that is cost of sales. The difference is the shop's gross profit.

So a trading business makes money in the gap between what it pays and what it charges. Buy low, sell higher. That gap has to pay for everything else: the electricity for the fridge, the rent, the helper's wage. Whatever is left after that is the net profit.

Modern shops scan barcodes at the till. Every beep does two jobs: it records the sale, and it takes one item off the stock list. That is the perpetual system you will use: stock is updated at every purchase and every sale.

## Subtopic: Working Out Mark-up Like a Shopkeeper

The shopkeeper decides how much to add to the cost price. That amount is the mark-up, and it is a percentage of the cost price.

A school bag costs the shop R600. The shop adds 50 percent. Half of 600 is 300. So the selling price is 600 + 300 = 900. When it sells, the gross profit is R300.

Now go backwards, which is where people slip. A shirt sells for R1 200 and the mark-up is 50 percent on cost. What did it cost? Many people take half of 1 200 and say R600. Wrong! The 50 percent was added to the cost, not taken from the selling price. Think of it like this: the cost is 100 parts, the mark-up is 50 parts, so the selling price is 150 parts. 1 200 is 150 parts, so one part is 8, and 100 parts is 800. The cost was R800. Check: 800 plus half of 800, which is 400, gives 1 200. Correct.

The shortcut: cost price equals selling price × 100 ÷ (100 + mark-up). For 40 percent mark-up, divide by 140 and multiply by 100. Sales of R7 000 at 40 percent mark-up: 7 000 × 100 ÷ 140 = 5 000. The cost of sales is R5 000, and the gross profit is R2 000.

Always check your answer by going forward again: cost plus mark-up should give the selling price.

## Subtopic: Two Sides of Every Sale

Every sale in a trading business has two halves. Picture a coin: heads is the selling price, tails is the cost price.

Heads: the customer pays R900. Bank goes up R900, and owner's equity goes up R900 because of sales.

Tails: the bag leaves the shelf. Trading stock goes down R600, and owner's equity goes down R600 because of cost of sales.

Put them together: assets up R300, owner's equity up R300. That is the profit.

Now three other moves. When the shop buys stock for R10 000 cash: trading stock up, bank down. Owner's equity does not move, because the shop just swapped money for goods.

When the owner takes a bag home for her daughter: trading stock goes down, owner's equity goes down, and it is drawings. Use the cost price, R600, not the selling price, because nothing was sold.

When the shop pays wages: bank down, owner's equity down. That is an expense.

Here is a quick way to spot which kind of move you are looking at. If the shop gets something it can sell or use and gives up money of the same value, it is a swap and owner's equity does not move. If something is used up to run the shop, it is an expense. If the owner gets the benefit, it is drawings. And if a customer pays for goods, it is a sale, with its two halves.

And every one of these needs paper proof: a till roll for cash sales, a receipt for other money in, and an EFT proof or bank statement for money out. No paper, no entry.
