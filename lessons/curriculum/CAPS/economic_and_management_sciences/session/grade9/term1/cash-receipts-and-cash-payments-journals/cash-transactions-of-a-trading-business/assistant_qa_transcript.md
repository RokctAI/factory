### subtopic_1 — What Makes a Trading Business Different
**Assistant:** Why is buying trading stock not recorded as an expense when the money is spent?

**Tutor:** Because the business has not used anything up; it has exchanged one asset for another of the same value. An expense is a resource consumed in earning income. When a business pays this month's rent, that resource is gone: the month passes and nothing remains to sell or use. When a business buys trading stock for R10 000, it still has R10 000 of goods sitting on its shelves, which it intends to sell. So the correct record is that one asset, trading stock, increases and another asset, bank, decreases, with no effect on owner's equity. The stock becomes an expense only when it is sold, because at that moment it leaves the business to earn sales income. Its cost is then matched against the income it helped to earn, as cost of sales. This matching is fundamental: income and the expenses incurred to earn it are recorded in the same period, so that the profit is measured correctly. Imagine a shop that buys R50 000 of stock in December for the January back-to-school rush. If the purchase were recorded as an expense in December, December would show a large loss and January a large profit, even though the stock was bought for January's sales. By recording stock as an asset and expensing it as cost of sales when sold, both months show a fair profit. In the perpetual inventory system you use in Grade 9, every sale reduces the trading stock account by the cost of the goods sold, so at any time the account shows the cost of the goods that should still be on hand. Comparing that with a physical stock count reveals theft or damage.

### subtopic_2 — Cost Price, Mark-up, Selling Price and Gross Profit
**Assistant:** Why do we divide by 150 and multiply by 100 to find cost when the mark-up is 50 percent?

**Tutor:** Because the mark-up is calculated on the cost price, so the cost is the base, the 100 percent. If you take the cost as 100 percent and add a mark-up of 50 percent of that cost, the selling price is 150 percent of the cost. The selling price and the cost price are therefore in the ratio 150 to 100. To get from selling price back to cost, you reverse the process: divide by 150 to find one percent of the cost, then multiply by 100 to find the whole cost. With a selling price of R1 200, one percent of cost is 1 200 ÷ 150 = 8, and the cost is 8 × 100 = 800. Check by going forward: 800 plus 50 percent of 800, which is 400, gives 1 200. The tempting mistake is to take 50 percent off the selling price, which gives R600. That would be correct only if the mark-up were calculated on the selling price, which it is not. You can see it is wrong by going forward: 600 plus 50 percent of 600 is 900, not 1 200. The same method works for any percentage. At a mark-up of 40 percent, divide by 140 and multiply by 100; at 25 percent, divide by 125 and multiply by 100; at 100 percent, divide by 200 and multiply by 100, which is the same as halving, because doubling the cost gives the selling price. You will use this calculation every time a cash receipts journal gives you total cash sales and asks for the cost of sales. A good habit is to write the formula once, substitute, calculate, and then check forwards.

### subtopic_3 — Cash Transactions and Their Source Documents
**Assistant:** Why does a business record all its cash sales for the day as one entry instead of one per customer?

**Tutor:** Because it would be impractical and would add nothing useful to the books. A busy shop may serve hundreds of customers a day, many buying a single item for a few rand. Recording each sale separately in the cash receipts journal would fill pages every day, take hours and multiply the chance of errors. Instead, the till records every individual sale on the cash register roll, the till roll, and at the end of the day the total of all cash sales is read from the till. That total, supported by the cash register roll as the source document, is entered in the cash receipts journal as one line: cash sales for the day. The detail is not lost; it remains on the till roll and in the point-of-sale system, so any single sale can be traced if necessary. This is a sound internal control as well. The till total is compared with the actual cash and card slips in the drawer when the cashier cashes up. If there is less money than the till total, there is a shortage that must be investigated; if there is more, a sale may not have been rung up correctly. The day's takings are then deposited in the bank, and the deposit slip is kept as evidence, so the amount in the journal, the cash in the till and the deposit at the bank should all agree. Other receipts, such as rent income or capital from the owner, are different: each is a separate transaction with its own duplicate receipt, so each gets its own line in the journal. The rule is one source document, one entry, and for cash sales the source document is the day's till roll.

### subtopic_4 — Trading Transactions in the Accounting Equation
**Assistant:** Why are drawings of trading stock recorded at cost price and not at selling price?

**Tutor:** Because no sale has taken place, so the business has earned no income and made no profit. When the owner takes goods home for personal use, the business loses those goods, and what it has lost is what it paid for them, their cost price. The correct entry is therefore: trading stock, an asset, decreases by the cost price, and owner's equity decreases by the same amount, with the reason drawings. If the goods were recorded at selling price, the business would be treated as if it had sold them to the owner and earned a gross profit. But the owner did not pay anything; the owner simply took part of what the owner already owns. Recording a profit on that would overstate the business's income and its profit, which would be misleading, for example to a bank considering a loan or to SARS. There is also a practical reason. The selling price is a choice made by the business and may change from day to day with specials and discounts. The cost price is a fact supported by the supplier's invoice. Accounting prefers figures that can be verified. So, if the owner takes a school bag that cost R600 and normally sells for R900, record assets minus R600, trading stock, and owner's equity minus R600, drawings. In the journals later this term, drawings of stock appear in a special way: because no money moves, they are not recorded in the cash journals at all but in a general journal, which you will meet in Grade 10. In Grade 9 you need to be able to show the effect on the accounting equation correctly.
