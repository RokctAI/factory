### subtopic_1 — What Posting Means and the Format of an Account
**Assistant:** Why does the details column show the contra account instead of describing the transaction in words?

**Tutor:** Because the contra account is the most useful single piece of information a ledger reader can have, and it keeps the double entry visible. Every transaction affects at least two accounts, one debited and one credited. When you look at a single account in the ledger, you see only half of each transaction. Writing the name of the other account in the details column shows you the other half immediately. If the capital account shows R80 000 on the credit side with Bank in the details, you know the owner's contribution went into the bank, not into equipment or a vehicle. If the equipment account shows R7 500 on the debit side with Bank in the details, you know the equipment was paid for immediately; later in the year, if equipment were bought on credit, the details would name the creditor, telling a different story. Words like bought shelving or paid rent would describe the event, but they would not tell you where the other half of the entry was recorded, and they would make the ledger longer and harder to check. The contra name, together with the folio, also allows an auditor to verify the double entry: open the account named in the details, find the matching amount on the opposite side, and confirm that it exists. If it does not, an error has been made. The bank account is the exception, because it is the contra account for almost every cash transaction; listing each one would duplicate the journals, so it shows the totals with the descriptions total receipts and total payments. In Grade 10, when you post from the general journal and the credit journals, you will apply the same rule, and the contra names will be creditors, debtors and other accounts.

### subtopic_2 — Posting the Cash Receipts Journal
**Assistant:** Why is the cost of sales total posted to two accounts when the bank total is posted to only one?

**Tutor:** Because the bank total and the cost of sales total belong to different double entries. Think of the CRJ as recording two separate things for each cash sale. First, money was received in exchange for goods: bank increases and sales income increases. That double entry is completed by posting the bank total as a debit to bank and the sales total, plus the sundry amounts, as credits to their accounts. The bank debit of R140 250 is matched by credits of R27 600 to sales and R112 650 to the sundry accounts, which add up to the same R140 250. So bank needs only one posting, and its partners are the sales and sundry postings. Second, goods left the business: the expense cost of sales increases and the asset trading stock decreases. This is a completely separate double entry, with no money involved, and both halves of it come from the same column in the journal. There is no other column that provides its partner. So the R18 400 must be posted twice, as a debit to cost of sales and as a credit to trading stock, to complete its own double entry. If you post it only once, the ledger will be out of balance by R18 400: either cost of sales will be missing, understating expenses and overstating profit, or trading stock will be too high, showing stock that is no longer on the shelves. The trial balance in the next lesson would then fail to balance, and you would have to search for the missing entry. A useful habit is to put a small mark next to the cost of sales total in the journal, and tick it twice, once for each posting.

### subtopic_3 — Posting the Cash Payments Journal
**Assistant:** How do I know which side of the ledger to post a sundry amount to?

**Tutor:** Start from the journal, then confirm with the debit and credit rules. The journal tells you the direction of the money. In the CRJ, money came in, so bank is debited and every other amount from that journal is credited. In the CPJ, money went out, so bank is credited and every other amount from that journal is debited. That gives you the side for every sundry amount: CRJ sundries go to the credit side of their accounts, CPJ sundries to the debit side. Then check that the result makes sense using the rules. In the CPJ, Ridgeview paid R5 000 for rent expense: an expense increasing is a debit, which agrees. It paid R7 500 for equipment: an asset increasing is a debit, which agrees. It paid R2 000 drawings: drawings increasing is a debit, which agrees. In the CRJ, it received R80 000 capital: capital increasing is a credit, which agrees. It received a R30 000 loan: a liability increasing is a credit, which agrees. The check matters most for unusual transactions. Suppose the business paid R3 000 to reduce its loan. That appears in the CPJ, so it is debited to the loan account. Does that make sense? A liability decreasing is a debit, so yes. Or suppose a customer's deposit was refunded. The rules will always confirm the journal if the journal entry was correct. If they disagree, look again at the transaction, because either the journal entry or your classification is wrong. Never let the position of the column on the page decide the side; the meaning of the account decides it.

### subtopic_4 — Balancing the Bank and Trading Stock Accounts
**Assistant:** Why is the balance carried down written on the smaller side and then brought down on the larger side?

**Tutor:** Because the two steps do two different jobs: one closes the month and the other opens the next. The balance carried down is a balancing figure. On the bank account, the debit side totals R140 250 and the credit side R56 590. To close the account for March with equal totals, you add the difference, R83 660, to the smaller side, the credit side. Now both sides total R140 250, the totals are written on the same line and double-underlined, and the account is closed for the month. That figure on the credit side does not mean money was paid out; it is simply the amount needed to make the sides equal, which is why it is labelled balance c/d, carried down. The balance brought down is the real balance, entered below the totals on the opposite side, the debit side, dated the first day of the next month. It says that the account begins April with a debit balance of R83 660, which for bank means the business has that much money in the bank. Because the c/d and the b/d are equal amounts on opposite sides, the double entry within the account is preserved. The rule to remember is that the balance b/d appears on the side that was larger before balancing. For an asset like bank, that is normally the debit side. If payments had been greater than receipts, the larger side would have been the credit side, and the balance b/d would be a credit balance: an overdraft, a liability. So the side on which the balance is brought down tells you the nature of the balance.
