# Part 1 — Expert

This session prepares the trial balance of a trading business from its general ledger, using Ridgeview Hardware's accounts at the end of March. The anchor is the format: a list of every account balance, in ledger order, with balance sheet accounts first and nominal accounts second, each balance placed in the debit or credit column, and the two columns totalled. The governing principle is that because every transaction was recorded with equal debits and credits, the total of the debit balances must equal the total of the credit balances. If they agree, the ledger is arithmetically correct; if they do not, there is an error to find.

## Subtopic: The Purpose and Format of a Trial Balance

A trial balance is a list of all the balances in the general ledger on a particular date, set out in two columns, debit and credit. It is prepared at the end of each month after the journals have been posted and the accounts balanced.

It has three purposes. First, it checks the arithmetical accuracy of the double-entry recording: if the totals agree, every debit has been matched by an equal credit. Second, it provides a convenient summary of all account balances on one page, so that the owner can see the position of the business at a glance. Third, it is the starting point for preparing the financial statements, the statement of comprehensive income, which calculates profit or loss, and the statement of financial position, which shows assets, owner's equity and liabilities. You will prepare those in Grade 10.

The heading names the business and the date: Trial balance of Ridgeview Hardware on 31 March. The columns are: the account name, the folio number, the debit column and the credit column. The trial balance is divided into two sections, matching the ledger: Balance sheet accounts section first, then Nominal accounts section. Within each section, accounts are listed in folio order, B1, B2 and so on, then N1, N2 and so on.

Each account balance is entered in the column on the side of its balance in the ledger. Accounts with debit balances, assets, expenses and drawings, go in the debit column. Accounts with credit balances, capital, liabilities and income, go in the credit column. Only the balance is entered, never both sides of an account. For an account like bank, with entries on both sides, use the balance brought down. For an account with a single entry, that amount is the balance.

A trial balance is not part of the double-entry system itself. It is a working document, a test run of the ledger, which is exactly what its name says: a trial of whether the ledger balances.

## Subtopic: Preparing Ridgeview Hardware's Trial Balance

After the March postings, Ridgeview's ledger balances are as follows.

Balance sheet accounts section. Capital, B1: R80 000, credit. Drawings, B2: R2 000, debit. Equipment, B3: R7 500, debit. Trading stock, B4: R10 600, debit, the balance brought down. Bank, B5: R83 660, debit, the balance brought down. Loan, B6: R30 000, credit.

Nominal accounts section. Sales, N1: R27 600, credit. Cost of sales, N2: R18 400, debit. Rent income, N3: R2 500, credit. Interest income, N4: R150, credit. Rent expense, N5: R5 000, debit. Wages, N6: R12 000, debit. Telephone, N7: R850, debit. Bank charges, N8: R240, debit.

Total the debit column: 2 000 + 7 500 + 10 600 + 83 660 + 18 400 + 5 000 + 12 000 + 850 + 240 = 140 250.

Total the credit column: 80 000 + 30 000 + 27 600 + 2 500 + 150 = 140 250.

The totals agree at R140 250. The trial balance balances, so the double-entry recording for March is arithmetically correct.

Write the totals on the last line, double-underlined. If the totals do not agree, do not force them: there is an error somewhere, and it must be found.

It is not a coincidence that the total equals the CRJ bank total. In this first month there were no opening balances, and the credit balances, capital, loan, sales, rent income and interest income, are exactly the amounts that were received, all recorded in the CRJ. In later months, when accounts carry opening balances and credit transactions begin, the totals will differ from the journal totals.

Notice also that bank and trading stock, the two accounts that were balanced, appear at their balances, not at their totals. A common error is to list the debit side total of bank, R140 250, instead of its balance, R83 660.

## Subtopic: Errors a Trial Balance Reveals and Errors It Hides

Because the trial balance only tests whether debits equal credits, it reveals some errors and not others.

Errors that make the trial balance fail to balance include: posting only one half of a double entry, for example debiting cost of sales but forgetting to credit trading stock; posting an amount to the wrong side of an account, for example crediting rent expense instead of debiting it; posting different amounts to the two accounts, for example telephone posted as R580 instead of R850; adding a journal column or balancing an account incorrectly; and leaving an account out of the trial balance or listing a balance in the wrong column.

Errors that do not affect the agreement of the totals include the following. An error of omission: a transaction left out of the books entirely, so neither the debit nor the credit is recorded. An error of commission: the right amount posted to the wrong account of the same type, for example telephone posted to the stationery account; both are expenses, both debits, so the totals still agree. An error of principle: an amount posted to the wrong type of account, for example equipment debited to trading stock or drawings debited to wages. An error of original entry: the wrong amount recorded in the journal, so both the debit and credit are wrong by the same amount. Compensating errors: two errors of equal amount that cancel each other out. And a reversal of entries: the correct accounts used but debited and credited the wrong way round.

Work through one revealed error to see its effect. Suppose the cost of sales total of R18 400 was debited to cost of sales but never credited to trading stock. Trading stock would then show a balance of R29 000 instead of R10 600, an extra R18 400 on the debit side. The debit total of the trial balance would be 140 250 + 18 400 = 158 650, while the credit total stayed at R140 250. The difference, R18 400, is exactly the missing credit, which points straight to the error. Now suppose wages of R12 000 had been posted to the credit side of wages instead of the debit side. The debit total would fall by R12 000 and the credit total rise by R12 000, giving a difference of R24 000, twice the amount; halving it gives R12 000, which leads you to the wages posting.

Because of these hidden errors, a balancing trial balance does not prove that the books are correct, only that they are arithmetically consistent. Other checks, such as comparing the bank account with the bank statement and counting the stock, are still needed.

When a trial balance does not balance, work systematically. Find the difference between the two totals. Check whether an amount equal to the difference, or half of it, appears in the ledger: half the difference often points to an amount entered on the wrong side, because that moves the totals by twice the amount. If the difference is divisible by 9, look for a transposition, two digits swapped. Telephone of R850 written as R580 gives a difference of 850 - 580 = 270, and 270 ÷ 9 = 30, a whole number. Then recheck the addition of the trial balance, then each balance against the ledger, then the balancing of each account, and finally the postings from the journals.

## Subtopic: Reading the Trial Balance: Gross Profit and Net Result

A trial balance is not just a check; it already tells the owner a great deal about the business. Using Ridgeview's March figures, we can calculate the gross profit and the net result for the month, as a preview of the financial statements of Grade 10.

Gross profit equals sales minus cost of sales: 27 600 - 18 400 = 9 200, or R9 200. The mark-up achieved is 9 200 ÷ 18 400 × 100 = 50, or 50 percent, which agrees with the store's mark-up policy, a useful check that cost of sales was calculated correctly.

Other income is rent income plus interest income: 2 500 + 150 = 2 650.

Operating expenses are rent expense, wages, telephone and bank charges: 5 000 + 12 000 + 850 + 240 = 18 090.

Net result: gross profit plus other income minus expenses: 9 200 + 2 650 - 18 090 = -6 240. Ridgeview made a net loss of R6 240 in its first month.

That may seem alarming, but it is common for a new business. Sales in the first month were low while the store built up its customers, but the rent and wages had to be paid in full. The owner should watch the next months closely: if sales grow, gross profit will rise while rent stays the same, and the business should move into profit. The owner might also consider whether wages are too high for the current level of sales.

Notice what the trial balance shows separately. The equipment of R7 500 is not an expense, so it does not reduce the profit; it is an asset. The drawings of R2 000 are not an expense either. And the bank balance of R83 660 is healthy, mainly because of the owner's capital and the loan, which are not income. A business can have plenty of money in the bank and still be making a loss, which is exactly why the trial balance separates balance sheet accounts from nominal accounts.

The error museum holds four mistakes. First, listing an account's side total instead of its balance. Second, putting drawings in the credit column; it has a debit balance. Third, believing that a balancing trial balance proves the books are free of errors. Fourth, adding capital or the loan to income when working out profit; they are not income.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same ideas again, through a class register, two columns that must agree, and a hunt for a missing rand, in plain words with the same facts.

## Subtopic: A Class Register for Accounts

At the start of the day a teacher reads the class register: every learner, one by one. A trial balance is a register for accounts. At the end of the month, you go through the ledger and call out every account and its balance.

You write them in two groups, just like the two drawers of the ledger. First the balance sheet accounts: capital, drawings, equipment, trading stock, bank, loan. Then the nominal accounts: sales, cost of sales, rent income, interest income, rent expense, wages, telephone, bank charges.

Each account answers with its balance, and you write it in one of two columns. If it is in the debit family, assets, expenses and drawings, it goes in the debit column. If it is in the credit family, capital, liabilities and income, it goes in the credit column.

Only the balance, never the whole account. Bank had R140 250 coming in and R56 590 going out, but its balance is R83 660, so that is what you write.

Why do we bother? Because every time we recorded a transaction, we wrote the same amount once on the left and once on the right. So if we add up all the lefts and all the rights, they must be the same. The trial balance is the test.

## Subtopic: Two Columns That Must Agree

Here is Ridgeview Hardware's register at the end of March.

Debit column: drawings 2 000, equipment 7 500, trading stock 10 600, bank 83 660, cost of sales 18 400, rent expense 5 000, wages 12 000, telephone 850, bank charges 240.

Credit column: capital 80 000, loan 30 000, sales 27 600, rent income 2 500, interest income 150.

Add the debits: 2 000 + 7 500 + 10 600 + 83 660 + 18 400 + 5 000 + 12 000 + 850 + 240 = 140 250.

Add the credits: 80 000 + 30 000 + 27 600 + 2 500 + 150 = 140 250.

Both columns: R140 250. They agree. The numbers never lie.

Now a bonus. Look at the nominal accounts and work out how the shop did. Sales 27 600 minus cost of sales 18 400 gives a gross profit of 9 200. Add the other income: 9 200 + 2 650 = 11 850. Take away the expenses of 18 090: 11 850 - 18 090 = -6 240. A loss of R6 240 in the first month. That is normal for a brand-new shop still finding customers, but the owner must keep an eye on it.

And the R83 660 in the bank? That came mostly from the owner's money and the loan, not from profit. Money in the bank does not mean profit.

## Subtopic: Hunting for the Missing Rand

What if the two columns do not agree? Then a mistake is hiding. Here is how to hunt it down.

Step one: find the difference between the two totals.

Step two: look for an amount equal to the difference. Maybe a whole account was left out of the register.

Step three: halve the difference and look for that amount. If something was written in the wrong column, it makes one side too big and the other too small, so the gap is double the amount.

Step four: see if the difference divides by 9. If it does, two digits may have swapped places. Suppose telephone of R850 was written as R580. The difference is 850 - 580 = 270, and 270 ÷ 9 = 30 exactly. A swap like that always gives a difference you can divide by 9.

Try one. The debit column says 158 650 and the credit column says 140 250. The difference is 18 400. Is there an amount of R18 400 in the books? Yes, cost of sales. So the cost of sales was probably put on one side but not taken off trading stock on the other side. Found it.

Step five: add up both columns again, check each balance against the ledger, and finally check the postings from the journals.

But be careful. Even when the columns agree, there can still be mistakes. If a whole transaction was forgotten, both sides are missing it, and the columns still agree. If telephone was posted to the stationery account by mistake, both are still debits, and the columns still agree. The trial balance catches many mistakes, but not all of them. That is why good bookkeepers also check the bank statement and count the stock.
