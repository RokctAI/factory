### subtopic_1 — The General Ledger and Its Two Sections
**Assistant:** Why are nominal accounts closed off at year end while balance sheet accounts carry on?

**Tutor:** Because they measure different things. Balance sheet accounts measure a position at a moment in time: how much is in the bank today, what the equipment cost, how much is still owed on the loan, what the owner's claim is. Those things do not disappear when the financial year ends. On the first day of the new year the business still has the same bank balance, equipment and loan, so the balances are carried forward, written as balance brought down, and the accounts continue. Nominal accounts measure a flow over a period: how much was sold during the year, how much was spent on wages during the year. A flow belongs to a particular period. If the sales account simply continued into the new year, by December it would show two years of sales added together, and nobody could tell how well the business did this year compared with last year. So at year end, every income and expense account is closed off by transferring its balance to a profit and loss account. That account then shows all the income on the credit side and all the expenses on the debit side, and its balance is the net profit or loss for the year. That profit is finally transferred to the capital account, because it belongs to the owner. After closing, every nominal account has a zero balance and starts afresh. This is why nominal accounts are sometimes called temporary accounts and balance sheet accounts permanent accounts. In Grade 9 you need to classify accounts correctly and know which section they belong to. The closing process itself is a central skill in Grade 10, and it is the reason the classification matters so much.

### subtopic_2 — Classifying Balance Sheet Accounts
**Assistant:** Why is drawings an owner's equity account if its balance is on the debit side like an asset?

**Tutor:** Because classification is about what the account represents, while the side of its balance is about whether it increases or decreases its element. Drawings represents assets the owner has taken out of the business for personal use. That reduces the owner's claim on the business, so it belongs with owner's equity; it is a reduction of capital. Owner's equity increases on the credit side, so a reduction of owner's equity is a debit. Rather than debiting the capital account every time the owner takes money, which would clutter capital with dozens of small entries and hide how much was withdrawn in the year, the business records withdrawals in a separate drawings account. The drawings account therefore collects all the debits, the reductions of owner's equity, for the year. At year end, the total of drawings is transferred to the debit side of the capital account, reducing capital. So drawings is like a holding account for reductions of capital. Its debit balance does not make it an asset. An asset has future value to the business; money taken by the owner has gone and brings no future benefit to the business. Nor is drawings an expense, because it was not incurred to earn income. In the trial balance, drawings appears in the balance sheet accounts section with a debit balance, directly below capital. A similar idea will meet you in Grade 10 with contra accounts, such as accumulated depreciation, which have the opposite balance to the element they belong to. The lesson is to classify by meaning first, and only then decide which side the balance falls on.

### subtopic_3 — Classifying Nominal Accounts: Income and Expenses
**Assistant:** Cost of sales and trading stock both relate to goods. Why is one an asset and the other an expense?

**Tutor:** Because they record the same goods at different stages of their life in the business. When Ridgeview Hardware buys paint from a wholesaler, the paint goes onto the shelves. It has not been used up; it is a resource the business owns and expects to turn into cash by selling it. That is the definition of an asset, so the cost of the paint is debited to trading stock, a current asset. While the paint sits on the shelf, it stays in trading stock. When a customer buys a tin of paint, the business earns sales income at the selling price. At the same moment, the tin leaves the business, so the business no longer owns that resource. Its cost has been used up in earning the sale, which is the definition of an expense. So the cost of that tin is moved out of trading stock, a credit, and into cost of sales, a debit. In the perpetual system you use, this happens every time goods are sold, recorded through the cost of sales column of the CRJ, and later the debtors journal. At the end of March, Ridgeview had bought paint and other goods costing R29 000 and sold goods costing R18 400, so trading stock holds R10 600, the cost of goods still on the shelves, and cost of sales holds R18 400, the cost of goods sold. Cost of sales is then compared with sales to work out gross profit: R27 600 minus R18 400 gives R9 200. Keeping the two accounts separate means the business always knows both what it still has to sell and what its sales actually cost it.

### subtopic_4 — Debit and Credit Rules and Normal Balances
**Assistant:** Is there a quick way to work out which account to debit and which to credit in any transaction?

**Tutor:** Yes. Use the same four steps you used for the accounting equation, then translate. Step one: name the two accounts affected. Step two: classify each one, asset, liability, capital, drawings, income or expense. Step three: decide whether each increases or decreases. Step four: apply the rule. The debit family, assets, expenses and drawings, increases on the debit side and decreases on the credit side. The credit family, liabilities, capital and income, increases on the credit side and decreases on the debit side. Then check that you have one debit and one credit of the same amount. Try it on a bank charge of R240 deducted by the bank. The accounts are bank charges and bank. Bank charges is an expense, increasing, so debit. Bank is an asset, decreasing, so credit. Debit bank charges R240, credit bank R240. Try a loan of R30 000 received. Bank is an asset, increasing, so debit. Loan is a liability, increasing, so credit. Try rent income of R2 500 received. Bank, an asset, increases: debit. Rent income, income, increases: credit. A useful extra check is that money received is always a debit to bank, and money paid is always a credit to bank. Once you know which side bank is on, the other account must be on the opposite side. That is why the CRJ, which records money received, results in debits to bank and credits to other accounts, while the CPJ, which records money paid, results in credits to bank and debits to other accounts. That symmetry will make posting from the journals in the next lesson straightforward.
