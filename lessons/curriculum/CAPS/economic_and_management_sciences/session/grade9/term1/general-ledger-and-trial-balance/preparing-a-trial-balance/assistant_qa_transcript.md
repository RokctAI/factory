### subtopic_1 — The Purpose and Format of a Trial Balance
**Assistant:** Why list balance sheet accounts and nominal accounts in separate sections of the trial balance?

**Tutor:** Because the two sections feed two different financial statements, and keeping them apart now makes the next step straightforward. The balance sheet accounts, capital, drawings, assets and liabilities, describe the financial position of the business on a particular date: what it owns, what it owes and what the owner's claim is. In Grade 10 these balances are used to prepare the statement of financial position. The nominal accounts, income and expenses, describe the performance of the business over a period: how much it earned and what it cost to earn it. These balances are used to prepare the statement of comprehensive income, which calculates the net profit or loss. Mixing the two would make it easy to confuse a balance sheet item with an income or expense item, which is one of the most common and costly errors in accounting. For example, if the loan of R30 000 were treated as income, the business would appear to have made a large profit when it actually made a loss. If the equipment of R7 500 were treated as an expense, the loss would appear larger than it is. By listing the sections separately, the owner can see at a glance which balances measure position and which measure performance. The order within each section follows the folio numbers of the ledger, which themselves follow a standard order: owner's equity, non-current assets, current assets, liabilities; then sales, cost of sales, other income and expenses. Following that order consistently means that anyone familiar with South African accounting formats can read the trial balance quickly, and that you will be less likely to leave out an account.

### subtopic_2 — Preparing Ridgeview Hardware's Trial Balance
**Assistant:** Why must the debit total equal the credit total in a trial balance?

**Tutor:** Because of the way every single transaction was recorded. Under the double-entry system, each transaction is recorded with a debit to one account and a credit of exactly the same amount to another account. When Ridgeview received R80 000 capital, bank was debited R80 000 and capital credited R80 000. When it paid wages of R12 000, wages was debited R12 000 and bank credited R12 000. When stock costing R18 400 was sold, cost of sales was debited and trading stock credited with R18 400. If every transaction adds the same amount to the total debits as to the total credits, then the total of all debit entries in the ledger must equal the total of all credit entries. Balancing an account does not change this. When you balance the bank account, you take the debits of R140 250 and the credits of R56 590 and replace them with a single debit balance of R83 660. You have removed R56 590 from both the debit and credit totals of the ledger, so the equality survives. The same is true for trading stock. So when you list only the balances, the debit balances must still add up to the same figure as the credit balances. For Ridgeview, both columns total R140 250. If they did not, it would mean that somewhere a transaction was recorded with unequal debits and credits, an amount was posted to the wrong side, an account was balanced incorrectly, or the trial balance itself was added up wrongly. That is why the trial balance is the standard monthly check in every bookkeeping system, from a spaza shop's books to the accounting software of a large company.

### subtopic_3 — Errors a Trial Balance Reveals and Errors It Hides
**Assistant:** If the trial balance balances, how can the books still be wrong?

**Tutor:** Because the trial balance tests only one thing: that the total debits equal the total credits. Any error that affects the debit and credit sides equally will pass the test. Consider the main types. If a transaction is left out completely, an error of omission, neither the debit nor the credit is recorded, so both totals are lower by the same amount and still agree. If the telephone account of R850 is posted to the stationery account instead, an error of commission, there is still a debit of R850 to an expense, so the totals agree, but telephone and stationery are both wrong. If equipment of R7 500 is debited to trading stock, an error of principle, the debit is still R7 500, so the totals agree, but the classification is wrong, and the stock count will show a large shortage. If the CPJ shows rent of R5 000 as R500 and that wrong figure is posted to both rent expense and bank, an error of original entry, both sides are R4 500 too low and the totals agree. If one account is overstated by R100 and another, on the same side, is understated by R100, the errors compensate. And if rent income is debited and bank credited instead of the other way round, a reversal of entries, both sides are wrong by equal amounts and the totals agree. None of these is caught by the trial balance. They are found by other controls: checking every entry against its source document, comparing the bank account with the bank statement, counting the stock and comparing it with the trading stock account, and reviewing the balances for anything that looks unreasonable. A careful accountant treats a balancing trial balance as a good sign, never as proof.

### subtopic_4 — Reading the Trial Balance: Gross Profit and Net Result
**Assistant:** How can Ridgeview have R83 660 in the bank but still make a loss?

**Tutor:** Because the bank balance and the profit measure different things, and the trial balance shows both. The bank balance measures cash: all the money received minus all the money paid, whatever the reason. Ridgeview received R140 250 in March, but most of that was not income. R80 000 was the owner's capital and R30 000 was a loan; together, R110 000 of the receipts came from financing the business, not from trading. Only R27 600 came from sales and R2 650 from other income. On the payments side, R29 000 went on stock, of which R10 600 is still on the shelves, R7 500 on equipment, which is an asset, and R2 000 on drawings, none of which are expenses. So the bank balance is high mainly because the owner and the bank put money in. Profit measures performance: income earned minus the expenses incurred to earn it. Sales of R27 600 minus cost of sales of R18 400 give a gross profit of R9 200. Add other income of R2 650 to get R11 850. Subtract operating expenses of R18 090, rent, wages, telephone and bank charges, and the result is a loss of R6 240. In the first month the store did not sell enough to cover its fixed costs. If this continued month after month, the losses would steadily eat into the bank balance, and the owner's capital would shrink. That is why owners and banks look at both figures. Cash tells you whether you can pay your bills this month; profit tells you whether the business is working. In Grade 10 you will prepare the full statement of comprehensive income to show this formally.
