# Part 1 — Expert

This session classifies the accounts of a trading business and sets out the rules that decide on which side of an account each amount is recorded. The anchor is a two-section general ledger: the balance sheet accounts section, holding assets, owner's equity and liabilities, and the nominal accounts section, holding income and expenses. The governing principle is that an account increases on the same side as its normal balance: assets, expenses and drawings increase on the debit side; liabilities, capital and income increase on the credit side. Every account in Ridgeview Hardware's books will be classified, given a folio number and its normal balance.

## Subtopic: The General Ledger and Its Two Sections

The general ledger is the main book of the accounting system, the book of final entry. It contains a separate account for every item that the business needs to keep track of: bank, trading stock, capital, sales, wages, and so on. The journals record transactions in date order; the ledger sorts them by account, so that the balance of each account can be seen at a glance. At the end of each month, the totals and sundry amounts in the journals are posted, that is, transferred, to the ledger accounts.

Each ledger account has a standard format, called a T-account in its simplest form. The account name and folio number are written at the top, centred. The left side is the debit side, abbreviated Dr, and the right side is the credit side, abbreviated Cr. Each side has columns for the date, the details, the folio of the journal from which the amount was posted, and the amount. Debit and credit do not mean good or bad, or plus and minus; they simply mean left and right.

The general ledger is divided into two sections.

The balance sheet accounts section contains the accounts that will appear in the statement of financial position, sometimes still called the balance sheet: assets, owner's equity and liabilities. These are the accounts of the accounting equation. Their balances are carried forward from one financial year to the next, because the business still has its assets and debts and the owner still has a claim on the business. Their folio numbers start with B: B1, B2, B3 and so on.

The nominal accounts section contains the accounts for income and expenses. These are the accounts used to calculate profit or loss. Their folio numbers start with N: N1, N2 and so on. At the end of the financial year, the nominal accounts are closed off, their balances transferred to a profit and loss account, and the net profit is transferred to capital, so each new year starts with nominal accounts at zero. That closing process is studied in Grade 10.

Why are income and expenses kept separately at all, if they ultimately affect owner's equity? Because the owner, the bank and SARS all want to know how the profit was made: how much was earned from sales, what the stock cost, how much was spent on wages, rent and electricity. If every income and expense were posted straight to capital, that detail would be lost.

## Subtopic: Classifying Balance Sheet Accounts

Balance sheet accounts are classified into three elements and further sub-groups.

Owner's equity accounts come first in the ledger. Capital records the owner's contribution to the business. Drawings records assets taken by the owner for personal use. Drawings is an owner's equity account because it reduces the owner's claim, even though its balance is on the debit side.

Assets are divided into non-current and current assets. Non-current assets are those the business intends to keep and use for longer than one year: land and buildings, vehicles and equipment. Current assets are those that are cash or will become cash within a year: trading stock, debtors control, bank and cash float. The cash float is a small amount of notes and coins kept in the till to give change; it is a current asset.

Liabilities are divided into non-current and current liabilities. A non-current liability is a debt repayable after more than a year, such as a long-term bank loan or mortgage. A current liability is due within a year, such as creditors control, the amount owed to suppliers for goods bought on credit, and a bank overdraft.

The usual order of accounts in the balance sheet section is: capital, drawings, then the non-current assets, then the current assets, then the liabilities. For Ridgeview Hardware at the end of March, the balance sheet accounts and their folios are: Capital B1, Drawings B2, Equipment B3, Trading stock B4, Bank B5 and Loan: the bank's name, B6.

Classifying an account correctly depends on understanding what it represents, not on its name alone. A bank account with a positive balance is a current asset; if it is overdrawn, it is a current liability. Trading stock is a current asset even though it may sit on the shelf for some months, because it is held for sale in the normal course of business. A loan payable in ten years is a non-current liability, but the part repayable within the next twelve months is a current liability; Grade 10 makes that split.

## Subtopic: Classifying Nominal Accounts: Income and Expenses

Nominal accounts are divided into income and expenses.

Income accounts record amounts earned by the business, which increase owner's equity. For a trading business, the main income account is sales, the selling price of goods sold. Other income accounts include rent income, interest income, commission income and, for a business that also provides a service, current income or service fees. Income is an increase in owner's equity that is not a capital contribution by the owner.

Expense accounts record the costs incurred to earn income, which decrease owner's equity. For a trading business, the main expense is cost of sales, the cost price of the goods that were sold. Other expenses, called operating expenses, include wages, salaries, rent expense, water and electricity, telephone, advertising, stationery, insurance, repairs, bank charges and interest on a loan.

The usual order of nominal accounts is: sales first, then cost of sales, then the other income accounts, then the expense accounts. For Ridgeview Hardware: Sales N1, Cost of sales N2, Rent income N3, Interest income N4, Rent expense N5, Wages N6, Telephone N7 and Bank charges N8.

Several pairs of accounts sound similar but sit on opposite sides. Rent income is earned when the business lets out space; rent expense is incurred when the business rents its premises. Interest income is earned on a positive bank balance or money lent; interest on loan is an expense paid to a lender. Sales is income; cost of sales is an expense. Read the full account name and ask whether the business is earning or paying.

Three common confusions need to be cleared up. Trading stock is not an expense; it is a current asset, and it becomes the expense cost of sales only when sold. Drawings is not an expense; it is an owner's equity account. And a loan received is not income; it is a liability, because it must be repaid.

## Subtopic: Debit and Credit Rules and Normal Balances

The rules for recording on the debit or credit side follow from the accounting equation, A = OE + L. Assets are on the left of the equation, so they increase on the left of their accounts, the debit side, and decrease on the credit side. Owner's equity and liabilities are on the right of the equation, so they increase on the right, the credit side, and decrease on the debit side.

Income increases owner's equity, so income accounts increase on the credit side. Expenses decrease owner's equity, so expense accounts increase on the debit side. Drawings decrease owner's equity, so the drawings account increases on the debit side.

The normal balance of an account is the side on which it increases, and therefore the side on which its balance normally appears. Debit balances: assets, expenses and drawings. Credit balances: liabilities, capital and income. A memory aid many learners use is that assets, expenses and drawings are the debit family, and the rest are the credit family.

Every transaction has an equal debit and credit. When Ridgeview receives capital of R80 000, Bank, an asset, is debited with R80 000 and Capital, owner's equity, is credited with R80 000. When it pays wages of R6 000, Wages, an expense, is debited and Bank, an asset decreasing, is credited. When it sells goods for R9 000 cash, Bank is debited and Sales, income, is credited; and Cost of sales, an expense, is debited with R6 000 while Trading stock, an asset decreasing, is credited. The total of debits always equals the total of credits, which is why a trial balance can later check the ledger.

Apply the rules to a full set of Ridgeview's March transactions and the pattern becomes automatic. Rent income of R2 500 received: debit Bank, an asset increasing; credit Rent income, income increasing. Trading stock of R20 000 bought by EFT: debit Trading stock, an asset increasing; credit Bank, an asset decreasing. Equipment of R7 500 bought: debit Equipment, credit Bank. Drawings of R2 000: debit Drawings, the debit family increasing; credit Bank. Telephone of R850 paid: debit Telephone, an expense; credit Bank. Interest income of R150: debit Bank; credit Interest income. In each case one account is debited and one credited with the same amount, and in each case where money is received Bank is on the debit side, and where money is paid Bank is on the credit side.

It is also worth knowing what an account balance on the wrong side signals. If an asset account such as equipment ever showed a credit balance, or an expense account such as wages a credit balance, something has almost certainly been posted to the wrong side. The bank account is the one common exception: a credit balance on bank means the account is overdrawn, a liability. Checking each balance against its normal side is a quick way to spot posting errors before the trial balance is drawn up.

The error museum holds four mistakes. First, believing that debit means increase and credit means decrease; that is only true for assets, expenses and drawings. Second, classifying drawings as an expense or a liability; it is owner's equity with a debit balance. Third, placing trading stock in the nominal section; it is a current asset in the balance sheet section. Fourth, putting rent income and rent expense in the same account; they are separate accounts with opposite normal balances.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same ideas again, through two drawers, a growing side for every account, and Ridgeview's own list, in plain words with the same facts.

## Subtopic: Sorting Accounts into Two Drawers

Picture a filing cabinet with two drawers. Every account in the business goes into one of them.

The top drawer is labelled balance sheet accounts, with a letter B. In it go the things the business owns, the assets, the owner's claim, which is capital and drawings, and what the business owes, the liabilities. These are the accounts from the accounting equation. They stay in the drawer year after year, because the shop still owns its shelves and still owes its loan next year.

The bottom drawer is labelled nominal accounts, with a letter N. In it go all the income and all the expenses: sales, cost of sales, rent income, wages, telephone, bank charges. These are the accounts we use to work out profit. At the end of each year this drawer is emptied out into the profit, and it starts fresh.

How do you know which drawer? Ask: is this something the shop owns, something it owes, or the owner's own stake? Top drawer. Is it money earned or money used up to run the shop? Bottom drawer.

Watch three tricky ones. Trading stock: is it owned? Yes, it sits on the shelves. Top drawer, an asset. Drawings: is it the owner's stake? Yes, it reduces what she owns in the business. Top drawer. A loan: is it owed? Yes. Top drawer, a liability, not income.

## Subtopic: Which Side Makes It Grow?

Every account has two sides, left and right. Accountants call the left side debit and the right side credit. That is all those words mean: left and right.

Now the rule. Think of the accounting equation written across the page: assets on the left, owner's equity and liabilities on the right. Assets live on the left, so they grow on the left side of their accounts: debit. Owner's equity and liabilities live on the right, so they grow on the right: credit.

Income makes the owner richer, so it grows on the owner's side, the right: credit. Expenses make the owner poorer, so they grow on the opposite side, the left: debit. Drawings also make the owner poorer, so drawings grow on the left: debit.

So there are two families. The debit family grows on the left: assets, expenses and drawings. The credit family grows on the right: liabilities, capital and income.

To make an account smaller, write on the opposite side. Paying money out of the bank makes the bank smaller, so bank is credited.

And every transaction always has one debit and one credit of the same amount. The owner puts in R80 000: bank debit 80 000, capital credit 80 000. Pay wages R6 000: wages debit 6 000, bank credit 6 000.

## Subtopic: Building the Ledger List for Ridgeview Hardware

Let's build Ridgeview Hardware's list of accounts and give each a number.

Top drawer, balance sheet accounts. B1 Capital: owner's equity, credit family. B2 Drawings: owner's equity, but debit family. B3 Equipment: non-current asset, debit. B4 Trading stock: current asset, debit. B5 Bank: current asset, debit, as long as there is money in it. B6 Loan: liability, credit.

Bottom drawer, nominal accounts. N1 Sales: income, credit. N2 Cost of sales: expense, debit. N3 Rent income: income, credit. N4 Interest income: income, credit. N5 Rent expense: expense, debit. N6 Wages: expense, debit. N7 Telephone: expense, debit. N8 Bank charges: expense, debit.

Why give numbers at all? Because when an amount is carried from a journal to an account, the journal writes the account's number next to it, and the account writes the journal's name next to it. That way you can follow any amount from one book to the other and back again. The numbers are like house addresses for the accounts.

Look at the pairs. Sales and cost of sales sit next to each other but in different families. Rent income and rent expense have the same word, rent, but one is earned and one is paid, so one is credit and one is debit.

Count the families. Debit family: Drawings, Equipment, Trading stock, Bank, Cost of sales, Rent expense, Wages, Telephone and Bank charges, which is nine. Credit family: Capital, Loan, Sales, Rent income and Interest income, which is five. Fourteen accounts in total. In the next lesson, the journals will send their totals into these accounts.
