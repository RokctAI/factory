# Part 1 — Expert

This session posts Ridgeview Hardware's March cash receipts journal and cash payments journal to the general ledger, and balances the accounts. The anchor is the posting rule: every amount is posted to the debit or credit side of an account, the details column names the contra account, the account on the other side of the double entry, and the folio column names the journal it came from. The governing principle is double entry: the debit postings and the credit postings from each journal are equal in total, so the ledger stays in balance. Every figure posted is traceable to a journal total or a sundry line.

## Subtopic: What Posting Means and the Format of an Account

Posting is the transfer of information from the books of first entry, the journals, to the book of final entry, the general ledger. It is done at the end of each month, after the journals have been totalled and cross-cast.

Each ledger account has a heading with the account name and its folio in the top right corner, for example Bank, B5. The debit side, on the left, and the credit side, on the right, each have four columns: date, details, folio and amount. The date column records the month on the first line and the day on each line. The details column records the name of the contra account, the other account affected by the same transaction, or a description such as total receipts or total payments for the bank account. The folio column records the journal and its page, CRJ1 or CPJ1, so that every posted amount can be traced back to its source. The amount column records the rand amount.

The contra account rule is the heart of posting. When the CRJ shows that sales of R27 600 were received into the bank, the bank account is debited and the sales account is credited. In the sales account, the details column reads Bank, because the other side of that transaction was bank. In the bank account, the details read Total receipts, because the bank account receives the total of all receipts at once rather than each contra account separately. Reading the details tells you the other half of the double entry without opening the journal.

Two kinds of amount are posted from the cash journals. Column totals, bank, sales, cost of sales, trading stock and wages, are posted as one figure on the last day of the month. Sundry amounts are posted one by one, each on its own date, to its own account. As each is posted, its ledger folio is written in the sundry folio column of the journal. That two-way referencing, folio in the ledger pointing to the journal, folio in the journal pointing to the ledger, is the audit trail.

A sensible order for posting is: post the CRJ first, starting with the bank total and the special column totals, then each sundry line; then the CPJ, in the same order. Tick or enter folios as you go so that nothing is posted twice or missed.

## Subtopic: Posting the Cash Receipts Journal

The totals of Ridgeview's CRJ for March were: bank R140 250, sales R27 600, cost of sales R18 400 and sundry R112 650, made up of capital R80 000 on the 1st, rent income R2 500 on the 10th, loan R30 000 on the 20th and interest income R150 on the 31st.

Bank total. Money received increases the bank, an asset, so post R140 250 to the debit side of Bank, B5. Date: 31 March. Details: Total receipts. Folio: CRJ1.

Sales total. Sales is income, which increases on the credit side. Post R27 600 to the credit side of Sales, N1. Date: 31. Details: Bank, the contra account. Folio: CRJ1.

Cost of sales total. This amount is posted twice, because it is a separate double entry. Cost of sales, an expense, increases: debit Cost of sales, N2, with R18 400, details Trading stock, folio CRJ1. Trading stock, an asset, decreases: credit Trading stock, B4, with R18 400, details Cost of sales, folio CRJ1.

Sundry amounts, each to the credit side of its own account, with Bank in the details and CRJ1 in the folio. Capital, B1: credit R80 000 on 1 March. Rent income, N3: credit R2 500 on 10 March. Loan, B6: credit R30 000 on 20 March. Interest income, N4: credit R150 on 31 March.

Now check that the CRJ postings balance. Debits: bank R140 250 and cost of sales R18 400. Credits: sales R27 600, trading stock R18 400 and the four sundry amounts totalling R112 650. Total debits: 140 250 + 18 400 = 158 650. Total credits: 27 600 + 18 400 + 112 650 = 158 650. They agree. Every rand debited to bank is matched by a credit to sales or a sundry account, and the cost of sales entry balances itself.

Notice the pattern. The bank account receives one large debit, while all the other accounts receive credits. This is exactly what the debit and credit rules predict: money received is always a debit to bank, so the contra accounts are credited.

## Subtopic: Posting the Cash Payments Journal

The totals of Ridgeview's CPJ for March were: bank R56 590, trading stock R29 000, wages R12 000 and sundry R15 590, made up of rent expense R5 000 on the 4th, equipment R7 500 on the 8th, drawings R2 000 on the 22nd, telephone R850 on the 29th and bank charges R240 on the 31st.

Bank total. Money paid decreases the bank, an asset, so post R56 590 to the credit side of Bank, B5. Date: 31 March. Details: Total payments. Folio: CPJ1.

Trading stock total. Trading stock, an asset, increases: debit Trading stock, B4, with R29 000. Details: Bank. Folio: CPJ1.

Wages total. Wages, an expense, increases: debit Wages, N6, with R12 000. Details: Bank. Folio: CPJ1.

Sundry amounts, each to the debit side of its own account, with Bank in the details and CPJ1 in the folio. Rent expense, N5: debit R5 000 on 4 March. Equipment, B3: debit R7 500 on 8 March. Drawings, B2: debit R2 000 on 22 March. Telephone, N7: debit R850 on 29 March. Bank charges, N8: debit R240 on 31 March.

Check: the credit to bank is R56 590; the debits are 29 000 + 12 000 + 15 590 = 56 590. They agree.

The pattern is the mirror image of the CRJ. Bank receives one large credit, and all the other accounts receive debits. Money paid is always a credit to bank, so the contra accounts are debited.

A frequent error is to post a sundry amount to the wrong side, for example crediting rent expense because it appeared in a journal. The journal does not decide the side; the rules do. Ask what kind of account it is and whether it increased or decreased.

## Subtopic: Balancing the Bank and Trading Stock Accounts

At the end of the month, accounts with entries on both sides are balanced, to find the balance that is carried into the next month.

The bank account has R140 250 on the debit side and R56 590 on the credit side. The debit side is larger, so the balance is a debit balance of 140 250 - 56 590 = 83 660. To balance the account: write Balance c/d, carried down, of R83 660 on the credit side, the smaller side, dated 31 March. Both sides now total R140 250; write these totals on the same line on each side and double-underline them. Then, on 1 April, write Balance b/d, brought down, of R83 660 on the debit side, below the totals. The balance b/d is on the side that was larger, which is the side of the account's normal balance for an asset with money in it.

The trading stock account has R29 000 on the debit side, from the CPJ, and R18 400 on the credit side, from the cost of sales in the CRJ. The balance is 29 000 - 18 400 = 10 600. Write Balance c/d R10 600 on the credit side on 31 March; both sides total R29 000; then Balance b/d R10 600 on the debit side on 1 April. This is the cost of stock that should be on hand, which a stock count can confirm.

Accounts with entries on one side only, but more than one entry, are not balanced in this way either. Their entries are simply added, and the total is the balance. In April, for example, the wages account will receive another debit, and its balance will be the sum of March and April wages.

Accounts with only one entry, such as capital, sales, cost of sales, rent income, loan, wages, rent expense and the others, do not need to be balanced; the single amount is the balance. Accounts with several entries on one side only are simply added up.

After posting and balancing, the balances of Ridgeview's accounts are: Capital R80 000 credit, Drawings R2 000 debit, Equipment R7 500 debit, Trading stock R10 600 debit, Bank R83 660 debit, Loan R30 000 credit, Sales R27 600 credit, Cost of sales R18 400 debit, Rent income R2 500 credit, Interest income R150 credit, Rent expense R5 000 debit, Wages R12 000 debit, Telephone R850 debit and Bank charges R240 debit. These are the figures that will be listed in the trial balance in the next lesson.

The error museum holds four mistakes. First, writing the account's own name in the details column; the details always name the contra account. Second, posting the sundry total as one figure; each sundry amount goes to its own account. Third, writing the balance c/d on the larger side; it goes on the smaller side so that both totals agree. Fourth, forgetting the second half of the cost of sales posting; the credit to trading stock is as important as the debit to cost of sales.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same ideas again, through carrying totals home, naming the other side, and closing an account like a till at night, in plain words with the same facts.

## Subtopic: Carrying Totals to Their Homes

The journals are like rough notebooks. The ledger is the neat filing cabinet with one card for every account. Posting means carrying each number from the notebook to the right card.

Start with the CRJ, money in. Its bank total, R140 250, goes to the bank card on the left side, the debit side, because money came in. Its sales total, R27 600, goes to the sales card on the right side, because income grows on the right. The cost of sales total, R18 400, goes to two cards: the left side of cost of sales, because it is an expense, and the right side of trading stock, because stock went out. Then each sundry line goes to its own card on the right side: capital R80 000, rent income R2 500, loan R30 000, interest income R150.

Now the CPJ, money out. Its bank total, R56 590, goes to the bank card on the right side, because money went out. Trading stock, R29 000, goes to the left side of the trading stock card, because stock came in. Wages, R12 000, to the left side of the wages card. Then each sundry line goes to its own card on the left side: rent expense, equipment, drawings, telephone and bank charges.

Notice the mirror. From the CRJ, bank is on the left and everything else on the right. From the CPJ, bank is on the right and everything else on the left.

Next to every number on a card, write where it came from: CRJ1 or CPJ1. That is the folio, and it lets anyone trace the number back.

## Subtopic: The Name on the Other Side

There is one rule that makes the ledger tell a story: in the details column, you write the name of the other account in the same transaction.

The sales card says Bank next to R27 600. Read it as: sales of R27 600, and the money went into the bank.

The capital card says Bank next to R80 000. Read it as: the owner put in R80 000, and it went into the bank.

The wages card says Bank next to R12 000. Read it as: wages of R12 000 were paid out of the bank.

The cost of sales card says Trading stock next to R18 400, and the trading stock card says Cost of sales next to R18 400. Each card names the other.

Only the bank card is different. It would need dozens of names, so for money in it just says Total receipts and for money out it says Total payments.

Why does this matter? Because when you look at one card, you can see both halves of the story without opening the journal. If the sales card said Sales next to R27 600, it would tell you nothing new. Always write the other one's name.

## Subtopic: Closing an Account Like a Till at Night

At the end of the day, a shopkeeper counts the till: money in, money out, what is left. Balancing an account is the same.

The bank card has R140 250 on the left, money in, and R56 590 on the right, money out. Which side is bigger? The left. What is the difference? 140 250 - 56 590 = 83 660.

Now write Balance c/d R83 660 on the smaller side, the right, so that both sides add up to the same total, R140 250. Draw two lines under both totals. Then, on the next day, 1 April, write Balance b/d R83 660 on the left side, under the lines. That is the money the shop starts April with.

Do the same for trading stock. Left side: R29 000 of stock bought. Right side: R18 400 of stock sold. Difference: 29 000 - 18 400 = 10 600. Balance c/d R10 600 on the right, both sides R29 000, then Balance b/d R10 600 on the left on 1 April. That is the stock still on the shelves.

Here is how the finished bank card reads, line by line. Left side: 31 March, Total receipts, CRJ1, 140 250. Right side: 31 March, Total payments, CPJ1, 56 590; then 31 March, Balance c/d, 83 660. Totals on the same line on both sides: 140 250 and 140 250, double-underlined. Under the lines, on the left: 1 April, Balance b/d, 83 660. Every number on that card can be checked against the notebooks.

Carried down, c/d, goes on the smaller side, to make both sides equal. Brought down, b/d, goes on the bigger side, below the totals, to start the new month. Cards with only one number need no balancing; that number is the balance.
