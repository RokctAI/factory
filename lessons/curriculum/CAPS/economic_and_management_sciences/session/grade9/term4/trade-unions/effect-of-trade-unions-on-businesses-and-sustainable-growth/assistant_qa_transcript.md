### subtopic_1 — Positive Effects of Trade Unions on Businesses
**Assistant:** How can a union actually save a business money, when it usually asks for more?

**Tutor:** Because some of the biggest costs a business faces are hidden costs of poor labour relations, and a well-functioning union can reduce them. The first is labour turnover. When workers feel unfairly treated, many leave as soon as they find another job. Each departure costs the business money: advertising the post, interviewing, training the new worker, and lower output and more mistakes while the new worker learns. At Karoo Canning, replacing 5 workers at about R6 000 each costs R30 000 a year, and in skilled jobs the cost is much higher. A union that secures fair treatment helps keep experienced workers. The second hidden cost is conflict. Without organised procedures, a grievance can simmer until workers stop working suddenly, in an unplanned stoppage that the business cannot prepare for. With a clear grievance procedure, the shop steward raises the problem early and it is solved in a meeting, which costs very little. The third is accidents. Unions insist on health and safety committees and on following the Occupational Health and Safety Act. Every accident means lost working days, possible compensation claims, damaged equipment and lower morale. The fourth is negotiation time. Negotiating separately with 40 workers would take management many hours and produce 40 different arrangements. A single collective agreement with the union covers everyone for a fixed period, so management can plan wage costs for the year ahead. Of course, unions also push wages up, which is a real cost. The point is that the overall effect depends on the balance between these savings and the higher wages, and in a good relationship the savings can be substantial.

### subtopic_2 — Negative Effects of Trade Unions on Businesses
**Assistant:** Why does a long strike sometimes hurt a business even after it ends?

**Tutor:** Because some of the damage is not just the production lost during the strike, but the loss of customers, reputation and investment, which can take years to recover. During a strike at Karoo Canning, the direct loss is easy to calculate: R60 000 of output a day, so R600 000 over 10 working days. But canning is seasonal. Fruit that is ripe must be processed quickly, and if it rots during the strike it is lost for good, along with the money paid to farmers. Customers, such as supermarket chains, need reliable supply. If their orders arrive late, they may sign contracts with other suppliers, possibly importers, and they may not come back even when the strike is over. The business may also have to pay penalties for late delivery. Then there is the effect on relationships inside the business. After a bitter strike, especially one involving intimidation or violence, trust between management and workers can be badly damaged, and that affects cooperation for a long time. Finally, investors watch how labour disputes are handled. If an industry becomes known for long, violent strikes, investors may choose to build new factories or mines in other countries, and that means fewer new jobs. The platinum strike of 2014 is an example: it lasted about five months, and its effects on the mining companies, the workers, who lost months of wages, and the towns around the mines were felt long after it ended. This is why both sides usually try hard to settle before a strike, and why the law requires conciliation first.

### subtopic_3 — Trade Unions and Sustainable Economic Growth
**Assistant:** What does sustainable growth mean, and how is it different from ordinary growth?

**Tutor:** Economic growth simply means that a country produces more goods and services over time, usually measured by the increase in real gross domestic product. Sustainable growth adds a condition: the growth must be able to continue over the long term. That means three things. It must not destroy the natural resources and environment on which future production depends, for example by polluting rivers or exhausting soil and water. It must include people, so that the benefits are shared widely rather than going only to a small group, because extreme inequality creates social tension and instability. And it must be stable, rather than a short boom followed by a crash. Trade unions can support sustainable growth in several ways. By negotiating fair wages, they help to spread the benefits of growth, and workers who earn more spend more, creating demand for other businesses. By pushing for training, they raise skills and productivity, which is the most reliable long-term source of growth. By participating in social dialogue at NEDLAC, they help government, business and labour reach agreements that all sides support, which creates stability. And through campaigns such as the just transition, some unions help ensure that moving to cleaner energy does not simply throw coal workers into unemployment. Unions can also hinder sustainable growth if strikes become long and violent, or if wage demands far exceed productivity, so that businesses employ fewer people. So unions are not automatically good or bad for growth: their effect depends on how they act and how employers and government respond.

### subtopic_4 — Weighing the Effects: Wages, Productivity and Jobs
**Assistant:** Why do wages and productivity need to rise together?

**Tutor:** Because the cost of producing each item depends on both. Productivity is the amount produced per worker in a given time. If a worker at Karoo Canning earns more but also produces more, the wage cost of each case of canned fruit can stay the same. Suppose wages rise by 6 percent and output per worker rises from 200 to 212 cases a day, which is also 6 percent. The wage bill is higher, but so is the output, so the cost per case is roughly unchanged. The business can keep its prices competitive, its profits stable, and its workforce. Workers enjoy a real improvement that the business can afford year after year. Now suppose wages rise by 6 percent, but output stays at 200 cases. Each case now costs more to produce. The business has three choices: raise its prices, which may lose customers to competitors or cheaper imports; accept lower profits, which may discourage the owners from investing further; or cut costs, which often means employing fewer people or buying machines to replace them. None of these is good for workers in the long run. That does not mean wages should never rise faster than productivity: sometimes wages are unfairly low and need to catch up, and fair pay can itself raise productivity by improving motivation. But over long periods, sustainable wage increases are those supported by rising productivity. This is why the most successful agreements combine wage increases with training, better work organisation and investment in equipment, so that both sides gain together.
