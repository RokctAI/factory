# Part 1 — Expert

In the previous lesson we learnt what a trade union is, what it does and how collective bargaining and strikes are regulated in South Africa. In this lesson we look at the other side of the table: how trade unions affect businesses, both positively and negatively, and how they can contribute to, or hold back, sustainable economic growth. Our running example is Karoo Canning, a fictional fruit-canning factory in the Western Cape with 40 production workers, most of whom belong to a union.

## Subtopic: Positive Effects of Trade Unions on Businesses

It is easy to think of trade unions only as a cost to business, but a well-functioning union can bring real benefits to an employer.

First, a union gives the employer one organised partner to negotiate with. Instead of dealing with 40 separate workers, each with their own complaint and their own demand, Karoo Canning's management negotiates with a few elected representatives who speak for all the members. Agreements reached through collective bargaining apply to everyone, usually for a fixed period such as one or two years. This saves management time and makes wage costs predictable, which helps the business plan its budget.

Second, unions provide orderly procedures for handling grievances and disputes. A grievance is a complaint by a worker about treatment at work. When there is a clear procedure, with the shop steward representing the worker and meetings with management at set stages, problems are dealt with early, before anger builds up. Without such procedures, frustration can explode into sudden, unplanned work stoppages, which are far more damaging.

Third, unions can improve morale and motivation. Workers who feel they are treated fairly, are paid a fair wage and have a voice are more likely to be loyal, to work carefully and to stay. This reduces labour turnover, which is the rate at which workers leave and must be replaced. Replacing workers is expensive: the business must advertise, interview, train the new workers and accept lower output while they learn. If Karoo Canning has to replace 5 workers in a year, and each replacement costs about R6 000 in recruitment and training, that is 5 × 6 000 = R30 000, money that a stable workforce would save.

Fourth, unions push for safe and healthy working conditions. In a canning factory, with hot machinery, boiling water and sharp metal, accidents are a real risk. Unions often help establish health and safety committees and insist that the Occupational Health and Safety Act is followed. Fewer accidents mean fewer lost working days, lower medical and compensation costs and a better reputation for the employer.

Fifth, many unions support skills development. They negotiate for training, and union representatives sit on bodies such as the Sector Education and Training Authorities, the SETAs, which fund training in each industry. Better-trained workers are more productive.

Finally, unions can be a channel of communication. When management needs to introduce a change, such as new machinery or new shift times, explaining and agreeing it through the union is often more effective than announcing it to each worker separately. Workers are more likely to accept a change they have helped to shape.

## Subtopic: Negative Effects of Trade Unions on Businesses

Trade unions can also have negative effects on a business, especially when relationships break down.

The most obvious effect is on costs. Unions negotiate for higher wages and better benefits, such as medical aid, pension contributions and more leave. Suppose Karoo Canning's 40 workers each earn R8 000 a month and the union negotiates an increase of 6 percent. Each worker earns 8 000 × 6 ÷ 100 = R480 more a month. For 40 workers, that is 40 × 480 = R19 200 a month, or 19 200 × 12 = R230 400 a year. If the business cannot increase its prices or its productivity to cover this, its profit falls. A business with a small profit margin, competing with cheaper imports, may find this very difficult.

Strikes cause the most visible damage. During a strike, production stops or slows down. If Karoo Canning normally produces canned fruit worth R60 000 a day, a strike of 10 working days means 60 000 × 10 = R600 000 of output is lost. Fruit that is not canned in season may rot, orders may be delivered late, and customers may switch to competitors, sometimes permanently. The workers lose too: under the principle of no work, no pay, a worker earning R8 000 a month over about 20 working days loses about 8 000 ÷ 20 = R400 a day, or 400 × 10 = R4 000 for a 10-day strike.

Long strikes can damage whole industries. In 2014, workers in South Africa's platinum mines went on strike for about five months, from January to June, one of the longest strikes in the country's history. Production and exports fell sharply, mining companies lost billions of rand in revenue, workers lost months of wages, and businesses in the mining towns, such as shops and taxi operators, suffered as spending fell.

Some strikes involve intimidation of workers who choose not to strike, damage to property or violence. These are illegal, even during a protected strike, and they harm the reputation of the union and the business, and the willingness of investors to invest.

Other negative effects include rigid work rules. Agreements sometimes make it difficult for a business to change shifts, introduce new technology or move workers between tasks. Negotiations can be slow and confrontational, and an adversarial relationship, in which each side sees the other as an enemy, wastes energy that could go into improving the business.

Finally, if wages rise much faster than productivity, businesses may respond by employing fewer people, replacing workers with machines, or moving production elsewhere. This is a serious concern in a country with high unemployment.

## Subtopic: Trade Unions and Sustainable Economic Growth

Economic growth is an increase in the production of goods and services in a country over time, usually measured by the growth in real gross domestic product, GDP. Sustainable growth is growth that can continue in the long term without destroying the natural environment, without leaving large groups of people behind, and without creating instability that later reverses it. Trade unions can contribute to sustainable growth in several ways.

They help to share the benefits of growth more fairly. When workers receive a fair share of the income their work creates, inequality is reduced, and workers have more money to spend. That spending creates demand for goods and services, which supports other businesses and jobs. South Africa is one of the most unequal countries in the world, so fair wages matter for social stability.

They contribute to labour peace through social dialogue. In South Africa, organised labour is represented at the National Economic Development and Labour Council, NEDLAC, established in 1995, where government, business, labour and community organisations discuss economic and labour policy before laws are passed. Agreements reached there, for example on the national minimum wage, have more support because all sides helped to shape them. Stable labour relations encourage investment, and investment is essential for growth.

They promote skills development. Through the SETAs and training agreements, unions help workers gain skills, which raises productivity, the amount produced per worker per hour. Higher productivity is the main source of rising living standards over time, because it allows wages to rise without increasing costs per unit.

They promote health, safety and decent work. A healthy workforce is a productive one, and the International Labour Organisation's idea of decent work, meaning work that is productive, pays fairly, is safe and respects workers' rights, is part of the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals, specifically Goal 8, decent work and economic growth.

Unions can also support environmental sustainability. Some unions campaign for a just transition, meaning that as the economy moves away from coal towards cleaner energy, workers in coal mines and power stations should be retrained and supported rather than simply losing their jobs.

However, unions can also hold back sustainable growth. Long and violent strikes discourage investment. Wage increases far above productivity growth can reduce employment, particularly of young and low-skilled workers, who are already most affected by unemployment. Resistance to new technology can make industries less competitive. Whether a union helps or hinders growth depends largely on how it acts and how employers and government respond.

## Subtopic: Weighing the Effects: Wages, Productivity and Jobs

The key to understanding the effect of unions on a business is the relationship between wages and productivity.

Return to Karoo Canning. The new agreement raises wages by 6 percent. Suppose that, as part of the same agreement, the union and management agree on a training programme and a new shift system, and output per worker rises from 200 cases a day to 212 cases a day. Productivity has risen by (212 - 200) × 100 ÷ 200 = 6 percent. Wages and output per worker have risen by the same percentage, so the wage cost of each case stays about the same. The workers are better off, the business remains competitive, and the increase can be sustained. This is the ideal outcome.

Now suppose the wage rises by 6 percent but productivity does not change. The cost of each case rises. The business must raise its prices, accept lower profits, or reduce costs in another way, possibly by employing fewer workers. In the long run, wages that rise much faster than productivity are difficult to sustain.

This is why many economists and business leaders argue that the best results come from cooperative, rather than adversarial, labour relations. In a cooperative relationship, management shares information about the business's performance honestly, the union understands the pressures the business faces, and both sides look for agreements that improve wages and productivity together. Strikes are a last resort, not a first move.

Both sides have responsibilities. Employers must pay fairly, respect workers' rights, keep workplaces safe and negotiate in good faith. Unions must represent their members honestly, keep strikes peaceful and lawful, and consider the long-term health of the business that provides the jobs. Government must enforce labour laws fairly and provide institutions such as the CCMA and NEDLAC.

In tests you may be asked to discuss the effect of trade unions on businesses or on economic growth. A good answer gives both positive and negative effects, explains each one with a reason, and reaches a balanced conclusion: unions can contribute greatly to fair, stable and sustainable growth when they and employers cooperate, and can harm businesses and growth when relationships break down.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Let's make this simple. A union can be a good partner for a business, or a difficult one. It depends on how both sides behave.

## Subtopic: Good for Business Too

Karoo Canning has 40 workers. Instead of 40 people arguing separately, management talks to a few elected representatives. One deal for everybody, for a fixed time. That saves time and makes costs predictable.

When a worker has a complaint, there is a clear process. Problems are solved early, before they explode.

Workers who feel treated fairly stay longer. That matters, because replacing workers costs money. If 5 workers leave and each costs R6 000 to replace, that is R30 000 gone.

Unions also push for safety and for training. Safe workers lose fewer days; trained workers produce more.

## Subtopic: When Things Go Wrong

Higher wages cost money. A 6 percent increase on R8 000 is R480 a month. For 40 workers that is R19 200 a month.

Strikes hurt everyone. If the factory makes R60 000 worth of canned fruit a day, a 10-day strike loses R600 000. Each worker loses about R400 a day, R4 000 over the strike. No work, no pay.

In 2014 the platinum mines were on strike for about five months. Companies, workers and whole towns lost out.

Violence and intimidation are never legal, and they scare away investors.

## Subtopic: Growing Together

Sustainable growth is growth that lasts: fair to people, kind to the environment and stable.

Unions help when they share the benefits fairly, train workers and talk things through. In South Africa, government, business and labour talk at NEDLAC.

The big idea is wages and productivity together. Wages up 6 percent, output up 6 percent: everyone wins and the business stays strong.

Wages up without more output: costs rise and jobs may be lost.

So the best result is cooperation: fair pay, honest talks, and strikes only as the very last step. Practise by listing two good effects and two bad effects of unions on a business you know.
