# Part 1 — Expert

This term we return to the economy, and to one of its most important relationships: the one between employers and workers. In this lesson we learn what trade unions are, what they do, how they developed in South Africa, and how the law governs their relationship with employers. In the next lesson we look at how they affect businesses and the economy.

## Subtopic: What a Trade Union Is

A trade union is an organisation of employees, formed to protect and promote the interests of its members, especially their wages, working conditions and job security, mainly by negotiating with employers. Members pay a small regular subscription, usually deducted from their wages every month, and in return the union represents them.

The basic reason for trade unions is an imbalance of power. An individual worker who asks for a higher wage can easily be refused, because the employer can usually find someone else to do the job. But if all the workers in a factory, a mine or a whole industry speak with one voice, the employer cannot replace them all. By acting collectively, workers gain bargaining power that no single worker has. The saying "unity is strength" sums up the idea.

Trade unions are organised in different ways. A craft union represents workers with a particular skill, whatever industry they work in, such as electricians. An industrial union represents all workers in one industry, whatever their job, such as all the workers in mining or in the motor industry. Most large South African unions are industrial unions: for example, the National Union of Mineworkers, NUM, founded in 1982, represents workers in mining, and the National Union of Metalworkers of South Africa, NUMSA, founded in 1987, represents workers in metal and motor manufacturing. There are also unions for public-sector workers, such as the South African Democratic Teachers Union, SADTU, founded in 1990.

Unions are organised from the workplace upward. In each workplace, members elect shop stewards, workers who represent their colleagues in day-to-day matters with management, while continuing to do their normal jobs. Above them are regional and national structures with full-time officials, who negotiate at industry level and run the union's administration.

Many unions belong to a federation, an umbrella body that brings unions together to speak for workers on national issues such as economic policy, labour laws and social grants. South Africa's main federations include the Congress of South African Trade Unions, COSATU, formed in 1985; the National Council of Trade Unions, NACTU, formed in 1986; the Federation of Unions of South Africa, FEDUSA, formed in 1997; and the South African Federation of Trade Unions, SAFTU, formed in 2017.

Employers organise too. Employers in the same industry often form an employers' organisation, which negotiates with the unions on behalf of all its members. So bargaining is often between two organised groups, not between individuals.

## Subtopic: The Functions of Trade Unions

Trade unions perform several functions for their members.

Negotiating wages and benefits. The best-known function is collective bargaining over wages and benefits such as medical aid, pension contributions, housing allowances and annual bonuses. The union negotiates on behalf of all its members, and the result is usually a written collective agreement that applies for one to three years.

Improving working conditions. Unions negotiate working hours, overtime, leave, shift arrangements and the working environment. They push for safe workplaces, which is especially important in dangerous industries such as mining and construction, where unions take part in health and safety committees.

Protecting job security. Unions try to protect members from unfair dismissal and to make sure that retrenchments, when a business must cut jobs, follow a fair process with consultation, fair selection criteria and severance pay.

Representing members. When a member faces a disciplinary hearing, a shop steward can represent them. When a member has a grievance, such as unfair treatment by a supervisor, the union helps to raise it with management. If a dispute cannot be settled in the workplace, the union can take the case to the Commission for Conciliation, Mediation and Arbitration, the CCMA, or to the Labour Court.

Education and training. Many unions train shop stewards in labour law and negotiation, and some negotiate skills development for members, which helps workers to earn more and businesses to become more productive.

Representing workers in society. Through federations, unions take part in national discussions about the economy, for example in the National Economic Development and Labour Council, NEDLAC, where government, business, labour and community organisations discuss economic and labour policy before laws are made. Unions have campaigned on issues such as the national minimum wage, social grants and access to health care.

Some unions also provide services such as funeral benefits, legal advice and bursaries for members' children. These services encourage workers to join and stay members.

It is important to note that unions represent their members, not all workers. People who are unemployed are not represented by unions, and their interests are not always the same as those of employed members. This is one of the debates we will meet in the next lesson.

## Subtopic: Trade Unions in South Africa: History and Federations

Trade unions in South Africa have a long and important history, closely tied to the country's political history.

Early unions in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries were formed mainly by white skilled workers, especially on the Witwatersrand gold mines. Black workers were excluded from the official labour relations system for most of the twentieth century. Under apartheid, black trade unions were not legally recognised, and their leaders were often harassed or banned. Nevertheless, black workers organised: the Industrial and Commercial Workers' Union in the 1920s was one of the largest African organisations of its time, and other unions continued under very difficult conditions.

A turning point came with the Durban strikes of 1973, when tens of thousands of black workers went on strike for higher wages. The strikes showed the strength of workers acting together and led to the growth of new independent unions. In 1979, following the recommendations of the Wiehahn Commission, the government allowed black workers to join registered trade unions legally. Unions grew rapidly in the 1980s, and in 1985 many of them joined to form COSATU, which played a major role in the struggle against apartheid as well as in the workplace.

After 1994, the new democratic government rebuilt labour law. The Constitution of 1996, in section 23, gives every worker the right to form and join a trade union, to take part in its activities and to strike, and gives every employer the right to form and join an employers' organisation. These rights are put into practice by several acts. The Labour Relations Act of 1995, the LRA, governs trade unions, collective bargaining, strikes and dispute resolution, and it established the CCMA. The Basic Conditions of Employment Act of 1997 sets minimum conditions, such as working hours and leave. The Employment Equity Act of 1998 addresses unfair discrimination in the workplace, and the National Minimum Wage Act of 2018 introduced a national minimum wage, which came into effect in 2019.

Today South Africa has many registered trade unions, organised mostly into federations. COSATU is the largest and has historically been in an alliance with the governing party. SAFTU was formed in 2017, partly by unions that had left or been expelled from COSATU, including NUMSA. FEDUSA and NACTU are independent of political parties. The number of union members is large, but union membership is concentrated in the public sector, mining and manufacturing, and many workers in small businesses, in agriculture and in the informal sector are not unionised.

## Subtopic: Collective Bargaining, the Law and the Right to Strike

Collective bargaining is the process in which a trade union and an employer, or an employers' organisation, negotiate wages and conditions of employment on behalf of a group of workers, with the aim of reaching a collective agreement.

Bargaining happens at different levels. At workplace level, a union negotiates with one employer. At industry level, unions and employers' organisations negotiate in a bargaining council, a body registered under the LRA, and the agreement can apply to the whole industry. The public service has its own bargaining council.

A typical negotiation starts with each side's opening position. Suppose a union representing workers who earn R8 000 a month demands an increase of 9 percent, which is 8 000 × 9 ÷ 100 = R720 a month, and the employer offers 5 percent, which is R400. After several rounds of talks, both sides move. If they settle at 7 percent, each worker's wage rises by 8 000 × 7 ÷ 100 = R560, to R8 560 a month, or 560 × 12 = R6 720 more over a year. Both sides gave up something, and both gained an agreement.

If negotiations deadlock, the law provides a process. The dispute must first be referred to the CCMA or to the bargaining council for conciliation, where an independent commissioner helps the parties to reach agreement. If the dispute is not resolved, the commissioner issues a certificate, or 30 days pass from the referral. Only then may the union give the employer at least 48 hours' written notice of a strike; where the employer is the state, the notice is 7 days. A strike that follows this procedure is a protected strike.

A strike is the collective refusal of workers to work, to put pressure on the employer. In a protected strike, workers may not be dismissed for striking, but the employer does not have to pay them for the time they are on strike: the principle of no work, no pay. In an unprotected strike, which does not follow the procedure, workers can be disciplined and even dismissed. Employers have a matching weapon, the lock-out, in which they exclude workers from the workplace to pressure them to accept the employer's terms.

Some workers may not strike at all. Workers in essential services, those whose interruption would endanger life, personal safety or health, such as emergency medical staff, cannot strike; their disputes go to compulsory arbitration instead.

Strikers must also act within the law. Picketing, where workers gather peacefully outside the workplace to publicise their cause, is allowed under agreed rules. Violence, intimidation of non-striking workers and damage to property are criminal offences, whether or not the strike is protected.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Let's make this simple. A trade union is a team of workers. One person asking the boss for more money is easy to ignore. Thousands of people asking together are not. That is the whole idea.

## Subtopic: Strength in Numbers

A trade union is a group of workers who join together so that their voice is louder. Each member pays a small amount every month, and the union speaks and acts for them.

Imagine one worker asking for a raise. The boss can say no and hire someone else. Now imagine every worker in the factory asking together. The boss cannot replace them all. That is bargaining power.

In each workplace, workers choose shop stewards to speak for them. Unions join together in big groups called federations, such as COSATU, FEDUSA, NACTU and SAFTU, to speak for workers across the whole country.

In South Africa, black workers' unions were only legally recognised in 1979, after years of struggle. Today the Constitution gives every worker the right to join a union.

## Subtopic: Sitting at the Table

What do unions actually do? Mostly, they talk. They sit at a table with the employer and negotiate wages, working hours, leave, safety and benefits. This is called collective bargaining.

Here is an example. Workers earn R8 000 a month. The union asks for 9 percent more, which is R720. The employer offers 5 percent, which is R400. They talk and meet in the middle at 7 percent: R560 more, so each worker now earns R8 560.

Unions also help members who are in trouble at work, push for safe workplaces, and train their members. The law that sets the rules for all of this is the Labour Relations Act of 1995.

## Subtopic: When Talks Fail

Sometimes the two sides cannot agree. Then they must first ask the CCMA to help them talk. If that fails after 30 days, the union must give the employer at least 48 hours' warning before a strike.

A strike that follows these steps is protected: workers cannot be fired for striking. But they are not paid while they strike. No work, no pay.

Some workers, like ambulance staff, are not allowed to strike, because people's lives depend on them. And no strike makes violence legal.

Remember: striking is the last step, not the first. The aim is always an agreement. Practise by explaining to someone at home what a trade union is and what it does.
