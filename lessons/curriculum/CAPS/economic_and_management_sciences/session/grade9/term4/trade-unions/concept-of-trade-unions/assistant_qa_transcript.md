### subtopic_1 — What a Trade Union Is
**Assistant:** Why does an individual worker have less bargaining power than a union?

**Tutor:** Because of the imbalance between what each side can do without the other. An employer with many workers can usually replace one worker who refuses its terms, especially where many people are looking for work, as in South Africa with its high unemployment. So a single worker asking for a higher wage has very little to bargain with: if the employer says no, the worker must either accept or leave. When workers act together, the situation changes. The employer cannot easily replace hundreds or thousands of workers at once, especially skilled ones, and a collective refusal to work would stop production. The employer therefore has a strong reason to negotiate. A union also brings expertise: full-time officials who understand labour law, the finances of the industry and negotiating techniques, which most individual workers do not have. This is why unions describe themselves with the phrase unity is strength. The same logic applies on the other side, which is why employers in an industry often form employers' organisations to bargain together.

### subtopic_2 — The Functions of Trade Unions
**Assistant:** What is the difference between a shop steward and a union official?

**Tutor:** A shop steward is a worker elected by union members in a particular workplace to represent them. The shop steward continues to do a normal job, as a machine operator, a clerk or a miner, but also handles union matters at that workplace: raising members' grievances with management, representing members at disciplinary hearings, recruiting new members and keeping members informed. The Labour Relations Act gives a union that represents enough workers in a workplace the right to have shop stewards, and they are entitled to reasonable time off to do this work. A union official is a full-time employee of the union itself, working at regional or national level. Officials negotiate in bargaining councils and with large employers, represent members at the CCMA and in the Labour Court, run the union's finances and administration, and train shop stewards. The two roles work together. Shop stewards know the workplace and its people closely, while officials bring specialised knowledge and represent members across many workplaces. Both are accountable to the members, who elect the union's leaders at congresses.

### subtopic_3 — Trade Unions in South Africa: History and Federations
**Assistant:** Why were the Durban strikes of 1973 and the Wiehahn Commission so important?

**Tutor:** Because together they changed the place of black workers in South Africa's labour relations. Before the 1970s, black workers were excluded from the official system: their unions could not register, could not bargain in the official structures, and their leaders were often banned. The Durban strikes of 1973, in which tens of thousands of workers stopped work to demand better wages, showed that black workers could organise effectively even without legal recognition, and they led to the growth of new, independent unions that built strong structures in workplaces. Employers and the government realised that the existing system could not continue. The Wiehahn Commission was appointed to investigate labour law, and following its recommendations, in 1979, black workers were allowed to join registered trade unions. This opened the way for the rapid growth of unions in the 1980s and the formation of COSATU in 1985. These unions became powerful forces not only in the workplace but in the wider struggle for democracy, which is why labour rights are written into the 1996 Constitution.

### subtopic_4 — Collective Bargaining, the Law and the Right to Strike
**Assistant:** What does collective bargaining usually involve, and why do both sides usually move from their opening positions?

**Tutor:** Collective bargaining usually begins with each side presenting its demands or offer, supported by arguments. The union may point to inflation, which reduces what wages can buy, to the company's profits, or to wages in similar industries. The employer may point to its costs, competition, the state of the economy or the risk of job losses. In the example, the union demands 9 percent, R720 on a wage of R8 000, and the employer offers 5 percent, R400. Opening positions are usually set with room to move, because both sides expect to negotiate. Over several meetings, they exchange proposals, sometimes trading one issue against another, for example a smaller wage increase in exchange for better leave or a housing allowance. Both sides have strong reasons to agree. A strike costs workers their wages and costs the employer its production and customers, so a settlement such as 7 percent, R560 a month, is usually better for both than a long dispute. The agreement is written down as a collective agreement, which binds both parties for its period, often one to three years.

### subtopic_5 — Strength in Numbers
**Assistant:** Why does the law require conciliation and notice before a strike?

**Tutor:** Because strikes are costly for everyone, and the law tries to make sure they happen only after a genuine attempt to resolve the dispute. Conciliation at the CCMA or a bargaining council brings in an independent commissioner who helps the parties to find common ground. Many disputes are settled at this stage, because the commissioner can suggest compromises that neither side wanted to propose first. If conciliation fails, a certificate is issued, or 30 days pass. The requirement of at least 48 hours' written notice, or 7 days where the state is the employer, gives the employer time to prepare, to protect equipment, to make arrangements for customers and, sometimes, to make a final offer. In return for following these steps, workers receive important protection: they may not be dismissed for taking part in a protected strike. A strike that ignores the procedure is unprotected, and strikers can be disciplined or dismissed. The procedure therefore balances two rights in the Constitution: the workers' right to strike and the need for orderly labour relations that protect the economy.

### subtopic_6 — Sitting at the Table
**Assistant:** If a strike is protected, why are workers not paid while they strike?

**Tutor:** Because the law protects workers' jobs during a protected strike, but it does not require the employer to pay for work that was not done. This is called the principle of no work, no pay. It balances the costs of the strike between the two sides. The employer loses production and possibly customers, and the workers lose their wages for every day they are on strike. Both sides therefore feel pressure to settle. Without this principle, workers could strike indefinitely at no cost to themselves, while the employer carried all the losses, which would make bargaining unfair in the other direction. The principle also explains why unions often hold strike funds and why strikes are usually a last resort: a worker earning R8 000 a month who strikes for a week loses about a quarter of a month's pay, which takes a long time to recover even with a good settlement. Some agreements may include payment in kind, such as food rations, which the employer continues to provide during a strike, but wages are not paid.

### subtopic_7 — When Talks Fail
**Assistant:** Why can workers in essential services not strike, and how are their disputes resolved?

**Tutor:** Because a strike in an essential service would endanger people's lives, safety or health. Essential services include emergency medical services, nursing in hospitals, fire-fighting and parts of the police service, as well as other services that the Essential Services Committee has designated. If ambulance staff stopped work to pressure their employer, the people who suffered would be patients, not the employer. The Constitution allows rights to be limited when the limitation is reasonable and justifiable, and protecting life is a strong reason. But taking away the right to strike would leave these workers with no bargaining power, so the law gives them something in its place: compulsory arbitration. If a dispute in an essential service cannot be settled through conciliation, an independent arbitrator hears both sides and makes a decision that is binding on both the employer and the union. In this way, essential workers can still have their wage demands considered fairly, while the public continues to receive the services it depends on. Some services are also declared maintenance services, where a minimum level of work must continue.
