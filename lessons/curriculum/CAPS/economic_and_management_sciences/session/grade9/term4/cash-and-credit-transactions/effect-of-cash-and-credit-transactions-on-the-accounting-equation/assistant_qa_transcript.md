### subtopic_1 — The Accounting Equation and Kasi Cycles
**Assistant:** Why must the accounting equation always balance?

**Tutor:** Because both sides describe the same resources from two different angles. The assets side lists what the business has: equipment, trading stock, amounts owed by debtors and money in the bank. The other side explains where the money for those assets came from: either from the owner, which is owner's equity, or from outsiders, which are liabilities. Every rand of assets must have come from somewhere, so the two sides must be equal. When Kasi Cycles started November, it had assets of R70 000. Of that, R8 000 was financed by suppliers who had not yet been paid, and R62 000 by the owner. There is no other source. When a transaction happens, it changes at least two things, and the changes always keep the balance. If Kasi Cycles buys stock on credit, assets increase and so do liabilities, because the supplier has now financed the extra stock. If it earns a profit on a sale, assets increase and so does owner's equity, because the profit belongs to the owner. If it pays an expense, assets decrease and so does owner's equity, because the owner's profit is reduced. If it receives money from a debtor, one asset increases and another decreases by the same amount, so nothing else changes. If after analysing a transaction the equation does not balance, that is a sign that you have missed one of the effects or recorded one in the wrong direction. That is why checking the totals after each transaction is such a useful habit: an error is found immediately, on the line where it happened, rather than at the end of the month, when it is much harder to trace.

### subtopic_2 — Cash Transactions and the Equation
**Assistant:** Why does a cash sale affect Trading stock and Cost of sales as well as Bank and Sales?

**Tutor:** Because a sale is really two events happening together. The first event is the earning of income: the customer pays R9 000, so Bank increases and Sales, which is income, increases owner's equity. The second event is the giving up of goods: the bicycles and parts that the customer takes away leave the shop, so Trading stock decreases. Those goods cost Kasi Cycles R6 000, and that cost is an expense called Cost of sales, which decreases owner's equity. In a business using the perpetual inventory system, which is what you use in Grade 9, both events are recorded at the time of the sale. The cost price is calculated from the selling price using the mark-up: at 50 percent on cost, the selling price is 150 percent of the cost, so the cost is the selling price times 100 divided by 150. For R9 000, that is R6 000. If we recorded only the first event, owner's equity would rise by R9 000, which would overstate the profit enormously, because it ignores the fact that the business gave up goods worth R6 000. And Trading stock would still show goods that are no longer in the shop. By recording both, the net effect on owner's equity is R3 000, which is the real gross profit on the sale, and the Trading stock account stays accurate. Notice that a cash purchase of stock, by contrast, affects only assets: Trading stock goes up and Bank goes down by the same amount. Nothing has been sold yet, so there is no income and no cost of sales. The expense arises only later, when that stock is sold.

### subtopic_3 — Credit Transactions and the Equation
**Assistant:** If a debtor pays us money, why is that not income?

**Tutor:** Because the income was already recorded when the goods were sold, and recording it again when the money arrives would count the same sale twice. Think about what happened to Mzansi Couriers. On 4 November, Kasi Cycles sold them goods on credit for R4 500. At that moment, Kasi Cycles earned the income: the goods were handed over and Mzansi became legally obliged to pay. So Sales increased owner's equity by R4 500, and the right to receive the money was recorded as an asset, Debtors control. Later, when Mzansi pays, Kasi Cycles is not earning anything new. It is simply converting one asset, the amount owed by Mzansi, into another asset, money in the bank. Bank goes up, Debtors control goes down by the same amount, and owner's equity is unchanged. The same logic applies to Mzansi's payment of R3 600 on 20 November, which settled an amount owed from October: that income was recorded in October, when the original sale was made. The mirror image applies to creditors. When Kasi Cycles buys stock on credit, it records the stock as an asset and the debt as a liability. When it later pays the supplier, it is settling a debt: Bank goes down and Creditors control goes down. That is not an expense, because nothing has been used up; the stock is still there, or has already been accounted for as cost of sales when it was sold. A useful test is to ask: did the owner become richer or poorer because of this transaction? A receipt from a debtor or a payment to a creditor leaves the owner neither richer nor poorer, so owner's equity does not change.

### subtopic_4 — Analysing a Whole Month
**Assistant:** How can I check that my analysis of the whole month is correct?

**Tutor:** There are two independent checks, and if both work, you can be confident. The first is the running balance. After each transaction, calculate total assets, total liabilities and owner's equity, and check that assets equal owner's equity plus liabilities. For Kasi Cycles, the month starts at 70 000 = 62 000 + 8 000 and ends at 73 800 = 62 800 + 11 000, and every line in between balances too. If one line does not balance, you know the error is in that transaction. The second check compares the change in owner's equity with the profit worked out from income and expenses. Owner's equity rose from R62 000 to R62 800, which is R800. Now calculate the profit separately: sales of R22 500 minus cost of sales of R15 000 gives a gross profit of R7 500, and subtracting wages of R3 500, rent of R3 000 and bank charges of R200, which together are R6 700, leaves a net profit of R800. The two figures agree. This works because, in a month with no capital contributions or drawings, the only things that change owner's equity are income and expenses. If you had wrongly treated a receipt from a debtor as income, owner's equity would be too high and would not match the profit calculation. A third, more detailed check is to calculate each asset and liability individually: Trading stock R18 000, Debtors control R7 500, Bank R18 300, Equipment R30 000 and Creditors control R11 000. Those individual balances are exactly what the ledger accounts should show after posting the journals, as we will see in the next lessons, so this analysis also prepares you for the trial balance.
