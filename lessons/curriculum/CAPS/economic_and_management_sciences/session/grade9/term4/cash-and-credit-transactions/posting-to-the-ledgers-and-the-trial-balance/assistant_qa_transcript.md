### subtopic_1 — Posting the Cash Journals to the General Ledger
**Assistant:** Why do we write the name of the other account in the details column?

**Tutor:** Because it lets anyone reading a ledger account understand where each amount came from and trace it to the other half of the double entry. Every transaction affects at least two accounts, once on the debit side and once on the credit side. When the CRJ Sales total of R15 000 is posted, it is credited to Sales and, as part of the R21 000 Bank total, debited to Bank. In the Sales account, the details say Bank: the sales were for cash, and the money went into the bank. In the Bank account, the details say Total receipts, because the Bank total covers several different credit entries, Sales and Debtors control, and naming them all would be clumsy. Now suppose an auditor, or the owner, looks at the Sales account and wonders whether a figure is correct. The details show the other account and the folio shows the journal page, such as CRJ11. The auditor can open the CRJ at that page, check the column total and even check each line against the source documents, the cash register roll or the duplicate invoices. This trail from source document to journal to ledger and back is called the audit trail, and it is one of the most important controls in accounting. It also helps you find your own errors. If the trial balance does not balance, you can work through each account, check that every amount has its matching entry in the account named in the details, and find a posting that was made only once. Without the details and folios, you would have to search through every journal and every account with no guide.

### subtopic_2 — Posting the Credit Journals to the General Ledger
**Assistant:** Why do the Cost of sales totals from the CRJ and the DJ go to Trading stock and not to Bank?

**Tutor:** Because cost of sales has nothing to do with money moving through the bank. When Kasi Cycles sells goods, it gives up stock that cost it money earlier. That cost is now an expense, Cost of sales, and the stock is no longer in the shop, so Trading stock decreases. The double entry is therefore a debit to Cost of sales, the expense increasing, and a credit to Trading stock, the asset decreasing. The money side of the sale, the R9 000 or R6 000 received for cash sales, is a separate double entry: debit Bank, credit Sales. In the CRJ both double entries appear on one line, because both happen at the same moment, but they are posted separately. That is why the Cost of sales column is left out of the cross-cast and why it is posted to Trading stock, not to Bank. The same applies in the DJ: the Sales total of R7 500 is posted to Debtors control and Sales, and the Cost of sales total of R5 000 is posted to Cost of sales and Trading stock. If you posted Cost of sales against Bank, the Bank account would show R15 000 leaving the bank that never actually left, and the Trading stock account would still include goods that had been sold. Both would be wrong, and the bank balance would not agree with the bank statement. Keeping the two double entries separate means that Trading stock always shows the cost of the goods still in the shop, R18 000 at the end of November, and Bank always shows real money.

### subtopic_3 — The Debtors and Creditors Ledgers
**Assistant:** If the control accounts already show the totals, why do we need the debtors and creditors ledgers?

**Tutor:** Because the business must know exactly who owes it money and whom it owes, not only the totals. Debtors control shows that Kasi Cycles is owed R7 500 at the end of November, but it cannot say how much of that is owed by Mzansi Couriers and how much by Greenway School. Without that detail, the business could not send statements to its customers, could not see which customers are paying late, and could not decide whether to give a particular customer more credit. The debtors ledger has a separate account for each customer: Mzansi Couriers owes R4 500 and Greenway School owes R3 000. In the same way, the creditors ledger shows that Kasi Cycles owes Bike Parts Wholesale R7 000 and Trail Supplies R4 000, which it needs in order to check suppliers' statements and plan its payments. The control accounts and the subsidiary ledgers also check each other. The control account is posted from journal totals; the subsidiary ledger is posted from individual lines. If both have been posted correctly, the list of debtors must equal Debtors control, and the list of creditors must equal Creditors control. For Kasi Cycles, 4 500 + 3 000 = 7 500 and 7 000 + 4 000 = 11 000, so both agree. If they did not, the bookkeeper would know that a line had been posted to the wrong customer's account, posted to the wrong side, missed, or added incorrectly in a journal. This is why they are called control accounts: they control the accuracy of the detailed records.

### subtopic_4 — The Trial Balance
**Assistant:** If the trial balance balances, does that mean the books are correct?

**Tutor:** Not necessarily. A balanced trial balance shows that, for every amount posted, the total debits equal the total credits. It catches errors that affect only one side, such as an amount posted to Wages but not to Bank, or a balance added incorrectly. But several kinds of error leave the trial balance balanced. An error of omission: if a whole transaction was never recorded, for example a credit sale invoice that was lost, neither the debit nor the credit was posted, so the totals still agree. An error of principle or of commission: if rent was posted to the debit of Wages instead of Rent expense, the debit is still on the correct side, so the totals agree, but both accounts are wrong. An error of original entry: if a payment of R3 000 was recorded as R300 in the journal, both the debit and credit are R300, so the totals agree, but both are wrong. Compensating errors: two separate mistakes of equal amount on opposite sides cancel each other out. That is why accountants use other checks too: the cross-casts of the journals, the comparison of the debtors and creditors lists with the control accounts, the comparison of the Bank account with the bank statement, and the comparison of suppliers' statements with the creditors ledger. For Kasi Cycles, every one of these checks agrees, and the profit of R800 from the trial balance matches the increase in owner's equity found from the accounting equation. When several independent checks all agree, you can be confident that the books are correct.
