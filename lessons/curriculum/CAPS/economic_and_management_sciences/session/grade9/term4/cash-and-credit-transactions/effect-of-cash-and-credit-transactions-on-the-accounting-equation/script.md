# Part 1 — Expert

This year you have learnt to record cash transactions and credit transactions in separate lessons. In this final accounting topic of Grade 9, we bring them together. In this lesson we analyse the effect of a whole month of cash and credit transactions on the accounting equation; in the next lessons we record the same month in the four subsidiary journals, post it to the ledgers and prepare a trial balance. Our business is Kasi Cycles, a fictional sole trader in Soweto that sells bicycles, parts and accessories at a mark-up of 50 percent on cost.

## Subtopic: The Accounting Equation and Kasi Cycles

The accounting equation states that assets equal owner's equity plus liabilities: A = OE + L. Assets are the possessions and amounts owed to the business, such as equipment, trading stock, debtors and money in the bank. Liabilities are the debts of the business to outsiders, such as creditors and loans. Owner's equity is the owner's interest in the business: the capital contributed, plus profits, minus losses and drawings.

The equation always balances, because every rand of assets was financed either by the owner or by outsiders. Every transaction has at least two effects, and after every transaction the equation must still balance. That is the principle of double entry.

On 1 November, the start of its financial year, Kasi Cycles had the following balances: Equipment R30 000, Trading stock R20 000, Debtors control R6 000 and Bank R14 000, so total assets were 30 000 + 20 000 + 6 000 + 14 000 = R70 000. Creditors control, the only liability, was R8 000. Capital was R62 000. Check: 62 000 + 8 000 = 70 000. The equation balances.

The debtors were Mzansi Couriers, who owed R3 600, and Greenway School, who owed R2 400; 3 600 + 2 400 = 6 000. The creditors were Bike Parts Wholesale, owed R5 000, and Trail Supplies, owed R3 000; 5 000 + 3 000 = 8 000.

When analysing a transaction, ask three questions. Which accounts are affected? Is each one an asset, a liability or owner's equity? Does each one increase or decrease? Then check that the equation still balances.

Remember how income and expenses affect owner's equity. Income, such as sales, increases owner's equity, because it adds to the owner's profit. Expenses, such as wages, rent, bank charges and cost of sales, decrease owner's equity, because they reduce the profit. Income and expenses are not separate parts of the equation; they are changes in owner's equity.

## Subtopic: Cash Transactions and the Equation

A cash transaction is one in which money is received or paid immediately. Kasi Cycles' November cash transactions show the main patterns.

On 2 November, Kasi Cycles made cash sales of R9 000. The cost price of the goods, at a mark-up of 50 percent on cost, is 9 000 × 100 ÷ 150 = R6 000. This transaction has two parts. The sale: Bank increases by R9 000, an asset, and owner's equity increases by R9 000, because Sales is income. The cost: Trading stock decreases by R6 000, an asset, and owner's equity decreases by R6 000, because Cost of sales is an expense. The net effect on assets is 9 000 - 6 000 = +R3 000, and on owner's equity also +R3 000, which is the gross profit on the sale. Assets become 70 000 + 3 000 = R73 000; owner's equity becomes 62 000 + 3 000 = R65 000; liabilities stay R8 000. Check: 65 000 + 8 000 = 73 000.

On 8 November, wages of R3 500 were paid by EFT. Bank decreases by R3 500 and owner's equity decreases by R3 500, because wages are an expense. Assets fall to R78 000 at that date, and owner's equity to R63 000, as we will see in the full analysis.

On 18 November, Kasi Cycles bought trading stock for R2 000 and paid immediately by EFT. Trading stock increases by R2 000 and Bank decreases by R2 000. Both are assets, so total assets do not change, and neither do liabilities or owner's equity. This is an exchange of one asset for another.

On 26 November, rent of R3 000 was paid by EFT, and on 30 November the bank statement showed bank charges of R200. Each of these decreases Bank, an asset, and decreases owner's equity, because each is an expense.

On 27 November, cash sales were R6 000, at a cost of 6 000 × 100 ÷ 150 = R4 000. As on 2 November: Bank +6 000, Trading stock -4 000, and owner's equity +6 000 and -4 000, a net increase of R2 000.

Notice the pattern. Every cash transaction affects the Bank account, because money moves. Cash sales and cash expenses change owner's equity; cash purchases of stock only swap one asset for another.

## Subtopic: Credit Transactions and the Equation

A credit transaction is one in which payment is made later. It creates a debtor, when the business sells on credit, or a creditor, when the business buys on credit. Bank is not affected at the time of the transaction.

On 4 November, Kasi Cycles sold goods on credit to Mzansi Couriers, invoice 301, for R4 500, cost price 4 500 × 100 ÷ 150 = R3 000. The sale: Debtors control increases by R4 500, an asset, and owner's equity increases by R4 500, Sales. The cost: Trading stock decreases by R3 000 and owner's equity decreases by R3 000, Cost of sales. Net: assets +R1 500, owner's equity +R1 500. The effect on owner's equity is exactly the same as for a cash sale: the profit is earned when the goods are sold, not when the money arrives. The only difference is that the asset increased is Debtors control instead of Bank.

On 15 November, goods were sold on credit to Greenway School, invoice 302, R3 000, cost 3 000 × 100 ÷ 150 = R2 000. Debtors control +3 000, Trading stock -2 000, owner's equity +3 000 and -2 000.

On 6 November, Kasi Cycles bought trading stock on credit from Bike Parts Wholesale, invoice BP77, R7 000. Trading stock increases by R7 000, an asset, and Creditors control increases by R7 000, a liability. Owner's equity does not change, because buying stock is not an expense; the stock is still an asset until it is sold. On 22 November, trading stock of R4 000 was bought on credit from Trail Supplies, invoice TS210, with the same effect.

Credit transactions lead to later cash transactions. On 10 November, Greenway School paid R2 400 in settlement of its account, receipt 088. Bank increases by R2 400 and Debtors control decreases by R2 400. Both are assets, so total assets do not change, and owner's equity is not affected. Receiving money from a debtor is not income, because the income was recorded when the goods were sold. On 20 November, Mzansi Couriers paid R3 600, receipt 089, with the same effect.

On 12 November, Kasi Cycles paid Trail Supplies R3 000 by EFT, settling its account. Bank decreases by R3 000, an asset, and Creditors control decreases by R3 000, a liability. Owner's equity does not change: paying a debt is not an expense. On 25 November, Kasi Cycles paid Bike Parts Wholesale R5 000, with the same effect.

So the full cycle has two stages. Credit sale: debtors up, profit earned. Receipt: bank up, debtors down, no profit effect. Credit purchase: stock up, creditors up. Payment: bank down, creditors down, no profit effect.

## Subtopic: Analysing a Whole Month

Now follow the totals through November, in date order. We write each date as assets = owner's equity + liabilities.

1 November: 70 000 = 62 000 + 8 000.
2 November, cash sales: 73 000 = 65 000 + 8 000.
4 November, credit sale to Mzansi: assets +1 500, owner's equity +1 500: 74 500 = 66 500 + 8 000.
6 November, credit purchase from Bike Parts: assets and liabilities +7 000: 81 500 = 66 500 + 15 000.
8 November, wages: 78 000 = 63 000 + 15 000.
10 November, receipt from Greenway: no change in totals: 78 000 = 63 000 + 15 000.
12 November, payment to Trail Supplies: 75 000 = 63 000 + 12 000.
15 November, credit sale to Greenway: 76 000 = 64 000 + 12 000.
18 November, cash purchase of stock: no change: 76 000 = 64 000 + 12 000.
20 November, receipt from Mzansi: no change: 76 000 = 64 000 + 12 000.
22 November, credit purchase from Trail Supplies: 80 000 = 64 000 + 16 000.
25 November, payment to Bike Parts: 75 000 = 64 000 + 11 000.
26 November, rent: 72 000 = 61 000 + 11 000.
27 November, cash sales: 74 000 = 63 000 + 11 000.
30 November, bank charges: 73 800 = 62 800 + 11 000.

The equation balanced after every one of the fourteen transactions.

At the end of November, the individual assets are: Equipment R30 000, unchanged; Trading stock 20 000 + 7 000 + 2 000 + 4 000 - 6 000 - 3 000 - 2 000 - 4 000 = R18 000; Debtors control 6 000 + 4 500 + 3 000 - 2 400 - 3 600 = R7 500; and Bank 14 000 + 9 000 + 2 400 + 3 600 + 6 000 - 3 500 - 3 000 - 2 000 - 5 000 - 3 000 - 200 = R18 300. Total 30 000 + 18 000 + 7 500 + 18 300 = R73 800. Liabilities: Creditors control 8 000 + 7 000 + 4 000 - 3 000 - 5 000 = R11 000.

Owner's equity increased from R62 000 to R62 800, an increase of R800. That increase is the profit for November. Check it from the income and expenses: sales 9 000 + 4 500 + 3 000 + 6 000 = R22 500; cost of sales 6 000 + 3 000 + 2 000 + 4 000 = R15 000; gross profit 22 500 - 15 000 = R7 500; expenses 3 500 + 3 000 + 200 = R6 700; net profit 7 500 - 6 700 = R800. Both methods agree.

Notice what did not change owner's equity: the credit and cash purchases of stock, the receipts from debtors and the payments to creditors. Students often treat a receipt from a debtor as income or a payment to a creditor as an expense. Both are wrong, and both would count the same income or expense twice.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Let's make this simple. The accounting equation is a scale. On one side: everything the business has. On the other side: who paid for it, the owner or outsiders. Every transaction moves something, but the scale must always balance.

## Subtopic: The Scale That Always Balances

Kasi Cycles sells bicycles in Soweto. On 1 November it has R70 000 of assets: equipment, stock, debtors and money in the bank. The owner's share is R62 000 and the business owes suppliers R8 000. Check: 62 000 + 8 000 = 70 000. Balanced.

Income makes the owner's share bigger. Expenses make it smaller. Cost of sales is an expense too: the stock that left the shop.

## Subtopic: Cash Now or Pay Later

Cash sale of R9 000: money in the bank goes up R9 000, stock goes down R6 000, and the owner gains R3 000 profit.

Credit sale of R4 500: the debtor goes up instead of the bank. The profit is the same, R1 500. Profit is earned when the goods are sold.

Buying stock on credit: stock goes up, and what we owe goes up. No profit or loss.

Later, the debtor pays: bank up, debtor down. No new income. We pay a supplier: bank down, creditor down. No expense.

## Subtopic: Checking the Month

After fourteen transactions, Kasi Cycles has assets of R73 800, owes R11 000, and the owner's share is R62 800. Check: 62 800 + 11 000 = 73 800. Balanced.

The owner's share grew by R800. That is the profit: R7 500 gross profit minus R6 700 of wages, rent and bank charges.

So the scale tips only one way for the owner: income up, expenses down. Everything else is just moving things around. Practise by analysing each November transaction yourself and checking the totals at the end.
