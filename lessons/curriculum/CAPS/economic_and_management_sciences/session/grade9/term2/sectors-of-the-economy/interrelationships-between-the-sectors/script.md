# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains the interrelationships between the primary, secondary and tertiary sectors and the role each plays in the economy. The anchor is the value chain: the sequence of stages through which a product passes from raw material to final consumer, with each stage adding value. The governing principle is interdependence: no sector can function on its own, because each one buys from and sells to the others, so growth or decline in one sector spreads to the rest through linkages. South African examples show how mining, manufacturing and services are tied together.

## Subtopic: The Value Chain: How Sectors Feed Each Other

A value chain is the full set of activities needed to bring a product from its origin in nature to the final consumer. At each stage, a business adds value by transforming, moving or selling the product, and the price rises accordingly.

Consider a cotton T-shirt. A cotton farmer, in the primary sector, grows and harvests cotton and sells the cotton for one T-shirt for R10. A textile and clothing factory, in the secondary sector, spins the cotton into yarn, weaves and dyes the fabric, cuts and sews the shirt, and sells it to a retailer for R60. The factory has added 60 - 10 = 50, or R50, of value. A clothing retailer, in the tertiary sector, transports the shirt, displays it and sells it to a customer for R120, adding 120 - 60 = 60, or R60, of value. The total value added along the chain is 10 + 50 + 60 = 120, exactly equal to the final price paid by the consumer. This is an important result: the price of a final product is made up of the value added at every stage, which is the income earned by the workers, landlords, lenders and owners at each stage.

The same pattern applies across the economy. Iron ore mined in the Northern Cape, primary, becomes steel and then car bodies in factories, secondary, and cars are sold by dealerships and financed by banks, tertiary. Sugar cane grown in KwaZulu-Natal, primary, is milled into sugar and used in soft drinks, secondary, which are sold in shops and restaurants, tertiary. Timber from Mpumalanga forests, primary, becomes paper and furniture, secondary, which is sold in stationery and furniture stores, tertiary.

The value chain shows two kinds of links. Backward linkages are a business's links to its suppliers: a car factory has backward linkages to steel makers, glass makers, tyre makers and, further back, to mines. Forward linkages are links to its customers: a steel works has forward linkages to car factories, construction companies and appliance makers. The more linkages an industry has inside a country, the more jobs and income it creates there. That is why the government is keen to see more of each value chain located in South Africa, rather than importing processed goods made from South African raw materials.

## Subtopic: How Each Sector Depends on the Others

Each sector depends on the other two, both as suppliers and as customers.

The primary sector depends on the secondary sector for its equipment and inputs: tractors, fertilisers, mining machinery, explosives, fishing trawlers, fuel and electricity. It depends on the tertiary sector for transport to get its products to factories and ports, for banks to lend money for land and equipment, for insurance against drought and accidents, for research and training, and for the traders who export its products. And it depends on both as customers: mills, smelters and canneries buy its raw materials, and supermarkets buy fresh fruit and vegetables.

The secondary sector depends on the primary sector for raw materials: wheat for flour, iron ore and coal for steel, crude oil and coal for fuel, cotton and wool for textiles, timber for furniture. Without primary products, factories have nothing to process. It depends on the tertiary sector for transport and logistics, for wholesale and retail businesses to sell its products, for finance to buy machinery, for advertising, and for the education and health services that produce skilled, healthy workers. It also depends on the secondary sector itself: factories buy machinery, components and electricity from other secondary businesses.

The tertiary sector depends on the primary and secondary sectors for the goods it sells and uses. A supermarket would be empty without farms and factories. Transport businesses need vehicles and fuel made in the secondary sector. Banks and insurers earn much of their income from lending to and insuring farms, mines and factories. And tourism depends on the natural environment, which is linked to the primary sector, and on hotels and roads built by the construction industry.

Government, which is part of the tertiary sector, plays a special role in supporting all three: it builds and maintains infrastructure such as roads, ports and railways through state-owned enterprises such as Transnet; it provides education and training; it regulates businesses; and it collects taxes from all sectors to pay for these services.

This web of dependence means that the sectors do not compete for importance; they complete each other. A country with a strong primary sector but weak manufacturing and services earns little from its resources. A country with strong services but no reliable supply of goods and raw materials depends on imports.

## Subtopic: The Role of Each Sector in the South African Economy

Each sector makes a distinctive contribution to the economy.

The primary sector supplies the raw materials on which the rest of the economy is built, and it is a major earner of foreign exchange. Mining exports, especially platinum group metals, gold, coal, iron ore and manganese, make up a large share of South Africa's export earnings, which pay for imports such as crude oil and machinery. Agriculture provides food security for the country, exports products such as citrus, wine, grapes and nuts, and is a major employer in rural areas. Mining and agriculture also support whole towns and their local economies.

The secondary sector adds value to raw materials, creating jobs and skills. Manufacturing has strong linkages to both the primary and tertiary sectors, so its growth has large spill-over effects. The motor industry is one of South Africa's largest manufacturing exporters. Construction builds the infrastructure, roads, dams, power stations, schools and houses, that the whole economy relies on, and it employs many workers. Electricity and water supply are essential inputs for every business and household; when electricity supply was unreliable during the years of severe load-shedding, production in all sectors was disrupted.

The tertiary sector is the largest contributor to South Africa's GDP and the largest employer. It distributes goods to consumers through trade and transport, provides finance that allows businesses to invest and households to buy houses and cars, and provides the education and health services that build human capital. Tourism earns foreign exchange as an export of services when foreign visitors spend money in South Africa. Government services, from policing to public health, are also part of this sector.

Together, the three sectors produce the country's total output, GDP; provide employment and incomes; earn foreign exchange through exports; and supply the goods and services that meet people's needs. Measuring how each sector grows from quarter to quarter allows Statistics South Africa and the government to see which parts of the economy are expanding and which are struggling.

## Subtopic: When One Sector Struggles: Ripple Effects

Because the sectors are linked, a shock in one spreads to the others. Economists call these spill-over or ripple effects.

Consider a drought that cuts the maize harvest, a primary sector shock. Farmers earn less and may lay off workers. Mills have less maize to process and may reduce shifts, so the secondary sector is affected. Maize meal becomes more expensive in shops, and households spend more on food and less on other things, so retailers of other goods sell less, affecting the tertiary sector. Transport companies carry less maize. Banks may face farmers who cannot repay loans. The government may need to spend money on drought relief and on importing maize.

Consider a mine closure, perhaps because the ore has run out or world prices have fallen. Thousands of mine workers lose their jobs. The suppliers of mining equipment and explosives, in the secondary sector, lose a customer. In the mining town, shops, taxis, hair salons and guest houses lose customers, because the miners and their families have less to spend. Municipal revenue falls because rates and service charges go unpaid. Families in other provinces and in neighbouring countries who received money sent home by mine workers also lose income. A single closure can shrink a whole town's economy.

Consider load-shedding, a shock to the secondary sector's electricity supply. Factories lose production time or must buy diesel for generators, raising costs. Mines must cut production for safety reasons. Shops lose sales and spoilt stock, and must pay for backup power. Every sector is affected, which is why reliable electricity is so important for economic growth.

Positive shocks ripple too. When a new car plant opens, it creates jobs directly and also orders components from local suppliers, who hire more workers, and those workers spend their wages in local shops. The total increase in income is larger than the plant's own wage bill. In Grade 11 you will learn to call this the multiplier effect.

Ripple effects also cross borders, because South Africa's sectors are linked to the global economy studied in Term 1. When demand for cars in Europe falls, orders to vehicle plants in the Eastern Cape fall, then orders to component suppliers, then spending in the towns where the workers live. When world platinum prices fall, mines cut jobs. Understanding these chains helps governments and businesses prepare: diversifying into several products and markets, building skills that can move between industries, and supporting communities that depend on a single employer.

The error museum holds four mistakes. First, thinking that the tertiary sector does not need the other two because it is the biggest; it sells and uses their goods. Second, thinking that a problem in mining affects only mine workers; it ripples through suppliers, shops and towns. Third, adding the full price at each stage of the value chain to find total value; only the value added at each stage is counted. Fourth, describing the sectors as competing; they depend on and complete each other.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same ideas again, through the journey of a T-shirt, a circle of dependence, and a mining town, in plain words with the same facts.

## Subtopic: The Journey of a T-shirt

Let's follow a T-shirt from a field to your cupboard and watch its price grow.

A farmer grows cotton. The cotton needed for one T-shirt sells for R10. That is the primary sector.

A factory buys the cotton, spins it into thread, weaves cloth, dyes it, cuts it and sews a T-shirt. It sells the shirt to a shop for R60. The factory added 60 - 10 = 50 rand of value. That is the secondary sector.

The shop transports the shirt, puts it on a hanger, advertises it, and sells it to you for R120. The shop added 120 - 60 = 60 rand of value. That is the tertiary sector.

Now add up what each step added: 10 + 50 + 60 = 120. That is exactly the price you paid! The R120 you paid is shared out along the chain: some to the farmer, some to the factory workers and owner, some to the shop workers and owner.

That chain is called a value chain. Every link adds value, and every link means jobs. So if South Africa sends its cotton overseas and buys back finished shirts, the jobs in the factory step happen somewhere else. If the shirt is made here, the jobs stay here. That is why people say buy local.

## Subtopic: Everyone Needs Everyone

Picture the three sectors holding hands in a circle.

The farmer needs a tractor, fertiliser and diesel. Who makes those? The secondary sector. The farmer needs a truck to take the crop to market, a bank loan to buy seed, and a phone to check prices. Who provides those? The tertiary sector.

The factory needs cotton, wheat, iron ore or wood. Who supplies those? The primary sector. It needs trucks, shops to sell its products, and a bank. That is the tertiary sector.

The shop needs things to sell. Who grows and makes them? The primary and secondary sectors. It needs electricity and a building, also from the secondary sector.

Even your school is part of the circle. The school is tertiary, but its desks came from a factory, the paper in your books came from trees in a forest, and the electricity for the lights came from coal dug out of a mine.

Let go of one hand and the circle breaks. Without farms and mines, factories have nothing to make. Without factories, shops have nothing to sell and farms have no tractors. Without shops, transport and banks, nothing gets to customers and nobody can borrow to grow.

Each sector also does a special job for the country. Primary: raw materials, food and a lot of our export money. Secondary: making things, adding value, building roads and making electricity. Tertiary: getting things to people, plus banking, schooling, health care and tourism. It is also where most South Africans work.

## Subtopic: When the Mine Closes

Imagine a town that grew up around a mine. Three thousand people work underground.

One day the mine closes. What happens?

The mine workers lose their jobs. That is the primary sector hit first.

The factory in the next town that made drills and safety boots for the mine loses its biggest customer and lays off workers. Now the secondary sector is hit.

In the mining town, people have less money. The supermarket sells less. The taxi drivers have fewer passengers. The hairdresser, the butcher and the guest house all struggle. Some close. Now the tertiary sector is hit.

The municipality collects less money for water and electricity, so services get worse. Families far away who received money from relatives at the mine get less.

One closure ripples like a stone thrown in a pond, spreading wider and wider.

It works the other way too. When a new car factory opens, it hires workers, buys parts from local suppliers who hire more workers, and all those workers spend their pay in local shops. The good news ripples outward too.

This is why, when a big factory or mine is in trouble, the whole town pays attention, and why the government sometimes steps in to help find new jobs or new businesses for the area.

That is why the sectors are not rivals. They are a team. When one does well, the others benefit; when one struggles, the others feel it.
