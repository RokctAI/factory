# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines the sustainable use of resources in each sector of the economy and the skills each sector requires. The anchor is the definition of sustainable development: development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs. The governing principle is that every sector draws on natural, human and capital resources, and that businesses and countries can only prosper in the long run if they use those resources wisely and develop the skills of their people. This topic links to Grade 10 Business Studies, Geography and Life Sciences.

## Subtopic: Renewable and Non-renewable Resources and Sustainable Development

Natural resources can be classified as renewable or non-renewable.

Renewable resources can be replaced naturally within a human lifetime if they are used carefully. Examples are sunlight, wind, water in rivers and dams, forests, fish populations, soil and grazing land. But renewable does not mean unlimited. A fish population can be fished so heavily that it collapses, a forest can be cut faster than it regrows, and soil can be eroded or exhausted. Renewable resources are only renewable if they are used at a rate no faster than they can recover.

Non-renewable resources exist in fixed quantities and cannot be replaced within a human timescale once used. Examples are coal, crude oil, natural gas, gold, platinum, iron ore and other minerals. Every tonne mined is gone for good. A mine has a limited life, after which the ore body is exhausted.

Sustainable development is the idea that economic growth should not destroy the resources and environment on which future growth depends. The most widely used definition comes from the Brundtland Commission of the United Nations, in its report Our Common Future in 1987: development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs. Sustainable development is often described as balancing three pillars: the economy, society and the environment. A business that makes a profit by polluting a river, harming the health of the community downstream, is not sustainable, even if it is profitable today.

South Africa faces particular sustainability challenges. It is a water-scarce country: its average annual rainfall of roughly 450 to 500 millimetres is well below the world average, and several cities have faced severe droughts, most famously Cape Town's day zero crisis of 2017 to 2018. Its electricity has come mainly from coal, which releases large amounts of carbon dioxide, a greenhouse gas that contributes to climate change. Many of its mineral resources, though large, are finite. And many communities depend directly on natural resources such as grazing land, firewood and fish for their livelihoods.

The Constitution of South Africa, in section 24, gives everyone the right to an environment that is not harmful to their health or well-being, and to have the environment protected for present and future generations through reasonable measures that secure ecologically sustainable development while promoting justifiable economic and social development. That makes sustainability a legal duty, not only a good idea.

## Subtopic: Sustainable Practices in Each Sector

Each sector has its own sustainability challenges and practices.

Primary sector. Farmers can use resources sustainably by rotating crops to keep soil fertile, using conservation agriculture that disturbs the soil less, using drip irrigation that delivers water directly to plant roots instead of flooding fields, controlling grazing to prevent overgrazing and soil erosion, and reducing the use of harmful pesticides. In fishing, the government sets quotas, the total allowable catch, and closed seasons, to prevent overfishing; abalone, known locally as perlemoen, has been severely depleted by poaching, which shows what happens when limits are ignored. Forestry companies replant trees after harvesting. Mines are required by law to rehabilitate land after mining, restoring it so that it can be used again, and to manage acid mine drainage, the polluted water that can flow from old mines into rivers.

Secondary sector. Factories can reduce their environmental impact by using energy more efficiently, switching to renewable energy such as solar panels on factory roofs, reducing waste by redesigning processes, recycling materials such as scrap metal, glass, paper and plastic, treating waste water before releasing it, and controlling air pollution with filters. Power generation is shifting gradually from coal towards renewable energy: since 2011, South Africa's Renewable Energy Independent Power Producer Procurement Programme has added large wind and solar farms, especially in the Northern Cape, Western Cape and Eastern Cape. Construction can use energy-efficient building designs and recycled materials.

Tertiary sector. Service businesses also use resources. Offices can save electricity and paper, using digital records instead of printing. Retailers can reduce plastic packaging, sell refill products and offer recycling points; since 2003 South African shoppers have been charged for plastic shopping bags to reduce their use. Transport companies can use more fuel-efficient vehicles and plan routes to save fuel. Tourism can be eco-friendly, with lodges using solar power and supporting conservation, which also attracts visitors to South Africa's national parks. Electronic waste, old phones and computers, should be recycled responsibly because it contains harmful substances and valuable metals.

Across all sectors, the principles of reduce, reuse and recycle apply, in that order of priority. Reducing use is best, because the resource is never consumed. Reusing keeps a product in use longer. Recycling turns waste into raw materials for new products, which creates a whole industry: in South Africa, informal waste pickers collect a large share of the recyclable packaging that is recovered, earning a living while reducing landfill.

Water deserves special attention in South Africa. Agriculture uses the largest share of the country's water, mainly for irrigation, so efficient irrigation saves a great deal. Mines and power stations use large amounts too, and both can pollute water sources if they are not managed well. Cities lose a significant share of their treated water through leaking pipes before it reaches any tap. Saving water therefore involves every sector: farmers, factories, municipalities and households all have a part to play.

Sustainability often saves money as well. Using less electricity, water and materials reduces costs. Customers increasingly prefer businesses with good environmental practices. And businesses that protect their resources, such as farmers who protect their soil, protect their own future income.

## Subtopic: Skills Required in Each Sector

Each sector needs particular skills, as well as general skills that every worker benefits from.

Primary sector skills. Agriculture needs knowledge of crops, soils, animal health and water management, and increasingly technical skills to operate and maintain machinery, irrigation systems and digital tools such as GPS-guided tractors and weather apps. Careers include farmers, farm managers, agricultural scientists, veterinarians and extension officers who advise farmers. Mining needs geologists to find ore bodies, mining engineers to plan mines safely, surveyors, metallurgists to process ore, and skilled operators of heavy machinery, as well as strict safety skills. Fishing and forestry need knowledge of marine and plant life, navigation and conservation.

Secondary sector skills. Manufacturing and construction need technical and practical skills: artisans such as electricians, fitters and turners, boilermakers, welders, millwrights and plumbers; machine operators; engineers in mechanical, electrical, chemical and civil engineering; quality controllers who check that products meet standards; and production managers who plan and organise output. Increasingly, factories use computer-controlled machines and robots, so technicians who can program and maintain them are in demand. Construction needs bricklayers, carpenters, quantity surveyors and project managers.

Tertiary sector skills. Services need strong interpersonal and communication skills, because they deal directly with people: customer service, sales and negotiation. Financial services need numeracy and financial literacy, such as accountants, bookkeepers, auditors and financial advisers, the careers that grow from the accounting topics in this course. Information and communication technology needs programmers, network technicians and data analysts. Health and education need nurses, doctors, teachers and social workers. Tourism and hospitality need chefs, guides, hotel managers and language skills. Transport needs drivers, logistics planners and pilots.

General skills are needed in every sector: literacy and numeracy, problem-solving, teamwork, reliability, computer literacy, and the ability to keep learning as technology changes. Entrepreneurial skills, spotting opportunities, planning, managing money and taking calculated risks, are valuable in every sector and are developed in the entrepreneurship topics of Term 3.

## Subtopic: Choosing a Path: Skills, Careers and the Green Economy

South Africa has a serious skills mismatch: high unemployment, especially among young people, alongside shortages of skilled workers in many fields. Artisans, engineers, ICT specialists, accountants, nurses and teachers of mathematics and science are among the skills in demand. At the same time, many young people leave school without the skills employers need. Closing that gap is one of the country's most important economic challenges.

Several routes lead to the skills each sector needs. Universities offer degrees in engineering, accounting, agriculture, medicine and education. Technical and vocational education and training colleges, TVET colleges, offer practical qualifications for artisans and technicians. Learnerships and apprenticeships combine study with workplace training, often funded through the Sector Education and Training Authorities, the SETAs, of which there is one for each broad sector of the economy, such as agriculture, mining, manufacturing, services and finance. Short courses and online learning help workers update their skills throughout their careers.

The subjects chosen at the end of Grade 9 influence these paths. Accounting leads to financial careers in the tertiary sector. Economics and Business Studies lead to management, economics and entrepreneurship in every sector. Mathematics and Physical Sciences are needed for engineering and most technical careers in the primary and secondary sectors. Agricultural Sciences leads into farming and food production.

The move towards sustainability is creating new jobs, sometimes called green jobs: installing and maintaining solar panels and wind turbines, managing water and waste, recycling, rehabilitating mined land, conservation and eco-tourism. These need a combination of technical skills and environmental knowledge, and they exist in all three sectors.

The error museum holds four mistakes. First, believing renewable resources cannot run out; overfishing and overgrazing can destroy them. Second, believing sustainability is only a concern of the primary sector; factories and offices also use energy, water and materials. Third, believing that recycling is the most important of the three Rs; reducing comes first. Fourth, believing that only university degrees lead to good careers; artisans and technicians trained at TVET colleges are in high demand.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same ideas again, through an inheritance, the three Rs at work, and matching skills to jobs, in plain words with the same facts.

## Subtopic: Don't Spend the Inheritance

Imagine your grandmother leaves you two gifts.

The first is a fruit orchard. Every year it gives fruit. If you pick only the fruit and look after the trees, you will have fruit forever. But if you chop down the trees for firewood, you get a lot once and then nothing. That orchard is like a renewable resource: sun, wind, water, fish, forests and soil. They keep giving, as long as you do not take more than they can grow back.

The second gift is a box of gold coins. Every coin you spend is gone forever. That box is like a non-renewable resource: coal, oil, gold, platinum and iron ore. When a mine has dug out all its ore, there is no more.

Sustainable development means living off the fruit without cutting down the orchard, and spending the gold coins wisely so that your children are not left with nothing. A famous United Nations report in 1987 put it like this: meet the needs of today without spoiling the chances of future generations to meet their needs.

South Africa has some special worries. We are a dry country, and Cape Town nearly ran out of water in 2018. Most of our electricity has come from burning coal, which pollutes the air and adds to climate change. And our Constitution says everyone has the right to an environment that is not harmful, protected for future generations too.

## Subtopic: Reduce, Reuse, Recycle at Work

Every sector can look after the inheritance. The rule is the three Rs, in this order: reduce, reuse, recycle.

On a farm: drip irrigation drips water right onto the roots instead of flooding the field, so less water is used. Rotating crops keeps the soil healthy. Not letting too many cattle graze one patch stops the soil washing away.

At sea: fishing companies may only catch a set amount each year, so the fish can breed. Perlemoen shows what happens when people ignore the rules: poaching nearly wiped it out.

At a mine: when the mine closes, the company must fix the land, cover the holes, replant, and stop dirty water running into rivers.

In a factory: use less electricity, put solar panels on the roof, recycle scrap metal and paper, and clean dirty water before letting it out.

In shops and offices: switch off lights, print less, charge for plastic bags so people bring their own, and collect old phones and batteries for recycling.

Why reduce first? Because the best rubbish is the rubbish you never made. Reusing a bottle is good; recycling it is good; never needing it is best.

And here is a bonus: saving electricity, water and materials saves money too. Being green is often good business.

## Subtopic: Matching Skills to Jobs

Every sector needs people with the right skills. Let's match them.

Primary sector. A farmer needs to understand soil, plants, animals and water, and today also how to use machines and apps. A mine needs geologists who find the minerals, engineers who plan safe tunnels, and operators who drive huge machines, all with strong safety habits.

Secondary sector. Factories and building sites need artisans, people who work with their hands and their heads: electricians, welders, fitters, plumbers, bricklayers. They need engineers and people who check quality. Many factories now use robots, so they need technicians who can program and fix them.

Tertiary sector. Shops, banks, hotels, hospitals and schools need people skills: talking to customers, explaining, listening. They also need number skills, like accountants and bookkeepers, computer skills, like programmers, and caring skills, like nurses and teachers.

Some skills every job needs: reading, writing, maths, working in a team, solving problems, being on time and being able to learn new things.

How do you get these skills? University, a TVET college for practical trades, learnerships and apprenticeships where you learn while you work, and short courses. The subjects you choose next year matter: Accounting for money careers, Maths and Science for engineering and trades, Business Studies and Economics for running businesses.

Think about what you enjoy. If you like being outdoors and working with plants or animals, look at the primary sector. If you like building, fixing and seeing how things work, look at the secondary sector. If you like people, numbers, computers or helping others, look at the tertiary sector. There is a place for every kind of strength.

And there are new green jobs too: fitting solar panels, recycling, saving water and protecting nature. Looking after the planet is becoming a career in its own right.
