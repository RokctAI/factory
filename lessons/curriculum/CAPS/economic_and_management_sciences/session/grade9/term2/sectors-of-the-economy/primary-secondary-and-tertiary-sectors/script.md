# Part 1 — Expert

This session describes the three sectors of the economy, primary, secondary and tertiary, and the types of businesses found in each. The anchor is a simple classification by what a business does: the primary sector extracts natural resources, the secondary sector processes and manufactures them into goods, and the tertiary sector provides services. The governing principle is that every business can be placed in a sector by asking what its main activity is, and that the relative size of the sectors tells us a great deal about how developed an economy is. This topic leads directly into Grade 10 Business Studies.

## Subtopic: Why Economists Divide the Economy into Sectors

An economy contains millions of businesses doing very different things: farming maize, mining gold, assembling cars, baking bread, selling clothes, transporting goods, teaching, banking and healing. To understand an economy, economists group these activities into sectors according to the kind of activity involved. The most widely used classification has three sectors.

The primary sector takes natural resources directly from nature: from the land, from under the ground and from the sea. The secondary sector takes raw materials and turns them into finished or semi-finished goods: it manufactures, processes and constructs. The tertiary sector provides services rather than physical goods: it distributes and sells goods, and provides services to people and businesses.

Grouping businesses this way is useful for several reasons. Governments use it to measure which parts of the economy are growing or shrinking, and to plan policies, for example to support manufacturing or agriculture. Statistics South Africa publishes the contribution of each industry to gross domestic product, GDP, every quarter. Businesses use it to understand their suppliers and customers. And learners use it to understand career options, because each sector needs different skills.

The relative size of the sectors changes as an economy develops. In poor, mainly agricultural economies, most people work in the primary sector, growing food. As countries industrialise, the secondary sector grows: factories and construction expand, as happened in Britain during the Industrial Revolution and in China since the 1980s. In developed economies, the tertiary sector becomes the largest, as rising incomes increase demand for services such as finance, health, education, tourism and entertainment. In South Africa, the tertiary sector now produces roughly two-thirds of GDP, the secondary sector just under a fifth, and the primary sector around a tenth, although mining remains very important for exports.

Some economists add further sectors, such as a quaternary sector for knowledge-based services like research and information technology. For the Grade 9 syllabus, these are included in the tertiary sector.

## Subtopic: The Primary Sector and Its Businesses

The primary sector consists of businesses that extract or harvest natural resources. Its main industries are agriculture, mining, fishing and forestry.

Agriculture includes crop farming, such as maize in the Free State and North West, wheat in the Western Cape, sugar cane in KwaZulu-Natal and citrus in Limpopo and the Eastern Cape; livestock farming, such as cattle, sheep, goats and poultry; and horticulture, such as fruit and flowers. Businesses range from large commercial farms to small-scale and subsistence farmers.

Mining extracts minerals from the earth. South Africa holds the world's largest reserves of platinum group metals, manganese and chrome, and has large deposits of gold, coal, iron ore and diamonds. Mining businesses range from large companies operating deep underground mines and open-cast pits to small-scale miners.

Fishing catches fish and other marine life, such as hake, pilchards and squid, mainly off the west and south coasts. Forestry grows and harvests trees for timber and pulp, mainly in Mpumalanga, KwaZulu-Natal and the Eastern Cape.

Primary sector products are usually raw materials, which are inputs for the secondary sector, although some, such as fresh fruit and vegetables, go directly to consumers through retailers.

The primary sector has special features. It depends heavily on natural conditions: droughts, floods, pests and diseases affect output. Many of its resources are non-renewable: once a mine's ore is extracted, it cannot be replaced. Its products are often traded on world markets at prices the producer cannot control, so earnings rise and fall with global commodity prices. And many primary activities, especially farming and mining, take place in rural areas, where they are often the main source of jobs.

Types of businesses in this sector include commercial farms, agricultural cooperatives, mining companies, fishing companies with fleets of trawlers, forestry plantations and small-scale producers such as emerging farmers and artisanal fishers.

## Subtopic: The Secondary Sector and Its Businesses

The secondary sector consists of businesses that transform raw materials into goods. Its main industries are manufacturing, construction, and electricity, gas and water supply.

Manufacturing turns raw materials and components into products. Examples include food processing, such as milling maize into maize meal and baking bread; beverages; textiles and clothing; chemicals and fuels, such as Sasol's production of fuel from coal and gas at Secunda; steel production; and the motor industry, which assembles vehicles in plants in the Eastern Cape, Gauteng and KwaZulu-Natal for local sale and export.

Construction builds houses, offices, factories, roads, bridges, dams and other infrastructure. Construction businesses range from large engineering companies building highways to small builders and bricklayers.

Electricity, gas and water supply are usually included in the secondary sector because they involve producing and processing a physical product. Eskom generates most of South Africa's electricity in coal-fired power stations, together with the Koeberg nuclear power station near Cape Town and a growing number of renewable energy plants run by independent producers. Water boards and municipalities purify and supply water.

The secondary sector adds value. Value added is the difference between the value of a product and the cost of the materials used to make it. A tonne of iron ore is worth far less than the steel made from it, and the steel is worth far less than the cars made from it. Each step adds value, and with it, jobs and income. That is why governments, including South Africa's, try to encourage beneficiation: processing raw materials locally rather than exporting them raw.

Types of businesses in the secondary sector include factories and manufacturing plants, mills, bakeries that produce bread for shops, breweries, steel works, refineries, construction companies, power stations and water purification works. Some are huge corporations; others are small manufacturers, such as a furniture workshop or a clothing manufacturer with twenty machinists.

## Subtopic: The Tertiary Sector and Its Businesses

The tertiary sector consists of businesses that provide services. It does not produce physical goods itself, but it is essential for getting goods to consumers and for meeting the many needs that are not goods at all.

Its main industries are: wholesale and retail trade, including supermarkets, clothing stores, spaza shops and wholesalers; transport, storage and communication, including road freight, rail, ports, airlines, minibus taxis, cellphone networks and internet providers; finance, insurance, real estate and business services, including banks, insurers, estate agents, accountants and lawyers; personal services, such as hairdressers, mechanics and dry cleaners; tourism and hospitality, including hotels, guest houses, restaurants and game lodges; and community and government services, including education, health care, policing and public administration.

The tertiary sector is the largest sector in the South African economy, both in its contribution to GDP and in employment. Finance and business services form one of its largest industries, and Johannesburg is the financial centre of Africa. Government services, including public schools and hospitals, are also a major part. Retail and wholesale trade employ large numbers of people, including many in the informal sector.

Types of businesses in the tertiary sector include retailers and wholesalers, banks and insurance companies, transport companies, telecommunication companies, hotels and restaurants, private schools and hospitals, professional practices such as law and accounting firms, and many small service businesses such as hair salons, car washes and repair shops.

Some businesses operate in more than one sector. A large agricultural company may farm sugar cane, a primary activity, mill it into sugar, a secondary activity, and sell it through its own distribution network, a tertiary activity. In that case, each activity is classified separately, or the business is classified by its main activity.

Businesses in every sector can also be described by their size and their formality. In each sector there are large businesses, such as a mining company employing tens of thousands of people, a national supermarket chain or a car manufacturer; medium businesses, such as a regional construction firm or a chain of guest houses; and small, micro and informal businesses, such as an emerging farmer, a backyard welding workshop or a street vendor. Small, medium and micro enterprises, often called SMMEs, are found in all three sectors and together employ a large share of South Africa's workers. The informal sector is concentrated in the tertiary sector, especially in retail trade and personal services, because these activities need little equipment to start. A person with a small amount of capital can buy stock from a wholesaler and sell it from a stall far more easily than open a factory or a mine. That is also why the tertiary sector offers many entrepreneurship opportunities for young people, a theme you will develop in Term 3 when you study business functions and the business plan.

Each sector also offers different careers. The primary sector needs farmers, agricultural scientists, geologists, mining engineers, mine workers and marine biologists. The secondary sector needs engineers, artisans such as electricians, fitters and welders, machine operators, quality controllers and builders. The tertiary sector needs teachers, nurses and doctors, accountants, bankers, salespeople, chefs, drivers, programmers and many more. In the third lesson of this topic you will look more closely at the skills each sector needs.

The error museum holds four mistakes. First, placing a bakery that bakes bread in the tertiary sector because it sells to customers; baking is manufacturing, so it is secondary, while the shop that resells bread is tertiary. Second, placing electricity generation in the tertiary sector; it is secondary. Third, believing that the primary sector is the largest in South Africa because of mining; the tertiary sector is the largest. Fourth, classifying a business by where it is, rather than what it does.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same ideas again, by following a loaf of bread from the ground to your table, a sorting game, and a walk down your own street, in plain words with the same facts.

## Subtopic: From the Ground, Through the Factory, to You

Follow a loaf of bread backwards.

You bought it at the corner shop. The shop did not make it; it sold it to you. Selling is a service. That is the tertiary sector, the service sector.

Before that, a delivery truck brought it from a big bakery. Driving the truck is also a service, so transport is tertiary too.

The bakery mixed flour, water, yeast and salt and baked them into bread. It turned ingredients into a new product. That is making things. That is the secondary sector, the making sector.

The flour came from a mill that ground wheat into flour. Grinding is making something new, so the mill is secondary too.

And the wheat? A farmer in the Western Cape or Free State grew it in a field. Growing it takes it straight from nature. That is the primary sector, the taking-from-nature sector.

So every loaf passes through all three sectors. Primary: taking from nature. Secondary: making. Tertiary: serving and selling.

The same is true of almost everything you own. Your school shoes started as cattle on a farm, primary; became leather and then shoes in a factory, secondary; and were sold to you in a shop, tertiary.

## Subtopic: Which Sector Is It?

Here is the test. Ask one question: what does this business mainly do?

Does it take things straight from nature: grow them, catch them, cut them down or dig them up? Primary sector. Farms, mines, fishing boats and forests.

Does it make things: turn materials into new products, or build things? Secondary sector. Factories, mills, bakeries that bake, builders, and the power stations that make electricity.

Does it provide a service or sell things it did not make? Tertiary sector. Shops, banks, taxis, hairdressers, hotels, schools, hospitals, cellphone companies.

Let's practise. A gold mine in Gauteng: digs from the earth, primary. A car factory in the Eastern Cape: makes cars, secondary. A car dealership selling those cars: tertiary. A hake trawler: primary. A fish canning factory: secondary. A fish and chips shop: it cooks, but mostly it serves meals to customers, so it is usually counted as tertiary, in the restaurant and food service group. A construction company building an RDP house: secondary. A minibus taxi: tertiary.

Some more for practice. A sugar cane farm in KwaZulu-Natal: primary. The sugar mill next door: secondary. A cellphone network: tertiary. A sawmill cutting logs into planks: secondary. A game lodge in the Kruger area: tertiary, because tourism is a service.

Two tricky ones. Eskom makes electricity, so it is secondary, not a service. A bakery that bakes bread is secondary, but a supermarket that only resells that bread is tertiary.

## Subtopic: Businesses on Your Street

Take a walk down a typical street in your town and sort what you see.

The spaza shop on the corner sells bread, airtime and cold drinks it bought from a wholesaler. Tertiary. The hair salon next to it: tertiary, a personal service. The taxi rank: tertiary, transport. The clinic: tertiary, a health service. Your school: tertiary, an education service.

The welder in the backyard workshop who makes burglar bars and gates: secondary, making things. The lady who sews school uniforms at home: secondary, manufacturing. The builder putting up a wall: secondary, construction.

The vegetable garden at the community centre: primary, growing food. Further out of town, maybe a farm, a quarry or a forestry plantation: all primary.

What do you notice? On most streets there are many more tertiary businesses than any other kind. That is true for South Africa as a whole: the service sector is by far the biggest. About two-thirds of the value of everything South Africa produces comes from services.

Try it on your own family too. Does someone work on a farm or a mine? Primary. In a factory, a building site or a power station? Secondary. In a shop, office, school, hospital, taxi or bank? Tertiary. Most South African families have someone working in the tertiary sector, which matches the numbers.

But the other two sectors matter enormously. Without primary, there would be nothing to make things from. Without secondary, there would be nothing to sell. The three sectors are like three links in a chain, and the next lesson is all about how they hold each other together.
