### subtopic_1 — The Value Chain: How Sectors Feed Each Other
**Assistant:** Why do we add only the value added at each stage, not the full selling prices, to measure production?

**Tutor:** Because adding the full selling prices would count the same thing several times. Look at the T-shirt. The farmer sells cotton for R10. The factory sells the shirt for R60, but that R60 already includes the R10 of cotton it bought. The shop sells the shirt for R120, which includes the R60 it paid the factory, which includes the R10 of cotton. If you added the three selling prices, 10 plus 60 plus 120, you would get R190, but only one T-shirt worth R120 was produced. The R10 of cotton would have been counted three times and the factory's work twice. That error is called double counting. To avoid it, economists count only the value added at each stage: the selling price minus the cost of the inputs bought from other businesses. The farmer adds R10, assuming for simplicity that the farmer's own inputs are not counted separately, the factory adds R50 and the shop adds R60. These add up to R120, exactly the value of the final product. There is another way to reach the same figure: count only the final goods and services sold to consumers, the R120 shirt, and ignore intermediate goods such as the cotton and the shirt sold to the shop. Both methods give the same answer, and both are used by Statistics South Africa when it calculates GDP. The value-added method has an extra advantage: it shows how much of the country's output each industry is responsible for, and therefore how much income each sector generates for workers and owners. You will study these methods of measuring GDP in Grade 11 Economics.

### subtopic_2 — How Each Sector Depends on the Others
**Assistant:** If the tertiary sector is the biggest, why can't South Africa simply focus on services?

**Tutor:** Because services depend heavily on the goods produced by the other two sectors, and because a country that does not produce goods must buy them from others. Think about what the largest service industries actually do. Retail and wholesale trade sell goods, food, clothing, appliances, cars, that were grown or made by the primary and secondary sectors. Transport moves those goods. Banks lend to farms, mines and factories, and much of their business depends on the health of those borrowers. Even a call centre needs buildings, computers and reliable electricity. If South Africa's farms, mines and factories shrank, the shops would still need goods, so they would have to import them. Imports must be paid for in foreign currency, which has to be earned by selling something abroad. Today much of South Africa's foreign currency is earned by exporting minerals, vehicles and agricultural products. Services such as tourism also earn foreign currency, but not enough to pay for all imports on their own. A second reason is jobs. South Africa has very high unemployment, and many people looking for work have limited formal education. Agriculture, mining, construction and manufacturing can employ large numbers of people with practical skills, while many of the fastest-growing services, such as finance and information technology, need highly skilled workers. A third reason is resilience: an economy with strength in all three sectors is better able to absorb shocks. So South African economic policy aims to grow all three sectors together, with particular attention to manufacturing, because of its strong linkages to the other two.

### subtopic_3 — The Role of Each Sector in the South African Economy
**Assistant:** Why is mining still so important to South Africa when it produces a fairly small share of GDP?

**Tutor:** Because its importance goes well beyond its share of GDP, for four reasons. First, exports and foreign exchange. Mining products, especially platinum group metals, gold, coal, iron ore, manganese and chrome, make up a large share of South Africa's exports of goods. The foreign currency they earn pays for imports of crude oil, machinery, medicines and electronics that the country does not produce in sufficient quantity. Without mining exports, the rand would be weaker and imports more expensive for everyone. Second, linkages. Mining buys large amounts of electricity, machinery, explosives, steel, transport and financial services, so it supports many businesses in the secondary and tertiary sectors. It also supplies raw materials to local industries, such as coal for power stations and iron ore for steel. Third, jobs and communities. Mining employs a large number of people, often in rural areas where few other jobs exist, and supports whole towns. Many mine workers send part of their wages to families in other provinces and neighbouring countries. Fourth, government revenue. Mining companies pay company tax and royalties to the state, which help fund public services. Mining also shaped South Africa's history: the discovery of diamonds at Kimberley in 1867 and gold on the Witwatersrand in 1886 led to the growth of Johannesburg, the railways and the financial sector. The challenge today is that mining is exposed to volatile world prices and that the ore bodies are finite, so the country also needs to build manufacturing and services that will last after the mines close.

### subtopic_4 — When One Sector Struggles: Ripple Effects
**Assistant:** Why did load-shedding affect all three sectors and not just electricity producers?

**Tutor:** Because electricity is an input into almost every activity in every sector, so when its supply is cut, production stops or becomes more expensive everywhere at once. In the primary sector, mines need electricity to run lifts, ventilation, pumps and processing plants, and for safety reasons they must cut production when power is unreliable. Farms need it for irrigation pumps, milking machines, cold storage and packhouses. In the secondary sector, factories need continuous power for machines, furnaces and production lines; an unexpected outage can ruin a batch of steel, chemicals or food and damage equipment. Many businesses had to buy generators and diesel, raising their costs and reducing their competitiveness against imports. In the tertiary sector, shops lost sales during outages, spoilt refrigerated stock and had to pay for backup power; banks, cellphone networks and internet providers needed batteries and generators to keep services running; restaurants and hotels lost customers. Even schools and hospitals were affected. Because all three sectors were hit together, load-shedding reduced the growth of the whole economy and discouraged investment. There was also a ripple through households: workers lost shifts and wages, and households spent more on candles, gas and backup power, leaving less to spend in shops. The experience showed clearly how interdependent the sectors are and why reliable infrastructure, provided largely by state-owned enterprises such as Eskom and Transnet, matters for every business. It also accelerated investment in rooftop solar and other renewable energy by businesses and households.
