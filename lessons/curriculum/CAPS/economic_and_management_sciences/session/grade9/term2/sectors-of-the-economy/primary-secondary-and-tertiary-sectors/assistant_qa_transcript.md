### subtopic_1 — Why Economists Divide the Economy into Sectors
**Assistant:** Why does the tertiary sector usually grow as a country becomes richer?

**Tutor:** Because of how people spend their money as their incomes rise, and because of how productivity changes in the other sectors. When a household is very poor, almost all its income goes on basic goods: food, clothing and shelter. Those come mainly from the primary and secondary sectors. As income rises, the household does not eat three times as much food or buy three times as many shirts. Instead, it spends the extra income on services: better health care, more education, a cellphone contract, banking, insurance, eating out, travel, entertainment. Economists observe this pattern in country after country: the share of spending on services rises with income. Businesses also demand more services as the economy grows more complex: advertising, legal advice, accounting, logistics and information technology. On the supply side, technology has made the primary and secondary sectors far more productive. A modern farm with tractors and harvesters can produce far more food with far fewer workers than a farm a century ago, and automated factories need fewer workers per car. So even if farm and factory output keeps rising, the share of workers they employ falls, and those workers move into services. Over time, the tertiary sector becomes the largest employer and the largest contributor to GDP, as it is in South Africa and in all developed economies. This shift is sometimes called structural change. It does not mean the primary and secondary sectors become unimportant. They still produce the goods everyone needs, and in South Africa mining and manufacturing remain vital for exports. But it explains why most new jobs in modern economies are service jobs, and why skills such as communication, problem-solving and digital literacy are increasingly important.

### subtopic_2 — The Primary Sector and Its Businesses
**Assistant:** Why are primary sector earnings so unpredictable compared with other sectors?

**Tutor:** For two main reasons: nature and world prices. First, nature. A farmer can do everything right, plant good seed, fertilise, control pests, and still lose much of the harvest to a drought, hailstorm, flood or disease outbreak. The 2015 to 2016 drought cut South Africa's maize harvest sharply, and outbreaks of avian influenza and foot-and-mouth disease have hit poultry and cattle farmers. Fishing depends on fish stocks and ocean conditions, which vary from year to year. Mining is less affected by weather, though floods can close mines, but it faces other natural limits: ore bodies run out, and deeper mining becomes more costly and dangerous. Second, world prices. Most primary products are commodities, goods that are basically the same whoever produces them: a tonne of platinum or maize from South Africa is much like a tonne from anywhere else. Their prices are set on world markets by global demand and supply, and individual producers cannot influence them. When the Chinese economy grows rapidly, demand for iron ore, coal and platinum surges and prices rise; when growth slows, prices fall. The rand exchange rate adds another layer: a weaker rand raises the rand earnings of exporters, a stronger rand reduces them. So a mine can see its revenue rise or fall sharply within a year for reasons entirely outside its control. Because South Africa depends heavily on mineral exports, these swings affect the whole economy, including tax revenue. That is one reason the government encourages beneficiation and the growth of manufacturing and services: a more diversified economy is less exposed to the swings of a few commodity prices.

### subtopic_3 — The Secondary Sector and Its Businesses
**Assistant:** What does it mean that the secondary sector adds value, and why does South Africa want more of it?

**Tutor:** Value added is the increase in value that happens when a business transforms inputs into a product worth more than those inputs. Suppose a mill buys wheat worth R5 000 and grinds it into flour that it sells for R7 000. The mill has added R2 000 of value. That R2 000 pays the mill's workers, its rent and interest, and its owner's profit: it is income created in the economy. A bakery that buys the flour for R7 000 and sells bread for R12 000 adds a further R5 000 of value. Each step in processing adds value, and therefore creates incomes and jobs. Now think about a country that exports its raw materials unprocessed. South Africa has for many decades exported large amounts of iron ore, chrome, manganese and platinum in raw or semi-processed form. Other countries buy those raw materials, process them into steel, alloys, catalytic converters and electronics, and capture much of the value added, with the jobs and incomes that go with it. If more of that processing were done in South Africa, the country would earn more per tonne of ore, employ more people, develop more skills and be less dependent on volatile raw-material prices. That is the idea behind beneficiation, which South African industrial policy has promoted for years. It is not easy: processing requires reliable and affordable electricity, skilled workers, investment, good transport and access to markets, and some of those, especially electricity, have been under strain. But the principle is sound, and it explains why manufacturing is considered so important for development even though the tertiary sector is larger. You will meet value added again in Grade 10 and 11 when you study how GDP is measured.

### subtopic_6 — Which Sector Is It?
**Assistant:** How do I classify a business that does more than one thing, like a farm that also runs a farm stall?

**Tutor:** Classify by the activity you are asked about, or, if you must classify the business as a whole, by its main activity. Many businesses carry out activities in more than one sector. A wine farm in the Western Cape is a good example. It grows grapes in its vineyards, which is a primary activity. It turns the grapes into wine in its cellar, which is manufacturing, a secondary activity. And it sells wine to visitors in its tasting room and runs a restaurant and guest house, which are services, tertiary activities. If an examination question asks which sector the grape growing belongs to, the answer is primary. If it asks about the wine-making, secondary. If it asks about the restaurant, tertiary. If it asks for the sector of the business as a whole, decide which activity earns most of its income or employs most of its people. For many wine farms that is the wine production, so the business would be classed as secondary, although Statistics South Africa sometimes treats such businesses differently for official statistics. The same reasoning works for a sugar company that grows cane, mills sugar and distributes it, or a mining company that also operates a smelter. The farm stall in your question is a small tertiary activity added to a mainly primary business, so the farm is primary overall, but the stall itself is tertiary. In your answer, it is good practice to explain your reasoning: state what each activity is, which sector it belongs to and why. That shows you understand the classification rather than guessing. It also prepares you for the next lesson, which shows how one business can link all three sectors together.
