### subtopic_1 — What Supply Means
**Assistant:** If a shop has 300 muffins on its shelves, why isn't its supply simply 300?

**Tutor:** Because supply in economics is a relationship between price and the quantity a producer is willing to offer, not a count of goods in the storeroom. The 300 muffins are the shop's stock. How many of them it is willing to sell depends on the price it can get. At a very low price, say R4, the shop might prefer to keep most of them for regular customers at a better price tomorrow, or even give some to a shelter rather than sell at a loss, so its supply at R4 might be only 30. At R12 it may be willing to sell 120, and at R20 all 300 and more if it can bake extra. Each price has its own quantity supplied, and the full list of those pairs is the supply schedule. Thinking of supply this way is essential for understanding markets. Suppose a sudden storm destroys part of the maize crop. The stock of maize falls, but what matters for the market is how the remaining farmers and traders respond to price: if the price rises, they may release maize from storage, import more or plant again. Supply also includes goods not yet produced. A farmer deciding whether to plant more wheat next season is making a supply decision based on the price expected at harvest. That is why supply is defined over a period: per day, per week, per season. In the short period, supply is limited by existing stock and capacity; over a longer period, producers can build new ovens, plant new fields and open new factories, so supply can respond more strongly to price.

### subtopic_2 — The Law of Supply and Why It Holds
**Assistant:** Why does it cost a producer more to make each extra unit when output increases?

**Tutor:** Because in the short run some of the producer's resources are fixed, so producing more means squeezing more out of the same equipment and people. Think about a small bakery with two ovens and three bakers working a normal shift. Up to a certain output, say 120 muffins a day, everything runs smoothly. To make more, the owner must ask the bakers to work overtime, often at a higher rate of pay. The ovens must run for longer, using more electricity, perhaps at the most expensive times of day. Flour may run out and need to be bought in small quantities from a nearby shop at a higher price than from the wholesaler. The bakery may have to use an old spare oven that heats unevenly and wastes more batter. Workers become tired, so mistakes and waste increase. Each of these makes the extra muffins more costly to produce than the earlier ones. Economists call this rising marginal cost: the cost of producing one more unit increases as output increases in the short run. A rational producer will only produce an extra unit if the price received covers the extra cost of making it. So as the price rises, it becomes worth producing units that were too costly before, and the quantity supplied increases. That is one of the main reasons for the upward slope of the supply curve. In the long run, the bakery could buy more ovens and hire more bakers, removing the bottleneck, which is why supply usually responds more strongly to price over longer periods. You will study costs in much more detail in Grade 11 and Grade 12 Economics.

### subtopic_3 — The Supply Schedule and Drawing the Supply Curve
**Assistant:** Why must demand and supply curves be drawn on exactly the same axes and scales?

**Tutor:** Because the whole purpose of price theory is to bring the two sides of the market together on one graph, and that is only possible if they use the same measuring sticks. The demand curve shows, for each price, how much buyers want; the supply curve shows, for each price, how much sellers offer. To find the price at which these quantities are equal, you need to compare them at the same price, which means reading both curves against the same vertical price axis and the same horizontal quantity axis. If the demand curve were drawn with price going up in steps of R4 and the supply curve in steps of R5, the curves would cross at a meaningless point, and any equilibrium you read off would be wrong. The quantity axis must also measure the same thing in the same units and over the same period: muffins per day for both, not muffins per day for demand and per week for supply. That is why, in this lesson, the supply schedule is drawn with exactly the same scales as the demand schedule from the previous lesson: price from 0 to R24 in steps of R4 and quantity from 0 to 240 in steps of 40. When you do this, you will notice that at R12 the quantity demanded and the quantity supplied are both 120 muffins. On the combined graph, the downward-sloping demand curve and the upward-sloping supply curve will cross exactly there. In an examination, when you are asked to draw both curves, draw them on one set of axes, label them D and S, and choose scales that fit every value in both schedules.

### subtopic_4 — Movements Along and Shifts of the Supply Curve
**Assistant:** How did the 2015 to 2016 drought affect the supply of maize and the price of maize meal?

**Tutor:** The drought is a clear example of a shift in supply. Maize farming depends heavily on rainfall, and the El Niño weather pattern of 2015 and 2016 brought one of the driest seasons on record to much of South Africa's maize-growing region in the Free State, North West and Mpumalanga. With less rain, farmers harvested far less maize from the same land. At every possible price, the quantity of maize that farmers could offer was lower than before. Notice that this was not caused by a change in the price of maize; it was caused by a natural condition, so it was not a movement along the supply curve. The whole supply curve shifted to the left: a decrease in supply. With demand for maize largely unchanged, because people still needed their staple food, the reduced supply meant that at the old price there was a shortage. As you will see in the next lesson, that pushes the price up. South Africa had to import large quantities of maize, and the price of maize meal in shops rose noticeably, which hit poorer households hardest because they spend a large share of their income on staple foods. When good rains returned in later seasons, the harvest recovered, the supply curve shifted back to the right, and prices eased. The same reasoning applies to many supply shocks: load-shedding, floods, livestock diseases such as foot-and-mouth disease, and increases in fuel or fertiliser prices all shift supply curves to the left and tend to push prices up.
