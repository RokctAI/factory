# Part 1 — Expert

This session introduces demand, the first half of price theory. The anchor is the law of demand: when the price of a good rises, the quantity demanded falls, and when the price falls, the quantity demanded rises, other things remaining the same. The governing tools are the demand schedule, a table of prices and quantities demanded, and the demand curve, the graph of that schedule, which slopes downward from left to right. We also separate two ideas that are constantly confused: a change in quantity demanded, which is a movement along the curve caused by a change in the good's own price, and a change in demand, which is a shift of the whole curve caused by something else.

## Subtopic: What Demand Means

In everyday speech, people say they demand something when they want it. In economics, demand means more than wanting. Demand is the quantity of a good or service that consumers are willing and able to buy at each possible price, during a given period of time. Three words in that definition matter.

Willing means the consumer wants the good enough to spend money on it. Able means the consumer has the money to pay for it. A learner may want a new smartphone very much, but if she cannot afford it, her want does not count as demand; economists call this effective demand, backed by the ability to pay. And during a given period means demand is measured over a time, per day, per week or per month, because buying ten loaves of bread a week is very different from ten loaves a year.

Demand is also always linked to price. It is not a single number but a whole relationship between price and quantity. At a high price, consumers buy little; at a low price, they buy more. That relationship is what the demand schedule and curve describe.

We distinguish individual demand, the demand of one consumer, from market demand, the total demand of all consumers in a market. Market demand at each price is found by adding the quantities that all individual consumers demand at that price. If at R12 one learner buys 2 muffins a day, another buys 1 and a third buys none, their combined demand at R12 is 2 + 1 + 0 = 3 muffins.

Demand is the buyers' side of the market. In the next lesson you will meet supply, the sellers' side, and in the third lesson you will put them together to find the market price. This is the price mechanism that coordinates a market economy, the system studied in Term 1.

## Subtopic: The Law of Demand and Why It Holds

The law of demand states that, other things remaining the same, the quantity demanded of a good falls as its price rises, and rises as its price falls. There is an inverse, or negative, relationship between price and quantity demanded.

The phrase other things remaining the same, often given in Latin as ceteris paribus, is essential. The law describes the effect of a change in the good's own price only, while consumers' incomes, the prices of other goods, tastes and everything else are held constant. If several things change at once, we cannot isolate the effect of price.

Why does the law hold? There are three main reasons.

The substitution effect: when the price of a good rises, it becomes more expensive relative to other goods that can be used instead, its substitutes, so consumers switch to them. If the price of beef rises, many households buy more chicken and less beef.

The income effect: when the price of a good rises, consumers' fixed incomes buy less than before; their real income, their purchasing power, falls. So they buy less of the good, and of many other goods too. A household spending R400 a month on bread finds that a price increase leaves less money for everything.

Diminishing marginal utility: each extra unit of a good gives a consumer less additional satisfaction than the one before. The first slice of pizza when you are hungry gives great satisfaction; the fifth gives very little. So consumers are willing to buy additional units only if the price is lower.

The three reasons can be seen together in a South African example. When the price of maize meal rises, households that can afford it switch partly to rice or bread, the substitution effect. Households on tight budgets find that the same grocery money buys less, and they cut back on maize meal and other items, the income effect. And because the last bag bought each month is the least essential, it is the first to be dropped. All three push in the same direction, which is why the law of demand is so reliable.

There are rare exceptions, such as luxury goods bought to show status, or goods people expect to become even more expensive, but for the Grade 9 syllabus and almost all ordinary goods the law of demand holds.

## Subtopic: The Demand Schedule and Drawing the Demand Curve

A demand schedule is a table that shows the quantity demanded at each price. Consider the market demand for muffins at a school tuck shop per day. At R20 per muffin, 50 muffins are demanded. At R16, 80 muffins. At R12, 120 muffins. At R8, 170 muffins. At R4, 230 muffins. As the price falls from R20 to R4, the quantity demanded rises from 50 to 230, exactly as the law of demand predicts.

A demand curve is a graph of the demand schedule. By convention, price is placed on the vertical axis, the y-axis, labelled price in rand, and quantity on the horizontal axis, the x-axis, labelled quantity demanded per day. Choose a scale for each axis that fits all the values: here, price from 0 to R24 in steps of R4, and quantity from 0 to 240 in steps of 40. Plot each pair as a point: R20 and 50, R16 and 80, R12 and 120, R8 and 170, R4 and 230. Join the points with a smooth line and label it D, for demand. Give the graph a heading, such as the demand for muffins at the tuck shop.

The curve slopes downward from left to right. That negative slope is the graphical form of the law of demand: higher prices, top left, go with smaller quantities; lower prices, bottom right, with larger quantities.

The schedule also shows how much consumers spend, the total revenue to the seller, at each price: price times quantity. At a price of R20 spending is 20 × 50 = 1 000, or R1 000. At R12 it is 12 × 120 = 1 440, or R1 440. At R4 it is 4 × 230 = 920, or R920. Notice that spending does not simply rise as price falls; it depends on how strongly quantity responds to price, an idea called price elasticity, which you will study in Grade 11.

Examination questions often give a schedule and ask you to draw the curve, or give a curve and ask you to read off a quantity. Always label both axes, include units, label the curve, and plot accurately.

## Subtopic: Movements Along and Shifts of the Demand Curve

A change in the good's own price causes a movement along the existing demand curve. This is called a change in quantity demanded. If the price of muffins falls from R16 to R12, the quantity demanded rises from 80 to 120: the point slides down the same curve. An increase in quantity demanded is an extension of demand; a decrease is a contraction.

A change in any other factor that affects buying shifts the whole curve. This is called a change in demand. An increase in demand shifts the curve to the right: at every price, more is demanded. A decrease in demand shifts the curve to the left: at every price, less is demanded.

The main factors that shift demand are the following. Consumers' income: for most goods, called normal goods, a rise in income increases demand. For some cheaper goods, called inferior goods, a rise in income decreases demand, as people switch to better alternatives. Prices of related goods: if the price of a substitute rises, demand for this good increases; if the price of a complement, a good used together with it, rises, demand for this good decreases. If the price of petrol rises sharply, demand for large cars falls, because petrol and cars are complements. Tastes and preferences: a successful advertising campaign or a health trend can increase demand. Population size and structure: a growing population, or more children in a town, increases demand for school uniforms. Expectations: if consumers expect prices to rise next month, demand increases now. Government policy: a tax on sugary drinks, such as South Africa's health promotion levy introduced in 2018, raises their price, while a ban or a subsidy affects demand directly.

Suppose a new school opens next door to the tuck shop. At every price, more muffins are demanded: the demand curve shifts to the right. At R12, for example, quantity demanded might now be 180 instead of 120.

To show a shift on a graph, draw the original curve and label it D1, then draw the new curve parallel to it, to the right for an increase or to the left for a decrease, and label it D2. Add an arrow between them showing the direction of the shift. Pick one price, such as R12, and draw a horizontal dotted line across to both curves: the quantities where it meets D1 and D2 show how much more, or less, is demanded at that same price. For a movement along the curve, by contrast, there is only one curve, and you mark two points on it with an arrow along the curve between them.

The error museum holds four mistakes. First, saying that a fall in price increases demand; it increases quantity demanded, a movement along the curve. Second, drawing the demand curve sloping upward; it slopes downward. Third, swapping the axes; price goes on the vertical axis. Fourth, confusing substitutes and complements; tea and coffee are substitutes, while cars and petrol are complements.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same ideas again, through muffins at a tuck shop, plotting points on a graph, and the difference between sliding and jumping, in plain words with the same facts.

## Subtopic: Muffins at the Tuck Shop

Imagine your school tuck shop sells muffins. If a muffin costs R20, only a few learners buy one: maybe 50 a day. If the price drops to R12, many more buy: 120 a day. If muffins cost only R4, almost everyone buys one, and some buy two: 230 a day.

That is the law of demand: when the price goes up, people buy less; when the price goes down, people buy more. As long as nothing else changes.

Why? Three reasons you already know from your own life. First, when muffins get expensive, you buy a vetkoek or an apple instead. You substitute. Second, your pocket money only stretches so far. If muffins cost more, your R50 a week buys fewer of them. Third, the first muffin of the day is great, the second is fine, the third is a bit much. So you will only buy that third muffin if it is really cheap.

Demand in economics means wanting something and being able to pay for it. You may dream of a new pair of sneakers that costs R3 000, but if you do not have the money, that is not demand. It is just a wish.

Add up all the learners in the school and you get the market demand: how many muffins the whole school wants at each price.

## Subtopic: Plotting the Points

Let's turn the muffin table into a picture.

Draw two lines that meet in the bottom left corner. The line going up is for price. Write price in rand next to it and mark 0, 4, 8, 12, 16, 20, 24. The line going across is for quantity. Write quantity demanded per day and mark 0, 40, 80, 120, 160, 200, 240.

Now plot the points. R20 and 50: go up to 20, across to 50, make a dot. R16 and 80: another dot. R12 and 120. R8 and 170. R4 and 230. Join the dots with a smooth line and write D at the end.

What does the line look like? It goes down from the top left to the bottom right, like a slide at the playground. That downward slope is the law of demand in picture form. High price, small quantity, up on the left. Low price, big quantity, down on the right.

Bonus sum: how much money does the tuck shop take in at each price? At R20 the shop takes 20 × 50 = 1 000, so R1 000. At R12 it takes 12 × 120 = 1 440, so R1 440. At R4 it takes 4 × 230 = 920, so R920. Cheapest is not always best for the shop!

Remember three things on every graph: label both lines, label the curve D, and give the graph a heading.

## Subtopic: Sliding or Jumping?

There are two very different ways the number of muffins sold can change.

Sliding. The tuck shop drops the price from R16 to R12. Learners buy 120 instead of 80. Nothing else changed; only the price. On the graph, the dot just slides down the same line. Economists call this a change in quantity demanded.

Jumping. A new school opens next door, and suddenly more people want muffins at every price. At R12, instead of 120, now 180 are sold. At R16, more than 80. The whole line jumps to the right. That is called a change in demand. If the opposite happens, maybe a doctor tells everyone muffins are unhealthy, the whole line jumps to the left.

What makes the line jump? Anything except the muffin's own price. People get more money. The price of something related changes: if vetkoek gets more expensive, more people switch to muffins. Tastes change because of an advert or a trend. More people move into the area. People expect prices to go up next week, so they buy now.

On a test, draw it like this. For a slide, one line with two dots and an arrow along the line. For a jump, two lines, the old one D1 and the new one D2, with an arrow from the old to the new.

So here is the test. Did the muffin's own price change? Slide along the line. Did something else change? The whole line jumps: right for more, left for less.
