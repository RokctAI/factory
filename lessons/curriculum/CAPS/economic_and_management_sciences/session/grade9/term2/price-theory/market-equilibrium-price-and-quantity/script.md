# Part 1 — Expert

This session brings demand and supply together to find the market price. The anchor is the equilibrium: the price at which the quantity demanded equals the quantity supplied, shown on a graph where the downward-sloping demand curve crosses the upward-sloping supply curve. The governing principle is that at any other price there is either a surplus, which pushes the price down, or a shortage, which pushes the price up, until the market settles at equilibrium. We use the muffin schedules from the previous two lessons, and then show how shifts in demand and supply move the equilibrium.

## Subtopic: Combining Demand and Supply in One Schedule

A market brings buyers and sellers together. Their plans are summarised in two schedules, which can be combined in one table with three columns: price, quantity demanded and quantity supplied. For muffins at the tuck shop, per day:

At R20, quantity demanded is 50 and quantity supplied is 200. At R16, demanded 80, supplied 160. At R12, demanded 120, supplied 120. At R8, demanded 170, supplied 80. At R4, demanded 230, supplied 30.

Reading down the table, the quantity demanded rises as the price falls, following the law of demand, while the quantity supplied falls as the price falls, following the law of supply. At high prices, sellers want to sell much more than buyers want to buy; at low prices, buyers want much more than sellers will offer. Somewhere in between, the two plans agree.

A fourth column can be added for the difference: quantity supplied minus quantity demanded. At R20 it is 200 - 50 = 150, a surplus of 150. At R16 it is 160 - 80 = 80, a surplus of 80. At R12 it is 120 - 120 = 0. At R8 it is 80 - 170 = -90, a shortage of 90. At R4 it is 30 - 230 = -200, a shortage of 200. A positive difference is a surplus, or excess supply; a negative difference is a shortage, or excess demand.

The same information can be drawn on one graph. Use one set of axes, price on the vertical axis and quantity on the horizontal axis, with scales that fit both schedules: price from 0 to R24, quantity from 0 to 240. Plot the demand points and join them as curve D; plot the supply points and join them as curve S. The two curves cross at one point.

Combining the schedules this way is exactly how real markets are analysed, from the Johannesburg Fresh Produce Market, where the prices of tomatoes and potatoes change daily with deliveries and buyers, to the market for petrol, cars and houses. In each case, the price reflects the interaction of buyers' plans and sellers' plans.

## Subtopic: Equilibrium Price and Equilibrium Quantity

Equilibrium means a state of balance, in which there is no pressure for change. Market equilibrium occurs at the price at which the quantity demanded equals the quantity supplied. That price is the equilibrium price, also called the market-clearing price, and the quantity bought and sold at that price is the equilibrium quantity.

In the muffin market, at R12 the quantity demanded is 120 and the quantity supplied is 120. The equilibrium price is R12 and the equilibrium quantity is 120 muffins per day. On the graph, the equilibrium is the point where the demand curve and the supply curve intersect. Label it E. Draw a dotted line from E across to the price axis, marking Pe at R12, and down to the quantity axis, marking Qe at 120.

At equilibrium, every buyer who is willing to pay R12 gets a muffin, and every seller willing to sell at R12 sells all their muffins. There are no unsold muffins and no disappointed customers at that price. Nobody has a reason to change the price. The total spent by buyers, and received by sellers, is the equilibrium price times the equilibrium quantity: 12 × 120 = 1 440, or R1 440 per day.

The equilibrium is not chosen by any single person. No government official sets it, and no single baker or learner decides it. It emerges from the separate decisions of all buyers and sellers. This is the price mechanism, the invisible hand of the market economy studied in Term 1. Prices act as signals: they tell producers what to make and how much, and they ration goods among consumers.

It is important to understand that equilibrium does not mean a fair price, or a price that everyone can afford. It simply means the price at which the plans of buyers and sellers match. A learner who is not willing or able to pay R12 does not get a muffin, even at equilibrium.

## Subtopic: Surpluses, Shortages and How the Market Clears

What happens if the price is not at equilibrium? The market itself pushes it back.

Suppose the tuck shop sets the price at R16. Bakers supply 160 muffins, but learners demand only 80. There is a surplus of 160 - 80 = 80 muffins unsold at the end of the day. Sellers are left with stock that will go stale. To get rid of it, they lower the price. As the price falls, two things happen: the quantity demanded rises, a movement down the demand curve, and the quantity supplied falls, a movement down the supply curve. The surplus shrinks, and the price keeps falling until it reaches R12, where the surplus disappears.

Now suppose the price is set at R8. Learners demand 170 muffins, but bakers supply only 80. There is a shortage of 170 - 80 = 90 muffins. Queues form, muffins sell out in the first five minutes of break, and disappointed buyers are willing to pay more. Sellers notice and raise the price. As the price rises, the quantity demanded falls and the quantity supplied rises, until at R12 the shortage disappears.

On the graph, a surplus is shown by drawing a horizontal line at a price above equilibrium: the gap between the demand curve on the left and the supply curve on the right is the surplus. A shortage is shown by a horizontal line at a price below equilibrium: the gap between the supply curve on the left and the demand curve on the right is the shortage.

This self-correcting process is why markets tend towards equilibrium. In real life it may take time, because prices on menus and price tags are not changed every minute, but the pressure is always there.

Sometimes governments deliberately keep a price away from equilibrium. A maximum price set below equilibrium, called a price ceiling, causes a persistent shortage, which is why planned economies with fixed low prices had queues, as studied in Term 1. A minimum price set above equilibrium, called a price floor, causes a persistent surplus; South Africa's national minimum wage is a price floor in the labour market, and economists debate how far it affects employment.

## Subtopic: Changes in Demand and Supply Move the Equilibrium

When demand or supply shifts, the equilibrium moves to a new point. There are four basic cases.

An increase in demand. Suppose a new school opens next door, and at every price 80 more muffins are demanded: at R20, 130; at R16, 160; at R12, 200; at R8, 250; at R4, 310. Supply is unchanged. At the old price of R12, quantity demanded is now 200 but quantity supplied is still 120: a shortage of 80. The price rises. The new equilibrium is at R16, where demand is 160 and supply is 160. An increase in demand raises both the equilibrium price, from R12 to R16, and the equilibrium quantity, from 120 to 160.

A decrease in demand: the curve shifts left, and both the equilibrium price and quantity fall.

A decrease in supply. Suppose the price of flour rises and at every price bakers supply 80 fewer muffins: at R12, 40; at R16, 80; at R20, 120. Demand is the original one. At the old price of R12, demand is 120 but supply is only 40: a shortage of 80. The price rises. The new equilibrium is at R16, where demand is 80 and supply is 80. A decrease in supply raises the equilibrium price, from R12 to R16, but lowers the equilibrium quantity, from 120 to 80.

An increase in supply: the curve shifts right, the equilibrium price falls and the equilibrium quantity rises.

Sometimes demand and supply shift at the same time. Suppose the new school opens and flour becomes more expensive in the same month. The increase in demand pushes the price up, and the decrease in supply also pushes the price up, so the price certainly rises. But the increase in demand pushes quantity up while the decrease in supply pushes quantity down, so what happens to quantity depends on which shift is larger. When two shifts happen together, one of the two results can be predicted with certainty and the other depends on the sizes of the shifts. Grade 10 and Grade 11 Economics return to these combined cases.

A useful summary: a change in demand moves price and quantity in the same direction as the change in demand. A change in supply moves quantity in the same direction as the change in supply, but price in the opposite direction.

To analyse any news story, follow four steps. Decide whether demand or supply is affected. Decide whether it increases or decreases. Draw the shift. Read off the new equilibrium price and quantity.

The error museum holds four mistakes. First, saying that a higher price reduces demand, which then lowers the price, in an endless loop; a price change causes movements along curves, not shifts. Second, thinking that equilibrium means a fair or affordable price. Third, showing a surplus at a price below equilibrium; surpluses occur above equilibrium. Fourth, saying that a decrease in supply lowers the price; it raises the price.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same ideas again, through the slide and the ladder meeting, a tuck shop with too many or too few muffins, and four moves to remember, in plain words with the same facts.

## Subtopic: Where the Slide Meets the Ladder

Put the learners' demand line and the bakers' supply line on the same graph. The demand line is a slide going down. The supply line is a ladder going up. Draw them both, and they cross at one point.

That crossing point is the equilibrium. It means balance. For the muffins, it is at R12 and 120 muffins. At R12, the learners want 120 muffins, and the bakers make 120 muffins. Every muffin baked is sold, and everyone willing to pay R12 gets one. Nobody wants to change anything.

On the graph, mark the crossing point E. From E, draw a dotted line across to the price line and write R12. Draw another dotted line down to the quantity line and write 120. That is the equilibrium price and the equilibrium quantity.

How much money changes hands each day? 12 × 120 = 1 440. So R1 440.

Nobody sat in an office and decided R12. It came from all the learners and all the bakers making their own choices. That is the market working on its own, the invisible hand you met in Term 1.

But be careful. Equilibrium does not mean everyone can afford a muffin. A learner without R12 still goes without. Equilibrium just means the buyers' plans and the sellers' plans match.

## Subtopic: Too Many Muffins or Too Few

What if the tuck shop gets the price wrong?

Too high. The shop charges R16. Bakers happily make 160 muffins, but only 80 learners buy one. At the end of the day, 160 - 80 = 80 muffins are left over. That is a surplus. Tomorrow the muffins are stale, so the shop drops the price. More learners buy, bakers make fewer, and the leftovers shrink until the price gets back to R12.

Too low. The shop charges R8. Now 170 learners want a muffin, but bakers make only 80. That is a shortage of 170 - 80 = 90. Muffins are gone in five minutes, a long queue forms, and some learners say: I will pay more! So the price goes up. Fewer learners want one, bakers make more, and the queue shrinks until the price is back at R12.

You can see this in real shops. At the end of the day, a bakery cuts the price of bread that will be stale tomorrow. That is a surplus being cleared. Before a big public holiday, when everyone wants to braai, the price of charcoal and meat can creep up. That is a shortage being cleared.

So the market fixes itself. Price too high: leftovers push it down. Price too low: queues push it up. It always heads back to where the slide meets the ladder.

When a government fixes a price too low on purpose, the queues never go away. That is why some countries with fixed low prices had long bread queues.

## Subtopic: Four Moves to Remember

When something in the news changes a market, there are only four moves.

Demand goes up. A new school opens, and more learners want muffins at every price. The slide jumps right. At the old price there is now a queue, so the price rises. The new meeting point is R16 and 160 muffins. Price up, quantity up.

Demand goes down. A health scare: fewer want muffins. The slide jumps left. Price down, quantity down.

Supply goes down. Flour gets expensive and bakers make fewer at every price. The ladder jumps left. Again a queue at the old price, so the price rises. The new meeting point is R16 and only 80 muffins. Price up, quantity down.

Supply goes up. A new, faster oven. The ladder jumps right. Price down, quantity up.

Here is the trick to remember. When demand moves, price and quantity move together, the same way as demand. When supply moves, quantity goes the same way as supply, but price goes the opposite way.

Try a real one. Load-shedding makes it more expensive for bakeries to bake bread. Which line moves? The sellers', supply. Which way? Making bread got harder, so supply goes down, the ladder jumps left. Result: the price of bread goes up and less bread is sold. Another one: in winter, more people want soup. Which line? Buyers, demand. Which way? Up, the slide jumps right. Result: soup prices rise and more soup is sold.

For any story, ask: which line moves, buyers or sellers? Up or down? Then draw it, and read off the new price and quantity. That is price theory in four moves.
