# Part 1 — Expert

This session introduces supply, the sellers' side of price theory. The anchor is the law of supply: when the price of a good rises, the quantity supplied rises, and when the price falls, the quantity supplied falls, other things remaining the same. The governing tools are the supply schedule and the supply curve, which slopes upward from left to right. As with demand, we separate a change in quantity supplied, a movement along the curve caused by the good's own price, from a change in supply, a shift of the whole curve caused by other factors such as costs, technology and weather.

## Subtopic: What Supply Means

Supply is the quantity of a good or service that producers are willing and able to offer for sale at each possible price, during a given period of time. As with demand, every word matters. Willing means the producer wants to sell at that price, because it is profitable. Able means the producer has the resources, the factors of production, to produce that quantity. And during a given period means supply is measured per day, per week or per season.

Supply is not the same as the stock a business has. A bakery may have 300 muffins on its shelves, but if at a price of R4 it is only willing to offer 30 for sale, its supply at R4 is 30. Supply is a relationship between price and quantity offered, not a single number.

We distinguish individual supply, the supply of one producer, from market supply, the total of all producers in the market. Market supply at each price is found by adding the quantities that all producers offer at that price. If three home bakers near a school each offer 40 muffins at R12, the market supply at R12 is 40 + 40 + 40 = 120.

Who are the suppliers? In the goods and services market of the circular flow, businesses are the suppliers and households the buyers. In the factor market, the roles reverse: households supply labour and other factors, and businesses demand them. So the same tools, supply and demand curves, can be used for the market for bread, the market for labour and even the market for rand, as you will see in later grades.

South African examples of supply decisions are everywhere. A citrus farmer in Limpopo decides how many oranges to send to the local market and how many to export. A taxi owner decides how many trips to run. A mine decides how much platinum to extract. In each case, the price the producer expects to receive is a key factor in the decision.

## Subtopic: The Law of Supply and Why It Holds

The law of supply states that, other things remaining the same, the quantity supplied of a good rises as its price rises, and falls as its price falls. There is a direct, or positive, relationship between price and quantity supplied.

Why does the law hold? There are three main reasons.

The profit motive: a higher price, with costs unchanged, means a higher profit on each unit sold. Producers therefore want to sell more. A farmer who receives more for tomatoes will plant more tomatoes next season and perhaps fewer cabbages.

Rising costs of producing more: in the short run, a producer's resources are limited. To produce more, a bakery may have to pay overtime, run its ovens for longer, buy flour in a hurry at a higher price or use older, less efficient equipment. Each extra unit costs more to make than the one before, so the producer will only make extra units if the price is high enough to cover the higher cost.

Entry of new producers: when the price of a good rises, producers who could not cover their costs at the lower price now find it worth producing. New firms enter the market, and existing firms switch resources from other products. When the price of platinum rises sharply, mines that had closed because they were too expensive to run may reopen, adding to market supply.

The same phrase applies as for demand: other things remaining the same, or ceteris paribus. The law describes the effect of the good's own price, while costs, technology, the number of producers, the weather and government policy are held constant.

Note the contrast with demand. Demand and price move in opposite directions; supply and price move in the same direction. Buyers want low prices; sellers want high prices. The market price will be found where the two sides meet, which is the subject of the next lesson.

## Subtopic: The Supply Schedule and Drawing the Supply Curve

A supply schedule is a table showing the quantity supplied at each price. Consider the market supply of muffins from the bakers who supply a school tuck shop, per day. At R4, 30 muffins are supplied. At R8, 80. At R12, 120. At R16, 160. At R20, 200. As the price rises from R4 to R20, the quantity supplied rises from 30 to 200, as the law of supply predicts.

A supply curve is the graph of the schedule. As with demand, price goes on the vertical axis, labelled price in rand, and quantity on the horizontal axis, labelled quantity supplied per day. Use the same scales as the demand curve: price from 0 to R24 in steps of R4, quantity from 0 to 240 in steps of 40. Plot each pair: R4 and 30, R8 and 80, R12 and 120, R16 and 160, R20 and 200. Join the points with a smooth line, label it S, and give the graph a heading such as the supply of muffins.

The supply curve slopes upward from left to right: low prices with small quantities at the bottom left, high prices with large quantities at the top right. This positive slope is the graphical form of the law of supply.

The schedule can also show the revenue producers would receive if they sold the whole quantity supplied: at a price of R4 it would be 4 × 30 = 120, or R120; at R12 it would be 12 × 120 = 1 440, or R1 440; and at R20 it would be 20 × 200 = 4 000, or R4 000. Of course, whether producers can sell that quantity depends on demand.

Notice that at a price of R12, the quantity supplied, 120, equals the quantity demanded in the muffin demand schedule from the previous lesson, which was also 120 at R12. That is not an accident; it is the point where the market settles, the equilibrium, which you will analyse next.

When drawing the curve in an examination, label both axes with units, label the curve, plot accurately and use a ruler for the axes. If a schedule produces a straight line, as this one nearly does, join the points carefully rather than forcing a curve.

## Subtopic: Movements Along and Shifts of the Supply Curve

A change in the good's own price causes a movement along the supply curve, called a change in quantity supplied. If the price of muffins rises from R12 to R16, the quantity supplied rises from 120 to 160: the point moves up along the same curve. An increase is an extension of supply; a decrease is a contraction.

A change in any other factor shifts the whole curve, called a change in supply. An increase in supply shifts the curve to the right: at every price, more is offered. A decrease in supply shifts it to the left.

The main factors that shift supply are the following. Costs of production: if the cost of inputs such as flour, electricity, fuel or wages rises, production becomes less profitable at every price and supply decreases. Load-shedding, which forced South African businesses to buy diesel for generators, raised costs and reduced supply in many industries. Technology: better machines or methods reduce costs and increase supply. Number of producers: more producers in the market increase market supply. Prices of other goods the producer could make: if the price of wheat rises, a farmer may plant wheat instead of maize, reducing the supply of maize. Natural conditions: good rains increase the supply of crops, while droughts, floods and diseases reduce it; the 2015 to 2016 drought cut South Africa's maize harvest sharply. Government policy: taxes on a product increase costs and reduce supply, while subsidies reduce costs and increase supply. Expectations: if producers expect higher prices later, they may hold back supply now.

Suppose the price of flour rises sharply. At every price of muffins, bakers offer fewer: the supply curve shifts to the left. At R12, perhaps only 80 muffins are now supplied instead of 120.

To show a shift of supply on a graph, draw the original curve, S1, and the new curve, S2, to its right for an increase or to its left for a decrease, with an arrow showing the direction. Choose a price, such as R12, and draw a horizontal dotted line across to both curves to show the different quantities supplied at that same price. A movement along the curve is shown on a single curve, with two points and an arrow along the curve between them.

A useful way to remember the direction of shifts is to ask how the change affects the cost or ease of producing. Anything that makes production cheaper or easier, such as lower input prices, better technology, more producers, good weather or a subsidy, increases supply and shifts the curve to the right. Anything that makes production more expensive or harder, such as higher input prices, load-shedding, a drought, a new tax or fewer producers, decreases supply and shifts the curve to the left. Learners sometimes think a leftward shift means up because the curve appears higher on the page; it is better to read the shift horizontally, as more or less quantity at the same price.

The error museum holds four mistakes. First, saying that a rise in price increases supply; it increases quantity supplied, a movement along the curve. Second, drawing the supply curve sloping downward; it slopes upward. Third, shifting the curve to the right when costs rise; higher costs decrease supply, a shift to the left. Fourth, confusing supply with stock on hand; supply is what producers are willing to sell at each price.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same ideas again, through the bakers behind the muffins, a ladder on a graph, and what happens when costs change, in plain words with the same facts.

## Subtopic: The Bakers Behind the Muffins

Last lesson we looked at the learners buying muffins. Now look at the bakers who make them.

If the tuck shop pays only R4 a muffin, hardly any baker thinks it is worth the trouble. Maybe they make 30 between them. If it pays R12, baking becomes worth it, and they make 120. If it pays R20, the bakers are thrilled, they bake through the night, a neighbour starts baking too, and 200 muffins arrive.

That is the law of supply: when the price goes up, sellers offer more; when the price goes down, they offer less. As long as nothing else changes.

Why? Three reasons. First, a better price means more profit on each muffin, so it is worth making more. Second, making lots of muffins gets harder and more expensive: extra hours, extra electricity, more flour bought in a hurry. Bakers only do that if the price pays for it. Third, a high price attracts new bakers who would not have bothered at a low price.

See the difference from last lesson? Buyers buy more when the price is low. Sellers sell more when the price is high. They want opposite things. That is exactly why there has to be a meeting point.

Supply means being willing and able to sell. A baker with a full shelf who will not sell at R4 is not supplying muffins at R4.

## Subtopic: Drawing the Ladder

Let's draw the bakers' table on a graph.

Use the same two lines as before. Price in rand going up: 0, 4, 8, 12, 16, 20, 24. Quantity supplied per day going across: 0, 40, 80, 120, 160, 200, 240.

Plot the points. R4 and 30. R8 and 80. R12 and 120. R16 and 160. R20 and 200. Join them and write S at the end.

What does it look like? This time it climbs from the bottom left to the top right, like a ladder leaning against a wall. Low price, few muffins, bottom left. High price, many muffins, top right. That upward slope is the law of supply in picture form.

So now you have two shapes to remember. The demand line is a slide going down. The supply line is a ladder going up.

Here is something interesting. At R12, the bakers offer 120 muffins. And from last lesson, at R12 the learners want 120 muffins. The same number! That is where the slide and the ladder cross, and it is the price the market settles on. Hold that thought for the next lesson.

Bonus sum: if the bakers sell all 120 muffins at R12, they earn 12 × 120 = 1 440 rand.

## Subtopic: When Costs Change

Just like demand, supply can change in two ways.

Sliding. The tuck shop raises the price it pays from R12 to R16. The bakers offer 160 instead of 120. Only the price changed, so the dot climbs up the same ladder. That is a change in quantity supplied.

Jumping. The price of flour shoots up. Now, at every price, muffins cost more to make, so bakers make fewer. At R12, maybe only 80 instead of 120. The whole ladder jumps to the left. That is a change in supply, a decrease.

What else makes the ladder jump? A new, faster oven, which makes muffins cheaper to bake: the ladder jumps right. More bakers starting up: right. Load-shedding forcing bakers to run generators on expensive diesel: left. A drought making wheat and flour scarce: left. A government tax on muffins: left. A government subsidy for bakers: right.

Here is the easy rule. If making the product gets cheaper or easier, supply jumps right: more at every price. If it gets more expensive or harder, supply jumps left: less at every price.

Think of a farmer too. If good rains fall, the same fields give a bigger harvest, so there are more tomatoes at every price: supply jumps right. If a hailstorm flattens the crop, there are fewer at every price: supply jumps left.

And the test is the same as for demand. Did the product's own price change? Slide along the line. Did something else change? The whole line jumps.
