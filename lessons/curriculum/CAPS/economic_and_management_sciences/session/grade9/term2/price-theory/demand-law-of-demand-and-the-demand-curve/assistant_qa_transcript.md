### subtopic_1 — What Demand Means
**Assistant:** Why does economics count only demand backed by money, and not everything people want?

**Tutor:** Because economics is trying to explain what actually happens in markets: what is bought, what is produced and at what price. Wants on their own do not move a market. A business deciding how many school shoes to produce does not care how many children would like new shoes; it cares how many parents will actually buy them at the price it charges. If wants without money counted as demand, the demand for almost everything would be enormous, and it would tell producers nothing useful. So economists define demand as effective demand: the quantity consumers are both willing and able to buy at a given price. Willing captures the want; able captures the purchasing power. This definition has a serious consequence that you should think about. In a market economy, goods are produced for those who can pay. A poor household may need medicine or nutritious food far more than a wealthy household needs a second television, but if the poor household cannot pay, its need does not register as demand, and the market will not respond to it. That is one of the disadvantages of the market economy you studied in Term 1, and it is why governments in mixed economies such as South Africa provide grants, free clinics and school nutrition programmes. Those policies turn needs into effective demand, or meet them directly outside the market. So the definition is not saying that wants without money do not matter; it is saying that markets alone do not see them. Keeping that distinction clear is part of thinking like an economist.

### subtopic_2 — The Law of Demand and Why It Holds
**Assistant:** Why do economists insist on adding other things remaining the same to the law of demand?

**Tutor:** Because in the real world many things change at the same time, and without that condition the law could appear to be broken when it is not. Imagine that over a year the price of chicken rises by 10 percent, but chicken sales also rise. Has the law of demand failed? Not necessarily. Perhaps over the same year incomes rose, so households could afford more chicken even at the higher price. Perhaps the price of beef rose even faster, so people switched from beef to chicken. Perhaps the population of the area grew. Each of those changes increases demand, shifting the whole demand curve to the right, and that shift can outweigh the fall in quantity demanded caused by the higher price. If you simply looked at the price and the quantity, you might wrongly conclude that higher prices make people buy more. The phrase other things remaining the same, ceteris paribus, tells us to imagine an experiment in which only the price of the good changes while income, the prices of related goods, tastes, population and expectations stay exactly as they were. In that experiment, the law of demand holds: a higher price means a lower quantity demanded. Economists separate the two effects by distinguishing a movement along the demand curve, caused only by the own price, from a shift of the curve, caused by everything else. This is not a trick to protect the theory; it is a way of thinking clearly about cause and effect. Scientists do the same when they change one variable in an experiment and keep the others constant.

### subtopic_3 — The Demand Schedule and Drawing the Demand Curve
**Assistant:** Why is price put on the vertical axis when quantity depends on price?

**Tutor:** It is a convention that goes back more than a century, and it is followed in every economics textbook, so you must follow it too. In mathematics, the variable that depends on the other is usually placed on the vertical axis. In the demand relationship, quantity demanded depends on price, so a mathematician might expect quantity on the y-axis. Economists, however, settled on the opposite layout in the late 1800s, largely through the influence of the British economist Alfred Marshall, whose diagrams of demand and supply became the standard. One way to make sense of it is to read the demand curve the other way round: for any quantity on the horizontal axis, the curve tells you the highest price consumers are willing to pay for that quantity. Read that way, price does depend on quantity, which fits the mathematical convention. Whatever the history, the important point for you is consistency. When you draw a demand curve, price goes on the vertical axis, labelled price in rand, and quantity goes on the horizontal axis, labelled with the quantity and the period, such as quantity demanded per day. When you combine demand and supply curves in the next two lessons, both will use these same axes, which is what allows them to be drawn on one graph and to intersect at the equilibrium. If you swap the axes, your demand curve will slope the wrong way and the supply curve will too, the equilibrium will be impossible to read, and an examiner will not award the marks even if your points are accurate.

### subtopic_4 — Movements Along and Shifts of the Demand Curve
**Assistant:** If the price of petrol rises, what happens to the demand for minibus taxis and for large private cars?

**Tutor:** The answer depends on whether each good is a substitute or a complement for petrol, and that is a good way to practise the distinction. Start with large private cars. Cars and petrol are used together, so they are complements. When the price of petrol rises, the cost of running a car rises, so at every price of cars, fewer people want to buy large, fuel-hungry cars. The demand curve for large cars shifts to the left. Notice that the price of cars did not change; what changed was the price of a related good, so the whole curve moves. Buyers who still want a car may switch to smaller, more fuel-efficient models, so the demand for small cars may even increase. Now minibus taxis. For many commuters, using a taxi is a substitute for driving a private car. When petrol becomes more expensive, driving becomes more costly, and some car owners switch to taxis or to buses, so the demand for taxi trips increases and its curve shifts to the right. There is a complication: taxis also use fuel, so taxi operators face higher costs and may raise fares. A higher fare would cause a movement along the new, shifted demand curve, a contraction of quantity demanded. This shows why economists analyse one change at a time: first the shift in demand caused by the related price, then any movement caused by a change in the good's own price. In the next lessons on supply and equilibrium, you will see how both effects together determine the final price and quantity.
