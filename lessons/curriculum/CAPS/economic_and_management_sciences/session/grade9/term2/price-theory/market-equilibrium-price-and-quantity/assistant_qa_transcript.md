### subtopic_2 — Equilibrium Price and Equilibrium Quantity
**Assistant:** If nobody sets the equilibrium price, how do buyers and sellers actually find it?

**Tutor:** By trial and error, guided by what happens when the price is wrong. No buyer or seller needs to know the whole demand and supply schedule. Each one only needs to react to what they see. A seller who charges too much notices that goods are left unsold at the end of the day; the sensible response is to lower the price, or produce less, or both. A seller who charges too little notices that goods sell out quickly, queues form and customers ask when more will arrive; the sensible response is to raise the price, or produce more. Buyers react too: when goods are scarce, some offer to pay more; when goods are plentiful, they shop around for the lowest price. These many small adjustments push the price towards the level at which the quantity sellers want to sell matches the quantity buyers want to buy. You can see this happening daily at the Johannesburg Fresh Produce Market, one of the largest fresh produce markets in the world. Farmers deliver tomatoes, potatoes and onions, and market agents sell them to buyers from shops, restaurants and street traders. If a large delivery of tomatoes arrives and buyers are few, agents lower the price to clear the stock before it spoils; if deliveries are small, the price rises. The published daily prices move up and down with these conditions. The equilibrium is therefore not a fixed point that someone calculates; it is a moving target that the market keeps chasing, which is exactly why economists describe prices as signals that coordinate the decisions of millions of people without a central planner.

### subtopic_3 — Surpluses, Shortages and How the Market Clears
**Assistant:** Why does a government price ceiling below equilibrium cause a shortage that does not go away?

**Tutor:** Because the ceiling blocks the very adjustment that would normally remove the shortage. In a free market, a shortage pushes the price up. As the price rises, the quantity demanded falls and the quantity supplied rises, until they meet at equilibrium. A price ceiling is a maximum price set by law. If it is set below the equilibrium price, sellers are not allowed to raise the price to the equilibrium level. Take the muffin market. Suppose a rule said muffins may not be sold for more than R8. At R8, learners demand 170 muffins but bakers supply only 80, a shortage of 90. Normally the price would rise towards R12, but the law forbids it. So the shortage stays. The 80 muffins must be shared out some other way: whoever gets to the front of the queue first, whoever knows the seller, or one per learner by ration. Some sellers may sell secretly above the legal price, which is called a black market. And because R8 barely covers costs, some bakers may stop baking, making the shortage worse over time. This is exactly what happened in planned economies with fixed low prices, and in many countries that have tried to control the price of rent, bread or fuel. That does not mean price ceilings are never used. Governments sometimes accept these costs because they want essential goods to be affordable for poor households. But economists usually argue that it is better to help poor households with income, such as grants, and let prices do their signalling job, than to fix prices and create shortages.

### subtopic_4 — Changes in Demand and Supply Move the Equilibrium
**Assistant:** Why does a decrease in supply raise the price but lower the quantity, while an increase in demand raises both?

**Tutor:** Because the two shifts start the adjustment from different sides of the market. Take the increase in demand first. A new school opens and, at every price, buyers want more muffins. At the old price of R12, buyers now want 200 but sellers still offer 120, a shortage. The shortage pushes the price up. As it rises, sellers move up their unchanged supply curve and offer more, while buyers move up the new demand curve and buy a little less than 200. The new equilibrium is at R16 and 160 muffins. Both price and quantity rose, because buyers wanting more pulled the market up along the supply curve, and moving up a supply curve means higher price and higher quantity together. Now the decrease in supply. Flour becomes more expensive and, at every price, bakers offer 80 fewer muffins. At the old price of R12, buyers still want 120 but sellers offer only 40, again a shortage, so the price rises. As it rises, buyers move up their unchanged demand curve and buy less, while sellers move up the new supply curve and offer more than 40. The new equilibrium is at R16 and 80 muffins. Price rose, but quantity fell, because the market moved up along the demand curve, and moving up a demand curve means higher price but lower quantity. So the rule is: when demand shifts, the market slides along the supply curve, so price and quantity move together; when supply shifts, the market slides along the demand curve, so price and quantity move in opposite directions.

### subtopic_6 — Too Many Muffins or Too Few
**Assistant:** At a price of R16 there are leftover muffins. Why don't sellers just make fewer instead of lowering the price?

**Tutor:** They do both, and that is exactly how the market reaches equilibrium. At R16, bakers supply 160 muffins and learners buy only 80, so 80 are left over. A baker facing leftovers has two possible responses. One is to bake fewer at the same price. But if every baker does that and the price stays at R16, learners will still only buy 80, because at R16 that is all they want. The bakers would end up producing just 80 muffins at R16, while plenty of learners who would happily buy a muffin at a lower price go without, and bakers who could profitably sell at a lower price miss out on sales. That is not a stable situation, because some baker will soon realise that by charging a little less, say R14, she can attract learners from the others and sell more. Competition between sellers therefore pushes the price down. The second response, lowering the price, does two things at once. It increases the quantity demanded, as more learners decide a muffin is worth it, and it reduces the quantity supplied, because at a lower price bakers find it worth making fewer. Both effects shrink the surplus, and they continue until the price reaches R12, where 120 muffins are demanded and 120 supplied. So making fewer muffins and lowering the price are not alternatives; they are two halves of the same adjustment. In the language of the graph, the price falls, and the market moves down the supply curve and down the demand curve until they meet.
