### subtopic_1 — The Accounting Cycle with Credit Transactions
**Assistant:** Why does a business need a separate debtors ledger as well as the debtors control account?

**Tutor:** Because the two answer different questions, and the business needs both answers. The debtors control account in the general ledger answers the question: how much do all our debtors owe us in total? That total is what appears in the trial balance and later in the statement of financial position as a current asset. But it cannot tell the business who owes what. Ridgeview Hardware cannot send Hilltop Builders a statement, decide whether Ace Plumbing has reached its credit limit, or chase an overdue account, if all it knows is the total. The debtors ledger answers the question: how much does each debtor owe, and what is the history of that account? It has a separate account for every customer, recording each invoice and each payment with dates and document numbers, and a running balance. That is what the business uses to manage credit day to day. Why not simply keep individual debtor accounts in the general ledger and leave out the control account? Because a business may have hundreds or thousands of debtors. A large retailer with store-card customers has many thousands. Putting each of them into the general ledger would make it enormous and the trial balance impossibly long. Using a control account keeps the general ledger and trial balance manageable, while the detail sits in the subsidiary ledger. The two must always agree: the total of all the individual balances in the debtors ledger, listed in a debtors list, must equal the balance of the debtors control account. If they differ, there is an error to find. This check is a valuable internal control, because different people can keep the two records.

### subtopic_2 — A Credit Sale in the Accounting Equation
**Assistant:** Why is the gross profit the same whether the customer pays in cash or on credit?

**Tutor:** Because gross profit depends only on the selling price and the cost price, and neither changes with the method of payment. Gross profit is sales minus cost of sales. When Ridgeview sells goods that cost R4 000 for R6 000, the sales income is R6 000 and the cost of sales is R4 000, so the gross profit is R2 000. That is true whether Hilltop Builders hands over R6 000 in cash today or promises to pay on 30 April. What differs between a cash and a credit sale is only the asset the business receives in exchange for its goods. In a cash sale, it receives money, so the bank increases. In a credit sale, it receives a legally enforceable promise to pay, so the debtors control account increases. Both are assets of R6 000. The effect on owner's equity, plus R6 000 for sales and minus R4 000 for cost of sales, is identical. This matters because the purpose of the profit figure is to measure how well the business performed in the period: how much it sold and at what margin. If profit depended on when customers chose to pay, a business could look profitable or unprofitable simply because of the timing of payments, which would mislead the owner and anyone else using the figures. The timing of payments is still very important, but it is measured separately, through the bank balance and, in later grades, the cash flow statement. That is why accountants keep two questions apart: how much profit did we make, and how much cash do we have? A credit sale increases profit immediately but cash only later.

### subtopic_3 — Receiving Payment from a Debtor
**Assistant:** Why does a payment from a debtor have no effect on owner's equity?

**Tutor:** Because it does not make the business any richer; it only changes the form of an asset the business already has. On 3 April, when Hilltop Builders took the goods, Ridgeview recorded the full effect of the sale: owner's equity increased by the R6 000 sales income and decreased by the R4 000 cost of sales. At that point, Ridgeview owned a debtor of R6 000, an asset representing money it was entitled to receive. On 20 April, Hilltop pays. Ridgeview now has R6 000 more in the bank, but it no longer has the R6 000 debtor, because the debt has been settled. One asset has increased by exactly the amount that another has decreased, so total assets are unchanged. Nothing has happened to owner's equity, because no new income has been earned and no expense incurred. If you recorded the payment as income, you would be counting the same sale twice: once when the goods were delivered and again when the money arrived. The owner would believe the business had earned R12 000 of sales when it had earned only R6 000, and the profit would be overstated by R6 000. This is one of the most common mistakes learners make, and it is the same mistake as treating a loan received as income. A useful test for any receipt of money is to ask whether the business has earned something new, or whether it is simply collecting what it was already owed, or borrowing, or receiving capital. Only the first increases owner's equity through income. Receipts from debtors are always collections, never new income.

### subtopic_4 — Comparing Cash and Credit Transactions in a Table
**Assistant:** In the April table, why does the total change in assets equal the total gross profit?

**Tutor:** Because in this particular set of transactions, the only things that changed owner's equity were the three sales, and nothing affected liabilities. The accounting equation says that any change in assets must equal the change in owner's equity plus the change in liabilities. In the four April transactions, liabilities did not change at all: Ridgeview did not borrow or repay anything, and it did not buy anything on credit. So the change in assets must equal the change in owner's equity. What changed owner's equity? Each sale increased it by the selling price and decreased it by the cost price, so the net effect of each sale was its gross profit: R2 000 on the Hilltop sale, R1 200 on the Ace sale and R1 500 on the cash sale. The receipt from Hilltop did not change owner's equity at all. Total change in owner's equity: 2 000 + 1 200 + 1 500 = 4 700. Therefore the total change in assets must also be R4 700, and when you add up the asset column, plus 6 000, minus 4 000, plus 3 600, minus 2 400, plus 4 500, minus 3 000, plus 6 000, minus 6 000, you get exactly that. In a fuller example, with expenses, drawings or loans, the totals would not simply equal gross profit, but the rule that the change in assets equals the change in owner's equity plus liabilities would still hold. Checking the totals this way at the end of a table is the best way to catch a mistake in any single row, so it is worth doing every time.
