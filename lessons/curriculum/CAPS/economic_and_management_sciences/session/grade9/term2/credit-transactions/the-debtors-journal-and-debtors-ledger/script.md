# Part 1 — Expert

In this lesson we learn to record credit sales in the debtors journal, to keep a separate account for every customer in the debtors ledger, and to post the totals to the general ledger. We continue with Ridgeview Hardware, which sells building supplies at a mark-up of 50 percent on cost. In April Ridgeview starts selling on credit to a few trusted customers, so its bookkeeper needs new books.

## Subtopic: The Debtors Journal and Its Columns

The debtors journal, abbreviated DJ, is the subsidiary journal in which a business records all its credit sales of trading stock. It records nothing else. Cash sales go to the cash receipts journal, and payments received from debtors also go to the cash receipts journal, because money is coming into the bank. The DJ only records the moment when goods leave the business and a customer promises to pay later.

The source document for the DJ is the duplicate invoice. When Ridgeview sells on credit, it prepares an invoice in duplicate: the original goes to the customer, as proof of what was bought and what is owed, and the copy stays with Ridgeview as the evidence for its own records. Each invoice has a number, and invoices are numbered in sequence, so that a missing invoice can be noticed immediately. This is a simple internal control. If invoice 103 is missing from the file, someone must explain why.

The DJ is ruled up with the following columns. The first is the document number, in which the invoice number is written. Then comes the day of the month. The month itself is written once at the top of the date column. Next is the column for the debtor's name, which tells us which customer bought the goods. Then comes the folio column, in which we write the folio of the debtor's account in the debtors ledger when we post to it. Finally there are two money columns: Sales, which records the selling price of the goods, and Cost of sales, which records the cost price of the same goods.

Why two money columns? Because every credit sale of trading stock has two parts, exactly as a cash sale does. The first part is the income: the business has earned the selling price, and the customer now owes that amount. The second part is the cost: the trading stock that left the business cost the business something, and that amount becomes an expense, cost of sales. Ridgeview uses a perpetual inventory system, which means it records the cost of the goods sold every time it makes a sale, so the stock account always shows what should be on the shelves.

At a mark-up of 50 percent on cost, the cost price is found by dividing the selling price by 150 and multiplying by 100. For a sale of R6 000, the cost is 6 000 × 100 ÷ 150 = 4 000. You can check your answer by going forwards: the cost of R4 000 plus a mark-up of 50 percent, which is R2 000, gives R6 000. Always do this check, because a wrong cost price would affect both the stock figure and the profit.

The DJ has no sundry column and no bank column. Everything in it is a credit sale of trading stock, so there is no need for any other column. That makes it the simplest of the four journals you will use in Grade 9.

## Subtopic: Recording Ridgeview's April Credit Sales

Here are Ridgeview Hardware's credit sales for April. On 3 April Ridgeview sold goods to Hilltop Builders on invoice 101 for R6 000, which cost R4 000. On 9 April it sold goods to Ace Plumbing on invoice 102 for R3 600, which cost R2 400. On 15 April it sold more goods to Hilltop Builders on invoice 103 for R4 500, which cost R3 000. On 22 April it sold goods to Sunset Guest House on invoice 104 for R2 700, which cost R1 800. On 28 April it sold goods to Ace Plumbing on invoice 105 for R1 800, which cost R1 200.

Before writing anything, check each cost price. Invoice 102 is 3 600 × 100 ÷ 150 = 2 400. Invoice 103 is 4 500 × 100 ÷ 150 = 3 000. Invoice 104 is 2 700 × 100 ÷ 150 = 1 800. Invoice 105 is 1 800 × 100 ÷ 150 = 1 200. Every cost is two thirds of the selling price, which is what a mark-up of 50 percent on cost always gives.

Now the entries. The heading at the top reads: Debtors journal of Ridgeview Hardware, April 20.., with the page number on the right, DJ4. Each invoice takes one line. On the first line we write 101 in the document column, 3 in the day column, Hilltop Builders in the debtor column, and the amounts 6 000 under Sales and 4 000 under Cost of sales. The folio column stays empty for now; it is filled in only when the entry is posted to the debtors ledger. The other four invoices follow in date order on the lines below.

Notice that Hilltop Builders appears twice and Ace Plumbing appears twice. That is completely normal. A journal records transactions in the order they happen, not grouped by customer. The grouping by customer happens in the debtors ledger.

At the end of the month the two money columns are totalled. Sales: 6 000 + 3 600 + 4 500 + 2 700 + 1 800 = 18 600. Cost of sales: 4 000 + 2 400 + 3 000 + 1 800 + 1 200 = 12 400. A useful check is that the cost total is also two thirds of the sales total, because all the goods were sold at the same mark-up: 18 600 × 100 ÷ 150 = 12 400. The difference between the totals is the gross profit on credit sales for the month: 18 600 - 12 400 = 6 200. The totals are double ruled, which shows that the journal for April is closed.

A common error is to write a cash sale in the DJ because it happened on the same day as a credit sale. The question to ask is always: did money come in today? If yes, the transaction belongs in the CRJ. Only if the customer is to pay later does it belong in the DJ. Another common error is to record the cost price in the Sales column; always check that the Sales figure is the bigger of the two.

## Subtopic: The Debtors Ledger and Posting to It

The general ledger has one account called Debtors control, which shows the total amount owed by all debtors together. That total is useful for the trial balance, but it cannot tell Ridgeview how much Hilltop Builders owes, or whether Ace Plumbing has paid. For that, the business keeps a subsidiary ledger, the debtors ledger, with a separate account for every debtor. The folios in the debtors ledger begin with D, so Hilltop Builders might be D1, Ace Plumbing D2 and Sunset Guest House D3.

The debtors ledger accounts in Grade 9 are usually kept in a three-column format, with columns for the date, the details, the folio, the debit amount, the credit amount and a running balance. Each line shows what changed and how much the debtor now owes. This format makes it easy to see the balance at any moment, which is exactly what a business needs when a customer asks to buy more on credit.

Posting from the DJ to the debtors ledger is done for every line, not as a total. Each invoice increases what that particular debtor owes, so it is entered on the debit side of the debtor's account. For invoice 101 we go to Hilltop Builders, D1, and write: date 3 April, details Invoice 101, folio DJ4, debit 6 000, balance 6 000. Then we return to the DJ and write D1 in the folio column next to that line, which shows that it has been posted. For invoice 103, on 15 April, Hilltop's account shows a further debit of 4 500, and the balance becomes 6 000 + 4 500 = 10 500.

Payments by debtors are posted to the debtors ledger from the cash receipts journal. In April, Hilltop paid R6 000 on 20 April with receipt 010, and Ace Plumbing paid R3 600 on 30 April with receipt 011. A payment reduces what the debtor owes, so it is entered on the credit side of the debtor's account. Hilltop's account therefore shows: 3 April Invoice 101 debit 6 000, balance 6 000; 15 April Invoice 103 debit 4 500, balance 10 500; 20 April Receipt 010 credit 6 000, balance 4 500. Hilltop owes R4 500 at the end of April.

Ace Plumbing's account shows: 9 April Invoice 102 debit 3 600, balance 3 600; 28 April Invoice 105 debit 1 800, balance 5 400; 30 April Receipt 011 credit 3 600, balance 1 800. Ace owes R1 800. Sunset Guest House's account shows only one line: 22 April Invoice 104 debit 2 700, balance 2 700.

Notice that the debtors ledger never records the cost of sales. A customer owes us the selling price, not what the goods cost us. The cost column of the DJ is posted only to the general ledger.

Notice also the order of the dates. A debtor's account can mix entries from the DJ and the CRJ, and they are entered in date order, so the running balance is always correct for that date.

## Subtopic: Posting to the General Ledger and the Debtors List

The general ledger receives only the monthly totals from the DJ, never the individual lines. That is the main advantage of a subsidiary journal: five invoices, or five hundred, become a single posting per account.

The Sales total of R18 600 is posted twice, because it affects two accounts. Debtors control is debited with R18 600, because debtors, an asset, increase. The details column says Sales, which names the contra account, and the folio is DJ4. Sales is credited with R18 600, because income increases; the details column says Debtors control.

The Cost of sales total of R12 400 is also posted twice. Cost of sales is debited with R12 400, because this expense increases, and its details column says Trading stock. Trading stock is credited with R12 400, because the asset decreases, and its details column says Cost of sales. After posting, we write the general ledger folios under the totals in the DJ, which shows that the totals have been posted.

The receipts from debtors in the CRJ add a further entry. The CRJ has a Debtors control column, and its April total of 6 000 + 3 600 = 9 600 is posted to the credit side of Debtors control, with the details Bank. So at the end of April, Debtors control shows a debit of 18 600 and a credit of 9 600, and its balance is 18 600 - 9 600 = 9 000, a debit balance, because debtors are an asset.

Now comes the most important check. The business prepares a debtors list, which is a list of every account in the debtors ledger with its balance at the end of the month. For Ridgeview at 30 April: Hilltop Builders R4 500, Ace Plumbing R1 800, Sunset Guest House R2 700. Total 4 500 + 1 800 + 2 700 = 9 000. This total must agree with the balance of the Debtors control account in the general ledger, and it does: R9 000 in both places.

Why must they agree? Because every amount posted to Debtors control as a total was also posted, line by line, to the individual debtor accounts. The control account is a summary, and the debtors ledger is the detail. If the totals differ, there is an error. Perhaps a line was not posted, an amount was copied incorrectly, or a payment was posted to the wrong side. The bookkeeper must find it before the trial balance is drawn up. This is why it is called a control account: it controls the accuracy of the subsidiary ledger.

The debtors list also helps with credit management. Ridgeview can see at a glance who owes money, how much, and for how long. In later grades you will learn to age these balances: to show how much is current, how much is 30 days old and how much is 60 days or older. Old balances are a warning sign of possible bad debts.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Let's make this simple. A shop that sells on credit needs to remember three things: what it sold on credit, who owes it money, and how much is owed in total. Each of those has its own book.

## Subtopic: A Notebook Only for Credit Sales

The debtors journal is a notebook only for credit sales. Every time Ridgeview gives goods to a customer who will pay later, it writes an invoice, keeps a copy, and writes one line in the DJ. Nothing else goes in this notebook. Cash sales do not go here, and money paid by customers does not go here.

Each line has the invoice number, the day, the customer's name, a space for a folio, and two amounts. The first amount is the selling price, under Sales. The second is what the goods cost Ridgeview, under Cost of sales. With a mark-up of 50 percent, the cost is always two thirds of the selling price. So a sale of R6 000 costs R4 000, and a sale of R3 600 costs R2 400.

At the end of April we add up both columns. The sales add up to R18 600 and the cost adds up to R12 400. The difference, R6 200, is the profit Ridgeview made on its credit sales in April, even though not all the money has arrived yet.

Ask one question to decide where a sale belongs: did the money come in today? Yes means CRJ. No means DJ.

## Subtopic: A Page for Every Customer

The DJ lists sales in date order, all mixed up. Hilltop is on line one and line three, and Ace is on line two and line five. To know how much each customer owes, we need a page for every customer. That is the debtors ledger.

On a customer's page, a new invoice goes on the debit side, because the customer owes more. A payment goes on the credit side, because the customer owes less. After each line we write the new balance.

Hilltop's page: owes R6 000, then buys R4 500 more and owes R10 500, then pays R6 000 and owes R4 500. Ace's page: owes R3 600, buys R1 800 more and owes R5 400, then pays R3 600 and owes R1 800. Sunset's page: owes R2 700.

The customer's page only ever shows the selling price. The customer does not care what the goods cost us; they owe what is on the invoice.

## Subtopic: Do the Totals Agree?

The general ledger has one account, Debtors control, for everyone together. It gets the DJ total of R18 600 on the debit side and the CRJ total of R9 600 from customer payments on the credit side. Its balance is R9 000.

Now add up every customer's page: 4 500 + 1 800 + 2 700 = 9 000. The two figures agree. That is the big check. If they did not agree, it would mean we made a mistake somewhere, maybe forgetting to post a line or posting a payment on the wrong side.

Remember the four general ledger postings from the DJ totals. Debtors control goes up on the debit side and Sales goes up on the credit side, each by R18 600. Cost of sales goes up on the debit side and Trading stock goes down on the credit side, each by R12 400.

So: the DJ is the notebook, the debtors ledger is a page per customer, and Debtors control is the total. The total must always match the sum of the pages. Practise until you can do all three without looking at the notes.
