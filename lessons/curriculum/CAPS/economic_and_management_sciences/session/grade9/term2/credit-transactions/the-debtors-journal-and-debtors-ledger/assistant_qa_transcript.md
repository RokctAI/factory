### subtopic_1 — The Debtors Journal and Its Columns
**Assistant:** Why does the debtors journal need a cost of sales column when the customer only owes the selling price?

**Tutor:** Because the debtors journal is not only a record of what customers owe; it is the book of first entry for the whole credit sale, and every sale of trading stock has two effects on the business. The first effect is the income. Ridgeview has earned the selling price, and the customer now owes that amount. That part is recorded in the Sales column, and it is the only figure that goes to the customer's account in the debtors ledger. The second effect is on the stock. The goods have physically left the shop, so the trading stock that Ridgeview owns has decreased by what those goods cost, and that cost has become an expense, cost of sales. Ridgeview uses a perpetual inventory system, which means it records this cost every time it sells, so that the Trading stock account always shows what should be on the shelves. If the DJ had no cost of sales column, the bookkeeper would have to record the cost somewhere else, or the Trading stock account would keep showing goods that had already been sold. The profit would also be wrong, because sales would appear in the books without the expense that goes with them. Keeping both figures on the same line, from the same invoice, makes it almost impossible to record one half of the sale and forget the other. It also gives a quick check on the gross profit: at the end of April, sales of 18 600 minus cost of sales of 12 400 gives a gross profit of 6 200 on credit sales, and because every sale used the same mark-up, the cost total must be exactly two thirds of the sales total. If it is not, there is an error in one of the lines.

### subtopic_2 — Recording Ridgeview's April Credit Sales
**Assistant:** Why are invoices numbered in sequence, and why does the DJ record them in date order?

**Tutor:** Both rules are about completeness and control. Numbering invoices in sequence means that every invoice can be accounted for. If the DJ shows invoices 101, 102, 104 and 105, the gap immediately raises a question: what happened to 103? Perhaps it was spoilt and should be in the file marked as cancelled, or perhaps a sale was made and never recorded, which could mean that goods left the shop without anyone being asked to pay for them. Without sequential numbers, a missing sale would be very hard to notice. This is a simple but powerful internal control, and auditors check it. Recording in date order is about the purpose of a journal. A journal is a book of first entry, a chronological diary of transactions as they happen. Writing each invoice on the next line, in the order the invoices were issued, means nothing is skipped and the record can be checked against the invoice file one by one. It would be tempting to group Hilltop's two invoices together, but that is the job of the debtors ledger, not the journal. If you grouped by customer in the journal, you would lose the chronological record, and it would be much harder to see whether every invoice had been entered. So the journal answers the question, what happened and when, and the ledger answers the question, what is the position of each account. Keeping those two jobs separate is what makes the whole system reliable. In tests, marks are often given for the correct order of entries, the correct document numbers and the correct dates, so it is worth being careful with these details rather than focusing only on the amounts.

### subtopic_3 — The Debtors Ledger and Posting to It
**Assistant:** Why do we post every line to the debtors ledger, but only totals to the general ledger?

**Tutor:** Because the two ledgers have different jobs. The debtors ledger exists to show how much each individual customer owes, and the only way to know that is to record every invoice and every payment in that customer's own account. If we posted only a total, we would know the business was owed R18 600, but not that R10 500 of it came from Hilltop and R5 400 from Ace. The business needs that detail to send statements to customers, to decide whether a customer has reached their credit limit, and to follow up on late payments. The general ledger, on the other hand, exists to show the overall financial position of the business, and for that the total is enough. Debtors control tells us that, in total, debtors owe R9 000, which is the figure that appears in the trial balance and later as a current asset. Posting every single invoice to the general ledger would make it long and cluttered without adding any useful information, because that detail is already kept in the debtors ledger. This is the main reason subsidiary journals and subsidiary ledgers were invented: they let the business keep full detail where detail is needed, and summaries where summaries are enough. One more point is worth noticing. When you post a line to a debtor's account, you write the debtor's folio, such as D1, in the DJ's folio column next to that line. When you post a total to the general ledger, you write the general ledger folios under the total. Those folio references create an audit trail, so anyone can trace an amount from the invoice, to the journal, to the ledger, and back again.

### subtopic_4 — Posting to the General Ledger and the Debtors List
**Assistant:** What should a bookkeeper do if the debtors list does not agree with Debtors control?

**Tutor:** Treat it as a signal that an error exists, and search for it systematically before going any further, because the trial balance and the financial statements depend on the right figure. Start with the difference itself, because it often points to the cause. If the debtors list is exactly one invoice amount lower than the control account, a line in the DJ has probably not been posted to the debtors ledger. Check every line in the DJ folio column: a line without a folio was never posted. If the difference is twice an amount, for example R7 200 when Ace paid R3 600, a payment has probably been posted to the wrong side of a debtor's account, debited instead of credited, which creates an error of double the amount. If the difference is divisible by 9, such as 540, two digits may have been swapped, for example 4 500 written as 5 040, or an amount may have been copied with a misplaced zero. Next, check the additions: re-add the DJ columns, the CRJ Debtors control column and each running balance in the debtors ledger. Then check that every receipt from a debtor in the CRJ has been posted to the correct customer's account. Once the error is found, it is corrected and the totals are compared again. In Ridgeview's case, the debtors list shows 4 500 + 1 800 + 2 700 = 9 000, and Debtors control shows 18 600 - 9 600 = 9 000, so the records agree. It is worth remembering that agreement does not prove that everything is right. For example, if a payment from Ace was posted to Hilltop's account by mistake, both totals would still agree, but the individual balances would be wrong. So the control check is very useful, but it does not replace careful posting.
