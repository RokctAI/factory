### subtopic_1 — Cash Sales and Credit Sales
**Assistant:** Why is a credit sale recorded as income now, when the business has not yet received any money?

**Tutor:** Because the business has already done what it promised: it has delivered the goods, so it has earned the income, and it has gained a legal right to be paid. Accounting records income when it is earned, not when the cash happens to arrive. Think about what has changed for Ridgeview Hardware on the day it delivers R6 000 of materials to Hilltop Builders on credit. The goods have left its shelves, so its trading stock has fallen. In exchange, it has a claim against Hilltop Builders for R6 000, a debt that the builder is legally bound to pay under their credit agreement. That claim has value; the business could even sell it to a finance company. So the business is better off by the profit on the sale, exactly as if it had sold the goods for cash, except that its new asset is a debtor rather than money in the bank. If the sale were only recorded when the builder paid at the end of the month, the books would show stock disappearing with no explanation, the income would be recorded in the wrong month, and the business would appear to own less than it does. When the builder pays later, there is no new income at all: one asset, debtors, is simply exchanged for another, bank. This principle, recognising income when it is earned, is called the accrual basis of accounting, and it applies equally to expenses: an expense is recorded when it is incurred, not necessarily when it is paid. You will meet it formally in Grade 10, in the topics on year-end adjustments, but credit sales are your first encounter with it.

### subtopic_2 — Debtors, Credit Policy and the Documents of a Credit Sale
**Assistant:** Why does a business set a credit limit for each debtor?

**Tutor:** To control its risk. Every rand owed by a debtor is a rand the business has not yet received and might never receive. A credit limit caps how much any single customer can owe at one time, so that if that customer fails to pay, the loss is limited to an amount the business can survive. Imagine Ridgeview Hardware without credit limits. A large building contractor might take R200 000 of materials over two months, then run into financial trouble and close down. If that is more than Ridgeview's profit for several months, the loss could push Ridgeview itself into difficulty, because it has already paid its suppliers for those materials. With a credit limit of, say, R20 000, the contractor can only owe up to that amount; any further orders must be paid in cash until the account is reduced. The limit is set using the information gathered when credit is granted: the customer's income or turnover, references from other suppliers, and the report from a credit bureau showing how reliably the customer has paid other debts. A new customer usually starts with a low limit, which is raised over time as the customer proves reliable. The credit limit also helps the business manage its own cash flow, because it can plan how much money is tied up in debtors at any time. Together with the credit terms, the period allowed for payment, and a firm procedure for following up overdue accounts, credit limits are the core of a sound credit policy. In Grade 10 you will learn to calculate the average collection period, which measures how quickly debtors actually pay.

### subtopic_3 — The National Credit Act and Consumer Rights
**Assistant:** What is reckless lending, and why does the National Credit Act prohibit it?

**Tutor:** Reckless lending is granting credit to a consumer without properly checking whether the consumer can afford it, or granting it when the assessment shows that the consumer cannot afford it, or when the consumer clearly does not understand the risks and obligations. Before the National Credit Act, some lenders gave credit freely to people who had little chance of repaying, sometimes deliberately, because they could profit from high fees, interest and penalties, or repossess goods. Many households became trapped in debt: borrowing from one lender to repay another, with a growing share of their income going to interest. The Act requires every credit provider to carry out an affordability assessment before granting credit. The provider must look at the consumer's income, regular expenses and existing debts and judge whether the new repayments can be afforded. It must also check the consumer's understanding of the agreement. If a provider fails to do this, or lends despite a clear inability to pay, a court may declare the agreement reckless. The court can then suspend the agreement, so that the consumer does not have to pay interest or fees for a period, or set aside all or part of the consumer's obligations. This puts the risk where it belongs: on the lender, who has the information and expertise to judge affordability, rather than on the consumer alone. The Act does not remove the consumer's responsibility: a consumer who gives false information on an application cannot later claim reckless lending. The aim is a credit market in which credit helps people to buy homes, study and build businesses, rather than one that traps them.

### subtopic_4 — The Cost of Credit and Using Credit Wisely
**Assistant:** Is taking a loan for a house a good use of credit but buying clothes on account a bad one?

**Tutor:** In general, yes, and the reason has to do with how long the benefit lasts compared with how long you pay. A house lasts for decades and usually keeps or increases its value over time. A home loan, called a bond in South Africa, lets a family live in the house while paying for it over twenty years or so. The family receives the benefit, shelter, for as long as it pays, and at the end it owns an asset that is likely to be worth more than when it was bought. Rent that would otherwise have been paid is saved. A student loan for a qualification is similar: the education increases earning power for a whole career. A business loan for equipment is similar too, if the equipment produces income that covers the repayments. Clothes bought on a store account are different. They are used, they wear out or go out of fashion, and they lose almost all their resale value immediately. If they are bought on six or twelve months of credit with interest and fees, the buyer may still be paying long after the clothes are worn out, and the total cost is higher than the cash price. Repeated over many purchases, store accounts and personal loans for consumption are one of the main routes into over-indebtedness. That said, rules of thumb are not absolute. A home loan can be a bad decision if the repayments are unaffordable or interest rates rise sharply, and small, short-term credit for an essential item can be reasonable if it is repaid quickly. The best test is to ask: will this purchase still be giving me value, or earning me money, while I am paying for it, and can I afford the repayments even if things go wrong?
