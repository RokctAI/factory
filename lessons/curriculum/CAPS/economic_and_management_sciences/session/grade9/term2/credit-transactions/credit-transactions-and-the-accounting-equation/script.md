# Part 1 — Expert

This session places credit transactions in the accounting cycle and analyses their effect on the accounting equation, A = OE + L. The anchor is the two-moment rule for a credit sale: at the moment of sale, debtors increase instead of bank, and the sale and cost of sales affect owner's equity exactly as a cash sale does; at the moment of payment, one asset, bank, increases and another, debtors, decreases, with no effect on owner's equity. The governing principle is that the profit on a sale is earned when the goods are delivered, not when the money arrives. The examples continue with Ridgeview Hardware's April transactions, at a mark-up of 50 percent on cost.

## Subtopic: The Accounting Cycle with Credit Transactions

The accounting cycle is the sequence of steps that a business follows to record and report its transactions in each financial period. With credit transactions added, it has these steps.

Step one: a transaction occurs and a source document is created. For a credit sale, the business issues an invoice and keeps the duplicate invoice. For a cash receipt, a receipt or cash register roll. For a payment, an EFT proof or cheque counterfoil.

Step two: the source documents are recorded in the subsidiary journals, the books of first entry. Cash received goes in the cash receipts journal, CRJ; cash paid in the cash payments journal, CPJ; credit sales in the debtors journal, DJ; and, in Term 3, credit purchases in the creditors journal, CJ. In Grade 10 you will add journals for returns and a general journal for other entries.

Step three: the journals are posted. Totals and sundry amounts are posted to the general ledger. Individual credit sales and receipts from debtors are also posted to the debtors ledger, a subsidiary ledger with one account for each debtor, so that the business knows how much each customer owes.

Step four: at the end of the month, the general ledger accounts are balanced and a trial balance is drawn up to check that total debits equal total credits. The debtors ledger is checked against the debtors control account by drawing up a debtors list.

Step five: at the end of the financial year, financial statements are prepared, the statement of comprehensive income and the statement of financial position, and the nominal accounts are closed off to start the next year. This step is studied in Grade 10.

The cycle then repeats. The word cycle is used because the same steps occur every period, and the closing balances of one period become the opening balances of the next.

Credit transactions add two new elements to what you have done so far: a new journal, the DJ, and a new type of ledger, the subsidiary debtors ledger. But the accounting equation and the debit and credit rules work exactly as before.

## Subtopic: A Credit Sale in the Accounting Equation

On 3 April, Ridgeview Hardware sold goods on credit to Hilltop Builders for R6 000, invoice 101. The mark-up is 50 percent on cost, so the cost price is 6 000 × 100 ÷ 150 = 4 000, or R4 000.

Analyse the transaction in two parts, exactly as for a cash sale.

The sales part. The account debtors control, an asset, increases by R6 000, because the business now has a right to receive R6 000 from Hilltop Builders. Owner's equity increases by R6 000, because income was earned from sales. Assets plus R6 000, owner's equity plus R6 000.

The cost part. Trading stock, an asset, decreases by R4 000, because the goods have left the business. Owner's equity decreases by R4 000, because of the expense cost of sales. Assets minus R4 000, owner's equity minus R4 000.

The net effect: assets increase by 6 000 - 4 000 = 2 000, or R2 000, and owner's equity increases by R2 000, the gross profit on the sale. Liabilities are not affected. The equation balances.

Compare this with a cash sale. The only difference is the first asset: in a cash sale, bank increases; in a credit sale, debtors increase. The effect on owner's equity, the sales income, the cost of sales and the gross profit, is exactly the same. This confirms that the profit is earned at the moment of sale, whether the customer pays immediately or later.

On 9 April, Ridgeview sold goods on credit to Ace Plumbing for R3 600, invoice 102. Cost: 3 600 × 100 ÷ 150 = 2 400. Debtors control plus R3 600 and owner's equity plus R3 600 for sales; trading stock minus R2 400 and owner's equity minus R2 400 for cost of sales. Net: assets plus R1 200, owner's equity plus R1 200.

In the examination format, each row of the table names the transaction and shows, for each element, the amount and sign: for invoice 101, A: plus 6 000 and minus 4 000; OE: plus 6 000 and minus 4 000; L: 0. Many textbooks also ask you to name the accounts and give reasons, such as debtors increased, income earned, stock decreased, cost of sales.

## Subtopic: Receiving Payment from a Debtor

On 20 April, Hilltop Builders paid R6 000 by EFT in settlement of invoice 101. Ridgeview issued receipt 010.

Analyse the transaction. Bank, an asset, increases by R6 000, because money was received. Debtors control, an asset, decreases by R6 000, because Hilltop Builders no longer owes that amount. Assets plus R6 000 and minus R6 000: total assets do not change. Owner's equity and liabilities are not affected.

This is an exchange of one asset for another: a claim to receive money has been turned into money in the bank. There is no income, because the income was already recorded on 3 April when the goods were delivered. If the payment were recorded as income again, the sale would be counted twice and the profit overstated.

Where is this payment recorded? In the cash receipts journal, because money was received, in the bank column and in a new column, debtors control. The name of the debtor is written in the details column so that the amount can be posted to that debtor's account in the debtors ledger.

Now consider what happens if a debtor pays only part of the account. Suppose Ace Plumbing pays R2 000 of the R3 600 it owes. Bank increases by R2 000, debtors control decreases by R2 000, and Ace Plumbing still owes 3 600 - 2 000 = 1 600, or R1 600. The debtors ledger will show that balance.

And if a debtor never pays? Then, after reasonable efforts to collect, the business must write the debt off as irrecoverable. Debtors control decreases, and owner's equity decreases because of an expense called bad debts. You will record bad debts in Grade 10, but it is important to see now why careful credit control matters: a bad debt turns a sale into a loss.


A useful habit when you analyse any transaction is to ask four questions in the same order every time. First, which accounts are affected? Second, is each account an asset, an owner's equity account or a liability? Third, does each account increase or decrease? Fourth, does the equation still balance after the change? When a business sells on credit, the answers are clear. Debtors control is an asset and it increases. Sales is income, which increases owner's equity. Trading stock is an asset and it decreases by the cost price. Cost of sales is an expense, which decreases owner's equity by that same cost price. The net effect on assets is the mark-up, and the net effect on owner's equity is also the mark-up, so the equation balances. When the debtor later pays, Bank increases and Debtors control decreases by the same amount. Both are assets, so total assets do not change at all, owner's equity does not change and liabilities do not change. Learners who work through these four questions slowly and in order make far fewer mistakes than learners who try to guess the answer in one step. Write the questions at the top of your page until they become automatic.

## Subtopic: Comparing Cash and Credit Transactions in a Table

The clearest way to master this topic is to analyse a set of transactions together. Ridgeview's selected April transactions are:

Transaction one: credit sale to Hilltop Builders, R6 000, cost R4 000. Assets: plus 6 000 debtors, minus 4 000 trading stock. Owner's equity: plus 6 000 sales, minus 4 000 cost of sales.

Transaction two: credit sale to Ace Plumbing, R3 600, cost R2 400. Assets: plus 3 600, minus 2 400. Owner's equity: plus 3 600, minus 2 400.

Transaction three: cash sale, R4 500, cost R3 000. Assets: plus 4 500 bank, minus 3 000 trading stock. Owner's equity: plus 4 500, minus 3 000.

Transaction four: Hilltop Builders pays R6 000. Assets: plus 6 000 bank, minus 6 000 debtors. Owner's equity: no effect.

Total effect on assets: 6 000 - 4 000 + 3 600 - 2 400 + 4 500 - 3 000 + 6 000 - 6 000 = 4 700, or R4 700. Total effect on owner's equity: 6 000 - 4 000 + 3 600 - 2 400 + 4 500 - 3 000 = 4 700, or R4 700. The two agree, so the equation still balances, and the total gross profit on the three sales is R4 700.

Debtors control after these transactions: 6 000 + 3 600 - 6 000 = 3 600, or R3 600, all owed by Ace Plumbing. The bank received 4 500 + 6 000 = 10 500, or R10 500, from these transactions.

Notice that transaction four has no effect on owner's equity, and that the gross profit of R4 700 includes the profit on the Ace Plumbing sale, even though Ace has not yet paid. This is the accrual principle again.

The error museum holds four mistakes. First, recording the receipt from a debtor as income; the income was recorded at the sale. Second, recording a credit sale as an increase in bank; bank only increases when money is received. Third, forgetting the cost of sales part of a credit sale; every sale, cash or credit, has a cost effect. Fourth, recording debtors as a liability; they are an asset.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same ideas again, through the bookkeeping loop, selling today and collecting later, and the two moments of a credit sale, in plain words with the same facts.

## Subtopic: The Five Steps of the Bookkeeping Loop

Every month, bookkeeping goes round the same loop. Here are the five steps.

One. Something happens and there is paper proof. A builder buys on credit: an invoice. Someone pays: a receipt. The shop pays someone: an EFT proof.

Two. The paper goes into the right notebook. Money in: the CRJ. Money out: the CPJ. Credit sales: a new notebook, the debtors journal, the DJ.

Three. At month end, the notebooks are carried to the ledger. The totals go to the general ledger. And each builder also gets a page of his own in a separate book, the debtors ledger, so the shop knows exactly how much each builder owes.

Four. Check that everything balances with a trial balance, and check that the builders' pages add up to the debtors total in the general ledger.

Five. At the end of the year, work out the profit and the financial position. You will learn that step in Grade 10.

Then it starts again next month. That is why it is called a cycle: it goes round and round.

What is new this term? Only two things: the debtors journal, a notebook for credit sales, and the debtors ledger, a book with a page for each customer who owes money. Everything else works just like before.

## Subtopic: Sell Today, Collect Later

Let's put a credit sale into the accounting equation.

On 3 April, Hilltop Builders takes R6 000 of goods on credit. The goods cost the shop R4 000.

Half one, the selling price. The shop now has something new: Hilltop owes it R6 000. That is debtors, an asset. Debtors plus 6 000. And owner's equity plus 6 000, because the shop made a sale.

Half two, the cost price. R4 000 of stock left the shelves. Trading stock minus 4 000. Owner's equity minus 4 000, for cost of sales.

Net result: plus 2 000 on both sides. That is the profit, and the shop made it today, even though Hilltop has not paid yet.

Now compare a cash sale of the same goods. Exactly the same, except that bank goes up instead of debtors. Cash or credit, the profit is the same and it is made on the day of the sale.

Another one. Ace Plumbing takes R3 600 on credit. The cost is 3 600 × 100 ÷ 150 = 2 400. Debtors plus 3 600, owner's equity plus 3 600; stock minus 2 400, owner's equity minus 2 400. Profit: R1 200.

## Subtopic: Two Moments, Two Records

A credit sale has two moments. The day the goods go, and the day the money comes.

Moment one, 3 April: the goods go. We just did that: debtors up, sales up, stock down, cost of sales up. The profit is made.

Moment two, 20 April: Hilltop pays R6 000. What changes? Bank goes up 6 000. Debtors go down 6 000, because Hilltop no longer owes it. That is all. Owner's equity does not move at all. Why? Because the sale was already counted on 3 April. If we counted it again, we would be pretending the shop sold twice.

So the payment is just a swap: the shop swaps a promise to pay for real money in the bank.

What if Ace pays only R2 000 of its R3 600? Bank up 2 000, debtors down 2 000, and Ace still owes 3 600 - 2 000 = 1 600.

And what if someone never pays? Then the shop has given away goods and got nothing. That loss is called a bad debt, and you will record it in Grade 10. That is why shops check customers before giving credit.

Now add up four transactions: Hilltop's sale, Ace's sale, a cash sale of R4 500 that cost R3 000, and Hilltop's payment. The profits are 2 000, 1 200 and 1 500, and the payment adds nothing. Total: 2 000 + 1 200 + 1 500 = 4 700. Both sides of the equation go up by R4 700. Balanced.

Here is a tip that helps many learners. Before you fill in a single column of the table, say the transaction out loud in plain words: "We gave goods, we will get money later." Then ask yourself whether money has actually arrived today. If it has not, Bank stays exactly as it is, and the amount owed goes into Debtors control instead. When the money does arrive, say it again: "The customer paid what they owed." Now Bank goes up and Debtors control goes down by the same amount. Nothing new was earned on that day, because the income was already recorded on the day of the sale. Saying it in plain words first stops you from recording the same income twice, which is one of the most common errors in tests.
