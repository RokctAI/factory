# Part 1 — Expert

This session introduces credit transactions in a trading business: selling goods on credit to customers who pay later, who become the business's debtors. It also introduces the National Credit Act, the law that protects consumers who buy on credit or borrow money in South Africa. The anchor is the definition of a debtor: a person or business that owes the business money for goods bought on credit, recorded as a current asset. The governing principle is that a credit sale is still a sale, earned when the goods are delivered, even though the money arrives later; and that credit has a cost and must be granted and used responsibly. The example business remains Ridgeview Hardware, which in April begins to sell to building contractors on credit.

## Subtopic: Cash Sales and Credit Sales

A cash sale is a sale in which the customer pays immediately, by cash, card or electronic transfer, at the time of the sale. All the sales recorded by Ridgeview Hardware in March were cash sales, recorded in the cash receipts journal.

A credit sale is a sale in which the goods are delivered now but the customer pays at an agreed later date. The customer who owes the money is called a debtor of the business. In April, Ridgeview Hardware decides to open accounts for several building contractors, such as Hilltop Builders and Ace Plumbing, who buy materials regularly and prefer to pay at the end of each month.

Why would a business sell on credit? The main advantage is more sales. Many customers, especially other businesses, expect credit terms, and if Ridgeview refused credit, contractors would buy from competitors who offer it. Credit also builds customer loyalty, because account customers tend to return to the same supplier. Larger orders may be possible because customers do not need cash immediately.

Selling on credit also has disadvantages. The business must wait for its money, which can cause cash flow problems: it may have to pay its own suppliers, wages and rent before its debtors pay. Some debtors pay late, and some never pay at all; those amounts become bad debts, which are an expense. Credit requires more administration: checking customers' creditworthiness, keeping a separate account for each debtor, sending monthly statements and following up overdue accounts. In Grade 10 you will also meet discount allowed, a reduction given to debtors who pay early, used to encourage prompt payment.

A credit sale is recorded as a sale on the date the goods are delivered, not when the money is received. This is because the business has earned the income when it hands over the goods: it has done its part of the agreement. The money owed is recorded as an asset, debtors, because the business has a right to receive it. This principle, recording income when it is earned rather than when cash is received, is central to accounting and is developed further in Grade 10.

## Subtopic: Debtors, Credit Policy and the Documents of a Credit Sale

A debtor is a person or business that owes money to the business for goods or services bought on credit. Debtors are a current asset, because the business expects to receive the money within a short period, usually 30 to 60 days. In the general ledger, the total owed by all debtors is kept in one account, the debtors control account. A separate account for each individual debtor is kept in a subsidiary book, the debtors ledger, which you will use in the third lesson of this topic.

Before granting credit, a sensible business follows a credit policy, a set of rules for deciding who may buy on credit and on what terms. A typical credit policy includes: an application form with the customer's details, identity or company registration number, address and bank details; references from other suppliers; a credit check with a credit bureau, which reports on the customer's history of repaying debts; a credit limit, the maximum amount the customer may owe at any time; and credit terms, the period within which the customer must pay, for example 30 days from the date of the statement. Regular review of overdue accounts, and stopping credit to customers who do not pay, are also part of the policy.

The source document for a credit sale is the invoice, also called a tax invoice if the business is registered for VAT. The business issues the original invoice to the customer and keeps a copy, the duplicate invoice, as its own source document. The invoice shows the invoice number, the date, the names and addresses of the seller and the buyer, a description of the goods, the quantities, unit prices and total, and the terms of payment. Duplicate invoices are recorded in the debtors journal, the DJ, the book of first entry for credit sales.

When a debtor pays, the business issues a receipt, and the payment is recorded in the cash receipts journal, in a new column called debtors control. At the end of each month, the business sends each debtor a statement of account, which lists the opening balance, the invoices for the month, the payments received and the balance owing. The statement reminds the debtor to pay and allows both parties to check that their records agree.

## Subtopic: The National Credit Act and Consumer Rights

When the customer is an individual consumer rather than a business, credit is regulated by the National Credit Act, Act 34 of 2005, which came fully into force on 1 June 2007. Its main purposes are to protect consumers from reckless lending and unfair practices, to promote a fair and transparent credit market, and to help consumers who are over-indebted.

The Act established the National Credit Regulator, the NCR, which registers credit providers, such as banks, retailers that offer store accounts and microlenders, as well as credit bureaus and debt counsellors, and investigates complaints. The National Consumer Tribunal hears cases where the Act has been breached.

Key consumer rights under the Act include the following. The right to apply for credit, and the right not to be unfairly discriminated against when applying. The right to be told the reasons if credit is refused. The right to receive information in plain and understandable language, including a pre-agreement statement and quotation that shows the full cost of the credit, the interest rate, fees and the total amount to be repaid, before signing. The right to a free credit report from each credit bureau once a year, and the right to challenge incorrect information on it. And the right to confidentiality of personal information.

The Act places important duties on credit providers. Before granting credit, they must carry out an affordability assessment: they must check the consumer's income, expenses and existing debts to make sure that the consumer can afford the repayments. Granting credit without this assessment, or to a consumer who clearly cannot afford it, is reckless lending, and a court may set aside or suspend such an agreement. The Act also limits the maximum interest rates and fees that may be charged on different types of credit, and requires that consumers be informed before any negative information is reported to a credit bureau.

Consumers who cannot pay their debts may apply to a registered debt counsellor for debt review. The counsellor assesses the consumer's situation and may negotiate reduced monthly repayments over a longer period with the credit providers, which can be made a court order, protecting the consumer from legal action while the plan is followed.

The Act also places responsibilities on consumers: to give truthful information when applying, to read and understand the agreement before signing, and to repay according to the agreement.

## Subtopic: The Cost of Credit and Using Credit Wisely

Credit is never free for the consumer. When you buy on credit, you usually pay interest and fees in addition to the cash price, so the total cost is higher.

Consider a television with a cash price of R6 000. A furniture store offers it on credit at 12 monthly instalments of R650. The total paid on credit is 12 × 650 = 7 800, or R7 800. The cost of credit is 7 800 - 6 000 = 1 800, or R1 800, which is 1 800 ÷ 6 000 × 100 = 30, or 30 percent of the cash price, for the convenience of paying over a year. If the consumer saved R500 a month instead, she could buy the same television for cash in 12 months and keep R1 800.

There are good and bad uses of credit. Credit can be sensible for assets that last a long time and increase in value or help earn income, such as a home loan for a house, a student loan for an education, or a loan to buy equipment for a business. Credit is risky for goods that lose value quickly or are used up, such as clothing, entertainment and holidays, because the consumer continues paying long after the benefit is gone. Credit for consumables often leads to over-indebtedness: borrowing to pay existing debts, a cycle that is hard to escape.

A consumer who misses payments damages his or her credit record. Credit bureaus record payment behaviour, and a poor record can make it difficult to get a cellphone contract, rent a flat or even get some jobs. A good credit record, built by borrowing modestly and paying on time, makes future credit cheaper and easier to obtain.

For businesses, the same principles apply from the other side. A business that sells on credit must manage its debtors carefully, because sales that are never paid are worse than no sales at all: the goods are gone and no money comes in.

The error museum holds four mistakes. First, recording a credit sale only when the money is received; the sale is recorded when the goods are delivered. Second, treating debtors as a liability; debtors owe the business, so they are an asset. Third, believing that the National Credit Act protects consumers who lied on their applications; consumers have a duty to give true information. Fourth, believing credit is free if there is no deposit; interest and fees make credit more expensive than cash.

## Subtopic: Buy Now, Pay Later

Picture Ridgeview Hardware in April. A builder from Hilltop Builders comes in every few days for cement, bricks and paint. Paying every time is a nuisance, so the builder asks: can I take the goods now and pay you at the end of the month?

If the shop agrees, that is a credit sale. The goods leave today; the money comes later. The builder now owes the shop money. A person or business that owes the shop money is called a debtor.

Is a debtor good or bad for the shop? The debt is something the shop will receive, so in accounting it is an asset, just like money in the bank, only not yet arrived.

Why sell on credit? Because builders want it, and if Ridgeview says no, they will buy from the shop down the road that says yes. More sales, and loyal customers.

What can go wrong? The shop has to wait for its money, but it still has to pay its own rent and wages now. Some builders pay late. A few may never pay. So the shop is careful. Before it says yes, it checks the builder's history with a credit bureau, sets a limit on how much the builder may owe, and agrees on when to pay, like 30 days.

The paper proof of a credit sale is the invoice. The builder gets the original; the shop keeps a copy, the duplicate invoice. At the end of each month, the shop sends a statement showing what the builder owes.

## Subtopic: The Rules That Protect Borrowers

When ordinary people buy on credit, like clothing on a store account or a fridge on instalments, there is a special law to protect them. It is the National Credit Act, which came into force in 2007.

The Act gives you rights. You may apply for credit and not be treated unfairly. If you are turned down, you must be told why. Before you sign, you must get the full cost in plain language: the interest, the fees and the total you will pay. You may get a free credit report once a year from each credit bureau, and fix anything wrong on it.

The Act also gives lenders a duty. Before they lend, they must check whether you can afford it: your income, your expenses and your other debts. Lending to someone who clearly cannot afford it is called reckless lending, and it is not allowed. A court can cancel such a deal.

If someone has too much debt and cannot pay, a debt counsellor can help work out smaller payments over a longer time.

There is a referee too: the National Credit Regulator registers lenders and investigates complaints.

But you have duties as well: tell the truth on your application, read before you sign, and pay what you agreed.

## Subtopic: What Credit Really Costs

Here is the question every buyer should ask: what does this cost if I pay later?

A television costs R6 000 cash. The store says: or pay just R650 a month for 12 months! R650 sounds small. Let's add it up: 12 × 650 = 7 800. So on credit, the television costs R7 800. That is 7 800 - 6 000 = 1 800 rand more. You pay an extra R1 800 just to have it now instead of later.

What if you saved R500 a month instead? After 12 months you would have R6 000 and could buy it for cash, and keep the R1 800 for something else.

So when is credit a good idea? When you are buying something that lasts a long time or helps you earn money: a house, your studies, a tool or a bakkie for your business. When is it a bad idea? For things that are quickly gone: clothes that go out of fashion, takeaways, parties. You keep paying long after the fun is over.

And always pay on time. Credit bureaus keep a record of how you pay. A good record makes borrowing easier and cheaper later. A bad record can stop you getting a phone contract or renting a flat.

Buy now, pay later is fine, as long as you know exactly how much later costs.
