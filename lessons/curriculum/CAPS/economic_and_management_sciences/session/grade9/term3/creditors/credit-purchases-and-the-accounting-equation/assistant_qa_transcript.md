### subtopic_1 — Buying on Credit and Creditors
**Assistant:** Why is the same transaction a credit sale for one business and a credit purchase for another?

**Tutor:** Because every transaction has two parties, and each records it from its own point of view. When BuildMart Wholesalers sends R8 000 of goods to Ridgeview and agrees to be paid later, BuildMart has made a credit sale. In BuildMart's books, Ridgeview becomes a debtor, an asset, and BuildMart records sales and cost of sales in its debtors journal from its duplicate invoice. In Ridgeview's books the same event is a credit purchase. BuildMart becomes a creditor, a liability, and Ridgeview records trading stock in its creditors journal from the original invoice it received. One piece of paper, two copies, two sets of books. This mirror image is a useful check when you are unsure. If you know how the seller records a transaction, you can work out the buyer's entry by reversing the roles: the seller's asset is the buyer's liability, and the seller's duplicate is the buyer's original. It also explains why the two businesses can compare records. A statement from BuildMart showing what Ridgeview owes should agree with BuildMart's account in Ridgeview's creditors ledger.

### subtopic_2 — The Accounting Cycle for Credit Purchases
**Assistant:** Why does Ridgeview check a supplier's invoice before recording it?

**Tutor:** Because the invoice creates a liability, and Ridgeview should accept that liability only for goods it actually ordered and received, at the agreed price. Suppliers make mistakes: an invoice may show the wrong quantity, the wrong price, goods that were never delivered, or an addition error. If Ridgeview recorded and paid every invoice without checking, it could pay for things it never received. So a careful business compares three documents: its own order, which shows what it asked for and at what price; the delivery note signed when the goods arrived, which shows what was actually received; and the supplier's invoice, which shows what the supplier is charging. Only when all three agree is the invoice recorded in the creditors journal and later paid. If they disagree, the business contacts the supplier, who may issue a correction. This is an important internal control, and it also protects against fraud, such as fake invoices from suppliers that do not exist. It is the purchasing function at work: buying the right goods, at the right price, and paying only for what was received.

### subtopic_3 — Credit Purchases in the Accounting Equation
**Assistant:** Why does buying trading stock on credit not reduce owner's equity?

**Tutor:** Because buying stock is not an expense at the time of purchase. When Ridgeview buys R8 000 of trading stock, it exchanges one thing for another: it receives goods worth R8 000, which it owns and will sell later, and in return it takes on a liability of R8 000. The business is no poorer and no richer; it simply has more stock and more debt. So assets increase by R8 000 and liabilities increase by R8 000, and owner's equity is unchanged. Owner's equity changes only when the stock is sold. At that moment, the selling price is recorded as sales, which increases owner's equity, and the cost price of the goods sold is recorded as cost of sales, which decreases owner's equity. The difference is the gross profit. Learners sometimes confuse cost of sales with purchases and show a decrease in owner's equity when stock is bought. That would make the business look as if it lost money every time it restocked its shelves, which would be wrong. Ridgeview's perpetual inventory system records the cost only when the goods are sold.

### subtopic_4 — Paying Creditors and the Full Analysis
**Assistant:** Why is stationery treated as an expense but equipment as an asset?

**Tutor:** Because of how long the business benefits from them. Equipment, such as the tile cutter, is used in the business for several years, so it remains something the business owns and is shown as a non-current asset. Its cost is spread over its useful life in later grades, through depreciation. Stationery, such as paper, pens and printer cartridges, is used up quickly, usually within weeks, and its value is small. Recording every packet of paper as an asset and then tracking when it is used up would cost more effort than it is worth, so the business treats stationery as an expense as soon as it is bought. The effect on the equation follows from this. Equipment on credit: assets up, liabilities up, no change in owner's equity. Stationery on credit: owner's equity down, liabilities up, no change in assets. Both transactions increase liabilities by the same kind of amount, because both were bought on credit. The question to ask is: does the business still own something of lasting value after the purchase? If yes, it is an asset; if it is quickly used up, it is an expense.

### subtopic_5 — Take Now, Pay Later
**Assistant:** Why does paying a creditor not reduce owner's equity, even though money leaves the business?

**Tutor:** Because the money is used to settle a debt, not to pay for a new expense. When Ridgeview pays BuildMart R5 000, it gives up R5 000 of an asset, money in the bank, and in exchange its liability to BuildMart falls by R5 000. Ridgeview is not worse off, because it owes exactly as much less as it paid. Assets decrease by R5 000 and liabilities decrease by R5 000, and the equation still balances with no change in owner's equity. The point when Ridgeview's owner's equity might have changed was the purchase itself. If Ridgeview had bought stationery, owner's equity decreased on the day of the purchase. If it bought stock, owner's equity will change when that stock is sold. Recording the payment as an expense as well would count the cost twice. This is the same principle as with debtors, in reverse. When a debtor pays Ridgeview, bank increases and debtors decrease, with no effect on owner's equity, because the income was recorded on the day of the sale. Income and expenses are recorded when they occur, not when money changes hands.

### subtopic_6 — What Goes Up When We Buy on Credit
**Assistant:** Is it a good idea for a business to buy everything on credit?

**Tutor:** Not necessarily. Credit has real advantages. It lets the business buy stock and sell it before paying, which reduces the money it needs in the bank, and it makes ordering easier. Many businesses could not operate without trade credit from their suppliers. But credit also has costs and risks. Every credit purchase is a promise to pay by a certain date, and if the business makes more promises than its future income can cover, it will fall behind. Late payment damages its reputation, suppliers may charge interest on overdue amounts, reduce its credit limit or stop supplying on credit altogether, and in serious cases they can take legal action. Some suppliers also offer a lower price or a discount for paying immediately or early, so buying on credit can be more expensive. A sensible approach is to use credit for trading stock that will be sold within the credit period, to keep total creditors at a level the business's sales can comfortably pay, and to pay on time every time, so that suppliers remain willing to help when the business has a difficult month.

### subtopic_7 — Paying What We Owe
**Assistant:** How does the liabilities column of the equation table relate to the creditors list?

**Tutor:** At the end of the period, the creditors list shows how much the business owes each supplier, and its total must equal the balance of Creditors control. In this example, Ridgeview owed none of these three suppliers at the start of September, so the change in liabilities in the table, plus R7 100, is also the total of the creditors list. BuildMart is owed 8 000 minus 5 000, which is R3 000; Office Hub is owed R600; and Toolquip is owed R3 500. Together, 3 000 plus 600 plus 3 500 is R7 100. In a real business, there would usually be opening balances from the previous month as well, so the creditors list would show the opening balance plus the month's credit purchases minus the month's payments. The equation table and the creditors list check each other. If the liabilities column showed R7 100 but the creditors list totalled a different amount, either a transaction was analysed incorrectly or a supplier's balance was worked out wrongly. In the next lesson, the creditors journal and the creditors ledger will produce the same figures through the books themselves.
