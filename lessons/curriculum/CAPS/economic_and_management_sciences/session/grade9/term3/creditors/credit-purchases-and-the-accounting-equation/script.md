# Part 1 — Expert

Ridgeview Hardware sells on credit to its customers, and it also buys on credit from its suppliers. In this lesson we study credit purchases: what they are, why businesses use them, how they move through the accounting cycle, and how they affect the accounting equation. Ridgeview is a sole trader that sells building supplies at a mark-up of 50 percent on cost.

## Subtopic: Buying on Credit and Creditors

A credit purchase is a purchase where the business receives goods or services now and pays for them later, usually within 30 days. The supplier who is owed the money is called a creditor of the business. Creditors are a current liability, because the business owes them money that must be paid within a short period, usually less than a year.

Notice the mirror image with debtors. When Ridgeview sells on credit, its customer becomes a debtor of Ridgeview, an asset, because money will come in. When Ridgeview buys on credit, its supplier becomes a creditor of Ridgeview, a liability, because money will go out. The same transaction looks different from each side: a credit sale for the supplier is a credit purchase for the buyer.

Why do businesses buy on credit? The main reason is cash flow. Ridgeview can buy trading stock in September, sell it to customers during September and October, and pay the supplier at the start of October with money from those sales. Without credit, Ridgeview would need much more money in the bank to keep its shelves full. Credit purchases also make buying convenient, because the business can order by phone or online without arranging a payment each time, and a good credit record with suppliers builds a relationship that can help in difficult months.

Credit purchases also have risks and costs. If Ridgeview buys too much on credit, it may not be able to pay all its creditors when payment is due. Late payment damages the business's reputation, suppliers may stop supplying on credit or charge interest on overdue amounts, and in serious cases the supplier can take legal action. A sensible business buys on credit only what it can pay for within the credit terms.

To buy on credit, a business applies for an account with the supplier. The supplier checks its credit record, much as Ridgeview checks its own customers, and then gives it a credit limit and credit terms, for example 30 days from the date of the invoice. If Ridgeview receives an invoice dated 3 September on 30-day terms, payment is due by 3 October.

Credit purchases are not only of trading stock. A business can also buy stationery, equipment, repairs or advertising on credit, and each of these creates a creditor in exactly the same way.

## Subtopic: The Accounting Cycle for Credit Purchases

The accounting cycle for credit purchases follows the same steps as every other transaction: source document, journal, ledger, trial balance and, in Grade 10, financial statements.

The source document is the original invoice received from the supplier. Notice the difference with credit sales. When Ridgeview sells, it issues the invoice and keeps the duplicate, the copy. When Ridgeview buys, the supplier issues the invoice, and Ridgeview receives and keeps the original. The invoice shows the supplier's name, the invoice number, the date, the goods supplied, the amount and the credit terms. Before recording it, Ridgeview checks it against its own order and against the goods actually received, so that it pays only for what it ordered and received.

The journal is the creditors journal, the CJ. It records all credit purchases, whatever was bought, with columns for the type of item: Trading stock, Stationery, Equipment and Sundry accounts, and a total column, Creditors control. We will study it in detail in the next lesson.

There are two ledgers. In the general ledger, Creditors control shows the total amount owed to all creditors, and the accounts for trading stock, stationery, equipment and so on show what was bought. In the creditors ledger, a subsidiary ledger, there is a separate account for each creditor, so the business knows exactly how much it owes to each supplier. The folios in the creditors ledger usually begin with C.

When the business later pays a creditor, the payment is recorded in the cash payments journal, the CPJ, in the Bank column and a Creditors control column, and posted to the creditor's account in the creditors ledger and to Creditors control in the general ledger.

At the end of the month the business prepares a creditors list, listing every creditor and the balance owed. The total must agree with the balance of Creditors control. Then the trial balance is prepared, with Creditors control shown as a credit balance, because it is a liability.

The pattern is the same as for debtors, but every side is reversed. Debtors have debit balances because they are assets; creditors have credit balances because they are liabilities. A credit sale increases debtors on the debit side; a credit purchase increases creditors on the credit side.

## Subtopic: Credit Purchases in the Accounting Equation

The accounting equation is assets = owner's equity + liabilities. Every transaction must keep it in balance. Let's analyse three of Ridgeview's credit purchases in September, using the four steps you know: which accounts, which element, increase or decrease, and the reason.

On 3 September, Ridgeview bought trading stock on credit from BuildMart Wholesalers for R8 000, invoice BM331. The accounts are Trading stock and Creditors control. Trading stock, an asset, increases by R8 000, because the business now owns more stock. Creditors control, a liability, increases by R8 000, because the business owes BuildMart. Owner's equity is not affected: buying stock is not an expense; it only becomes cost of sales when the stock is sold. Check: assets up 8 000 equals liabilities up 8 000.

On 8 September, Ridgeview bought stationery on credit from Office Hub for R600, invoice OH77. The accounts are Stationery and Creditors control. Stationery is an expense, so owner's equity decreases by R600. Creditors control, a liability, increases by R600. Assets are not affected. Check: 0 = -600 + 600.

On 12 September, Ridgeview bought a tile cutter for use in its own workshop, on credit from Toolquip, for R3 500, invoice TQ512. The accounts are Equipment and Creditors control. Equipment, an asset, increases by R3 500, because the business owns a new piece of equipment that it will use for several years. Creditors control increases by R3 500. Owner's equity is not affected. Check: 3 500 = 0 + 3 500.

Compare these three. All three increase liabilities, because all three were bought on credit. But what was bought determines the other effect. Trading stock and equipment are assets, so assets increase. Stationery is used up quickly and is treated as an expense, so owner's equity decreases. This is why the CJ has separate columns for different items.

It is also worth comparing a credit purchase with a cash purchase. If Ridgeview had paid R8 000 by EFT for the trading stock, one asset, trading stock, would increase by R8 000 and another asset, bank, would decrease by R8 000. Total assets would not change and no liability would arise. Buying on credit instead lets Ridgeview keep the R8 000 in the bank for now, at the cost of owing it to BuildMart.

Finally, remember that buying stock is not the same as selling it. Owner's equity changes only when the stock is sold: if goods that cost R1 200 are later sold on credit for R1 800, owner's equity increases by the R1 800 of sales and decreases by the R1 200 cost of sales, a net gain of R600.

## Subtopic: Paying Creditors and the Full Analysis

On 20 September, Ridgeview paid BuildMart Wholesalers R5 000 by EFT on account. The accounts are Creditors control and Bank. Bank, an asset, decreases by R5 000, because money left the bank. Creditors control, a liability, decreases by R5 000, because Ridgeview owes BuildMart less. Owner's equity is not affected: paying a debt is not an expense. The expense, if there was one, was recorded when the item was bought. Check: -5 000 = 0 + -5 000.

Now put the four transactions together in a table with columns for assets, owner's equity and liabilities. Transaction one: assets +8 000, owner's equity 0, liabilities +8 000. Transaction two: assets 0, owner's equity -600, liabilities +600. Transaction three: assets +3 500, owner's equity 0, liabilities +3 500. Transaction four: assets -5 000, owner's equity 0, liabilities -5 000.

Add each column. Assets: 8 000 + 0 + 3 500 - 5 000 = +6 500. Owner's equity: 0 - 600 + 0 + 0 = -600. Liabilities: 8 000 + 600 + 3 500 - 5 000 = +7 100. Check the equation: owner's equity plus liabilities is -600 + 7 100 = 6 500, which equals the change in assets. The equation balances.

The liability column also tells us what Ridgeview owes at the end of these transactions: BuildMart 8 000 - 5 000 = R3 000, Office Hub R600 and Toolquip R3 500, a total of 3 000 + 600 + 3 500 = R7 100. That is exactly the increase in liabilities, because Ridgeview owed none of these creditors at the start of September.

Notice the pattern of a credit purchase compared with a credit sale. A credit purchase has two moments. When the goods arrive, assets or expenses change and liabilities increase. When the creditor is paid, bank and liabilities both decrease. Owner's equity can change only at the first moment, and only if an expense was bought. A credit sale also has two moments. When the goods leave, debtors increase and owner's equity changes through sales and cost of sales. When the debtor pays, bank increases and debtors decrease, with no effect on owner's equity.

Common errors in tests are to show a payment to a creditor as an expense, which reduces owner's equity wrongly; to show the purchase of trading stock as a decrease in owner's equity, as if it were cost of sales; and to forget that stationery, unlike stock and equipment, is an expense. Always ask: what did the business receive, and does it still own it?

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Let's make this simple. When you buy on credit, you get the goods today and promise to pay later. The person you owe is your creditor. Everything in this lesson follows from that one idea.

## Subtopic: Take Now, Pay Later

Ridgeview buys from suppliers too. If it buys on credit, it gets the goods now and pays later. The supplier it owes is its creditor.

Creditors are the opposite of debtors. Debtors owe us money, so they are an asset. We owe creditors money, so they are a liability.

Why buy on credit? Because Ridgeview can sell the stock first and pay for it later with the money from the sales. But it must be careful: if it buys more on credit than it can pay, suppliers will stop trusting it.

The document is the invoice from the supplier. Ridgeview keeps the original, because the supplier wrote it. It goes in the creditors journal, and each supplier gets a page in the creditors ledger.

## Subtopic: What Goes Up When We Buy on Credit

When Ridgeview buys on credit, one thing always goes up: what it owes, the liability.

What else changes depends on what it bought. Stock for R8 000: Ridgeview owns more stock, so assets go up R8 000. A tile cutter for R3 500: it owns new equipment, so assets go up R3 500. Stationery for R600: stationery is used up, so it is an expense, and owner's equity goes down R600.

Ask yourself: does the business still own it? Stock and equipment, yes: asset. Stationery, it gets used up: expense.

Buying stock does not change owner's equity. Only selling it does.

## Subtopic: Paying What We Owe

When Ridgeview pays BuildMart R5 000, two things go down: money in the bank, and what it owes. Owner's equity does not change, because paying a debt is not an expense.

Now add up all four transactions. Assets: up 6 500. Owner's equity: down 600. Liabilities: up 7 100. And -600 + 7 100 = 6 500. Balanced.

Ridgeview now owes BuildMart 3 000, Office Hub 600 and Toolquip 3 500. That is 7 100, the same as the liabilities column. Practise by drawing the table yourself and checking that every line balances.
