### subtopic_1 — Paying Creditors in the Cash Payments Journal
**Assistant:** Why is a payment to a creditor recorded in the CPJ and not in the creditors journal?

**Tutor:** Because each journal records one kind of event, and paying is a different event from buying. The creditors journal records credit purchases: the moment when Ridgeview receives goods or services and takes on a debt. Its source document is the supplier's invoice, and no money moves. The cash payments journal records every payment of money out of the bank, whatever the reason. When Ridgeview pays a creditor, money leaves the bank, so the transaction belongs in the CPJ, with the EFT proof of payment or cheque counterfoil as the source document. If payments were recorded in the CJ, the CJ would mix events that increase the debt with events that decrease it, its total would mean nothing, and the Bank account would not show the money that left. The rule that always works is to ask the money question: did money go out of the bank today? If it did, the CPJ is the home. Within the CPJ, the Creditors control column then shows that this particular payment settles a debt, so that it can be posted to the creditors ledger and to Creditors control.

### subtopic_2 — Recording Ridgeview's October Payments
**Assistant:** Why does a payment to a creditor never go in the Trading stock column, even if the creditor supplied stock?

**Tutor:** Because the stock has already been recorded, and recording it again would count it twice. When BuildMart delivered R6 500 of stock on credit, the CJ recorded it in the Trading stock column, and at month end Trading stock was debited with the CJ total. The Trading stock account already includes those goods. When Ridgeview later pays BuildMart, no new stock arrives; the payment only settles the debt. If the bookkeeper entered the payment in the CPJ's Trading stock column, Trading stock would be debited a second time for the same goods, so the account would show more stock than is on the shelves, and Creditors control would not be reduced, so it would show that Ridgeview still owed money it had already paid. The trial balance might still balance, because the debits and credits would be equal, which makes this error dangerous: it is not caught automatically. The only time a payment to a supplier goes in the Trading stock column is a cash purchase, where the stock is bought and paid for at the same time, and so has not been recorded anywhere else.

### subtopic_3 — Posting Payments to the Creditors Ledger
**Assistant:** Why would a business agree to a part payment, as Toolquip did?

**Tutor:** A part payment is an arrangement between the business and its supplier in which the business pays some of what it owes now and the rest later. Toolquip agreed to accept R2 000 in October and R1 500 in November. There can be good reasons for this on both sides. For Ridgeview, October had large payments due, because the September purchases from several suppliers fell due at the same time, and paying everything at once could have left too little in the bank for wages and stock. Asking a supplier for a short extension, and keeping to the new arrangement, is far better than simply paying late without explanation. For Toolquip, receiving part of the money on time and the rest according to a clear plan is better than chasing a customer who cannot pay, and it keeps a good customer. In the books, a part payment is recorded exactly like any other payment, for R2 000, and the creditors ledger shows the remaining balance of R1 500. The important thing is that the arrangement is agreed in advance and honoured, so that the business's credit record remains good.

### subtopic_4 — Posting to the General Ledger and Reconciling
**Assistant:** Why is the supplier's statement compared with the creditors ledger before paying?

**Tutor:** Because two sets of records describe the same debt, and they should agree. Toolquip keeps an account for Ridgeview in its debtors ledger, and Ridgeview keeps an account for Toolquip in its creditors ledger. The supplier's monthly statement is a copy of Toolquip's version. If the two agree, Ridgeview can pay with confidence. If they differ, there are several possible reasons. A payment may be in transit, made by Ridgeview near the end of the month but not yet received by Toolquip when it prepared the statement. An invoice may be on the statement but not in Ridgeview's books, perhaps because it was lost, or because the goods were never delivered. An amount may have been copied incorrectly by either side. Ridgeview should investigate each difference, using its source documents such as EFT proofs and delivery notes, and resolve it with the supplier before paying. Paying the statement amount blindly could mean paying for goods never received or paying the same invoice twice. This comparison is one of the most important internal controls for creditors.

### subtopic_5 — Keeping the Promise
**Assistant:** What do the totals of the creditors journal and the payments to creditors tell the owner?

**Tutor:** They show whether the business's debt to suppliers is growing or shrinking, and why. In October, Ridgeview bought R9 600 on credit and paid R13 500 to creditors, so its total debt fell by 13 500 minus 9 600, which is R3 900, from R15 000 to R11 100. That is usually a healthy sign: it means Ridgeview paid its September bills as they fell due, while its new purchases were smaller. If the pattern were the other way round, with credit purchases much larger than payments month after month, creditors would keep rising. That might be fine if sales are growing, but it could also mean the business is struggling to pay and is relying on its suppliers to finance it, which is risky. The owner should also look at the individual balances. BuildMart and Highveld are owed only for October purchases, which are not yet due, but Toolquip's R1 500 dates from September and is due under the agreed arrangement in November. Watching these figures every month allows the owner to plan payments and keep every supplier's trust.

### subtopic_6 — The Supplier's Page Goes Down
**Assistant:** How does the cash purchase from Cash and Carry differ in the books from a credit purchase from BuildMart?

**Tutor:** The cash purchase is recorded once, in the CPJ, because the buying and paying happen at the same moment. On 18 October Ridgeview bought R3 300 of stock from Cash and Carry and paid by EFT immediately. The CPJ line shows R3 300 in the Bank column and in the Trading stock column. At month end, Trading stock is debited and Bank is credited. Cash and Carry never becomes a creditor, so it has no account in the creditors ledger, and Creditors control is not affected. The credit purchase from BuildMart is recorded in two steps, in two journals. On 6 October the CJ records R6 500 in Creditors control and Trading stock; at month end, Trading stock is debited and Creditors control credited, and BuildMart's account in the creditors ledger is credited. Later, when Ridgeview pays BuildMart, perhaps in November, the CPJ records the payment in Bank and Creditors control, and BuildMart's account is debited. In both cases, Trading stock increases by the cost of the goods exactly once. The difference is whether a creditor exists in between.

### subtopic_7 — Checking the Total
**Assistant:** What should a bookkeeper check if the creditors list does not agree with Creditors control?

**Tutor:** The most likely errors are those that affect only one side of the comparison. First, check the arithmetic of the creditors list and of the Creditors control balance. Second, check that every line in the CJ and every amount in the CPJ's Creditors control column has a creditors ledger folio next to it, which shows that it was posted. A missing folio points to an unposted entry. Third, check the sides: purchases should be on the credit side of each creditor's account and payments on the debit side. An amount on the wrong side causes a difference of double that amount. Fourth, check whether a payment that was not to a creditor, such as wages or a cash purchase, was wrongly posted to the creditors ledger, or whether a payment to a creditor was wrongly entered in the Trading stock column, so that it was posted to a creditor's account but never reached Creditors control. Finally, look at the size of the difference: if it equals one entry, that entry is probably missing; if it divides exactly by 9, two digits may have been swapped. Fix the error before preparing the trial balance.
