# Part 1 — Expert

In this lesson we study the business functions: the main areas of work that every business must perform if it is to survive and grow. CAPS names nine: general management, administration, purchasing, production, marketing, financing, human resources, public relations and risk management. In a large company each function is usually a separate department with its own manager. In a small business, one or two people may perform all nine, sometimes in the same afternoon. Either way, none of them can be ignored.

Throughout the lesson we follow Khaya Wood Works, a fictional small furniture manufacturer in Gqeberha that makes chairs, tables and school desks from locally grown pine. It employs twelve people and sells to schools, offices and households.

## Subtopic: General Management and Administration

General management is the function that holds all the others together. It is responsible for the four classic management tasks: planning, organising, leading and controlling. Planning means setting goals and deciding how to reach them, for example deciding that Khaya Wood Works will increase sales to schools by a fifth next year. Organising means arranging people, equipment and money so that the plan can be carried out: who will make the extra desks, which machine is needed, and where the timber will be stored. Leading means guiding and motivating people, communicating the plan and making decisions. Controlling means checking whether the results match the plan and taking corrective action when they do not.

General management is usually divided into three levels. Top management, such as the owner or managing director, sets the long-term vision and strategy. Middle management, such as a production manager or a marketing manager, turns that strategy into plans for a department. Lower management, such as a workshop supervisor, oversees daily work and makes sure that tasks are completed correctly and on time. In Khaya Wood Works, the owner is top management, the workshop foreman is lower management, and there is no separate middle level because the business is small.

Administration is the function that handles information. It collects, records, processes, stores and communicates the information that the business needs to operate. This includes keeping the financial records you have learnt about this year: source documents, journals, ledgers and the trial balance. It also includes filing customer orders, keeping employee records, managing correspondence and, today, running the computer systems and data. Without good administration, managers would make decisions blindly. They would not know how many orders are outstanding, which customers owe money, or whether the business is making a profit.

Good administration has certain features: information must be accurate, up to date, easy to find and kept secure. In South Africa, the Protection of Personal Information Act, usually called POPIA, requires businesses to protect personal information about customers and employees. That makes careful administration a legal duty, not just good practice.

## Subtopic: Purchasing, Production and Marketing

The purchasing function buys everything the business needs to operate: raw materials, trading stock, equipment, stationery and services. For Khaya Wood Works, the most important purchases are pine timber, screws, glue, varnish and sandpaper. The purchasing manager must buy the right quality, in the right quantity, at the right price, at the right time and from the right supplier. These are often called the five rights of purchasing.

Each right matters. If the timber is poor quality, the chairs will crack and customers will return them. If too little is bought, production stops while the workshop waits for timber. If too much is bought, money is tied up in stock that sits in the yard and may warp in the rain. If the price is too high, profit falls. If the timber arrives late, orders are delivered late. Purchasing also involves choosing reliable suppliers, comparing quotations, negotiating discounts and checking that deliveries match what was ordered.

The production function turns inputs into outputs. Inputs are raw materials, labour, machines and energy; outputs are the finished goods or services the business sells. At Khaya Wood Works, production means cutting, shaping, joining, sanding and varnishing timber to make furniture. The production function decides which production method to use, plans the layout of the workshop, schedules the work, maintains the machines, and controls quality. Quality control means checking products at different stages, so that faults are found before they reach the customer. It is far cheaper to fix a wobbly leg in the workshop than to replace a chair in a school hall.

Production can be by job, by batch or by mass production. Job production makes one item to a customer's specific order, such as a custom boardroom table. Batch production makes a group of identical items, such as fifty desks for one school. Mass production makes very large numbers of identical items continuously, usually with machines, such as plastic chairs from a factory. Khaya Wood Works uses job and batch production.

The marketing function identifies what customers want and makes sure the business supplies it profitably. It is much more than advertising. Marketing starts with market research: finding out who the customers are, what they need and what competitors offer. It then makes decisions about the four elements of the marketing mix, often called the four Ps: product, price, place and promotion. Product is what is sold, including its design, quality and packaging. Price is what the customer pays. Place is how the product reaches the customer, such as direct delivery or through a retail shop. Promotion is how customers are informed and persuaded, through advertising, social media, discounts or personal selling. For Khaya Wood Works, marketing might mean visiting school principals before the new budget year, offering a sample desk, and setting a price that is competitive with imported furniture.

## Subtopic: Financing, Human Resources and Public Relations

The financing function makes sure that the business has enough money, at the right time, and that money is used wisely. It has two sides: obtaining funds and managing funds. Funds can come from the owner's capital, from loans, from retaining profits in the business, or, for a company, from selling shares. Each source has a cost and a risk. A loan must be repaid with interest whatever happens to sales, while the owner's own capital has no fixed repayment but is limited by how much the owner has.

Managing funds means budgeting, controlling costs, managing cash flow and keeping enough cash to pay suppliers, wages and the bank. A profitable business can still fail if it runs out of cash, for example because its debtors pay late. Khaya Wood Works must pay its timber supplier within thirty days, but a school may take sixty days to pay for desks. The financing function must plan for that gap. It also prepares budgets, analyses financial statements and advises management on whether an investment, such as a new sanding machine, is worthwhile.

The human resources function, often called HR, manages the people who work in the business. Its tasks include planning how many workers are needed and with what skills, recruiting and selecting new employees, preparing employment contracts, induction, training and development, performance appraisal, remuneration and benefits, and handling discipline, grievances and dismissals. HR must comply with South African labour law, including the Basic Conditions of Employment Act, which sets minimum conditions such as working hours and leave, the Labour Relations Act, which deals with unions, disputes and fair dismissal, the Employment Equity Act, which prohibits unfair discrimination and promotes fair representation, and the Skills Development Act, which supports training. At Khaya Wood Works, HR might mean training a new apprentice to use a router safely and keeping proper records of leave.

The public relations function, often called PR, builds and maintains a positive image of the business with the public and with its stakeholders: customers, the community, the media, government, suppliers and investors. Public relations is different from advertising. Advertising is paid communication that tries to persuade people to buy a particular product. Public relations tries to create goodwill and trust in the business as a whole, often through news coverage, community involvement, sponsorships, open days, newsletters and social media. Khaya Wood Works might donate desks to a local crèche, or invite learners from a technical school to see the workshop. Such activities do not sell a specific product, but they build a reputation that makes customers, workers and suppliers more willing to deal with the business. PR also includes managing a crisis well, for example by responding openly and quickly if a batch of chairs turns out to be faulty.

## Subtopic: Risk Management

The risk management function identifies the risks that could harm the business, assesses how likely and how serious they are, and decides how to deal with them. A risk is any uncertain event that could cause a loss. Every business faces risks; the aim is not to remove them all, which is impossible, but to manage them sensibly.

Risks can be grouped in several ways. Some are physical: fire, flood, theft or damage to equipment. A workshop full of timber and sawdust has a real fire risk. Some are financial: customers who do not pay, rising interest rates, or a weaker rand that makes imported machine parts more expensive. Some are human: accidents at work, the loss of a key skilled worker, or fraud by an employee. Some are legal: being sued by a customer injured by a faulty product, or fined for breaking safety laws. Some are market risks: a new competitor, or a change in what customers want.

Once risks are identified, the business chooses how to respond. It can avoid a risk by not doing the risky activity at all, such as refusing credit to customers with a poor payment record. It can reduce a risk with controls, such as installing fire extinguishers, training workers in machine safety, locking the timber yard and checking customers' credit before selling on credit. It can transfer a risk to someone else, mainly by taking out insurance: the business pays a regular premium to an insurer, and the insurer pays for covered losses. It can accept a risk when it is small or unavoidable, keeping some money aside to cover it.

Some risks cannot be insured, for example the risk that customers simply stop buying a product because tastes change. Those are business risks that the owner must carry, and they are part of the reason the owner earns profit. In South Africa, employers must also register with the Compensation Fund under the Compensation for Occupational Injuries and Diseases Act, which pays benefits to workers injured at work, and the Occupational Health and Safety Act requires a safe workplace.

Risk management links to every other function. Purchasing manages supplier risk, production manages safety and quality risk, finance manages credit and cash risk, HR manages people risk and PR manages reputation risk. That is why it is listed as a function of its own: someone must look at the whole picture.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Let's make it simple. Imagine running even a tiny business, such as selling handmade chairs. Someone must decide what to do, keep the records, buy wood, make the chairs, find customers, look after the money, hire helpers, keep a good name and protect the business from disaster. Those are the nine business functions.

## Subtopic: Nine Jobs Every Business Must Do

Here is a short way to remember the nine functions, each with its main question.

General management: where are we going and how do we get there? Administration: what are the facts and figures, and where are they kept? Purchasing: what do we need to buy, and from whom? Production: how do we make it well? Marketing: who wants it, and how do we reach them? Financing: where does the money come from, and is it used wisely? Human resources: who works here, and are they trained and treated fairly? Public relations: what do people think of us? Risk management: what could go wrong, and what will we do about it?

In a big company, each question has its own department. In a one-person business, the owner answers all nine. That is why starting a business is hard work: you have to wear nine hats.

## Subtopic: Following One Chair Through the Business

Follow one school chair at Khaya Wood Works. The owner plans to win a school order; that is general management. Marketing visits the school and agrees on a design and a price. Purchasing orders pine, screws and varnish at a good price from a reliable supplier. HR makes sure there are trained workers to do the job. Production cuts, joins, sands and varnishes the chairs and checks every one. Administration writes up the order, the invoice and the records. Financing makes sure there is cash to pay for the wood and wages until the school pays. Risk management makes sure the workshop is insured and safe. And public relations? When the chairs are delivered, a photo of happy learners goes on the business's social media page.

Every function touched that one chair. If any one failed, the order could fail too.

## Subtopic: Matching Problems to Functions

A good test of your understanding is to match a problem to the function that should solve it. The workshop runs out of timber halfway through an order: that is purchasing. Chairs are coming back with wobbly legs: that is production and quality control. Nobody knows which customers have paid: that is administration. A newspaper reports that the business dumps sawdust in a river: that is public relations, and also management. Sales are falling because a cheaper competitor has opened: that is marketing. There is not enough cash to pay wages at month end: that is financing. A skilled worker resigns and nobody else knows how to use the machine: that is human resources. A fire destroys the stock and there is no insurance: that is risk management.

Notice that some problems belong to more than one function. Late deliveries to a school might be a purchasing problem if the timber arrived late, a production problem if the machines broke down, or a management problem if the owner accepted too many orders at once. When you answer a question about functions, look for the root cause, the first thing that went wrong, and name the function responsible for that. Then say how that function could prevent the problem next time, for example by keeping a minimum stock of timber, servicing the machines regularly or planning the workload before accepting an order. Examiners usually reward a clear link between the problem, the function and a practical solution.

Practise with your own examples. If you can say which function owns a problem, you understand how a business works.
