# Part 1 — Expert

In the previous lesson we named the nine business functions and described what each one does. In this lesson we look at why they matter: how they depend on each other, how a business organises them, what happens when one fails, and how their role changes as a business grows. We continue with Khaya Wood Works, the fictional furniture workshop in Gqeberha, which makes school desks, chairs and tables and employs twelve people.

## Subtopic: How the Functions Depend on Each Other

No business function can succeed on its own. Each one relies on information, resources or results from the others, and each one provides something that the others need. This is called interdependence.

Consider an order of 200 desks from a school district. Marketing wins the order, but it can only promise a delivery date and a price if it knows what production can make and what the materials will cost. Production can only make the desks if purchasing has bought enough timber of the right quality, and if human resources has provided enough trained workers. Purchasing can only buy the timber if the financing function has made sure there is cash or credit available to pay the supplier. Financing, in turn, needs administration to provide accurate records of what is owed, what is due, and what is in the bank. Risk management must make sure that the workshop, the stock and the workers are protected while the order is being made. Public relations helps the business win such orders in the first place, by building a reputation for reliability. And general management coordinates all of this, setting the goal, allocating resources and resolving conflicts.

This chain of dependence means that the strength of the business depends on its weakest function. Excellent marketing is wasted if production cannot deliver on time. Brilliant production is wasted if nobody buys the product. A large, profitable order can still ruin the business if the financing function has not planned for the cash needed before the customer pays.

Interdependence also means that functions sometimes have conflicting goals, which management must balance. Marketing might want a wide range of designs and colours to attract customers; production prefers a few standard designs, because long runs of the same item are cheaper and faster to make. Purchasing might want to buy in bulk to get a discount; financing prefers smaller purchases, so that less cash is tied up in stock. Human resources might want to spend on training; financing wants to control costs. None of these goals is wrong. General management must weigh them against the overall objectives of the business and make a decision that serves the whole.

Good communication is what makes interdependence work. Regular meetings, shared plans, clear reports and accessible records allow each function to know what the others are doing and need. In a small business, much of this happens in conversation at the workbench. In a large business, it requires formal systems.

Interdependence also extends beyond the walls of the business. Each function links the business to outside stakeholders: purchasing to suppliers, marketing to customers, financing to banks and investors, human resources to workers and trade unions, public relations to the community and media, and administration and risk management to government bodies such as SARS and to insurers. When one function manages its outside relationship badly, for example when purchasing pays a supplier late, the effect can return to harm another function, such as production, when that supplier refuses to deliver urgently next time. Seeing the business as a connected system, both inside and outside, is one of the key ideas of this topic.

## Subtopic: The Organisational Structure and the Organogram

An organisational structure is the way a business arranges its people and functions: who does what, who reports to whom, and how decisions flow. It is usually shown in an organogram, also called an organisational chart, which is a diagram of boxes and lines. Each box represents a position or department, and the lines show the lines of authority and communication.

In a typical functional organogram, the owner or managing director is at the top. Below that are the managers of the main functions, such as the production manager, marketing manager, financial manager and human resources manager. Below each manager are the supervisors and workers in that department. The vertical lines show who reports to whom: a worker in the workshop reports to the workshop supervisor, who reports to the production manager, who reports to the managing director. This is called the chain of command.

An organogram shows several important things at a glance. It shows the levels of management: top, middle and lower. It shows the span of control, which is the number of people who report directly to one manager. A narrow span of control means a manager supervises only a few people closely; a wide span means a manager supervises many people with less direct supervision. It shows who has authority to make which decisions, and who is accountable for which results. It also shows the formal channels of communication.

Not every function needs its own box. In a medium-sized business, administration may be combined with finance, and public relations with marketing. Risk management may be the responsibility of the financial manager or of general management. The structure depends on the size and needs of the business.

For Khaya Wood Works, a simple organogram might show the owner at the top, acting as general manager and handling marketing and public relations personally. Reporting to the owner are a workshop foreman, responsible for production and quality, and an office administrator, responsible for administration, the books, purchasing orders and HR records. Nine workers report to the foreman. The owner's accountant, an outside firm, helps with financing and tax.

A clear structure has real benefits. Everyone knows their duties and their manager. Work is not duplicated or forgotten. Decisions are made at the right level. New employees can quickly see how the business works. When the business grows, the structure can be extended in a planned way rather than in a muddle.

## Subtopic: What Happens When a Function Fails

One of the best ways to understand the importance of each function is to imagine what happens when it fails. Here are examples from a business like Khaya Wood Works.

If general management fails, the business has no clear direction. Different departments pursue different goals, resources are wasted, and problems are noticed too late. A business might expand too quickly, accept orders it cannot complete, or fail to respond to a new competitor.

If administration fails, records are lost or wrong. The business does not know which customers have paid, which suppliers must be paid, or how much stock it has. Invoices are not sent, so money is not collected. Tax returns are late, leading to penalties from SARS. Managers make decisions based on wrong information.

If purchasing fails, materials arrive late, in the wrong quantity or of poor quality. Production stops or produces faulty goods. Alternatively, too much is bought, tying up cash and storage space.

If production fails, goods are faulty, late or too expensive to make. Customers return products, complain, and do not come back. Costs rise and profit falls.

If marketing fails, the business makes products nobody wants, sets the wrong price, or fails to reach customers. Sales fall even if the product is excellent.

If financing fails, the business runs out of cash. It cannot pay suppliers, wages or loan instalments, even if it is profitable on paper. In extreme cases, creditors can apply to court to have the business closed.

If human resources fails, the business struggles to find and keep skilled people. Workers are poorly trained, unmotivated or treated unfairly, which leads to mistakes, accidents, high staff turnover, disputes and possible cases at the Commission for Conciliation, Mediation and Arbitration, the CCMA.

If public relations fails, the business develops a poor reputation. Customers, suppliers and potential employees avoid it. A single badly handled complaint shared on social media can do serious damage.

If risk management fails, a single event, such as a fire, a theft, an injury or a customer who never pays, can destroy years of work, especially if the business is not insured.

Managers can use this kind of thinking before a problem occurs. A simple exercise is to list each function and ask two questions: what is the most likely way this function could fail, and what would the effect be on the rest of the business? The answers show where to spend time and money first. For Khaya Wood Works, the most likely serious failures might be a timber shortage, a key machine breaking down, or a large customer paying late. Each points to a practical action: keep a minimum stock of timber, service the machines regularly, and check customers' payment records before giving credit.

Notice that many failures spread. A purchasing failure becomes a production failure, which becomes a marketing and public relations failure when customers are disappointed, which becomes a financing failure when sales fall. That is interdependence working in reverse.

## Subtopic: Functions in Small and Large Businesses

All businesses perform all nine functions, but the way they perform them changes with size.

In a very small business, such as a spaza shop, a hair salon or a one-person furniture workshop, the owner usually performs almost every function personally. The owner plans, keeps the books, buys stock, serves customers, decides prices, chats to the community and worries about theft. Some functions may be performed informally, without written plans or separate records, and some may be outsourced, meaning paid for from an outside specialist: an accountant for tax, an insurance broker for risk, a printer for advertising flyers. The advantages are quick decisions, low overheads and close contact with customers. The disadvantages are that one person cannot be an expert at everything, the owner may be overloaded, and some functions, often administration, risk management and human resources, tend to be neglected until a problem arises.

As a business grows, functions are gradually separated and given to specialists. First, the owner may employ a bookkeeper, then a supervisor, then a salesperson. Eventually, a large company has a separate department for each major function, led by a manager with specialist training, and the top managers form an executive team. The advantages are expertise, efficiency and the capacity to handle large volumes of work. The disadvantages are higher costs, slower decisions, and the risk that departments become isolated and stop communicating well, which is sometimes called working in silos.

The role of general management therefore grows with the size of the business. In a small business, coordination happens naturally because one person does everything. In a large business, it requires deliberate effort: meetings, reports, shared goals, and managers who understand how their department affects others.

Some businesses grow in a different direction. Instead of creating departments for every function, they deliberately keep the business small and outsource more and more functions, using specialist firms for bookkeeping, payroll, delivery, marketing and even production. This can keep costs flexible and let the owner concentrate on what the business does best. But the functions still have to be performed, and the owner remains responsible for making sure that each outside provider does its job well.

For a Grade 9 learner planning a small business, such as the one you will plan in your business plan project, the lesson is clear. You will be responsible for all nine functions. Think about each one honestly, and plan how you will perform it, outsource it, or get help with it.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Let's make it simple. A business is like a soccer team. Each player has a position, and each position matters. A brilliant striker cannot win if the goalkeeper is missing. The business functions are the positions, and the manager is the coach.

## Subtopic: A Team, Not a List

The nine functions are not a list of separate jobs; they are a team. Marketing finds the customer. Purchasing buys the wood. HR finds the workers. Production makes the desks. Finance makes sure there is money for wood and wages. Administration keeps the records. Risk management keeps everything safe. Public relations keeps the good name. General management makes sure everyone pulls in the same direction.

Sometimes team members want different things. Marketing wants many colours to please customers. Production wants one colour because it is quicker. The manager decides what is best for the whole team. That is the job of general management.

Talking to each other is what makes the team work. If marketing promises 200 desks by Friday without asking production, the business has a problem.

## Subtopic: Drawing a Simple Organogram

An organogram is a picture of who works where and who reports to whom. Draw a box at the top for the owner. Below it, draw boxes for the people who report to the owner, such as the workshop foreman and the office administrator. Below the foreman, draw boxes for the workers. Join them with lines.

The lines show the chain of command: a worker reports to the foreman, and the foreman reports to the owner. The number of people who report to one person is called the span of control. If nine workers report to the foreman, the foreman's span of control is nine.

An organogram helps everyone know their job and their boss, so that work is not forgotten or done twice. When the business grows, you simply add new boxes, such as a salesperson reporting to the owner, and the picture shows clearly how the new person fits in. Try drawing one for your school: who is at the top, and who reports to whom?

## Subtopic: One Weak Link

A chain is only as strong as its weakest link. A business is the same. Imagine the wood arrives late. Production stops. The desks are late. The school is unhappy and tells other schools. Fewer orders come in. Money gets tight. One small purchasing problem has turned into a big business problem.

That is why every function matters, even the ones that seem boring. A good owner looks for the weak link before it breaks. Keeping records, checking insurance and training workers do not bring in money directly, but without them the business can collapse.

In a small business, one person does all nine jobs, so the owner must be honest about which jobs they are weak at and get help early. That help might be an accountant, an insurance broker or a friend who is good at marketing. Practise by listing a small business you know and saying who does each function there.
