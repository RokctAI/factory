### subtopic_1 — How the Functions Depend on Each Other
**Assistant:** If departments have conflicting goals, isn't one of them simply wrong?

**Tutor:** Usually not. Each department's goal is sensible from its own point of view, and the conflict arises because the business has limited resources and several objectives at once. Take marketing and production. Marketing is right that customers like choice: offering desks in three heights and four colours may win more orders. Production is right that variety costs money: every change of design means resetting machines, buying more kinds of material and training workers on more procedures, which raises the cost of each desk and slows production. Both are describing real effects. The question is not which department is right, but which balance best serves the overall goals of the business. If Khaya Wood Works aims to be the cheapest supplier to government schools, production's argument should probably win, and the range should be kept small. If it aims to supply premium furniture to private schools that pay more for choice, marketing's argument may win. That is why general management is needed: it sets the overall goals and makes the trade-offs in light of them. It also means that good managers in each department must understand the other departments' goals, rather than defending their own at all costs. A purchasing manager who insists on bulk buying without considering the cash position may save a little on price while putting the whole business at risk. A business works best when each function pursues excellence in its own area while keeping the goals of the whole in mind. In exams, a strong answer to a question about interdependence often names two functions, explains what each needs from the other, and shows how management resolves any conflict between them.

### subtopic_2 — The Organisational Structure and the Organogram
**Assistant:** Is a wide span of control better or worse than a narrow one?

**Tutor:** Neither is always better; each suits different situations, and the right choice depends on the work and the people. A narrow span of control, where a manager supervises only a few people, allows close supervision, more guidance and quick help with problems. It suits complex or risky work, inexperienced workers, or tasks that change often. Its disadvantages are that it needs more managers, which costs more, and it creates more levels in the organisation, which can slow down communication and decision-making, because messages pass through more people. A wide span of control, where a manager supervises many people, needs fewer managers and gives a flatter structure with quicker communication. Workers usually have more independence, which can motivate them. But it only works if the work is fairly routine, the workers are skilled and reliable, and the manager is not overloaded. If a manager with a span of twenty must also handle purchasing and customer complaints, workers may not get the guidance they need, and mistakes may go unnoticed. At Khaya Wood Works, the foreman has a span of control of nine. That is reasonable for experienced woodworkers doing familiar tasks. But if the business hired several new apprentices who must learn to use dangerous machines, the foreman might need to appoint a senior worker as an assistant supervisor, reducing the number who report directly to the foreman. An organogram makes these decisions visible: you can see at a glance whether a manager has too many or too few people reporting to them, and adjust the structure as the business changes.

### subtopic_3 — What Happens When a Function Fails
**Assistant:** Which function is the most important?

**Tutor:** It is tempting to name one, but the honest answer is that no single function is most important, because the business needs all of them, and the weakest one tends to set the limit on what the business can achieve. You could argue for marketing, because without customers there is no business. You could argue for financing, because a business without cash closes immediately, however good its product. You could argue for general management, because it coordinates everything else. Each argument has some truth. But imagine a business with excellent marketing and finance, and no attention to risk management: a single uninsured fire could end it. Or excellent production and marketing, and very poor administration: it could sell a great deal and never collect the money, or be shut down for ignoring tax obligations. The importance of a function also changes over time. When a business starts, marketing and financing are often the most urgent, because the owner must find customers and start-up money. As it grows, human resources and administration become critical, because more people and more transactions need managing. In a crisis, such as a product recall, public relations and risk management become most visible. So rather than ranking the functions, a good manager regularly asks: which function is currently our weakest, and what would happen if it failed? Strengthening that function usually brings the greatest benefit. In an exam answer, it is fine to argue that one function is especially important in a particular situation, as long as you explain why with an example and acknowledge that all nine are needed.

### subtopic_4 — Functions in Small and Large Businesses
**Assistant:** Why do small business owners often neglect administration and risk management?

**Tutor:** Mainly because these functions do not bring in money directly and their benefits are invisible until something goes wrong, while the owner faces urgent demands from customers every day. A small business owner works long hours making products, serving customers and chasing sales, and those activities produce immediate, visible results: a sale, a happy customer, money in the till. Recording transactions, filing invoices, checking insurance policies and assessing safety risks feel like paperwork that takes time away from earning. So they are postponed. The problem is that the cost of neglect builds up quietly. Without records, the owner cannot tell which products are profitable, which customers owe money, or how much tax is due. Debtors are not followed up, so cash is lost. A SARS audit or a loan application becomes very difficult. Without risk management, the business may operate for years without insurance or safety measures, and then lose everything in one fire, theft or accident. Many small businesses in South Africa fail within their first few years, and poor record-keeping and lack of planning are among the common reasons. The practical solutions are straightforward. The owner can set aside a fixed time each week for administration, use simple tools such as a cash book or an inexpensive accounting app, outsource bookkeeping and tax to an accountant, and speak to an insurance broker about the main risks. These steps cost something, but much less than the losses they prevent. When you write your business plan, include them deliberately, so that your plan covers all nine functions and not just the exciting ones.
