### subtopic_1 — General Management and Administration
**Assistant:** Why is general management listed as a separate function if every manager already manages?

**Tutor:** Because someone has to look at the business as a whole and make sure all the parts work in the same direction. Every department head does plan, organise, lead and control within their own area: the production manager plans production, the marketing manager plans campaigns. But each of them naturally sees the business through the lens of their own function. Production may want to make long batches of one product, because that is efficient for the workshop. Marketing may want many different designs, because that attracts customers. Finance may want to hold very little stock, to save cash, while purchasing may want to buy in bulk to get a discount. These goals conflict, and if nobody settles the conflicts, the business pulls in several directions at once. General management sets the overall goals and strategy, allocates resources between departments, and resolves conflicts in the interest of the whole business. It also represents the business to its owners and to the outside world. In Khaya Wood Works, the owner might decide that the strategy for next year is to specialise in school furniture. That decision then guides every function: purchasing buys timber suited to desks, production organises batch work, marketing targets principals and school governing bodies, and finance plans for the slower payments typical of schools. Without that central direction, each department might be doing a good job of the wrong thing. Administration supports general management by providing the information it needs. A manager cannot control what is not measured, so accurate, timely records of sales, costs, stock and cash are what make good decisions possible. That is why administration and general management are so closely linked.

### subtopic_2 — Purchasing, Production and Marketing
**Assistant:** Why is marketing described as much more than advertising?

**Tutor:** Because advertising is only one tool within one of the four Ps, promotion, while marketing is the whole process of understanding customers and meeting their needs profitably. Think about what would happen if a business advertised brilliantly but got the other parts wrong. If the product did not meet the customer's needs, for example school desks that were too high for younger learners, the advertising would only spread bad news faster. If the price were too high compared with competitors, customers would see the advertisement and buy elsewhere. If the place were wrong, for example if the business could not deliver to rural schools, customers who wanted to buy could not. So marketing starts long before any advertisement. It begins with market research: who are our customers, what do they need, how much can they pay, who are our competitors, and what do they offer? Then it shapes the product itself, sets a price that covers costs and attracts buyers, chooses how the product reaches customers, and only then decides how to promote it. Marketing also continues after the sale, through customer service and gathering feedback to improve the product. For Khaya Wood Works, good marketing might reveal that schools care more about durability than style, and that they buy at the start of the financial year. That information could change the product design, the timing of sales visits and the message in any promotion. A good way to remember it is that advertising tries to sell what you have made, while marketing tries to make sure you make what customers will want to buy. Both matter, but marketing comes first.

### subtopic_3 — Financing, Human Resources and Public Relations
**Assistant:** How can a profitable business still run out of cash?

**Tutor:** Because profit and cash are measured differently, and the timing of money coming in and going out can be very different from the timing of sales and expenses. You saw this with credit transactions this year. When Khaya Wood Works sells desks to a school on credit, the sale and the profit are recorded on the day the desks are delivered, but the cash may only arrive sixty days later. Meanwhile, the business has to pay its timber supplier within thirty days and pay wages every week or month. If it grows quickly, taking on many large orders, it may need to buy much more timber and pay more wages long before the schools pay. On paper, the business is making excellent profits; in its bank account, there may be nothing left. If it cannot pay its suppliers or workers, they may stop supplying or working, and a profitable business can be forced to close. That is why the financing function plans cash flow carefully. It prepares a cash budget, a forecast of when money will come in and go out, so it can see a shortage coming. It can then arrange an overdraft or loan in advance, ask customers for a deposit, negotiate longer payment terms with suppliers, or follow up firmly with debtors who pay late. Buying a large machine with cash can also create a shortage, even if the machine will be profitable over several years. A useful saying in business is that turnover is vanity, profit is sanity, and cash is reality. All three matter, but a business that ignores cash can fail even while its profit figures look healthy. The financing function exists to stop that from happening.

### subtopic_4 — Risk Management
**Assistant:** Why can't a business simply insure against every risk?

**Tutor:** Because insurance only works for certain kinds of risk, and even for those, it has a cost that must be weighed against the benefit. Insurance works by pooling. Many businesses each pay a relatively small premium, and the insurer uses that pool to pay the few who suffer a loss. For this to work, the risk must be one that can be measured: the insurer must be able to estimate how often fires or thefts happen and how much they cost, so that it can set a fair premium. Risks like fire, theft, accidental damage, injury to the public and vehicle accidents fit that pattern, so they can be insured. But many business risks cannot be measured in that way. No insurer will cover the risk that customers stop wanting your product, that a competitor offers a better design, or that you make poor management decisions. Those are the normal risks of being in business, and the owner carries them in exchange for the chance of earning profit. Insurance can also be too expensive for a small risk. If it would cost more in premiums than the likely loss, it may be wiser to accept the risk and set some money aside. Insurers also expect the business to manage its risks: if Khaya Wood Works stores timber next to an open flame and has no fire extinguishers, an insurer may refuse to pay, or charge a much higher premium. That is why risk management combines several approaches. A business avoids some risks, reduces many through good controls, transfers some through insurance, and accepts the rest consciously. The skill lies in choosing the right approach for each risk rather than relying on any single one.
