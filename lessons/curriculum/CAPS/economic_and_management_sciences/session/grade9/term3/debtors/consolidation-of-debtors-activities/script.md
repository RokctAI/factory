# Part 1 — Expert

In Term 2 you learnt to record credit sales in the debtors journal and to keep a debtors ledger. In this lesson we consolidate that work by following the complete debtors cycle for a whole month, from the opening balances to the debtors list, and we add the management side: how a business keeps its debtors under control. We continue with Ridgeview Hardware, a sole trader that sells building supplies at a mark-up of 50 percent on cost.

## Subtopic: The Full Debtors Cycle

A debtor is a person or business that owes money to the business because it bought goods on credit. Debtors are a current asset, because the money is expected to come in within a short time, usually 30 days. Every activity involving debtors fits into the accounting cycle that you already know: source document, journal, ledger, trial balance.

There are two kinds of debtor transactions in Grade 9. The first is a credit sale. The source document is the duplicate invoice. It is recorded in the debtors journal, the DJ, with the selling price in the Sales column and the cost price in the Cost of sales column. Each line is posted to the debit side of the debtor's account in the debtors ledger, and at the end of the month the totals are posted to the general ledger: Debtors control is debited and Sales is credited with the sales total, and Cost of sales is debited and Trading stock credited with the cost total.

The second kind is a payment received from a debtor. The source document is the duplicate receipt. It is recorded in the cash receipts journal, the CRJ, in the Bank column and in a special column headed Debtors control. Each amount is posted to the credit side of the debtor's account in the debtors ledger, and at the end of the month the Debtors control column total is posted to the credit side of Debtors control in the general ledger, with the details Bank.

So two journals feed the debtors records: the DJ increases what debtors owe, and the CRJ decreases it. The debtors ledger holds the detail, one account per debtor, and Debtors control in the general ledger holds the total. At the end of the month the two must agree.

It helps to see this as a loop. Ridgeview sells on credit, the debtor's balance increases, Ridgeview sends a monthly statement, the debtor pays, and the balance decreases. A well-run business keeps this loop turning smoothly, so that credit sales become cash within the agreed credit period. If the loop gets stuck, because debtors do not pay, the business may show a profit on paper while running out of money in the bank.

Remember the two questions that sort every transaction. Did money come in today? Then the transaction belongs in the CRJ. Was something sold today with payment later? Then it belongs in the DJ.

## Subtopic: Opening Balances and July's Credit Sales

On 1 July, Ridgeview's debtors list showed: Hilltop Builders R4 800, Ace Plumbing R2 100 and Sunset Guest House R1 500. Total 4 800 + 2 100 + 1 500 = R8 400. This was also the debit balance of Debtors control in the general ledger, brought down from June.

In each debtor's account in the debtors ledger, the opening amount is written on the first line with the date 1 July, the details Balance, and the amount in the balance column. In the general ledger, Debtors control shows 1 July, Balance b/d, R8 400, on the debit side.

Ridgeview's credit sales in July were as follows, all at a mark-up of 50 percent on cost. On 4 July, invoice 141 to Hilltop Builders for R3 900. On 11 July, invoice 142 to Sunset Guest House for R2 400. On 16 July, invoice 143 to Ace Plumbing for R4 200. On 23 July, invoice 144 to Hilltop Builders for R1 500.

Before recording, calculate each cost price by multiplying the selling price by 100 and dividing by 150. Invoice 141: 3 900 × 100 ÷ 150 = 2 600. Invoice 142: 2 400 × 100 ÷ 150 = 1 600. Invoice 143: 4 200 × 100 ÷ 150 = 2 800. Invoice 144: 1 500 × 100 ÷ 150 = 1 000. Check one forwards: 2 600 plus half of 2 600, which is 1 300, gives 3 900.

The DJ for July, page DJ7, has four lines in date order, one per invoice. Totals: Sales 3 900 + 2 400 + 4 200 + 1 500 = R12 000; Cost of sales 2 600 + 1 600 + 2 800 + 1 000 = R8 000. As a check, 12 000 × 100 ÷ 150 = 8 000, and the gross profit on July's credit sales is 12 000 - 8 000 = R4 000.

Notice that the opening balances do not appear in the DJ at all. The DJ records only this month's credit sales. The opening balances are already in the ledger accounts, carried forward from June. A common error is to add the opening balance to the DJ total; that would count the old sales a second time.

The four lines are posted, one by one, to the debit side of each debtor's account: Hilltop receives invoices 141 and 144, Sunset receives invoice 142 and Ace receives invoice 143. The DJ totals are posted to the general ledger: Debtors control debit R12 000, details Sales, folio DJ7; Sales credit R12 000; Cost of sales debit R8 000; Trading stock credit R8 000.

## Subtopic: Receipts from Debtors in the CRJ

In July, three debtors paid Ridgeview. On 10 July, Hilltop Builders paid R4 800 by EFT, which settled its opening balance; Ridgeview issued receipt 061. On 18 July, Ace Plumbing paid R2 100, its opening balance, receipt 062. On 25 July, Sunset Guest House paid R1 000 of the R3 900 it owed by then, receipt 063.

Each receipt is recorded in the CRJ on its own line: the receipt number, the day, the debtor's name in the details column, the amount in the Bank column, and the same amount in the Debtors control column. Nothing is written in the Sales or Cost of sales columns, because no goods were sold at the time of the payment; the sale was recorded earlier, in the DJ. This is one of the most common errors in tests: recording a debtor's payment as a sale counts the income twice.

The Debtors control column of the CRJ totals 4 800 + 2 100 + 1 000 = R7 900. This total is posted to the credit side of Debtors control in the general ledger, with the details Bank and the folio of the CRJ page. The Bank column total, which includes these receipts as well as all cash sales and other receipts, is posted to the debit side of the Bank account as before.

Each individual payment is also posted to the credit side of the debtor's account in the debtors ledger, with the details Receipt and the receipt number, and the folio CRJ7. The debtor's folio is then written in the folio column of the CRJ next to the line, to show that it has been posted.

Notice that a debtor can pay part of what it owes, as Sunset did. Partial payments are common, and the running balance in the debtors ledger shows exactly what is still outstanding. It is also normal for a debtor to pay for an old invoice while new invoices are added, which is why the dates matter: entries in a debtor's account are written in date order, mixing DJ and CRJ entries, so that the balance is correct on every day.

Payments from debtors have no effect on the profit. They simply change one asset, debtors, into another asset, bank. The income was earned on the day of the sale.

## Subtopic: Debtors Ledger, Control Account and Debtors List

Now we can complete the debtors ledger. Hilltop Builders, D1: 1 July balance 4 800; 4 July invoice 141 debit 3 900, balance 8 700; 10 July receipt 061 credit 4 800, balance 3 900; 23 July invoice 144 debit 1 500, balance 5 400. Ace Plumbing, D2: 1 July balance 2 100; 16 July invoice 143 debit 4 200, balance 6 300; 18 July receipt 062 credit 2 100, balance 4 200. Sunset Guest House, D3: 1 July balance 1 500; 11 July invoice 142 debit 2 400, balance 3 900; 25 July receipt 063 credit 1 000, balance 2 900.

The debtors list at 31 July is Hilltop R5 400, Ace R4 200 and Sunset R2 900, a total of 5 400 + 4 200 + 2 900 = R12 500.

In the general ledger, Debtors control shows: debit side, balance b/d 8 400 and sales 12 000; credit side, bank 7 900. The balance is 8 400 + 12 000 - 7 900 = R12 500, a debit balance carried down to August. The debtors list and the control account agree, so the debtors records are in order.

If they do not agree, the bookkeeper must look for the error. Typical causes are a line from the DJ or CRJ not posted to the debtors ledger, a payment posted to the debit side instead of the credit side, an amount posted to the wrong debtor's account, which does not change the total but makes two accounts wrong, or an amount copied incorrectly. If R2 900 were written as R2 090, the difference would be R810; a difference that divides exactly by 9, as 810 does, often points to two digits swapped.

The debtors list is also a management tool. Ridgeview can compare each balance with the debtor's credit limit before allowing another credit sale. It sends each debtor a monthly statement, a document listing the opening balance, the invoices, the payments and the amount due, which reminds the debtor to pay and gives a chance to query errors. It watches how long each balance has been outstanding, because the older a debt becomes, the less likely it is to be paid. Ridgeview's terms are 30 days, so a balance from June that is still unpaid in August would be followed up by phone, then in writing, and credit would be stopped until it is paid.

Managing debtors well is a balance. Generous credit attracts customers and increases sales, but money tied up in debtors cannot be used to pay suppliers or wages, and some debtors may never pay. A business must sell on credit only to customers who have been checked, set sensible limits and collect on time.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Let's make this simple. When a shop sells on credit, three things happen over and over: it sells, it writes down who owes what, and it collects the money. This lesson puts all three together for one month.

## Subtopic: Sell, Record, Collect

Let's follow the money. Ridgeview sells to a builder on credit. It writes an invoice and records it in the debtors journal, the book for credit sales. The builder now owes Ridgeview money, so the builder is a debtor.

Later the builder pays. Ridgeview writes a receipt and records it in the cash receipts journal, the book for money coming in, in a special column called Debtors control.

So the DJ makes debtors go up, and the CRJ makes debtors go down. In July, Ridgeview's DJ adds up to R12 000 of credit sales, which cost R8 000. Its CRJ shows R7 900 received from debtors.

A payment is not a new sale. The sale already happened. Never write a debtor's payment in the Sales column.

## Subtopic: Every Customer's Story

Each customer has a page in the debtors ledger that tells their story for the month. Start with what they owed on 1 July. Add each invoice. Subtract each payment. Write the new balance after every line.

Hilltop: owed 4 800. Bought 3 900, now 8 700. Paid 4 800, now 3 900. Bought 1 500, now 5 400.

Ace: owed 2 100. Bought 4 200, now 6 300. Paid 2 100, now 4 200.

Sunset: owed 1 500. Bought 2 400, now 3 900. Paid only 1 000, now 2 900.

Every page tells you exactly who owes what, which is what you need when you phone someone to ask for payment.

## Subtopic: One Total, Two Proofs

Now the big check. Add the pages: 5 400 + 4 200 + 2 900 = 12 500. That is the debtors list.

Now look at Debtors control in the general ledger. It started at 8 400, went up by the DJ total of 12 000 and down by the CRJ total of 7 900. 8 400 + 12 000 - 7 900 = 12 500. Same answer. The records are right.

If the answers are different, something was missed or put on the wrong side. Find it before you go on.

Finally, remember why this matters. A sale on credit is only finished when the money arrives. So send statements every month, keep customers within their limits and phone them when they are late. Practise by drawing up one debtor's page from start to finish.
