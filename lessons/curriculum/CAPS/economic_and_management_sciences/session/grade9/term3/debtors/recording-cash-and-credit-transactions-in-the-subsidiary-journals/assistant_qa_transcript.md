### subtopic_1 — The Subsidiary Journals of a Sole Trader
**Assistant:** Why does a business use several subsidiary journals instead of one big journal?

**Tutor:** Because grouping similar transactions saves a great deal of work and reduces errors. If every transaction were written in one general journal, each one would have to be posted separately to the general ledger, so twelve transactions would mean at least twenty-four postings, and a busy shop with hundreds of transactions a month would need thousands. In a subsidiary journal, transactions of the same kind share the same columns, so the bookkeeper can total each column and post only the totals. For example, two cash sales in the CRJ become one posting of R10 500 to Sales and one of R7 000 to Cost of sales. Subsidiary journals also allow work to be divided: one person can handle receipts and another payments, which is both faster and a form of internal control, because no single person handles all the money. And they make information easy to find. If the owner wants to know how much was received from debtors in August, the total of one column, R9 600, gives the answer immediately. Only unusual items go in the Sundry accounts column, and those are posted individually.

### subtopic_2 — Which Journal? Sorting August's Transactions
**Assistant:** How should a bookkeeper decide where a trading stock purchase belongs?

**Tutor:** By asking how it was paid for, not what was bought. Trading stock can be bought in two ways. If Ridgeview pays immediately, by EFT, cheque or cash, money leaves the bank on that day, so the transaction belongs in the cash payments journal, in the Bank and Trading stock columns. That was the case on 12 August, when stock of R5 000 was paid for by EFT. If Ridgeview buys the stock on credit and pays the supplier later, no money leaves the bank on the day of purchase, and the transaction belongs in a different journal, the creditors journal, which you will learn next. The same reasoning applies to sales. A cash sale is recorded in the CRJ because money comes in, and a credit sale in the DJ because it does not. Learners often see the words trading stock and reach for a stock-related book without thinking. The safest habit is always to ask the money questions first: did money come in, did money go out, or is payment to follow? The answer determines the journal; the item bought or sold only determines the column.

### subtopic_3 — Recording and Totalling the CRJ and CPJ
**Assistant:** Why is the cost of sales recorded in the CRJ if it is not money received?

**Tutor:** Because the CRJ is where the cash sale is recorded, and every sale of trading stock has two parts: the selling price, which is income, and the cost price, which is an expense. Ridgeview uses a perpetual inventory system, which means it records the cost of goods sold at the moment of each sale, so the Trading stock account always shows the cost of the goods that should be on the shelves. When cash sales of R4 500 are recorded, the goods that left the shop cost R3 000, and that cost must be recorded somewhere. The simplest place is on the same line, in a Cost of sales column, so that the two halves of the sale cannot be separated. But because the cost of sales is not money received, it is not part of the Bank column, and it is left out of the cross-cast. At month end its total of R7 000 is posted to the debit side of Cost of sales and the credit side of Trading stock, neither of which involves the bank. The DJ works the same way, with a Cost of sales column next to its Sales column.

### subtopic_4 — The DJ and Checking the Whole Month
**Assistant:** What is the purpose of cross-casting a journal?

**Tutor:** Cross-casting checks that the journal is internally consistent before its totals are posted. In the CRJ, every amount in the Bank column must be analysed in at least one of the money columns that explain it: Sales, Debtors control or Sundry accounts. So the sum of those analysis column totals must equal the Bank total. For August, 10 500 plus 9 600 plus 2 500 equals 22 600, which agrees. In the CPJ, the analysis columns are Trading stock, Wages and Sundry accounts, and 5 000 plus 2 800 plus 1 650 equals 9 450, which also agrees. If the cross-cast does not agree, there is an error, perhaps an amount entered in the Bank column but not analysed, an amount analysed in two columns, or a mistake in adding a column. Finding it at this stage is important, because the totals are posted to the general ledger, and an error in a total would then appear in the trial balance, where it is much harder to trace. A cross-cast does not prove that every entry is in the right column, though. Rent income entered as sales would still cross-cast correctly.

### subtopic_5 — Three Questions, Three Books
**Assistant:** Why are drawings recorded in the Sundry accounts column and not as an expense?

**Tutor:** Because drawings are not a cost of running the business. An expense is a cost incurred to earn income, such as wages paid to staff who serve customers, or bank charges for using the bank account. When the owner of a sole trader withdraws R1 500 for personal use, the money leaves the business but does nothing to earn income; it simply returns part of the owner's investment to the owner. In the accounting equation, assets decrease and owner's equity decreases, but the decrease in owner's equity is not caused by an expense, so the profit is not affected. That is why Drawings has its own account in the owner's equity section of the general ledger, and why it is never included in the calculation of profit. In the CPJ, there is usually no special column for drawings, because they do not happen often enough, so the amount goes in the Sundry accounts column with the account name Drawings written in the details. At month end it is posted individually to the debit side of Drawings. Bank charges, which are an expense, are entered in the Sundry column in the same way.

### subtopic_6 — Money In, Money Out
**Assistant:** How can the cash journals tell us how much the bank balance changed?

**Tutor:** Every rand that came into the bank in August is in the Bank column of the CRJ, and every rand that went out is in the Bank column of the CPJ. So the difference between the two Bank totals is the change in the bank balance for the month: R22 600 received minus R9 450 paid gives an increase of R13 150. If the bank balance on 1 August was known, adding R13 150 would give the balance on 31 August, which must agree with the Bank account in the general ledger after posting. This is also why the bank statement matters. Items such as bank charges and interest appear on the bank statement before the business knows about them, so they must be recorded in the journals from the statement. Otherwise, the business's records would show more money than the bank does. In Grade 10 you will compare the business's records with the bank statement in a bank reconciliation. For now, the important idea is that the cash journals together give a complete picture of the money that moved, while the DJ shows sales for which money is still to come.

### subtopic_7 — Promises to Pay
**Assistant:** Why does August's gross profit of R6 000 include credit sales not yet paid for?

**Tutor:** Because in accounting, income is recorded when it is earned, not when the money is received. Ridgeview earned the income from a credit sale on the day the goods were delivered and the invoice issued, because at that moment it had done everything it needed to do, and the customer became legally obliged to pay. So the R7 500 of credit sales belongs to August, together with its cost of R5 000, even though some of the money will arrive only in September. Total sales were 10 500 plus 7 500, which is R18 000; total cost of sales was 7 000 plus 5 000, which is R12 000; and the gross profit was R6 000. This principle has an important consequence: profit and cash are not the same thing. A business that sells a lot on credit can report a healthy profit while its bank balance is low, because its money is tied up in debtors. That is why managing debtors carefully, collecting on time and watching the bank balance, is as important as making sales. In August, Ridgeview actually collected more from debtors than it sold to them on credit, which is a healthy sign.
