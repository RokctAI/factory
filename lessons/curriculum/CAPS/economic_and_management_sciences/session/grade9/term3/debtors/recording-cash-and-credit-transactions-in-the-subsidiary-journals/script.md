# Part 1 — Expert

In earlier lessons you learnt each journal on its own. In practice, a bookkeeper does not receive transactions sorted by journal: invoices, receipts, EFT confirmations and bank statements arrive mixed together, and the first skill is to decide where each one belongs. In this lesson we record a full month of transactions of Ridgeview Hardware, a sole trader selling building supplies at a mark-up of 50 percent on cost, in its three subsidiary journals.

## Subtopic: The Subsidiary Journals of a Sole Trader

A subsidiary journal is a book of first entry in which similar transactions are recorded together, in date order, before they are posted to the ledger. Grouping similar transactions saves work: instead of posting every transaction separately to the general ledger, the bookkeeper posts the column totals once a month. So far you have used three subsidiary journals.

The cash receipts journal, the CRJ, records all money received by the business. Its source documents are duplicate receipts for money from debtors and other receipts, and the cash register roll for cash sales. Its money columns are Bank, Sales, Cost of sales, Debtors control and Sundry accounts. There may also be an Analysis of receipts column, used when several receipts are banked together on one deposit, so that the Bank column shows the total deposit.

The cash payments journal, the CPJ, records all money paid out by the business. Its source documents are cheque counterfoils, EFT proofs of payment and, for bank charges and interest, the bank statement. Its money columns are Bank, Trading stock, Wages, Creditors control, which you will meet in the next lessons, and Sundry accounts.

The debtors journal, the DJ, records all credit sales of trading stock. Its source document is the duplicate invoice, and its money columns are Sales and Cost of sales.

In a sole trader the owner is the business's only owner, so transactions with the owner also appear in these journals. When the owner contributes capital in money, it goes in the CRJ, in the Sundry column, to the Capital account. When the owner withdraws money for personal use, it goes in the CPJ, in the Sundry column, to the Drawings account. Drawings are not an expense; they reduce owner's equity directly, because the money is used for the owner, not to earn income for the business.

The important rule is that each transaction is recorded in only one journal. A cash sale goes in the CRJ, not the DJ as well. A payment from a debtor goes in the CRJ, not the DJ. Recording one transaction in two journals would count it twice when the journals are posted.

## Subtopic: Which Journal? Sorting August's Transactions

Here are Ridgeview's transactions for August. Read each one and decide where it belongs before we record anything.

On 2 August, cash sales according to the cash register roll were R4 500. On 4 August, Ridgeview sold goods on credit to Hilltop Builders, invoice 151, R3 000. On 6 August, wages of R2 800 were paid by EFT. On 9 August, Ace Plumbing paid R4 200 in settlement of its account, receipt 064. On 12 August, Ridgeview bought trading stock for R5 000 and paid by EFT. On 15 August, goods were sold on credit to Sunset Guest House, invoice 152, R1 800. On 18 August, rent income of R2 500 was received from a tenant for a storeroom, receipt 065. On 21 August, goods were sold on credit to Ace Plumbing, invoice 153, R2 700. On 24 August, the owner withdrew R1 500 by EFT for personal use. On 27 August, Hilltop Builders paid R5 400, receipt 066. On 30 August, cash sales were R6 000. On 31 August, the bank statement showed bank charges of R150.

Now sort them with three questions. Did money come into the bank? Then the CRJ: the cash sales of 2 and 30 August, Ace's payment, the rent income and Hilltop's payment, five transactions. Did money go out of the bank? Then the CPJ: the wages, the trading stock bought by EFT, the owner's drawings and the bank charges, four transactions. Was trading stock sold with payment to follow? Then the DJ: invoices 151, 152 and 153, three transactions. Twelve transactions, twelve homes, no duplicates.

Some transactions need a second look. The trading stock bought on 12 August was paid for immediately by EFT, so it is a cash purchase and goes in the CPJ, even though it involves trading stock. The bank charges are a payment even though Ridgeview did not write anything; the bank took the money, and the bank statement is the source document. Ace's payment on 9 August and its new purchase on 21 August are separate transactions in different journals: the payment reduces what Ace owes, the purchase increases it.

The cost prices of the sales are calculated at a mark-up of 50 percent on cost, by multiplying by 100 and dividing by 150. Cash sales of R4 500 cost R3 000 and cash sales of R6 000 cost R4 000. Invoice 151 for R3 000 cost R2 000, invoice 152 for R1 800 cost R1 200, and invoice 153 for R2 700 cost R1 800.

## Subtopic: Recording and Totalling the CRJ and CPJ

The CRJ for August, page CRJ8, has five lines. On 2 August: cash register roll, details Cash sales, Bank 4 500, Sales 4 500, Cost of sales 3 000. On 9 August: receipt 064, Ace Plumbing, Bank 4 200, Debtors control 4 200. On 18 August: receipt 065, details Rent income, Bank 2 500, Sundry accounts 2 500 with the details Rent income. On 27 August: receipt 066, Hilltop Builders, Bank 5 400, Debtors control 5 400. On 30 August: cash register roll, Cash sales, Bank 6 000, Sales 6 000, Cost of sales 4 000.

The totals are: Bank 4 500 + 4 200 + 2 500 + 5 400 + 6 000 = R22 600; Sales 4 500 + 6 000 = R10 500; Cost of sales 3 000 + 4 000 = R7 000; Debtors control 4 200 + 5 400 = R9 600; Sundry accounts R2 500.

Now cross-cast. The Bank total must equal the sum of the columns that make up each receipt: Sales plus Debtors control plus Sundry accounts. 10 500 + 9 600 + 2 500 = 22 600. It agrees. Cost of sales is left out of the cross-cast, because it is not money received; it is a memorandum of the cost of the goods sold, recorded in the CRJ only because the cost is known at the moment of the sale.

The CPJ for August, page CPJ8, has four lines. On 6 August: EFT, Wages, Bank 2 800, Wages 2 800. On 12 August: EFT, the supplier's name, Bank 5 000, Trading stock 5 000. On 24 August: EFT, the owner's name, Bank 1 500, Sundry accounts 1 500, details Drawings. On 31 August: bank statement, details Bank charges, Bank 150, Sundry accounts 150, details Bank charges.

The totals are: Bank 2 800 + 5 000 + 1 500 + 150 = R9 450; Trading stock R5 000; Wages R2 800; Sundry accounts 1 500 + 150 = R1 650. Cross-cast: 5 000 + 2 800 + 1 650 = 9 450, which agrees with the Bank total.

Together, the cash journals show that the bank balance increased during August by 22 600 - 9 450 = R13 150. That figure will be confirmed when the Bank account in the general ledger is balanced.

A cross-cast that does not agree means that an amount has been entered in the Bank column without being analysed, or in an analysis column without the Bank column, or that a column was added incorrectly. It must be corrected before posting, because the totals are what will be posted.

## Subtopic: The DJ and Checking the Whole Month

The DJ for August, page DJ8, has three lines. On 4 August, invoice 151, Hilltop Builders, Sales 3 000, Cost of sales 2 000. On 15 August, invoice 152, Sunset Guest House, Sales 1 800, Cost of sales 1 200. On 21 August, invoice 153, Ace Plumbing, Sales 2 700, Cost of sales 1 800. Totals: Sales 3 000 + 1 800 + 2 700 = R7 500; Cost of sales 2 000 + 1 200 + 1 800 = R5 000. Check: 7 500 × 100 ÷ 150 = 5 000.

Now check the debtors. On 1 August the debtors list showed Hilltop R5 400, Ace R4 200 and Sunset R2 900, a total of R12 500. The DJ adds R7 500 and the Debtors control column of the CRJ subtracts R9 600. Debtors control at 31 August is therefore 12 500 + 7 500 - 9 600 = R10 400.

The debtors ledger gives the detail. Hilltop: 5 400 + 3 000 - 5 400 = R3 000. Ace: 4 200 - 4 200 + 2 700 = R2 700. Sunset: 2 900 + 1 800 = R4 700. Debtors list total: 3 000 + 2 700 + 4 700 = R10 400. It agrees with the control account.

We can also see the month's total sales and gross profit from the journals. Cash sales in the CRJ were R10 500 and credit sales in the DJ were R7 500, so total sales were 10 500 + 7 500 = R18 000. Total cost of sales was 7 000 + 5 000 = R12 000. Gross profit for August was 18 000 - 12 000 = R6 000. Notice that the gross profit includes credit sales whose money has not yet been received, because income is recorded when it is earned, not when the money arrives.

Finally, a bookkeeper should check the month as a whole. Have all the source documents been recorded, with no gaps in the numbered sequences of invoices and receipts? Have the bank statement items, such as bank charges, been recorded? Is each transaction in exactly one journal? Have all three journals been totalled, and have the cash journals been cross-cast? Only then is the month ready to post to the general ledger, which is where these totals go next.

These skills are tested often, usually by giving a list of mixed transactions and asking you to record them in the correct journals. The most frequent errors are recording a debtor's payment as a sale, putting a cash purchase of stock in a journal other than the CPJ, forgetting the cost of sales for a cash sale, and treating drawings as an expense.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Let's make this simple. Every transaction in a shop is one of three kinds: money coming in, money going out, or goods going out on a promise to pay later. Each kind has its own book.

## Subtopic: Three Questions, Three Books

When a document lands on your desk, ask three questions.

Did money come in? Write it in the CRJ, the money-in book. Cash sales, rent received, and payments from debtors all go here.

Did money go out? Write it in the CPJ, the money-out book. Wages, stock paid for immediately, the owner taking money, and bank charges all go here.

Did goods leave on a promise to pay later? Write it in the DJ, the credit sales book.

Every transaction goes in one book only. Ridgeview had twelve transactions in August: five in the CRJ, four in the CPJ and three in the DJ.

## Subtopic: Money In, Money Out

In the CRJ, the Bank column shows every rand that came in: R22 600 in August. The other columns explain where it came from: R10 500 from cash sales, R9 600 from debtors paying and R2 500 of rent. Add them up: 22 600. That is the cross-cast, and it must match. The cost of sales column is not money, so leave it out of the check.

In the CPJ, the Bank column shows every rand that went out: R9 450. The other columns explain why: R5 000 for stock, R2 800 for wages and R1 650 of other payments, which are the owner's drawings and the bank charges. Add them up: 9 450. It matches.

Money in minus money out: 22 600 - 9 450 = 13 150 more in the bank than at the start of August.

## Subtopic: Promises to Pay

In the DJ, Ridgeview sold R7 500 of goods on credit, which cost R5 000.

Now check the customers. They owed R12 500 at the start of August. They bought R7 500 more and paid R9 600. 12 500 + 7 500 - 9 600 = 10 400. And their pages say Hilltop 3 000, Ace 2 700, Sunset 4 700. Add them: 10 400. Same answer.

One last idea: Ridgeview sold R18 000 of goods in August, cash and credit together, and made a gross profit of R6 000, even though some of that money has not arrived yet. Practise by sorting a list of transactions into the three books before you record any of them.
