### subtopic_1 — The Full Debtors Cycle
**Assistant:** Why does a business need both a debtors ledger and a Debtors control account?

**Tutor:** Because they answer different questions. The Debtors control account in the general ledger shows the total amount owed by all debtors together, and that single figure is what the trial balance and, later, the balance sheet need. But it cannot tell Ridgeview whether Hilltop has paid or how much Sunset still owes. The debtors ledger answers those questions, with one account per debtor and a running balance. Each system also checks the other. Every invoice is posted twice: individually to the debtor's account and, as part of the DJ total, to Debtors control. Every receipt is posted twice in the same way, from the CRJ. So at the end of the month the sum of the individual balances, the debtors list, must equal the balance of the control account. If the totals differ, an error has been made somewhere, and the bookkeeper knows to look for it before drawing up the trial balance. That is where the name comes from: the control account controls the accuracy of the subsidiary ledger. For Ridgeview at 31 July, both show R12 500.

### subtopic_2 — Opening Balances and July's Credit Sales
**Assistant:** Why do opening balances matter, and how are they shown?

**Tutor:** Because debtors do not start from zero each month. At the end of June some customers still owed Ridgeview money, R8 400 in total, and that debt carries on into July. If the bookkeeper ignored the opening balances, the debtors ledger would show only July's activity, and when Hilltop paid its R4 800 for June's purchases, its account would go into credit, which makes no sense. The opening balance is written on the first line of each debtor's account, dated 1 July with the details Balance, and Debtors control shows Balance b/d of R8 400 on the debit side. The abbreviation b/d means brought down, because the balance was carried down from the previous month. What must not happen is that the opening balances are added into the DJ. The DJ records only the credit sales of the current month. The old sales were recorded in June's DJ and posted then. Counting them again in July would overstate both sales and debtors. The opening balances are already in the ledger, so they simply continue.

### subtopic_3 — Receipts from Debtors in the CRJ
**Assistant:** Why is a payment from a debtor never recorded in the Sales column?

**Tutor:** Because the sale and the payment are two separate events, and the income belongs to the first one. When Ridgeview sent goods to Ace Plumbing on credit, it earned the income at that moment. The DJ recorded the selling price in the Sales column and the cost in the Cost of sales column, and owner's equity increased by the gross profit. When Ace pays weeks later, nothing is sold. No goods leave the shop, so there is no cost of sales, and no new income is earned. All that happens is that one asset, the amount owed by Ace, turns into another asset, money in the bank. That is why the receipt goes in the CRJ, in the Bank column and the Debtors control column, and the Sales and Cost of sales columns stay empty. If the payment were recorded as a sale, the R2 100 of income would be counted twice, once in June and once in July, and the profit would be overstated. The Debtors control account would also not decrease, so it would wrongly show that Ace still owes money it has already paid.

### subtopic_4 — Debtors Ledger, Control Account and Debtors List
**Assistant:** What does a part payment such as Sunset's R1 000 mean for the business?

**Tutor:** It means that Sunset paid some, but not all, of what it owed. On 25 July Sunset owed R3 900: the opening balance of R1 500 plus invoice 142 for R2 400. It paid R1 000, so R2 900 is still outstanding. In the books, a part payment is recorded exactly like a full payment: R1 000 in the CRJ in the Bank and Debtors control columns, and R1 000 on the credit side of Sunset's account in the debtors ledger. The running balance shows the R2 900 left. For the business, though, a part payment is worth paying attention to. The opening balance of R1 500 dates from June, so under Ridgeview's 30-day terms it is already due. If Sunset's payment covered only part of the old debt, the bookkeeper should check the age of what remains and send a statement with a reminder. Part payments are sometimes normal, for example when the customer has agreed to pay in instalments, but repeated part payments can be an early warning that a customer is struggling, and Ridgeview should be careful about allowing further credit until the account is up to date.

### subtopic_5 — Sell, Record, Collect
**Assistant:** How can the bookkeeper find an error if the debtors list and the control account disagree?

**Tutor:** By working systematically, starting with the most likely causes. First, check the arithmetic: add the debtors list again, and recalculate the balance of Debtors control. Second, check that every line in the DJ and every Debtors control entry in the CRJ has a debtors ledger folio next to it, which shows that it was posted. A missing folio points to a line that was never posted. Third, check that invoices are on the debit side and receipts on the credit side of each debtor's account; a receipt posted to the wrong side causes a difference of double the amount. Fourth, look at the size of the difference. If it equals a single invoice or receipt, that item is probably missing. If it divides exactly by 9, for example R810, two digits may have been swapped, such as R2 900 written as R2 090. Note that some errors do not cause a difference at all. If Ace's invoice were posted to Hilltop's account, the total would still agree, but both accounts would be wrong. That kind of error is usually found when a customer queries the monthly statement.

### subtopic_6 — Every Customer's Story
**Assistant:** Why should Ridgeview send monthly statements to its debtors?

**Tutor:** A statement serves three purposes. First, it reminds the debtor that money is owed and when it is due, which speeds up payment. Busy customers often pay whoever reminds them. Second, it lets the debtor check Ridgeview's records against its own. The statement shows the opening balance, every invoice, every payment and the closing balance, so if Hilltop's own records show a payment that Ridgeview has not recorded, or an invoice it never received, the difference comes to light and can be corrected. This is the best way to find errors that the control account cannot detect, such as an invoice posted to the wrong debtor. Third, a statement is a record that the business asked for payment, which matters if the debt must later be collected through a lawyer or written off. The statement is prepared from the debtors ledger, which is another reason why the ledger must be posted accurately and kept up to date. In practice, many businesses now email statements automatically at the end of each month.

### subtopic_7 — One Total, Two Proofs
**Assistant:** Is it a good thing that Ridgeview's debtors increased from R8 400 to R12 500 in July?

**Tutor:** It depends on why they increased. On the positive side, the increase came mainly from good credit sales of R12 000, which earned a gross profit of R4 000, so credit is helping Ridgeview to sell more to builders who would not buy for cash. On the other side, R12 500 of Ridgeview's money is now in its customers' hands rather than in its bank account, and that money cannot be used to pay suppliers, wages or rent until it is collected. The longer it stays outstanding, the greater the risk that some of it will never be paid. So the bookkeeper should look behind the total. Are the balances within each customer's credit limit? How old are they? Hilltop and Ace paid their June balances in full, which is a good sign, but Sunset paid only part of its debt. A useful rule is that growth in debtors is healthy when it follows growth in sales and customers pay on time, and unhealthy when it happens because customers are paying more slowly. Watching this every month is a central part of managing a business that sells on credit.
