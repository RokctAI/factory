# Part 1 — Expert

In the previous lesson we set out the components of a business plan. In this lesson we work through two of them in detail: the SWOT analysis, which belongs to the market analysis, and the description of the product or service. Both are about one question: why would this business succeed where others might not? We continue with Shine on Wheels, the fictional mobile car wash that a young entrepreneur plans to start as a sole trader in a Gauteng township, washing cars at customers' homes and workplaces.

## Subtopic: What a SWOT Analysis Is

SWOT stands for strengths, weaknesses, opportunities and threats. A SWOT analysis is a structured way of looking at a business, or a planned business, so that the entrepreneur can see clearly what is working in its favour and what is working against it. It is usually set out as a grid of four blocks, with strengths and weaknesses in the top row and opportunities and threats in the bottom row.

The most important idea in a SWOT analysis is the difference between internal and external factors. Strengths and weaknesses are internal. They are features of the business itself, and the business has control over them. Examples are the skills of the owner, the quality of the equipment, the location, the price, the reputation of the business and the amount of capital available. Opportunities and threats are external. They come from the business environment outside the business, and the business cannot control them, it can only respond to them. Examples are changes in customer needs, new competitors, government regulations, the state of the economy, the weather, crime and new technology.

A strength is an internal factor that gives the business an advantage, such as a skilled owner or low costs. A weakness is an internal factor that puts the business at a disadvantage, such as a lack of experience, little capital or no transport. An opportunity is an external factor that the business could use to grow or earn more, such as a growing market or a gap that no competitor fills. A threat is an external factor that could harm the business, such as a new competitor, rising fuel prices or water restrictions.

A common mistake is to put an external factor under strengths or weaknesses. For example, "there is no mobile car wash in the area" is not a strength of Shine on Wheels; it is a fact about the market, so it is an opportunity. "The owner has a driver's licence" is internal, so it is a strength. A useful test is to ask: if this business closed tomorrow, would this factor still exist? If yes, it is external, so it is an opportunity or a threat. If it would disappear with the business, it is internal, so it is a strength or a weakness.

The environment outside the business is often divided into two parts. The market environment includes customers, competitors and suppliers, which the business can influence a little through its actions. The macro environment includes the economy, politics and the law, society, technology and the natural environment, which the business cannot influence at all. Both are sources of opportunities and threats.

A SWOT analysis is only useful if it is honest and specific. "We are good" is not a strength; "the owner has two years' experience valeting cars at a dealership" is. "Competition" is not a threat; "a filling station 2 km away offers a hand wash for R150" is.

## Subtopic: Shine on Wheels' SWOT Analysis

Let's build the SWOT analysis for Shine on Wheels. The entrepreneur first gathered information. A survey of 50 car owners in the area found that 32 of them, which is 32 ÷ 50 × 100 = 64 percent, would use a mobile car wash at least twice a month. The entrepreneur also visited competitors, priced equipment and spoke to the municipality.

Strengths. The owner has two years of experience cleaning cars at a dealership and knows how to do a careful valet. The service is convenient, because the car is washed where it is parked, at home or at work, so the customer does not lose time. The price of R120 for a standard wash is lower than the nearest competitor's R150, which is R30, or 20 percent, cheaper. The business uses a water-saving method: a standard wash uses about 20 litres of water, compared with about 150 litres when a car is washed with an open hose. The start-up costs are low compared with a fixed car wash, because no building is needed.

Weaknesses. The owner has no formal business training and no experience in managing money. The business has little capital, so it cannot afford a vehicle at first and will move equipment on a trolley within a limited area. It is new and unknown, so it has no reputation or regular customers yet. The owner will work alone with one helper, so if either is ill, the business cannot serve its customers.

Opportunities. The survey shows demand: 64 percent of the car owners questioned would use the service. There is no other mobile car wash in the area. Many residents commute by car and have little free time over weekends. Two office parks nearby have large parking areas, where cars stand for eight hours a day. Social media and community WhatsApp groups make it cheap to advertise. There is also a growing interest in water-saving services, which could be a selling point.

Threats. Gauteng sometimes has water restrictions, which could limit car washing. Rising fuel and product prices could increase costs. The filling station's hand wash could lower its price to compete. Crime could put equipment or staff at risk. During an economic downturn, people cut back on spending they do not need, and car washing is one of the first things they cut. Heavy summer rain could also reduce demand for a few days at a time.

Look at how specific each point is. Every strength and weakness is about Shine on Wheels itself. Every opportunity and threat exists in the environment, whether or not Shine on Wheels opens.

## Subtopic: Turning the SWOT into Strategy

A SWOT analysis is not finished when the four lists are written. Its real value lies in what the entrepreneur does with them. The basic rules are simple: build on strengths, fix or reduce weaknesses, take advantage of opportunities and protect the business against threats.

The most powerful strategies come from combining the blocks. Use a strength to seize an opportunity: Shine on Wheels' convenience and the two nearby office parks fit perfectly, so the business can offer a workplace service where cars are washed in the parking area while their owners work. Use a strength to reduce a threat: the water-saving method means that the business can keep operating during most water restrictions, when businesses using hoses might be forced to stop. That turns a threat into a selling point.

Use an opportunity to overcome a weakness: the lack of a reputation can be addressed through cheap social media marketing, such as before-and-after photographs, customer reviews and a first-wash discount shared in community groups. The weakness of no business training can be reduced by attending a free short course, for example through a local enterprise development programme, and by asking an experienced business owner to act as a mentor.

Protect the weakest points against the biggest threats: little capital and the risk of crime together mean that a stolen pressure washer could end the business, so insurance and a secure storage place are essential, even though they cost money. The risk of illness can be reduced by training the helper fully, so that either person can do every task.

The SWOT also helps the entrepreneur set priorities. Not every weakness needs to be fixed at once. A small business with limited money should first tackle the weaknesses that are linked to its most serious threats.

Finally, a SWOT analysis is not done once and forgotten. The environment keeps changing: a competitor may close, a new regulation may appear or a new opportunity may arise. A business should review its SWOT at least once a year and whenever something important changes. In your Grade 9 project, your SWOT analysis must be specific to your own business idea, honest about its weaknesses, and followed by at least one action for each block.

## Subtopic: Describing the Product or Service

The description of the product or service tells the reader exactly what the business will sell. It must be clear enough that someone who has never heard of the business could explain it to a friend. It usually covers the following points.

What is sold. Shine on Wheels offers three services: a standard wash of the exterior, wheels and windows for R120; an inside-and-out wash, which adds vacuuming and wiping the dashboard, for R180; and a full valet, which adds polishing and seat cleaning, for R350. All are done at the customer's home or workplace.

Features and benefits. A feature is a fact about the product; a benefit is what the customer gains from it. Customers buy benefits. The feature "we come to you" brings the benefit "you save an hour of your weekend". The feature "about 20 litres of water per wash" brings the benefit "a clean car without wasting water, even during restrictions". The feature "biodegradable products" brings the benefit "no harm to your garden or paving". A good description links every important feature to a benefit.

The target market. The target market is the specific group of customers the business aims to serve. For Shine on Wheels it is working adults in the area who own cars, especially commuters who park at the two office parks during the day, and households with more than one car. Describing the target market precisely helps with every later decision, from price to advertising.

The unique selling point. The unique selling point, or USP, is the feature that makes the business different from its competitors and gives customers a reason to choose it. Shine on Wheels' USP is a convenient, water-wise car wash where the car is parked. The filling station is cheaper per wash than a full valet, but it cannot offer convenience, and it uses far more water.

How the product meets a need. The description should explain the customer problem that the product solves. Commuters do not have time to queue at a car wash, and many are worried about water use. Shine on Wheels solves both problems at once.

Quality and future plans. The description can also explain how quality will be maintained, for example with a checklist for every wash and a re-wash free of charge if a customer is not satisfied, and how the range might grow, for example with monthly subscription packages.

A product description should be factual, not boastful. "The best car wash in Gauteng" means nothing to a lender. "A standard wash for R120, done at your workplace in about 40 minutes using about 20 litres of water" tells the reader exactly what to expect.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Let's make this simple. Before you start a business, you need to know two things very well: yourself and the world around you. Then you need to explain clearly what you are selling and why anyone should buy it. This lesson covers exactly those two jobs.

## Subtopic: Inside and Outside

A SWOT analysis is four lists. Two lists are about the inside of your business: your strengths, the things you are good at, and your weaknesses, the things you are not good at yet. You can change these. Two lists are about the world outside: opportunities, the chances out there, and threats, the dangers out there. You cannot change these; you can only get ready for them.

Here is the easy test. Imagine your business closed tomorrow. Would the thing still be there? The office parks full of cars would still be there, so that is an opportunity. Water restrictions would still happen, so that is a threat. But the owner's experience and the water-saving method belong to the business, so those are strengths, and the lack of capital is a weakness.

Be specific. Do not write "we are good"; write what exactly you are good at. Do not write "competition"; write who the competitor is and what they charge.

## Subtopic: Strengths Meet Opportunities

The four lists are only the start. Now you ask: how do I use them? Use your strengths to grab opportunities. Shine on Wheels is convenient, and there are office parks nearby, so it washes cars in the parking lot while people work.

Use your strengths to protect yourself from threats. It uses about 20 litres of water instead of 150, so during water restrictions it can keep working when others cannot.

Fix weaknesses with opportunities. Nobody knows the business yet, but social media is cheap, so post before-and-after photographs and ask happy customers to share them.

And protect your weakest spot from the biggest danger. The equipment is the whole business, so insure it and store it safely.

Remember the survey: 32 out of 50 car owners said yes. That is 64 percent. Numbers like that make your SWOT believable.

## Subtopic: Selling the Benefit

Now describe what you sell. Say exactly what it is, what it costs and where it happens: a standard wash for R120 at your home or office.

Then turn every feature into a benefit. A feature is a fact. A benefit is what the customer gets. "We come to you" is a feature. "You get your Saturday back" is a benefit. People buy benefits.

Say who your customers are. Not "everyone", but working people with cars who park at the office parks all day.

Finally, say why they should pick you. That is your unique selling point. For Shine on Wheels, it is a car wash that comes to you and does not waste water. If you can say your USP in one sentence, your customers can remember it, and so can the bank. Practise by writing a SWOT and a one-sentence USP for a business idea of your own.
