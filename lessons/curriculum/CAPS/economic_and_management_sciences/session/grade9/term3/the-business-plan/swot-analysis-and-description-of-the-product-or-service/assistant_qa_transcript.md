### subtopic_1 — What a SWOT Analysis Is
**Assistant:** Why does it matter so much whether a factor is internal or external?

**Tutor:** Because the classification tells the entrepreneur what kind of action is possible. Internal factors, the strengths and weaknesses, are under the control of the business. If Shine on Wheels lacks business training, the owner can take a course; if its equipment is poor, it can buy better equipment. External factors, the opportunities and threats, are not under its control. The owner cannot stop water restrictions, cannot stop a competitor from lowering its price and cannot change the economy. The only thing the business can do is prepare for them or take advantage of them. If a factor is placed in the wrong block, the plan responds wrongly. For example, writing "no competitor in the area" as a strength suggests that the advantage belongs to the business, when it could disappear the day a competitor opens. Seen correctly as an opportunity, it reminds the owner to move quickly and to build loyal customers before someone else notices the gap. The test is simple: would the factor still exist if the business closed tomorrow? If so, it is external.

### subtopic_2 — Shine on Wheels' SWOT Analysis
**Assistant:** How do the survey figures make the SWOT analysis stronger?

**Tutor:** They replace opinion with evidence. Anyone can claim that there is demand for a mobile car wash, but a lender wants to know how the entrepreneur knows. The survey of 50 car owners found that 32 would use the service at least twice a month, which is 32 divided by 50, times 100, or 64 percent. That figure supports the opportunity of an unmet need in the market, and it can later be used in the financial plan to estimate sales, as long as the entrepreneur is cautious, because people who say yes in a survey do not always buy. The other figures work in the same way. Saying the price is lower is vague; saying it is R120 against the competitor's R150, which is R30 or 20 percent cheaper, is precise and can be checked. Saying the method saves water is vague; saying it uses about 20 litres instead of about 150 is convincing. In your own project, try to support each important SWOT point with a fact, a number or an observation, and state where the information came from, so that the reader can trust it.

### subtopic_3 — Turning the SWOT into Strategy
**Assistant:** Why is a SWOT analysis useless if the entrepreneur only writes the four lists?

**Tutor:** Because the lists describe the situation but do not say what to do about it. The purpose of the analysis is to shape decisions. The useful step is to combine the blocks. When a strength meets an opportunity, the business has a natural growth path, such as washing cars at the office parks while their owners are at work. When a strength meets a threat, the business may be able to defend itself, or even turn the threat into an advantage, as the water-saving method does during restrictions. When an opportunity can repair a weakness, the business can grow out of its problem, as cheap social media can build a reputation. And when a weakness meets a threat, the business is in danger: little capital plus the risk of theft means one stolen pressure washer could end it, so insurance and safe storage come first. An entrepreneur who works through these combinations ends up with a short list of priorities. That list, not the four boxes themselves, is what goes into the marketing, production and financial plans that follow.

### subtopic_4 — Describing the Product or Service
**Assistant:** Why should a product description focus on benefits rather than features?

**Tutor:** Because customers buy what a product does for them, not its technical details. A feature is a fact: the car is washed where it is parked, the method uses about 20 litres of water, the products are biodegradable. A benefit is what the customer gains: an hour of the weekend saved, a clean car without guilt during a drought, no damage to the garden. When a commuter reads an advertisement, the benefit is what makes them want the service. Features still matter, because they make the benefit believable. If Shine on Wheels claims to save water, it should state the figure. So the best descriptions link the two: feature, therefore benefit. The same approach helps in finding the unique selling point. The USP is usually the benefit that competitors cannot easily match. The filling station can lower its price, but it cannot come to the customer's office. A business plan that states its USP clearly shows the lender that the entrepreneur understands why customers will choose this business, which is the heart of every successful business.

### subtopic_5 — Inside and Outside
**Assistant:** Can the same factor be both a strength and a weakness?

**Tutor:** It can look that way, but a careful analysis usually separates it into two specific points. Take the fact that Shine on Wheels has only the owner and one helper. That keeps wage costs low, which is a strength, because the business can charge lower prices. It also means the business cannot serve customers if one of them is ill, which is a weakness. These are two different consequences of the same situation, so they are written as two separate, specific points: low labour costs as a strength, and a dependence on two people as a weakness. The same happens outside the business. A growing interest in saving water is an opportunity for a water-wise service, but water restrictions are a threat to car washing in general. Writing both down is not a contradiction; it is an honest description. Problems arise only when points are vague. "Small business" means little; "two workers, so low wage costs" and "two workers, so no cover for illness" are clear and lead directly to actions, such as training the helper to do every task.

### subtopic_6 — Strengths Meet Opportunities
**Assistant:** How should an entrepreneur choose which weakness to fix first?

**Tutor:** By linking each weakness to the threats it makes worse and the opportunities it prevents. A small business never has the money or time to fix everything at once, so it must prioritise. For Shine on Wheels, the most dangerous combination is little capital together with the risk of crime, because a single theft of equipment could end the business. That weakness therefore comes first, and it is dealt with through insurance, a secure storage place and a record of the serial numbers of the equipment. Next comes the lack of a reputation, because it stops the business from using its biggest opportunity: the demand shown by the survey. That is fixed cheaply through social media, reviews and a first-wash discount. The lack of business training is serious but less urgent, so the owner can take a short course over the first few months and use a mentor in the meantime. The lack of a vehicle can wait until the business earns enough to buy one. Writing down this order of priorities in the plan shows a lender that the entrepreneur thinks realistically.

### subtopic_7 — Selling the Benefit
**Assistant:** What makes a unique selling point convincing to a lender?

**Tutor:** Three things: it must be true, it must matter to customers and it must be hard for competitors to copy. Shine on Wheels' USP, a convenient, water-wise car wash at the customer's home or workplace, passes all three tests. It is true, because the service really is mobile and the method really does use about 20 litres of water per wash. It matters, because the survey showed demand, and commuters at the office parks have little free time. And it is hard to copy, because the filling station is a fixed site: it can match a price but not the convenience. A weak USP is usually price alone. If Shine on Wheels claimed only to be cheaper, the competitor could simply lower its price, and both businesses would earn less. A lender reading the plan will ask: what stops a competitor from doing the same thing tomorrow? If the plan has a good answer, the lender has more confidence that the sales forecast is realistic. Your own USP should be one sentence that you can defend with facts.
