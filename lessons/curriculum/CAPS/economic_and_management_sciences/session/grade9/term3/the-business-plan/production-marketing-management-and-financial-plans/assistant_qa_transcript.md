### subtopic_1 — The Production Plan
**Assistant:** Why does a service business need a production plan if it does not manufacture anything?

**Tutor:** Because a service still has to be produced, even though nothing physical is made. The customer is buying an outcome, a clean car, and that outcome depends on equipment, materials, people, a process and quality control, exactly as a manufactured product does. The production plan for Shine on Wheels explains where the equipment is stored, what is used up in every wash and what it costs, how long a wash takes, and how many washes two workers can realistically complete. These details matter beyond the production section. The cost of about R30 per wash flows into the financial plan, and the capacity of five washes a day over 24 working days, which is 120 washes a month, tests whether the sales goal is achievable. If the production plan showed that two workers could only manage three washes a day, the goal of 120 would be impossible, and the financial plan would be wrong. A lender reads the production plan to check that the entrepreneur has thought about how the work will really be done, not only about how much it might earn.

### subtopic_2 — The Marketing Plan and the Four Ps
**Assistant:** How should a small business decide on its price?

**Tutor:** It should look at three things together: its costs, its competitors and its customers. The price must cover the cost of each unit, about R30 of materials for a standard wash, and contribute enough towards the fixed costs to leave a profit. The price must also make sense next to competitors. The filling station charges R150 for a hand wash, so Shine on Wheels can charge R120 and still offer more convenience. Charging much more than the competitor would require a very strong reason. Finally, the price must be acceptable to customers, which is why the survey asked what people would pay. A business can also use pricing to shape behaviour. The monthly package of four washes for R440 instead of 4 × 120 = R480 gives a R40 discount, but it brings regular income and loyal customers, which makes planning easier. A common mistake for new entrepreneurs is to set the price too low in the hope of winning customers quickly. If the price barely covers costs, the business cannot survive a bad month, and raising prices later is difficult.

### subtopic_3 — The Management Plan
**Assistant:** Why do lenders care so much about the management plan?

**Tutor:** Because most small businesses that fail do so because of management problems, not because the idea was bad. A lender wants to know who will make the decisions, whether they have the skills and experience to make them well, and what will happen when they face a problem they do not know how to solve. The management plan answers those questions. It shows the form of ownership, the owner's background, the staff and their roles, and an organogram of the structure. The most important part is often the honest discussion of gaps. The owner of Shine on Wheels has practical experience but no bookkeeping skills, so the plan says that the owner will take a short course and that a bookkeeper will check the records every month, and it names a mentor. A plan that claims the owner is good at everything is less believable than one that admits weaknesses and explains how they will be covered. The management plan also shows that labour laws will be followed, such as a written contract and at least the minimum wage, which protects the business from legal problems later.

### subtopic_4 — The Financial Plan
**Assistant:** Why is the break-even point rounded up and not to the nearest whole number?

**Tutor:** Because the break-even point is the smallest number of washes that covers all the costs, and a business cannot do part of a wash. The calculation gives 5 300 divided by 90, which is about 58.9. If we rounded to the nearest whole number we would get 59 anyway, but if the answer had been, say, 58.2, rounding to the nearest would give 58, and 58 washes would not cover the costs. So we always round up. We can check: 58 washes contribute 58 × 90 = R5 220, which is R80 short of the fixed costs, so the business makes a small loss; 59 washes contribute 59 × 90 = R5 310, which covers the R5 300 and leaves R10. The R90 is called the contribution per wash, because it is what each wash contributes towards the fixed costs after paying for its own materials. Once the fixed costs are covered, every further wash adds R90 to the profit. That is why 120 washes give a profit of R5 500: the 61 washes above break-even add 61 × 90 = R5 490, plus the R10 left over at 59 washes.

### subtopic_5 — How Will We Do It?
**Assistant:** What is the difference between start-up costs and monthly costs?

**Tutor:** Start-up costs are paid once, before the business opens, to buy what it needs to begin operating. For Shine on Wheels these are the pressure washer, the water tanks, the vacuum cleaner, the trolley and cleaning tools, the branding and the permit, a total of R13 500. Monthly costs, also called operating costs, are paid again and again while the business runs. They are divided into variable costs, which rise and fall with the number of washes, such as the products, water and petrol at about R30 per wash, and fixed costs, which stay the same whatever the number of washes, such as the helper's wage of R4 000, the storeroom rent of R600, phone and data of R400 and insurance of R300, together R5 300. The distinction matters because they are financed differently. Start-up costs are covered by capital and loans, here R6 000 of savings and a R7 500 family loan. Monthly costs must be covered by income from customers, which is why the break-even point is based on the monthly fixed costs and not on the start-up costs.

### subtopic_6 — Who Buys and Who Runs It?
**Assistant:** How does the marketing plan link to the other plans?

**Tutor:** Every decision in the marketing plan has consequences elsewhere. The price of R120 per wash is used in the financial plan to calculate income, so if the marketing plan changes the price, the projected profit and the break-even point change too. A lower price of R100 would leave a contribution of only R70 per wash, and the break-even point would rise to 5 300 divided by 70, about 75.7, so 76 washes. The decision about place, taking the service to the customer, determines the production plan: the business needs a trolley, portable water tanks and a petrol pressure washer rather than a fixed building with a water connection. The promotion methods determine part of the start-up costs, such as the R1 200 for the logo, flyers and branded T-shirts. And the target of 40 regular customers affects the management plan, because more customers will eventually need more staff. This is why a business plan must be read as a whole. Lenders deliberately compare figures across sections, and an inconsistency, such as two different prices, quickly damages their trust in the entrepreneur.

### subtopic_7 — Will It Make Money?
**Assistant:** Why is a cash budget needed if the plan already shows a monthly profit of R5 500?

**Tutor:** Because the R5 500 is the profit in a typical month once the business has reached its goal of 120 washes, and it will not reach that goal on the first day. In the first months, the business will have fewer customers, perhaps 40 washes in the first month and 70 in the second, while the fixed costs of R5 300 must be paid from the start. At 40 washes, the contribution is 40 × 90 = R3 600, which leaves a shortfall of R1 700 against the fixed costs. A cash budget plans the money coming in and going out month by month, so the owner can see when cash will run short and how much is needed to survive until sales grow. It also includes payments that are not expenses, such as the R625 monthly loan repayment and any drawings by the owner. Many profitable businesses fail because they run out of cash before the profits arrive. A plan that includes a realistic cash budget, with a reserve to cover the slow early months, shows a lender that the entrepreneur understands this danger.
