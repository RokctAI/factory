# Part 1 — Expert

This term you will plan a business of your own, and the business plan is the document in which you do it. In this lesson we learn what a business plan is, why entrepreneurs and lenders need one, what its components are, and how it should be set out. In the next two lessons we work through the content of the main sections in detail. Our running example is Shine on Wheels, a fictional mobile car-wash business that a young entrepreneur plans to start in a township in Gauteng, washing cars at customers' homes and workplaces.

## Subtopic: What a Business Plan Is and Why It Matters

A business plan is a written document that describes a business idea and explains, in detail, how the entrepreneur intends to turn that idea into a successful business. It sets out what the business will sell, to whom, how it will make and deliver the product or service, who will run it, how much money it needs, where that money will come from, and how the business expects to make a profit. It covers a period into the future, usually the first one to three years.

A business plan has several important purposes. First, it forces the entrepreneur to think. Many business ideas sound good in conversation and fall apart when someone asks practical questions: who exactly will buy this, and why from you? What will it cost to start? How many customers do you need each month to cover your costs? Writing a plan makes the entrepreneur answer these questions before risking money, which is far cheaper than discovering the answers through failure.

Second, a business plan is needed to obtain finance. Banks, development finance institutions such as the National Youth Development Agency, and investors will not lend or invest money without a plan that shows how the money will be used and how it will be repaid or earn a return. A clear, realistic plan builds their confidence. In the plan, the entrepreneur must show that they understand the market, the costs and the risks.

Third, a business plan is a management tool once the business starts. It sets goals and targets, such as the number of cars to be washed each month, against which actual performance can be measured. If the business falls short, the owner can see where and adjust. A plan is therefore not written once and forgotten; it should be reviewed and updated regularly, at least once a year, or whenever circumstances change.

Fourth, a business plan communicates. It explains the business to partners, key employees, suppliers and even landlords, so that they understand what the business is trying to do.

It is worth being honest about what a business plan cannot do. It cannot guarantee success, because the future is uncertain. Customers may behave differently from what the research suggested, costs may rise, and competitors may react. But a good plan greatly reduces avoidable mistakes, and it helps the entrepreneur respond quickly when things change, because they understand how the parts of the business fit together.

Before writing a plan, an entrepreneur should gather information. This includes talking to potential customers, observing competitors, collecting quotations for equipment and supplies, checking which permits or licences are needed, and asking people who already run similar businesses about their experience. For Shine on Wheels, that might mean surveying car owners in the area, asking the municipality whether a permit is needed to wash cars on the street, and pricing pressure washers and biodegradable cleaning products. Good information makes a good plan; guesses make a weak one.

## Subtopic: The Components of a Business Plan

Business plans vary in length and detail, but most contain the same main components, in roughly this order.

The cover page shows the name of the business, its logo if it has one, the name and contact details of the owner, and the date. The table of contents lists the sections with page numbers.

The executive summary is a short overview of the whole plan, usually one page. It states what the business is, what it sells, who its customers are, how much money it needs and what the expected results are. It is placed first but is written last, because it summarises everything else. Many readers, especially busy lenders, read only the executive summary before deciding whether to read the rest, so it must be clear and convincing.

The description of the business explains the business idea, the name of the business, its location, the vision, mission and goals, the form of ownership, such as a sole trader or a partnership, and the owner's background and skills.

The description of the product or service explains exactly what will be sold, its features, how it meets customers' needs, and what makes it different from competitors. This is often called the competitive advantage or unique selling point.

The market analysis describes the target market, meaning the specific group of customers the business aims to serve, their needs and buying habits, the size of the market, and the competitors. It includes a SWOT analysis of the business: its internal strengths and weaknesses, and the external opportunities and threats it faces. We study the SWOT analysis in detail in the next lesson.

The marketing plan explains how the business will apply the four Ps: product, price, place and promotion.

The production or operations plan explains how the product will be made or the service delivered: the premises, equipment, materials, suppliers, processes and quality control.

The management plan explains who will run the business, their roles and experience, the staff needed and the organisational structure, often with an organogram.

The financial plan sets out the start-up costs, the sources of finance, projected income and expenses, a cash budget and, where possible, the break-even point. This is often the section that lenders study most closely.

Finally, the appendices contain supporting documents, such as quotations for equipment, market research results, the owner's curriculum vitae, permits and letters of support.

You will notice that the components follow the business functions you studied earlier this term. The marketing plan relates to marketing, the production plan to production and purchasing, the management plan to general management and human resources, and the financial plan to financing. A business plan is, in a sense, a plan for every business function.

## Subtopic: Vision, Mission, Goals and Legal Form

The description of the business usually includes a vision statement, a mission statement and goals. These are often confused, so it is worth defining them carefully.

A vision statement describes what the business hopes to become in the long term. It is inspiring and future-focused, and usually short. For Shine on Wheels, the vision might be: To be the most trusted mobile car-care service in our community.

A mission statement describes what the business does now, for whom, and how, in order to work towards the vision. It is more practical. For Shine on Wheels: We provide convenient, affordable, high-quality car washing at our customers' homes and workplaces, using water-saving methods and friendly, reliable staff.

Goals, sometimes called objectives, are specific targets the business wants to achieve in a set time. Good goals are SMART: specific, measurable, achievable, realistic and time-bound. A vague goal such as "to wash many cars" is not useful, because it cannot be measured. A SMART goal is: to wash 120 cars per month by the end of the sixth month of trading. It is specific, it can be measured by counting, it is achievable with one helper and the right equipment, it is realistic given the market research, and it has a deadline. Other goals might cover profit, customer satisfaction or expansion, such as: to have 40 regular monthly customers by the end of the first year.

The business description should also state the legal form of ownership. In Grade 9, the most common forms for a small new business are the sole trader, also called a sole proprietor, and the partnership. A sole trader is owned by one person, who makes all the decisions and keeps all the profit, but who also has unlimited liability: if the business cannot pay its debts, the owner's personal possessions can be used to pay them. A partnership is owned by two or more people, often with a written partnership agreement, who share decision-making, profit and, generally, unlimited liability. Some entrepreneurs register a private company, which has limited liability, meaning the owners' personal possessions are protected, but this requires registration with the Companies and Intellectual Property Commission, known as the CIPC, and more formal administration. Shine on Wheels will start as a sole trader, because it is simple and inexpensive to set up.

The description also mentions the location and why it was chosen. Shine on Wheels is mobile, but it still needs a base where equipment is stored and a defined area where it operates, chosen because many residents commute by car and there is no competing mobile service.

## Subtopic: Format, Language and Presentation

A business plan is a formal document, and its format and presentation matter, because the reader will judge the entrepreneur partly by how professional the plan looks.

The plan should follow a logical order, with clear headings and numbered sections, so that a reader can find any part quickly. The table of contents should match the headings. Each section should start on a new page or be clearly separated.

The language should be clear, concise and professional. Avoid slang, exaggeration and vague claims. Instead of writing that the business will be "the best ever", give evidence: our survey of 50 car owners in the area found that 32 would use a mobile car wash at least twice a month. Use the third person or the first person plural consistently, for example "Shine on Wheels will" or "we will", rather than mixing styles. Check spelling and grammar carefully; careless errors suggest a careless business.

Numbers should be presented in tables, with headings and totals, and should be consistent throughout the plan. If the marketing plan says the price is R120 per wash, the financial plan must use R120 too. Inconsistent figures are one of the quickest ways to lose a lender's trust. All calculations should be checked, and assumptions should be stated. For example: we assume 120 washes per month from month six, based on our survey and the experience of similar businesses.

Visual aids help. A logo gives the business identity, an organogram shows the management structure, a map shows the operating area, and simple charts can show projected sales. But visuals should support the text, not replace it.

The plan should be realistic. Over-optimistic sales forecasts and underestimated costs are common mistakes. Lenders have read many plans and will quickly notice if the numbers are too good to be true. It is better to show a modest, achievable plan, with a clear explanation of the risks and how they will be managed.

Finally, the plan should be the entrepreneur's own work. Templates can help with structure, but the content must reflect the real business, its real market and its real numbers. Your Grade 9 project will be assessed on exactly these qualities: completeness of the components, logical format, realistic and consistent content, and clear presentation.

A useful final check is to give the plan to someone who knows nothing about the business, such as a teacher or family member, and ask them to explain it back to you. If they cannot say what the business sells, to whom, and how it will make money, the plan is not yet clear enough. Their questions will show you which sections need more detail or better explanation. Lenders will ask the same questions, so it is far better to hear them first from a friendly reader. Keep a copy of every version of the plan, because comparing them later shows how your thinking developed, and that reflection is valuable in itself.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Let's make it simple. A business plan is a map. Before you drive somewhere far away, you check the route, how much fuel you need and where the dangers are. A business plan does the same for a new business.

## Subtopic: A Map Before the Journey

A business plan is a written document that explains your business idea and how you will make it work. It answers simple questions. What will I sell? Who will buy it? How will I make it or deliver it? Who will help me? How much money do I need, and where will I get it? How will I make a profit?

You need a plan for three main reasons. It makes you think carefully before you spend money. It helps you get a loan, because banks want to see a plan. And it helps you check your progress once the business starts.

Before you write, find out. Ask possible customers what they want, look at what competitors charge, and get prices for the things you must buy. A plan built on real information is much stronger than one built on guesses.

A plan is not a promise that everything will work. But it helps you avoid silly mistakes and react quickly when something changes.

## Subtopic: The Plan Section by Section

Here are the main sections, in order. Cover page and contents. Executive summary: the whole plan on one page, written last. Description of the business: name, location, vision, mission, goals and owner. Product or service: what you sell and why it is special. Market analysis: your customers, your competitors, and a SWOT analysis. Marketing plan: the four Ps. Production plan: how you make it. Management plan: who does what. Financial plan: the money. Appendices: quotes, surveys and other proof.

Notice how the sections match the business functions. Marketing, production, management and finance all have their own section.

## Subtopic: Writing Goals That Work

A vision is the big dream: the most trusted mobile car wash in our area. A mission is what you do every day: convenient, affordable car washes at people's homes and workplaces.

Goals are targets, and good goals are SMART: specific, measurable, achievable, realistic and time-bound. "Wash lots of cars" is not SMART. "Wash 120 cars a month by month six" is SMART, because you can count it and it has a deadline.

When you write your own plan, keep it neat, use headings and tables, check your numbers, and make sure the same figures appear everywhere. A tidy, honest plan earns trust, and it shows the reader that you take your business seriously from the very first page. Start your project by writing your business name, your vision, your mission and three SMART goals.
