# Part 1 — Expert

A business plan becomes convincing when it explains how the business will actually work. In this lesson we complete the four main functional plans for Shine on Wheels, the fictional mobile car wash that a young entrepreneur plans to start as a sole trader in a Gauteng township: the production plan, the marketing plan, the management plan and the financial plan. Each plan matches business functions you studied earlier this term, and every figure must agree across all four plans.

## Subtopic: The Production Plan

The production plan, also called the operations plan, explains how the business will make its product or deliver its service. For a manufacturing business it describes the factory, machines, raw materials and production process. For a service business such as Shine on Wheels it describes how the service is delivered. It matches the production and purchasing functions.

Premises and location. Shine on Wheels is mobile, but it needs a secure place to store its equipment overnight. The owner will rent a lockable storeroom near home for R600 a month. The operating area is the township and the two nearby office parks, within walking distance with a trolley, because the business cannot afford a vehicle yet.

Equipment. The business needs a petrol pressure washer, two 50-litre water tanks, a vacuum cleaner, a trolley, buckets, microfibre towels and brushes. The plan lists each item with its supplier and price, and quotations are attached in the appendices.

Materials and suppliers. Materials are the items used up in every wash: biodegradable car shampoo, tyre shine, glass cleaner, water and petrol for the pressure washer. The owner has compared prices from two suppliers and chosen a wholesaler that delivers once a month. On average, the materials, water and fuel for one standard wash cost about R30: R12 for products, R3 for water and R15 for fuel. Keeping this figure accurate is important, because it is used in the financial plan.

The process. The plan describes the steps of a standard wash: book the slot, arrive and check the car for existing damage with the customer, pre-rinse with the pressure washer, wash with shampoo using two buckets, rinse, dry with microfibre towels, clean the windows and wheels, and do a final inspection with the customer. A standard wash takes about 40 minutes, so two workers can complete about five washes in a working day, with time for walking between customers. Over 24 working days that is 5 × 24 = 120 washes a month, which matches the SMART goal.

Quality control. Every wash follows a written checklist, and customers sign it at the end. If a customer is not satisfied, the car is re-washed free of charge. Equipment is serviced every three months.

Legal and safety requirements. The owner has checked with the municipality that a permit is needed to operate in public parking areas, and the cost is included in the start-up costs. Workers wear gloves when handling chemicals, and only biodegradable products are used, so that run-off does not harm the environment. At about 20 litres per wash, 120 washes use about 2 400 litres of water a month.

## Subtopic: The Marketing Plan and the Four Ps

The marketing plan explains how the business will attract and keep customers. It begins with the target market, identified in the market analysis, and then applies the marketing mix, known as the four Ps: product, price, place and promotion. It matches the marketing and public relations functions.

Product. This repeats briefly what is sold: a standard wash, an inside-and-out wash and a full valet, with the benefits of convenience and water saving. It may also describe packaging or branding. For a service, the brand is the logo, the branded T-shirts and the way staff behave.

Price. The price must cover costs, leave a profit and be acceptable to customers. Shine on Wheels considered three things. The cost of a standard wash is about R30 in materials, plus a share of fixed costs. The competitor's hand wash costs R150. And the survey asked customers what they would pay. The owner set the standard wash at R120, the inside-and-out wash at R180 and the full valet at R350. The business will also offer a monthly package of four standard washes for R440, which is R40 cheaper than four single washes at 4 × 120 = R480, to encourage regular customers.

Place. Place, or distribution, is how the product reaches the customer. For Shine on Wheels, the service goes to the customer, which is the whole point. Bookings are taken by phone and WhatsApp, and on two days a week the business operates at the office parks with the permission of their managers.

Promotion. Promotion is how the business communicates with customers. Shine on Wheels' budget is small, so it will use low-cost methods: flyers handed out at the office parks and taxi rank, posts in community WhatsApp and Facebook groups with before-and-after photographs, branded T-shirts, and word of mouth encouraged by a referral offer: a free wash for every three new customers referred. A launch discount of R20 off the first wash will attract customers to try the service.

Some textbooks add more Ps for services, such as people, process and physical evidence, because the quality of a service depends on the people who deliver it. Grade 9 focuses on the four Ps, but it is worth remembering that friendly, reliable staff are a large part of the product for any service business.

The marketing plan should also set marketing goals, such as 40 regular monthly customers by the end of the first year, and explain how the business will measure them, for example by recording how each new customer heard about the business.

## Subtopic: The Management Plan

The management plan explains who will run the business and how. Lenders often say that they invest in people as much as in ideas, so this section must show that the business has the skills it needs or a realistic plan to get them. It matches the general management and human resources functions.

Ownership and the owner. The plan states the form of ownership, a sole trader, and describes the owner: experience, qualifications and personal qualities. The owner of Shine on Wheels has two years of experience cleaning cars at a dealership, a driver's licence, and has completed Grade 12. The owner's curriculum vitae is included in the appendices.

Staff. The business will employ one helper at R4 000 a month to begin with. The plan describes the job: washing cars, keeping equipment clean, and treating customers politely. It explains how the helper will be recruited, through community networks and an interview, and trained, by working alongside the owner for the first two weeks. The plan should also state that the helper will receive a written contract and that the business will comply with the minimum wage and other labour laws.

Roles and responsibilities. Even in a two-person business, the tasks of all the business functions must be allocated. The owner is responsible for general management, finance, purchasing, marketing and the administration of bookings and records. The helper is responsible for production, together with the owner. An organogram, a diagram of the structure, shows the owner at the top and the helper below. As the business grows, the organogram will show more workers, perhaps a team leader for each area.

Gaps and support. A good management plan is honest about gaps in skills. The owner has no bookkeeping experience, so the plan says that the owner will attend a short business course and that a local bookkeeper will check the records once a month for a small fee. An experienced businessperson in the community has agreed to act as a mentor. Showing that you know your weaknesses and have a plan for them builds the reader's confidence.

Working hours and policies. The plan may also set out the working hours, how bookings are managed, and simple policies, for example on safety, customer complaints and the use of the business's equipment. These policies show that the business is organised before it opens.

## Subtopic: The Financial Plan

The financial plan shows how much money the business needs, where it will come from, and whether the business is expected to make a profit. Lenders read this section most closely, so every figure must be realistic, calculated correctly and consistent with the rest of the plan. It matches the financing function.

Start-up costs. These are the once-off costs of opening the business. For Shine on Wheels: a petrol pressure washer R6 500, two 50-litre water tanks R1 800, a vacuum cleaner R2 200, a trolley, buckets, towels and brushes R1 300, a logo, flyers and branded T-shirts R1 200, and a permit and registration R500. Total: 6 500 + 1 800 + 2 200 + 1 300 + 1 200 + 500 = R13 500.

Sources of finance. The owner will contribute R6 000 from personal savings as capital, and a family member will lend R7 500 at no interest, to be repaid over twelve months at 7 500 ÷ 12 = R625 a month. Together that is 6 000 + 7 500 = R13 500, which covers the start-up costs exactly. The plan should also mention other sources that were considered, such as a loan from a bank or a youth development agency, and why they were or were not chosen.

Projected income and expenses. The plan then forecasts a typical month once the business reaches its goal of 120 standard washes. Income: 120 × R120 = R14 400. Variable costs, which change with the number of washes: 120 × R30 = R3 600. Fixed costs, which stay the same each month: the helper's wage R4 000, phone and data R400, storeroom rent R600 and insurance R300, a total of R5 300. Projected profit: 14 400 - 3 600 - 5 300 = R5 500 a month. After repaying the family loan of R625, the owner has 5 500 - 625 = R4 875 left. Note that the loan repayment is not an expense; it reduces a liability, which is why it is subtracted only after the profit is calculated.

Break-even point. The break-even point is the number of washes at which income exactly covers all costs, so there is neither profit nor loss. Each wash brings in R120 and uses R30 of materials, so it contributes R120 - R30 = R90 towards the fixed costs. The fixed costs are R5 300, so the business needs 5 300 ÷ 90 = 58.9 washes, which we round up to 59 washes a month. Check: 59 × R90 = R5 310, which just covers R5 300; 58 × R90 = R5 220, which does not. Below 59 washes Shine on Wheels makes a loss; above 59 it makes a profit. The goal of 120 washes is about double the break-even point, which gives a reasonable safety margin.

A cash budget, which plans month-by-month receipts and payments, is often included as well. In the first months, sales will be lower than 120 washes, so the plan must show that the owner can survive while the business grows. Finally, the plan states its assumptions, such as the number of washes and the price, so that the reader can judge how realistic they are.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Let's make this simple. A business plan has four working parts. One says how you will make or do the thing. One says how you will sell it. One says who will run it. And one says what it will cost and what it will earn.

## Subtopic: How Will We Do It?

The production plan answers: how will we do the work? Shine on Wheels needs a storeroom for R600 a month, a pressure washer, water tanks, a vacuum cleaner and some cleaning products. Each wash uses about R30 of products, water and petrol.

Then it writes down the steps of a wash, like a recipe, so every wash is done the same way. Check the car, rinse, wash, rinse, dry, windows, wheels, final check with the customer. Forty minutes.

Two people can do about five washes a day. Five washes a day for 24 days is 120 washes a month. That is the goal.

And it plans for quality: a checklist for every wash, and a free re-wash if the customer is unhappy.

## Subtopic: Who Buys and Who Runs It?

The marketing plan uses the four Ps. Product: what we sell. Price: R120 a wash, cheaper than the R150 competitor. Place: we go to the customer. Promotion: flyers, WhatsApp groups, photographs and a free wash for referring friends.

The management plan says who is in charge. The owner manages, buys, markets and keeps the books. The helper washes cars with the owner and earns R4 000 a month. The plan also admits what the owner is not good at, like bookkeeping, and says how they will get help: a short course and a bookkeeper once a month.

## Subtopic: Will It Make Money?

The financial plan answers the big question. First, the start-up costs: R13 500 in total for equipment, branding and the permit. Second, where the money comes from: R6 000 in savings and R7 500 borrowed from family.

Third, a normal month: 120 washes at R120 bring in R14 400. Products cost R3 600. Fixed costs, like the helper's wage, rent, phone and insurance, are R5 300. The profit is R5 500.

Fourth, the break-even point. Each wash leaves R90 after products. The fixed costs are R5 300. Divide 5 300 by 90 and round up: 59 washes. Wash fewer than 59 cars a month and you lose money. Wash more and you make money.

If your numbers are the same in all four plans, and they make sense, your plan is ready. Practise by working out the break-even point for your own business idea.
