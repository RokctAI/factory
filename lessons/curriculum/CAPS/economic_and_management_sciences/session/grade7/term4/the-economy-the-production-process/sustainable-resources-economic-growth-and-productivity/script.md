# Part 1 — Expert

This session links production to productivity, economic growth and sustainability. The governing idea is that a country raises its standard of living through economic growth, that growth depends heavily on increasing productivity, and that growth can only last if natural resources are used sustainably so that future generations can also meet their needs. The meaning and measurement of productivity, the meaning and importance of economic growth, sustainable and unsustainable use of resources, and examples from South Africa are examinable.

## Subtopic: Productivity

Productivity measures how efficiently inputs are turned into outputs. It is usually expressed as output per unit of input, for example the number of loaves baked per worker per hour, tonnes of maize harvested per hectare, or cars produced per worker per year.

A business becomes more productive when it produces more output with the same inputs, or the same output with fewer inputs. If a bakery's four bakers used to bake 400 loaves a day and now bake 600 with the same hours, their productivity has risen by fifty percent.

Productivity can be increased in many ways. Better skills and training help workers work faster and make fewer mistakes. Better tools, machines and technology, such as a bigger oven or a dough mixer, let each worker produce more. Better organisation and management reduce waste and waiting time, for example by planning the order of tasks. Healthy, motivated workers who are treated fairly and work in safe conditions tend to be more productive. Reliable infrastructure, such as electricity, water, roads and internet, prevents interruptions; load-shedding, for example, has reduced productivity in many South African businesses.

Higher productivity has important benefits. It lowers the cost of producing each unit, so businesses can lower prices or earn more profit. It allows businesses to pay higher wages without raising prices. It makes local businesses more competitive against imports and in export markets. Across the whole economy, rising productivity is one of the main sources of economic growth and higher living standards.

## Subtopic: Economic Growth

Economic growth is an increase in the total amount of goods and services produced in a country over a period, usually a year. It is measured by the change in gross domestic product, GDP, which is the total value of all final goods and services produced within the country in a year. If GDP, after adjusting for price increases, rises by three percent, the economy has grown by three percent.

Economic growth matters because it is the main way a country raises its standard of living. When the economy grows, businesses produce and sell more, so they employ more people and pay more wages. Households have more income to spend. Government collects more tax without raising tax rates, so it can spend more on schools, clinics, housing and grants. Growth makes it easier to reduce poverty and unemployment.

Growth must also be compared with population growth. If the economy grows by one percent but the population grows by one and a half percent, the average income per person falls. South Africa's economy has grown slowly for more than a decade, often by less than two percent a year, which is one reason why unemployment has stayed so high.

Sources of economic growth include more and better capital, such as factories, machines and infrastructure; more and better-skilled workers; better technology; higher productivity; new businesses started by entrepreneurs; and the discovery and use of natural resources. Stable government, low crime, reliable electricity and transport, and trust in institutions encourage businesses to invest.

Growth is not everything. A country can grow while inequality stays high, or while the environment is damaged. That is why economists also care about how growth is shared and whether it is sustainable.

## Subtopic: Sustainable Use of Resources

Sustainable development means meeting the needs of the present without making it impossible for future generations to meet their own needs. Applied to production, it means using natural resources in a way that they can continue to support production and life in the future.

Natural resources can be renewable or non-renewable. Renewable resources can be replaced naturally over time if they are not overused: water, forests, fish, soil, sunlight and wind. Non-renewable resources exist in fixed amounts and are used up: coal, oil, natural gas and minerals such as gold and platinum.

Unsustainable use damages resources and future production. Over-fishing has reduced stocks of some fish off South Africa's coast, such as abalone, which is also threatened by poaching. Overgrazing and poor farming practices cause soil erosion, washing fertile topsoil into rivers. Mining can pollute water, as with acid mine drainage on the Witwatersrand, and leave scarred land. Burning coal for electricity, which still produces most of South Africa's power, causes air pollution and releases greenhouse gases that contribute to climate change. Wasting water in a water-scarce country threatens farms, industry and households.

Sustainable practices include using renewable energy such as solar and wind; saving water and electricity; recycling and reusing materials; fishing within quotas so stocks can recover; conservation farming that protects soil; replanting forests; rehabilitating land after mining; and protecting ecosystems through national parks and nature reserves. Many sustainable practices also save money and create jobs, as you saw with green jobs in Term 1.

Laws support sustainability: the National Environmental Management Act requires many projects to assess their environmental impact, and fishing is controlled by quotas and permits.

Economic growth and sustainability can support each other when growth comes from cleaner, more efficient production, but there are trade-offs that must be managed fairly, for example for workers in coal mining towns as the country moves towards cleaner energy.

The questions for this section are with you now: the meaning of sustainable development, renewable and non-renewable resources, and examples of unsustainable and sustainable practices.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through one small farm over three seasons, in plain words with the same facts.

## Subtopic: Mama Dube's Productive Farm

Mama Dube farms two hectares of maize in KwaZulu-Natal with two workers.

In her first year she harvests two tonnes of maize per hectare. That is her productivity: output per unit of land.

She joins a farmers' training programme and learns better planting methods. She buys better seed with a small grant and installs drip irrigation, which waters the roots directly. She plans the planting dates more carefully.

By the third year, with the same two hectares and the same two workers, she harvests four tonnes per hectare. Her productivity has doubled.

What changed? Skills, better seed and tools, and better organisation. Not more land, not more workers.

Now she earns more, pays her workers a bit more, and sells maize to the local school feeding scheme. When many farmers and businesses become more productive, the whole country produces more: that is economic growth.

## Subtopic: Will the Farm Still Work in Twenty Years?

Mama Dube's neighbour also wanted bigger harvests, but he did it differently. He ploughed right up to the river bank, pumped as much river water as he could, and used lots of chemical fertiliser every year. For two years his harvest grew. Then the soil started washing away in the rain, the river ran low in winter, and his harvest fell.

That is unsustainable: using resources faster than they can recover.

Mama Dube thinks about twenty years from now. Her drip irrigation uses far less water than flooding the field. She rotates maize with beans, which put nutrients back into the soil, and covers the soil with mulch so rain does not wash it away. She leaves a strip of grass along the river. Last year she replaced her diesel pump with a solar pump, which costs nothing to run and does not pollute.

Her farm is productive today and will still be fertile for her grandchildren. That is sustainable use of resources.

Remember: productivity is more output from the same inputs; economic growth is a country producing more; and sustainability means growing in a way that does not destroy what tomorrow needs.
