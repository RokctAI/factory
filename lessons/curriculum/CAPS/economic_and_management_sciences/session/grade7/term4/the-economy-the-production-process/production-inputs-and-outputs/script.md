# Part 1 — Expert

This session defines production and analyses the production process in terms of inputs, processes and outputs. The governing idea is that production combines the factors of production, natural resources, labour, capital and entrepreneurship, to create goods and services that satisfy needs and wants, and that each factor earns an income in return. The meaning of production, inputs, processes and outputs, the factors of production and their rewards, and the primary, secondary and tertiary sectors are examinable, and they lead into sustainability, productivity and technology.

## Subtopic: What Is Production?

Production is the process of creating goods and services to satisfy people's needs and wants. Growing maize, baking bread, building a house, teaching a class, repairing a car and transporting people are all forms of production. In each case, resources are used to make something of value.

Every production process can be described in three parts. Inputs are the resources that go into the process: raw materials, labour, equipment, energy, water and the entrepreneur's organisation. The process is what is done to the inputs to change them: growing, mixing, cutting, shaping, assembling, cooking, transporting or serving. Outputs are the goods or services that come out at the end.

For a bakery, the inputs include flour, yeast, water, salt, the bakers' labour, ovens, electricity and the owner's management. The process is mixing, kneading, rising and baking. The output is bread. For a school, the inputs include teachers, classrooms, textbooks and learners' time; the process is teaching and learning; the output is educated learners.

Production also creates waste and by-products, such as offcuts, packaging and emissions, which must be managed responsibly, as you learned in Term 1.

At each stage of production, value is added: the output is worth more than the inputs used up. This added value is what pays the people and owners involved in production, and it is the source of a country's income.

Production is linked to scarcity: because resources are limited, producers must use them carefully to get the most output, which is the idea of productivity that the next lesson explores.

## Subtopic: The Factors of Production and Their Rewards

The inputs to production are grouped into four factors of production. Each is owned by someone, and each earns a reward, an income, for its use.

Natural resources, also called land, are everything provided by nature: land itself, soil, water, minerals, forests, fish, sunlight and wind. The reward for natural resources is rent, paid to the owner for their use.

Labour is human effort, both physical and mental, used in production: the work of farmers, miners, factory workers, teachers, nurses, engineers and cleaners. The reward for labour is wages or salaries.

Capital refers to man-made resources used to produce other goods and services: tools, machines, equipment, vehicles, buildings and infrastructure such as roads and power lines. Money that is used to buy these is often called financial capital. The reward for capital is interest, paid to those who provide the money.

Entrepreneurship is the ability and willingness to combine the other three factors, take risks and make decisions in order to produce goods and services. The reward for entrepreneurship is profit, which is what is left after all the other factors have been paid; if there is nothing left, the entrepreneur makes a loss.

These rewards link production to the incomes of households. Households own the factors of production and sell or lend them to businesses; businesses pay rent, wages, interest and profit back to households, who spend that income on goods and services, completing the circular flow you met in Term 1.

## Subtopic: The Three Sectors of Production

Production in an economy is grouped into three sectors, depending on the kind of activity.

The primary sector takes natural resources directly from nature. It includes agriculture, forestry, fishing and mining. South African examples are maize and fruit farming, sheep and cattle farming, fishing off the West Coast, and gold, platinum, coal and iron ore mining.

The secondary sector processes raw materials into finished or semi-finished goods. It includes manufacturing, construction and the production of electricity and water. Examples are car factories in the Eastern Cape and Gauteng, food processing, clothing factories, steel mills, building houses and roads, and power stations.

The tertiary sector provides services rather than goods. It includes trade, transport, communication, banking and insurance, tourism, education, health, government services, and entertainment. Examples are supermarkets, taxis, banks, cellphone networks, hotels, schools and hospitals.

Some economists also speak of a quaternary sector for knowledge-based services, such as research, information technology and consulting.

The sectors are linked in production chains. A cotton farmer, primary, sells to a clothing factory, secondary, which supplies a clothing shop, tertiary. Each depends on the others.

As economies develop, the share of production and jobs usually shifts from the primary sector to the secondary and then to the tertiary sector. In South Africa today, the tertiary sector produces the largest share of output and employs the most people, although mining and agriculture remain very important for exports and for employment in rural areas.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through one wooden school desk from forest to classroom, in plain words with the same facts.

## Subtopic: The Desk's Journey

Look at the wooden desk in your classroom. Where did it come from?

It started as pine trees in a plantation in Mpumalanga. Forestry workers planted, grew and cut them. That is the primary sector: taking from nature.

The logs went by truck to a sawmill, where they were cut into planks. A furniture factory cut, sanded, glued and varnished the planks and fitted metal legs. That is the secondary sector: making things.

A transport company delivered the desks to a school furniture supplier, who sold them to the provincial education department, which delivered them to your school. Transport, selling and government services are the tertiary sector.

At every step there were inputs, a process and an output. For the factory: inputs were planks, glue, varnish, metal, machines, electricity and workers; the process was cutting, sanding and assembling; the output was a desk.

And at every step, value was added. A pine tree is worth much less than a finished desk in a classroom.

## Subtopic: Who Gets Paid?

Everyone who helped make the desk gets a reward.

The plantation stands on land owned by someone. The forestry company pays rent for the land. That is the reward for natural resources.

The forestry workers, sawmill workers, factory workers and truck drivers all get wages. That is the reward for labour.

The factory borrowed money from a bank to buy its cutting machines. It pays the bank interest. That is the reward for capital.

The factory owner had the idea, organised everything and took the risk. If the desks sell for more than all the costs, she earns a profit. That is the reward for entrepreneurship.

Remember: production turns inputs into outputs; land, labour, capital and entrepreneurship are the four factors; rent, wages, interest and profit are their rewards; and primary, secondary and tertiary are the three sectors.
