### subtopic_1 — Productivity
**Assistant:** Does higher productivity mean fewer jobs?

**Tutor:** Sometimes in a particular business, but usually not across the whole economy, and the effects need careful thought. If a factory installs a machine that lets one worker do the work of three, it may need fewer workers for the same output, and some people may lose their jobs. That is a real cost, and it is why change must be managed fairly, with retraining and support. But higher productivity also lowers costs and prices, which means customers can buy more, and the business may expand and sell more, sometimes hiring more workers in the end. Lower prices leave customers with money to spend on other goods and services, creating jobs elsewhere. More productive businesses can pay higher wages, and new technologies create new kinds of jobs, such as technicians, programmers and repairers. Historically, countries that became more productive, such as South Korea, also created many more and better-paid jobs over time. South Africa's challenge is that productivity growth has been slow while unemployment is high, so it needs both: more productive businesses and faster growth that creates jobs. A balanced answer mentions both the risk to some jobs and the long-run benefits.

### subtopic_2 — Economic Growth
**Assistant:** Why has South Africa's economy grown so slowly?

**Tutor:** Economists point to several causes, and they debate how much each matters. Electricity shortages and load-shedding, which became severe from 2008 and especially in 2022 and 2023, cut production in mines, factories and shops and discouraged investment. Problems at state-owned enterprises, such as Eskom and Transnet, which runs the railways and ports, made it harder and more expensive to move goods. Corruption and state capture during the 2010s wasted public money and damaged trust. Low levels of skills, due partly to the history of unequal education, limit productivity. High crime raises business costs. Complicated regulations can make it hard to start and grow businesses. Global events also played a part, such as falling commodity prices, the 2008 financial crisis and the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020. On the positive side, government and business have worked on reforms, such as allowing private electricity generation, which has helped reduce load-shedding, and opening rail and ports to more operators. Different political parties and economists favour different solutions. In an exam, mention several causes, such as electricity, infrastructure, skills and governance, rather than just one.

### subtopic_3 — Sustainable Use of Resources
**Assistant:** Should South Africa stop using coal immediately to protect the environment?

**Tutor:** Most people who study the issue say no, not immediately, but there is wide agreement that the country must shift gradually towards cleaner energy, and debate about how fast. Coal still produces most of South Africa's electricity and supports tens of thousands of jobs in mines and power stations, mostly in Mpumalanga, as well as whole towns that depend on them. Stopping overnight would cause severe electricity shortages and mass job losses. On the other hand, burning coal causes serious air pollution, which harms the health of people living nearby, and releases greenhouse gases that drive climate change, which threatens South Africa with more droughts, floods and heat. Coal is also non-renewable. South Africa has committed to reducing emissions and is building more solar and wind power, which has become cheaper and can be built faster than new coal stations. The idea of a just transition means moving away from coal in a planned way that creates new jobs and training for affected workers and communities, so that they are not left behind. In an exam, a balanced answer recognises the economic importance of coal, its environmental costs and the need for a fair, gradual transition.
