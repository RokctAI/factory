# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines the role of technology in the production process. The governing idea is that technology, meaning the application of knowledge to create tools, machines and methods, raises productivity and changes the mix of labour and capital used in production, bringing benefits such as lower costs and better quality, and challenges such as job losses in some areas and the need for new skills. The meaning of technology, labour-intensive and capital-intensive production, examples of technology in each sector, advantages and disadvantages, and the importance of skills are examinable.

## Subtopic: Technology and How It Changes Production

Technology is the application of scientific knowledge and skills to create tools, machines, materials and methods that solve problems and help people produce goods and services. Technology includes simple things like a hoe or a wheelbarrow, machines like tractors and sewing machines, and advanced systems like computers, robots, the internet and artificial intelligence.

Technology has changed production throughout history. Early farmers used digging sticks and hoes; ploughs pulled by oxen let them farm more land; tractors and combine harvesters let one farmer harvest hundreds of hectares. Spinning wheels and handlooms were replaced by factory machines in the Industrial Revolution. Production lines, introduced in car factories in the early twentieth century, allowed cars to be made quickly and cheaply. Computers and automation transformed factories, banks and offices in the late twentieth century, and the internet has transformed trade and communication in the twenty-first.

Technology is used in every sector today. In the primary sector, farmers use GPS-guided tractors, drip irrigation, weather apps and drones to check crops; mines use drilling machines, conveyor belts and remote-controlled equipment. In the secondary sector, factories use computer-controlled machines, robots and 3D printers. In the tertiary sector, shops use barcode scanners and stock systems, banks use apps and online banking, and schools use computers and online learning.

The main effect of technology on production is to raise productivity: more output per worker and per unit of input. It can also improve quality and consistency, make work safer by having machines do dangerous tasks, and create entirely new products and services, such as cellphones and streaming.

Some people speak of the Fourth Industrial Revolution, the current wave of change driven by artificial intelligence, robotics, the internet of things and big data.

## Subtopic: Labour-Intensive and Capital-Intensive Production

Production methods differ in how much they rely on labour compared with capital.

Labour-intensive production uses a large amount of labour relative to capital. Many workers use simple tools. Examples include hand-picking fruit on a citrus farm, hand-made crafts and beadwork, clothing made by machinists in a small factory, building roads with picks and shovels in public works programmes, and most personal services such as hairdressing and cleaning.

Capital-intensive production uses a large amount of capital, such as machines and technology, relative to labour. Fewer workers operate or supervise machines. Examples include an automated car assembly line with robots welding and painting, a modern bottling plant, a large grain farm using combine harvesters and an oil refinery.

Businesses choose their method by comparing costs and considering the scale of production, the product and the skills available. Where labour is plentiful and cheap compared with machines, and production is small-scale or needs care and flexibility, labour-intensive methods may be better. Where very large quantities of a standard product are needed, where precision is essential, or where labour is expensive or scarce, capital-intensive methods may be better.

South Africa faces a particular challenge. It has very high unemployment, especially among people with few skills, so the country benefits from growth in labour-intensive industries such as agriculture, tourism, construction, clothing and many services, which absorb many workers. At the same time, to compete internationally, many industries must adopt modern technology. Government policies try to balance these needs, for example by supporting labour-intensive sectors and public works, while encouraging skills development so that workers can operate and maintain new technology.

## Subtopic: Advantages, Disadvantages and Skills for the Future

Technology in production brings many advantages. It increases productivity, so more goods and services are produced with the same resources. It lowers the cost per unit, which can lower prices for consumers. It improves quality and consistency, because machines can be very precise. It makes work safer, as machines take over dangerous or heavy tasks, for example in mining. It allows businesses to produce new products and reach customers through the internet, and lets small businesses, such as a farmer using a weather app or a crafter selling online, compete more widely. It can reduce waste and pollution, for example with efficient machines and renewable energy.

There are also disadvantages and challenges. Some jobs, especially routine ones such as some factory, clerical and checkout jobs, can be replaced by machines and software, causing unemployment for workers whose skills are no longer needed. New technology is expensive to buy, install and maintain, which can be hard for small businesses. It depends on reliable electricity and internet, which are not always available in South Africa. Workers must be trained to use it. Rapid change can widen inequality between those with digital skills and access and those without. There are also concerns about privacy and data security as more information is collected digitally.

Technology also creates new jobs: in designing, building, programming, installing, repairing and selling technology, and in new industries that did not exist before, such as app development, drone operation, solar installation and online marketing.

To succeed in a changing economy, young people need skills that complement technology: strong reading, mathematics and science; digital skills; problem-solving, creativity and critical thinking; communication and teamwork; and the willingness to keep learning throughout life. Technical and vocational skills, such as those of electricians and mechatronics technicians who install and maintain machines, are in high demand.

## Subtopic: The Durban Shirt Factory

A clothing factory in Durban makes school shirts. Five years ago it had 50 workers cutting fabric by hand with scissors and sewing on old machines. Together they made 500 shirts a day.

Then the owner bought new technology: a computer-controlled cutting machine that cuts dozens of layers of fabric at once with perfect accuracy, and modern sewing machines that are faster and break down less.

Now the factory has 40 workers and makes 1 200 shirts a day. Each worker produces three times as many shirts as before: productivity has more than doubled. The shirts are more even in size, so fewer are rejected, and the factory can sell them more cheaply than imported shirts.

The factory moved from being very labour-intensive towards being more capital-intensive: more machines, fewer workers per shirt.

Because its shirts are now cheaper and better, the factory wins a big contract from a school uniform shop chain. Next year it plans to hire more workers for a second shift.

## Subtopic: Ten Workers and a New Future

Not everyone at the factory was happy. Ten workers who used to cut fabric by hand lost their jobs when the computer cutter arrived. That is the hard side of technology.

The factory did three things. It retrained some cutters to operate the new sewing machines. It hired a machine technician to maintain the cutter, a new job that did not exist before. And it hired a young designer who uses computer software to create shirt patterns. Two of the workers who left found jobs at a solar company after a short course.

So technology took away some jobs, created others and changed many more. The new jobs need different skills: working with computers, maintaining machines, solving problems.

That is why your maths, reading, science and computer skills matter. The machines will keep getting smarter, and the people who can work with them, fix them and come up with new ideas will be in demand.

Remember: technology raises productivity, lowers costs and improves quality, but can replace some jobs; labour-intensive uses many workers, capital-intensive uses many machines; and the answer for workers is skills and lifelong learning.
