### subtopic_1 — Technology and How It Changes Production
**Assistant:** Is a simple tool like a wheelbarrow really technology?

**Tutor:** Yes. Technology does not have to be electronic or modern; it is any application of knowledge to make a task easier or more productive. A wheelbarrow uses the principle of the lever and the wheel to let one person carry a load that would otherwise need two or three people, so it raises productivity in building, gardening and farming. The same is true of the plough, the hoe, the needle, the pulley and the water pump. These are sometimes called appropriate technology when they suit the needs, skills and resources of the people using them. In many parts of South Africa and Africa, simple, affordable technologies make a big difference: a hand-operated maize sheller, a treadle pump for irrigation, a solar lamp or a basic cellphone with a banking or weather app. They are cheaper to buy and repair than complex machines and do not need constant electricity. Advanced technologies such as robots are useful where large volumes justify their cost. In an exam, you can define technology broadly and give examples ranging from simple tools to computers, and explain that the right technology depends on the situation.

### subtopic_2 — Labour-Intensive and Capital-Intensive Production
**Assistant:** Should South African businesses avoid machines to protect jobs?

**Tutor:** Not as a general rule, because avoiding technology can end up destroying more jobs than it protects. If a South African factory refuses to modernise while competitors in other countries use efficient machines, its costs will be higher and its quality lower, and it may lose customers to imports and close, so all its jobs are lost. Modern technology can keep a business competitive, help it grow and allow it to employ people in new roles such as machine operators, technicians and sales staff. That said, the choice of technology matters a great deal in a country with very high unemployment. Where labour-intensive methods are genuinely efficient, for example in fruit picking, tourism, construction and many services, encouraging them creates jobs. Government can support labour-intensive sectors, public employment programmes and small businesses, and invest in skills so that workers can move into new jobs as technology changes. The aim is not to choose between jobs and technology, but to have growing, productive businesses that create many jobs, with workers who have the skills to fill them. In an exam, explain the trade-off and the need for both competitiveness and job creation.

### subtopic_3 — Advantages, Disadvantages and Skills for the Future
**Assistant:** Will artificial intelligence take all the jobs?

**Tutor:** No one can predict the future exactly, but most economists do not expect artificial intelligence to take all jobs. They do expect it to change many jobs and replace some tasks. AI systems are good at tasks that involve recognising patterns in large amounts of data, writing routine text, answering standard questions and some kinds of analysis. That means some jobs, or parts of them, in areas like data capturing, basic customer service and simple document work may be reduced. At the same time, AI is a tool that can make many workers more productive: a doctor who uses AI to help read scans, a farmer who uses an app to detect crop disease, or a small business owner who uses it to write adverts. New jobs are also created in building, managing and checking AI systems. Many jobs depend heavily on human skills that are hard to automate: caring for people, hands-on technical work like plumbing and electrical installation, creativity, leadership, negotiating and building trust. History shows that past waves of technology destroyed some jobs but created others, though the change was painful for those affected. The best preparation is strong basic skills, digital literacy and a habit of continuous learning.
