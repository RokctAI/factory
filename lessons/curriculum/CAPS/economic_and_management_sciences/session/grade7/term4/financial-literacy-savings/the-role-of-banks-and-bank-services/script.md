# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines the role of banks in the economy and the services they provide. The governing idea is that banks are financial intermediaries that accept deposits from savers and lend them to borrowers, earning a profit from the difference between the interest they charge and the interest they pay, while providing payment and other services that make the economy work. The functions of banks, the main types of accounts and services, interest and bank charges, and the regulation and safety of banks in South Africa are examinable.

## Subtopic: Banks as Financial Intermediaries

A bank is a business that accepts deposits of money from customers, keeps it safe, makes payments on their behalf and lends money to individuals, businesses and government.

The central role of a bank is to be a financial intermediary, which means a go-between. On one side are savers: households and businesses that have money they do not need right now. On the other side are borrowers: people who want to buy a house or car, students who need fees, and businesses that want to buy equipment or expand. Savers and borrowers usually do not know each other, and a saver would not want to lend directly to a stranger. The bank brings them together: it collects many small deposits and lends them out in loans of the sizes borrowers need.

Banks pay savers interest on their deposits, as a reward for letting the bank use their money. Banks charge borrowers interest on loans, at a higher rate. The difference between the interest earned on loans and the interest paid on deposits, together with fees for services, covers the bank's costs, such as staff, branches, technology and security, and covers losses when some borrowers do not repay. What remains is the bank's profit.

Banks do not keep all deposits as cash in a vault. They keep enough cash and reserves to meet normal withdrawals, and lend the rest. This works because, on any day, only a small share of depositors withdraw their money.

Through lending, banks help households buy homes and businesses grow, which creates jobs and economic growth. Through payment services, they let money move quickly and safely around the economy.

## Subtopic: Bank Accounts and Services

Banks offer a wide range of services to individuals and businesses.

Transactional accounts, sometimes called current or cheque accounts, are used for everyday banking: receiving a salary, paying accounts by debit order or EFT, buying with a debit card and drawing cash. They usually pay little or no interest and have monthly fees or transaction fees.

Savings accounts pay interest on money saved and are designed for keeping money rather than frequent payments. Fixed deposits pay a higher interest rate if the money is left for a fixed period, such as six months or a year. Notice deposits require notice, such as 32 days, before withdrawal and pay more than an ordinary savings account. Some banks offer savings accounts for children and young people with low or no fees.

Loans and credit: home loans, called bonds, to buy property; vehicle finance to buy cars; personal loans; overdrafts, which allow an account to go below zero up to a limit; credit cards; student loans; and business loans to buy equipment or stock.

Payment and convenience services: debit and credit cards, ATMs, electronic funds transfers, debit orders and stop orders, cellphone and app banking, instant payments, card machines for businesses, and prepaid electricity and airtime purchases.

Other services: foreign exchange for people travelling or businesses importing and exporting; safe custody of documents and valuables; insurance; investment products such as unit trusts; and financial advice.

Businesses use banks for business accounts, merchant services to accept card payments, cash deposit services and loans.

Choosing the right account depends on what you need: someone who wants to save should use a savings account; someone who receives a salary and pays accounts needs a transactional account.

## Subtopic: Interest, Bank Charges and Choosing a Bank

When you deal with a bank, money flows both ways in the form of interest and charges.

Interest earned: the bank pays interest on savings accounts and deposits. The rate depends on the type of account, how long the money is left and the general level of interest rates in the economy, which is influenced by the South African Reserve Bank's repo rate.

Interest paid: when you borrow, you pay interest on the amount owed. Credit cards, store cards and personal loans usually have much higher interest rates than home loans, because they are riskier for the lender. Interest on loans is the main cost of borrowing.

Bank charges: banks charge fees for their services. These may include a monthly account fee, fees for each transaction, such as withdrawals at ATMs, especially other banks' ATMs, cash deposits, or EFTs, fees for SMS notifications and penalties for bounced debit orders. Some accounts have a fixed monthly fee covering a bundle of transactions; others charge per transaction.

Bank charges can add up, especially for people with low incomes. Ways to keep them low include choosing an account that suits how you bank, using digital banking and card payments rather than cash withdrawals where possible, drawing cash at your own bank's ATM or at a till point when buying groceries, avoiding bounced debit orders by keeping enough money in the account, and checking the bank's fee guide.

When choosing a bank and account, compare: monthly and transaction fees; interest rates on savings; minimum balance requirements; convenience, such as branches, ATMs and app quality near where you live; and the quality of service. Banks must publish their fees, and comparison tools are available.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through one R1 000 deposit and where it travels, in plain words with the same facts.

## Subtopic: Where Does My R1 000 Go?

Gogo Mabaso deposits R1 000 in her savings account. The bank promises to pay her about five percent interest a year. After a year she will have about R1 050.

But the bank does not lock her R1 000 in a safe. It joins it with deposits from thousands of other savers and lends some of it to Mr Petersen, who wants to buy a bigger oven for his bakery. Mr Petersen pays the bank about twelve percent interest a year.

With the new oven, Mr Petersen bakes more bread and hires another worker. That worker earns wages and spends them in local shops.

The bank earns twelve percent from Mr Petersen and pays five percent to Gogo. The difference pays for the bank's staff, buildings, computers and security, covers the few borrowers who do not pay back, and leaves the bank a profit.

That is what a bank is: a go-between that takes money from people who want to save and lends it to people who want to borrow, and both sides benefit.

## Subtopic: Choose the Right Account

Different people need different bank services.

Gogo Mabaso wants to keep her money safe and earn interest: a savings account. She has R5 000 she will not need for a year, so she puts that in a fixed deposit, which pays more because she promises to leave it there.

Her grandson Lwazi has just started his first job. His salary is paid into a transactional account, and he uses his debit card and the bank app to pay rent, buy electricity and send money home. He sets up a stop order to move R300 into a savings account on payday: paying himself first.

Mr Petersen's bakery has a business account and a card machine so customers can tap to pay, and a loan for the oven.

All of them watch the fees. Lwazi draws cash at the supermarket till when he buys groceries instead of at another bank's ATM, and checks his statement every month for any debit order he did not agree to.

Remember: banks are go-betweens for savers and borrowers; they offer accounts, loans and payment services; they pay interest on savings and charge more on loans; and they are regulated to keep money safe.
