### subtopic_1 — Banks as Financial Intermediaries
**Assistant:** If a bank lends out my deposit, is my money safe?

**Tutor:** Under normal circumstances, yes, and there are several layers of protection. First, banks lend to many different borrowers and are required to assess whether borrowers can repay, so that losses from a few bad loans are covered by income from the many good ones. Second, banks must hold a minimum amount of capital, their own money that can absorb losses, and keep enough liquid assets to meet withdrawals. In South Africa these rules are set and enforced by the Prudential Authority, which is part of the South African Reserve Bank. Third, since April 2024, the Corporation for Deposit Insurance protects qualifying deposits up to a limit, R100 000 per depositor per bank, if a bank fails, which covers the full balance of most ordinary savers. Fourth, the Reserve Bank can lend to banks that face a temporary shortage of cash. Bank failures are rare, though they have happened, for example VBS Mutual Bank collapsed in 2018 because of fraud. That case showed both the risks and why regulation matters. The practical lesson is to use registered banks, which can be checked with the Reserve Bank, and to be wary of unregistered schemes that promise bank-like safety.

### subtopic_2 — Bank Accounts and Services
**Assistant:** What is the difference between a debit order and a stop order?

**Tutor:** Both are ways of making regular payments automatically from a bank account, but they are set up differently. A stop order is an instruction that you give to your own bank to pay a fixed amount to someone on a regular date, for example R200 on the first of every month into your savings account or to a stokvel's account. You control it, and you can change or cancel it with your bank. A debit order is an instruction you give to another organisation, such as an insurance company, a gym, a cellphone company or a loan provider, allowing it to collect money from your account on a regular date. The amount may vary, for example a cellphone bill. You sign a mandate to authorise it. Debit orders are convenient, but unauthorised debit orders, where money is taken without proper permission, are a common problem. You have the right to dispute a debit order you did not authorise, and banks have processes to reverse such debits. Checking your bank statement every month helps you spot them quickly. For savings, a stop order or scheduled transfer on payday is a good way to pay yourself first automatically.

### subtopic_3 — Interest, Bank Charges and Choosing a Bank
**Assistant:** Why do credit cards charge much more interest than home loans?

**Tutor:** Mainly because of risk and security. A home loan is secured by the house: if the borrower stops paying, the bank can, after a legal process, sell the house to recover the money. That security makes the loan less risky, so the bank can charge a lower interest rate. A credit card or store card is usually unsecured: there is no specific asset the bank can take if the borrower does not pay, so the lender faces a higher risk of losing money. Credit card balances are also small, flexible and costly to administer per rand lent. To cover these risks and costs, lenders charge higher rates. In South Africa the National Credit Act sets maximum interest rates for different types of credit, and unsecured credit has a much higher maximum than home loans. The practical lesson is important: carrying a balance on a credit card or store card month after month is one of the most expensive ways to borrow. If you use a credit card, paying the full balance by the due date avoids interest altogether. Expensive short-term debt is a major cause of financial stress in many households.
