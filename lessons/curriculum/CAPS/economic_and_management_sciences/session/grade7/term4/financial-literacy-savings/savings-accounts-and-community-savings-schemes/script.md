# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the practical process of opening a savings account and the role of community savings schemes in South Africa. The governing idea is that formal savings accounts offer safety, interest and regulation, while community schemes such as stokvels and burial societies offer discipline, mutual support and access for people who may be underserved by banks, and that each has advantages and risks. The requirements and steps for opening a savings account, reading a bank statement, the types and functions of community savings schemes, and the comparison between formal and community saving are examinable.

## Subtopic: Opening a Savings Account

Opening a savings account in South Africa follows a few steps.

First, choose a bank and an account. Compare fees, interest rates, minimum opening deposits and minimum balances, and whether the account can be managed on a cellphone. Many banks offer savings accounts for children and teenagers with low or no monthly fees.

Second, gather the required documents. The Financial Intelligence Centre Act, known as FICA, requires banks to verify the identity and address of customers, to prevent fraud and money laundering. Adults usually need a South African identity document or smart ID card, or a passport for non-citizens, and proof of residential address, such as a recent municipal account, a lease agreement or a letter from a school or ward councillor confirming where the person lives. Many banks now simplify proof of address for low-value accounts.

Third, for a learner under eighteen, a parent or legal guardian usually opens the account with the child, providing the child's birth certificate or ID and the parent's own ID and proof of address. The parent may need to sign as well.

Fourth, complete the application, at a branch, online or in the bank's app. Some banks require an opening deposit.

Fifth, receive the account number and, depending on the account, a card, online banking access and the app. Choose a secret PIN and password and never share them.

Finally, start saving: deposit money at a branch, ATM, retail till point or by EFT, or set up a regular stop order or scheduled transfer.

Once the account is open, the bank provides a bank statement, monthly or on request, which lists every deposit, withdrawal, interest payment and fee, with the balance after each. Checking it helps the saver track progress, notice fees and spot any transaction that looks wrong.

## Subtopic: Stokvels, Savings Clubs and Burial Societies

Long before many South Africans had access to banks, communities created their own ways to save together. These community savings schemes are still very popular today, with millions of members.

A stokvel is a group of people who agree to contribute a fixed amount of money regularly, usually monthly, into a common pool. The name is believed to come from the stock fairs, cattle auctions held in the Eastern Cape in the nineteenth century. There are several types.

In a rotating stokvel, each month the whole pool is paid to one member in turn, so each member eventually receives a large lump sum. If ten members each contribute R500 a month, each month one member receives R5 000.

In a savings stokvel or savings club, the contributions are saved, often in a bank account in the group's name, and shared out with any interest at an agreed time, often in December.

In a grocery stokvel, members contribute through the year and then buy groceries in bulk together at the end of the year, often getting discounts from wholesalers, and share them out.

Investment stokvels pool money to invest in property, shares or a business.

A burial society is a group whose members make regular contributions so that when a member or a member's family dies, the society pays towards funeral costs and often helps with food, tents and support. Funerals in South Africa can be expensive, and burial societies prevent families from falling into debt.

Community schemes are built on trust, written or agreed rules, a constitution, elected office bearers such as a chairperson, secretary and treasurer, regular meetings and records of contributions. Members support each other socially as well as financially.

## Subtopic: Comparing Bank Saving and Community Schemes

Both formal savings accounts and community schemes help people save, but they have different strengths and risks.

Savings in a bank account are regulated by the Prudential Authority and protected by deposit insurance up to a limit, so the risk of loss is very low. They earn interest. The saver can usually access the money when needed. Records are kept automatically. But bank accounts may charge fees, require documents, and offer no social pressure to keep saving, so it is easy to withdraw money for wants.

Community schemes provide strong discipline and mutual support, lump sums without interest charges, bulk-buying power and help in emergencies such as funerals. They are flexible and run by people the members know. But they also have risks. If money is kept in cash, it can be stolen. A treasurer or member may disappear with the money, or a member may receive the payout early and then stop contributing. Records may be poor, leading to disputes. Most stokvels are not regulated in the same way as banks, so there is little legal protection. In a rotating stokvel, money waiting for a later turn earns no interest.

Good practice reduces these risks: write a constitution with clear rules about contributions, payouts, late payments and what happens if someone leaves; elect trustworthy office bearers and require two signatures for withdrawals; keep written records and share them at every meeting; and keep the money in a stokvel bank account rather than in cash, so that it is safe and earns interest.

Many families use both: a personal savings account for their own goals and emergencies, and a stokvel or burial society for discipline, community and specific purposes. Choosing well means understanding the benefits and risks of each.

Beware of fake stokvels or schemes that promise very high returns or require recruiting new members; these are often pyramid schemes, not genuine stokvels.

Now the same lesson again through one learner opening her first account and her mother's stokvel, in plain words with the same facts.

## Subtopic: Ayanda Opens Her First Account

Ayanda is twelve and has saved R50 from her birthday. She wants to keep it safe and earn interest, so her mother takes her to open a savings account.

Before going, they check the bank's website. They need Ayanda's birth certificate, her mother's ID and proof of address, a recent municipal account. These are FICA documents: the bank must know who its customers are.

At the bank, the consultant explains the youth savings account: no monthly fee, a little interest every month, and an app her mother can help her use. Her mother signs as guardian.

Ayanda deposits her R50. She chooses a secret PIN and the consultant reminds her: never share it with anyone, not even someone who says they work for the bank.

A month later, her first statement arrives in the app: deposit R50, interest 21 cents, balance R50,21. Small, but it is growing. She sets herself a goal of adding R20 every month.

## Subtopic: Mama's Stokvel

Ayanda's mother belongs to a stokvel with eleven friends from church. Twelve members each pay R500 on the last Sunday of every month. That is R6 000 a month.

Each month one member gets the whole R6 000. Last March it was Mama's turn: she used it to pay for building a new room. This December it will be someone else's turn for school uniforms and groceries.

The stokvel has written rules signed by everyone: how much, when, the payout order, and a fine for paying late. They elected a chairperson, a secretary who writes down every payment, and a treasurer. The money goes into a stokvel bank account straight away, and two people must sign to take any out.

Gogo belongs to a burial society. She pays every month, and when a family member dies, the society helps pay for the funeral so the family does not have to borrow.

Ayanda's family uses both: her bank account for her own goals, Mama's stokvel for big lump sums and support, and Gogo's burial society for peace of mind.

Remember: open an account with your documents and keep your PIN secret; stokvels and burial societies help communities save together; and keeping the group's money in a bank with clear rules keeps it safe.
