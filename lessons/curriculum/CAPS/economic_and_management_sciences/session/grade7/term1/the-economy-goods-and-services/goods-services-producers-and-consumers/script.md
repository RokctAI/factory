# Part 1 — Expert

This session distinguishes goods from services and producers from consumers. Goods are physical, tangible products; services are intangible activities performed for others. Producers create goods and services, and consumers use them to satisfy needs and wants. The governing idea is that the economy is a network of exchanges between producers and consumers, and that most people play both roles. The definitions, examples, types of goods, and the relationship between producers and consumers in the circular flow are examinable, and they lead into the efficient use of goods and services.

## Subtopic: Goods and Services

Everything people buy to satisfy their needs and wants is either a good or a service.

Goods are physical, tangible products: you can see them, touch them, store them and own them. Bread, school shoes, cellphones, maize, cement and cars are goods. Goods can be counted, transported and sold again.

Services are intangible: they are activities that someone performs for you. You cannot put a service in a cupboard. A haircut, a taxi ride, a lesson from a teacher, a doctor's consultation, banking, insurance, cellphone airtime used for a call, and the work of a plumber are all services. A service is usually produced and used at the same time; the taxi ride is produced while you travel.

Goods can be grouped in useful ways. Consumer goods are bought by households for their own use, such as food and clothing. Capital goods are used by businesses to produce other goods and services, such as a bakery's oven, a farmer's tractor or a hairdresser's chair. The same item can be either, depending on who uses it and why: a car bought by a family is a consumer good, while a car bought by a taxi business is a capital good.

Goods can also be durable or non-durable. Durable goods last a long time and are used many times, such as fridges, furniture and bicycles. Non-durable goods are used up quickly or in one go, such as food, toothpaste and electricity.

Many purchases combine both. At a restaurant you buy goods, the food, and services, the cooking and serving. When you buy a fridge, delivery and the guarantee are services.

The questions for this section are with you now: the difference between goods and services, and consumer, capital, durable and non-durable goods.

## Subtopic: Producers and Consumers

A producer is a person or business that makes goods or provides services. Farmers, factories, bakers, builders, teachers, nurses, hairdressers, taxi owners and spaza shop owners are producers. Producers use resources such as land, labour and capital to create something people want, and they usually sell it to earn an income or a profit.

A consumer is a person who buys and uses goods and services to satisfy needs and wants. When you buy a pie, catch a taxi or pay for data, you are a consumer.

Producers are often grouped by the kind of work they do. Primary producers take resources directly from nature: farmers, fishers, miners and foresters. Secondary producers turn raw materials into finished goods: millers, bakers, clothing factories, car manufacturers and builders. Tertiary producers provide services: shops, transport, banks, schools, hospitals and hairdressers.

Producers and consumers need each other. Producers need consumers to buy what they produce, or they will not earn an income. Consumers need producers to supply the goods and services they cannot make themselves. This exchange is what an economy is.

Most people are both. A baker produces bread during the day, then becomes a consumer when she buys groceries and pays for her son's school transport. A learner is mainly a consumer, but when she sells sweets at break or helps in a family business she is also a producer.

The questions for this section are with you now: the meaning of producers and consumers, primary, secondary and tertiary producers, and why most people play both roles.

## Subtopic: From Farm to Table: the Production Chain

Most goods pass through many producers before they reach a consumer. Following one product shows how the three kinds of producers connect in a production chain.

Take a loaf of brown bread. A farmer in the Western Cape or the Free State grows wheat. That is primary production. The wheat is harvested and trucked to a mill, where it is ground into flour. A bakery mixes the flour with water, yeast and salt and bakes the bread. Milling and baking are secondary production. Trucks deliver the bread to supermarkets and spaza shops, and the shop sells it to you. Transport and retail are tertiary production. Along the way, banks lend money to the farmer and the baker, insurers protect the trucks and advertisers tell you about the brand; all of these are tertiary services too.

At every stage, value is added. A bag of wheat is worth less than the flour made from it, and the flour is worth less than the bread on the shelf. Each producer earns an income for the value it adds, and each employs workers who then become consumers themselves.

The same chain exists for almost everything you buy. A cotton T-shirt starts with a cotton farmer, passes through spinning, weaving, dyeing and sewing factories, and ends in a clothing shop. A cellphone starts with mines that dig metals out of the ground, including in South Africa, and passes through factories in several countries before it reaches a cellphone store.

The questions for this section are with you now: tracing a product through primary, secondary and tertiary production, and the idea of adding value.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through one Saturday on a busy township street, in plain words with the same facts.

## Subtopic: Saturday on Vilakazi Street

It is Saturday morning and Ayanda walks down a busy township street with R150.

First he buys two vetkoek from Mama Joyce's stall. He can hold them and take them home: goods. Then he stops at the barber for a haircut. He cannot take the haircut home in a bag; the barber did something for him: a service. Next he buys a bag of oranges from a fruit seller: goods. He tops up his phone with airtime and data: he pays the network to carry his calls and messages, so that is a service. On the way back he catches a taxi: the ride is a service. He passes a car wash where young men wash cars for R40: a service again.

The test is simple. Can I touch it, keep it and take it home? Yes means a good. No, someone did something for me, means a service.

By the end of the morning Ayanda has spent his money on both. Every street in South Africa is full of goods and services being bought and sold.

Try the questions for this part: sort Ayanda's morning into goods and services.

## Subtopic: Everyone Wears Two Hats

Now look again at the people Ayanda met. Mama Joyce makes and sells vetkoek, so she is a producer. But early that morning she went to the wholesaler and bought flour, oil and yeast, so she was also a consumer. The barber produces haircuts all day, and at lunchtime he buys a pie from Mama Joyce: now he is the consumer.

Ayanda himself is mostly a consumer. But in the afternoon he helps his uncle wash cars at the car wash and earns R100. For those hours, he is a producer.

So almost everyone wears two hats: a producer hat when they work and earn, and a consumer hat when they spend. The money Ayanda pays Mama Joyce becomes her income; she spends part of it at the wholesaler; the wholesaler pays its workers; and the workers buy vetkoek. Round and round it goes.

Remember: goods you can touch, services someone does for you; producers make, consumers use, and most people do both.

Try the questions for this part: say which hat each person is wearing.
