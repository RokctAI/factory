# Part 1 — Expert

This session links scarcity to responsible consumption. Because resources are limited, households, businesses and government must use goods and services efficiently, getting the most from each resource, and effectively, achieving what they set out to achieve. The governing idea is that reducing waste, reusing goods and recycling materials lets scarce resources satisfy more needs and wants, protects the environment and can create income. The meaning of efficient and effective use, the waste hierarchy of reduce, reuse and recycle, and examples from South Africa are examinable.

## Subtopic: Using Goods and Services Efficiently and Effectively

Because resources are scarce, every household, business and government must get as much as possible out of what it has.

Using goods and services efficiently means getting the most use out of a resource with the least waste. A household uses electricity efficiently when it switches off lights in empty rooms, boils only the water it needs in the kettle and uses energy-saving light bulbs. It uses water efficiently when it fixes dripping taps, takes short showers and collects rainwater for the garden. It uses food efficiently when it plans meals, stores food properly and uses leftovers.

Using goods and services effectively means using them in a way that actually achieves the purpose. A learner who buys a calculator but never learns to use it has not used it effectively. A family that pays for a taxi to the clinic but arrives after it has closed has used the service ineffectively. Effectiveness is about doing the right thing; efficiency is about doing it without waste.

Looking after goods so that they last is also efficient. Covering school books, polishing shoes, repairing a torn school jersey instead of throwing it away, and servicing a bicycle make durable goods last longer, so the household does not have to replace them as often.

Businesses and government must also be efficient. A business that wastes raw materials has higher costs and lower profits. Government that wastes money on unnecessary spending has less left for schools and clinics. In South Africa, reports by the Auditor-General each year identify wasteful and unauthorised spending in some departments and municipalities.

The questions for this section are with you now: the difference between efficient and effective use, and examples of each in a household.

## Subtopic: Reduce, Reuse and Recycle

Waste is a problem for families and for the environment. Every time we throw something away, we lose the resources used to make it, and the rubbish must go somewhere. South Africa produces many millions of tonnes of general waste a year, and most of it still goes to landfill sites, which are filling up. Litter blocks drains, pollutes rivers and the sea, and harms animals.

The waste hierarchy is a simple guide to dealing with waste, in order of preference.

Reduce comes first: use less and buy less in the first place. Buy only what you need, choose products with less packaging, take your own shopping bag, and repair things before replacing them. Reducing saves money as well as resources.

Reuse comes next: use an item again, for the same purpose or a new one. Glass jars become storage containers, plastic bottles become plant pots or water bottles, old clothes are handed down to younger family members or donated, and school textbooks are passed on to next year's learners. Second-hand shops and markets help goods find new users.

Recycle comes third: collect used materials so that they can be processed into new products. Paper, cardboard, glass, cans, many plastics and old electronics can be recycled. Aluminium cans and glass bottles can be recycled many times. Recycling uses less energy and fewer raw materials than making products from scratch.

Only what cannot be reduced, reused or recycled should be disposed of, safely, in a bin or a proper landfill. Organic waste such as vegetable peels can be composted to make fertiliser for gardens.

The questions for this section are with you now: the reasons waste is a problem, and the meaning and order of reduce, reuse and recycle.

## Subtopic: Recycling as an Economic Opportunity

Waste can also be a resource that creates income. In South Africa, tens of thousands of informal waste pickers, sometimes called reclaimers, collect recyclable materials such as cardboard, paper, plastic and cans from streets and bins and sell them to buy-back centres, which pay per kilogram. Studies suggest that waste pickers collect most of the paper and packaging that is recycled in the country, saving municipalities large amounts of landfill space and money. Many work long hours for low pay, and some municipalities now work with waste picker organisations to register them, give them safety equipment and include them in recycling programmes.

Buy-back centres sort and bale the materials and sell them to recycling factories, which turn them into new products: cardboard becomes new boxes, PET plastic bottles become new bottles or polyester fibre for clothing and pillows, and aluminium cans become new cans.

Small businesses and entrepreneurs upcycle waste, which means turning it into products of higher value. Examples include bags and mats woven from plastic shopping bags, jewellery from bottle tops, furniture from pallets and art from wire and cans that is sold to tourists. Some schools run recycling competitions and use the money for school projects.

Recycling therefore links the environment and the economy. It conserves scarce natural resources, reduces pollution, creates jobs for people with few other options and can become the basis of a small business. This is a good example of how the same goods can satisfy more needs and wants when we use them wisely.

The questions for this section are with you now: the role of waste pickers and buy-back centres, the meaning of upcycling, and the economic benefits of recycling.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through one family's rubbish bin and one week, in plain words with the same facts.

## Subtopic: What Is in Our Bin?

Nomsa's class has homework: look inside your family's rubbish bin for one week. On Sunday night she tips it out on newspaper, wearing gloves.

She finds stale bread and half a pot of rice that went off. That is wasted food and wasted money. Next week the family plans meals, keeps bread in a bread bin and turns vegetable peels into compost for Gogo's spinach patch. That is reduce.

She finds glass jars and plastic tubs. Her mother washes them and uses them for sugar, beans and leftovers. That is reuse.

She finds cans, cardboard boxes, newspapers and plastic bottles. Nomsa puts them in a separate bag. Every Thursday Mr Khumalo, a waste picker, collects them and sells them at the buy-back centre. That is recycle.

By the end of the second week, the family's bin is half as full. They also spend less on food because less goes off. Using goods efficiently saved money and helped the environment.

Try the questions for this part: decide what to do with each item in the bin.

## Subtopic: Switch Off, Fix It, Pass It On

Efficient use is not only about rubbish. Nomsa's family makes three more habits.

Switch off. Lights go off when nobody is in a room, the kettle is filled only with the water needed, and the TV is switched off at the wall at night. The electricity runs out more slowly, and the family buys less.

Fix it. A dripping tap gets a new washer instead of dripping all month. A torn school jersey is stitched. A bicycle chain is oiled. Things last longer, so the family buys fewer new ones.

Pass it on. Nomsa's old school uniform goes to her cousin, and her Grade 6 textbooks go to the school's book bank for next year's class.

Remember the two words. Efficient: no waste. Effective: it does the job. A family that switches off, fixes and passes on is using its goods efficiently and effectively, so the same income satisfies more needs.

Try the questions for this part: choose the efficient habit in each situation.
