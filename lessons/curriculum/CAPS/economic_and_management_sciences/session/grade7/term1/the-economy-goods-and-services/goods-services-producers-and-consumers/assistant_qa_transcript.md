### subtopic_1 — Goods and Services
**Assistant:** Is cellphone data a good or a service?

**Tutor:** It is best classified as a service, even though you buy it in bundles that look like a product. When you buy data, you are paying a network company to carry information to and from your phone over its towers and cables. You cannot touch the data, store it in a cupboard or hand it to someone as a physical object; what you are buying is the network's performance of that activity. The phone itself is a good, a tangible durable product. The SIM card is a small good, but it is really a key that gives you access to the service. This is a useful example because many modern purchases mix goods and services. A streaming subscription is a service; the television you watch it on is a good. A meal at a restaurant combines goods, the food, and services, the cooking and waiting. In an exam, if you are asked to classify an item, use the test: can I touch it, store it and own it as a thing? If yes, it is a good. If what I am really paying for is someone's work or an activity, it is a service.

### subtopic_2 — Producers and Consumers
**Assistant:** Why do producers and consumers need each other?

**Tutor:** Because the economy only works when goods and services move from those who make them to those who use them, and money moves back the other way. Imagine a bakery that bakes a thousand loaves but has no customers: the bread goes stale, the baker earns nothing, cannot pay workers or buy flour, and soon closes. Now imagine a town with hungry people but no bakery, farm or shop: the people have money but cannot buy food. Producers need consumers' spending to earn income; consumers need producers' output to satisfy needs and wants. Economists show this with the circular flow. Households, which are consumers, sell their labour to businesses, which are producers, and receive wages. Households spend those wages on goods and services from businesses. The money flows in a circle, and goods, services and labour flow the other way. When consumers spend less, for example during a recession, producers sell less and may cut jobs, so households earn less and spend even less. That is why a healthy economy needs both strong production and steady spending.

### subtopic_3 — From Farm to Table: the Production Chain
**Assistant:** Why does the farmer get only a small part of the price of a loaf of bread?

**Tutor:** Because many other producers add value after the farmer, and each must be paid. When you pay for a loaf of brown bread, part of the price pays for the wheat, but other parts pay for milling, packaging, baking, the baker's electricity and ovens, the wages of bakery workers, transport and fuel, the shop's rent, staff and electricity, and VAT is not charged on brown bread because it is zero-rated, which keeps the price lower. Each business also needs a profit to stay open. Studies of food prices in South Africa have found that the farmer's share of the retail price of bread is often well under a fifth. That does not mean the others are cheating the farmer; their work is real and costly. But it does show why farmers sometimes struggle when input costs such as fuel and fertiliser rise, and why a small farmer who can add value herself, for example by milling her own maize or selling vegetables directly at a market, can earn more. In an exam, explain that the final price reflects the value added at every stage of the production chain.
