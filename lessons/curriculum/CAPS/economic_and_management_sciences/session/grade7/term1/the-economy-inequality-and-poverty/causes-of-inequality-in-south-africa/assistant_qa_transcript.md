### subtopic_1 — Poverty and Inequality: Meaning and Measurement
**Assistant:** Can a country have less poverty but more inequality at the same time?

**Tutor:** Yes, and South Africa's history since 1994 shows something like this. Poverty is about whether people have enough to meet basic needs; inequality is about the size of the gap between those with the most and those with the least. After 1994 government expanded social grants enormously, built millions of RDP houses and extended water and electricity to many more households. Measures of poverty, especially extreme poverty, improved for many years because of these changes. At the same time, people with good education and skills, from all population groups, saw their incomes rise quickly as the economy opened up, while unemployment stayed very high. So the gap between the top and the bottom stayed very wide, and by some measures widened, even while fewer people lived in the deepest poverty. This is why economists look at both measures. Reducing poverty means lifting the bottom up; reducing inequality also needs the incomes of the poorest to grow faster than those of the richest, mainly through jobs, skills and access to opportunities. In an exam, define each term separately and give an example of how they can move differently.

### subtopic_2 — Historical Causes: Dispossession and Apartheid
**Assistant:** Apartheid ended in 1994. Why does it still affect inequality today?

**Tutor:** Because wealth, skills, location and opportunities carry over from one generation to the next, and they change slowly. Consider wealth: a family that was allowed to own land and a house in a well-located suburb could pass that property on to their children, who could borrow against it to start a business or pay for university. A family that was forcibly removed or barred from owning land had no such asset to pass on. Consider education: parents who received Bantu Education had fewer chances to get skilled jobs, and today many children still attend under-resourced schools in the same areas. Consider location: townships were built far from city centres, so many workers still travel long distances at high cost. Consider networks: people often find jobs through contacts, and these networks still reflect the past. Since 1994 there have been real changes, including a growing black middle class, land restitution, social grants and free basic services, and people debate how fast and how well these policies have worked. But inequality built over centuries does not disappear in one generation, which is why it remains a central challenge for the country.

### subtopic_3 — Present-Day Causes and Effects
**Assistant:** What can young people do about inequality?

**Tutor:** Quite a lot, even though the biggest causes need action from government and the economy as a whole. The first and most powerful thing is to stay in school and work hard at it, especially at mathematics and reading, because education is the strongest route to skilled and better-paid work. Second, find out about opportunities: bursaries, NSFAS funding for tertiary study, learnerships, TVET colleges and youth employment programmes. Many young people miss them because they do not know they exist. Third, build skills outside school: computer skills, a trade, sport, volunteering, or a small business such as selling snacks or doing car washes, which builds confidence and experience. Fourth, look after health and avoid choices, such as substance abuse, that can block a future. Fifth, be an active citizen: register to vote at eighteen, take part in community and ward meetings, and support peaceful efforts to improve schools and services. And finally, treat others with respect across the lines that history drew. No single person can end inequality, but millions of young people gaining skills, starting businesses and voting can change the country over time.
