### subtopic_1 — What Makes a Job Sustainable?
**Assistant:** If public works jobs are temporary, why does government run them?

**Tutor:** Because they meet urgent needs while longer-term solutions take effect. Millions of South Africans are unemployed, and many have been out of work for years. Public works programmes, such as the Expanded Public Works Programme and the Presidential Youth Employment Initiative, offer short-term paid work on projects that benefit communities: maintaining roads, clearing alien vegetation that uses up water, cleaning streets, helping teachers as school assistants, or caring for people in home-based care. This brings several benefits. Households receive income immediately, which they spend in local shops, supporting other jobs. Participants gain work experience, references and some training, which improves their chances of finding permanent work. Communities get useful services. There are also criticisms: the jobs end, the pay is low, and some argue that the money could be better spent on helping businesses grow. Most economists see public works as a useful bridge rather than a solution. The lasting answer is faster economic growth, better skills and a business environment in which companies, especially small ones, want to hire more workers.

### subtopic_2 — Small Businesses and Entrepreneurship
**Assistant:** Why do so many small businesses fail, and how can they survive?

**Tutor:** Many small businesses close within their first few years, for a mix of reasons. Some start without checking whether there are enough customers willing to pay, so sales are too low. Others underestimate costs such as rent, stock, transport and electricity, or mix personal and business money so that the owner cannot see whether the business is profitable. Cash-flow problems are common: customers pay late while suppliers must be paid now. Competition from larger shops, crime, load-shedding and difficulty getting loans make things harder. Owners may also lack experience in pricing, marketing and keeping records. Businesses that survive usually do several things well. They research the market and offer something customers really want, at a price that covers costs. They keep simple, accurate records of income and expenses, and keep business money separate. They start small, control costs and grow step by step. They look after customers so that people return and tell their friends. They seek advice from mentors, support agencies and other business owners. You will practise several of these skills in Term 2, with budgets and income and expenses, and in Term 3, when you plan a business for Entrepreneur's Day.

### subtopic_3 — Government, Business and Green Jobs
**Assistant:** Can protecting the environment and creating jobs go together?

**Tutor:** Yes, and South Africa has good examples, although there can be trade-offs to manage. The Working for Water programme has employed many thousands of people to clear invasive alien trees, such as some eucalyptus, pine and wattle species, that use far more water than indigenous plants; this saves water and creates jobs at the same time. Renewable energy projects, such as solar and wind farms in the Northern Cape and Eastern Cape, have created construction and maintenance jobs and new local businesses, while reducing pollution. Recycling supports waste pickers, buy-back centres and recycling factories. Eco-tourism in places such as game reserves and community-owned lodges earns income from protecting nature rather than destroying it. The trade-offs are real too. Moving away from coal will affect jobs in coal mines and power stations, mostly in Mpumalanga, which is why people talk about a just transition: planning new jobs, training and support for the workers and towns affected. A balanced answer recognises that green jobs can be sustainable jobs, but that the change must be managed fairly so that workers are not left behind.
