# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines how education and skills development help to reduce inequality and injustice. The governing idea is that education raises people's productivity and earning power, opens access to skilled work and entrepreneurship, and helps break the cycle of poverty from one generation to the next, provided that quality and access are fair. The link between education and income, government programmes that widen access, the role of skills development and vocational training, and the responsibilities of learners are examinable.

## Subtopic: Why Education Reduces Inequality

Education is often called the great equaliser, because it is one of the few ways a child from a poor household can reach the same opportunities as a child from a wealthy one.

In South Africa, the link between education and employment is very strong. Statistics South Africa's labour force surveys consistently show that unemployment is far lower among people with a degree than among people who did not finish matric. People with a tertiary qualification are much more likely to find work, and on average they earn several times more than people without matric.

There are good reasons for this. Education and training give people knowledge and skills that make them more productive: an electrician, a nurse or an accountant can do work that an untrained person cannot. Employers pay more for skills that are in short supply. Education also teaches people to read instructions, solve problems, use technology and keep learning, which helps them adapt as the economy changes.

Education can break the cycle of poverty. In the cycle, children of poor parents get little schooling, find only low-paid or no work, and their own children start life poor. When a child from a poor home completes school and gains a qualification, the whole family's future can change, and the next generation starts further ahead.

Education also brings benefits beyond income. Educated people tend to be healthier, make better financial decisions, take part in democracy and understand their rights, which helps fight injustice. A more educated population helps the whole economy grow.

## Subtopic: Government Programmes for Fair Access

Because education is so important, the Constitution gives everyone the right to a basic education, and government spends a larger share of its budget on education than on almost any other function. Several programmes aim to make access fairer.

No-fee schools: schools in the poorest communities do not charge school fees, and today a large majority of public school learners attend no-fee schools. Parents at fee-paying schools who cannot afford the fees can apply for exemption.

The National School Nutrition Programme provides a cooked meal every school day to millions of learners in poorer schools, so that hunger does not stop them from learning. Scholar transport is provided in some rural areas where learners live far from school. Government also provides textbooks and workbooks, such as the DBE workbooks.

For tertiary education, the National Student Financial Aid Scheme, NSFAS, gives bursaries to students from poor and working-class households to study at public universities and TVET colleges, covering tuition, accommodation and some living costs. The Funza Lushaka bursary pays for students who train to become teachers in subjects where teachers are needed.

Early childhood development matters too: children who attend good early learning programmes before Grade 1 start school better prepared, and Grade R is now part of the school system.

These programmes have widened access a great deal since 1994. The main challenge now is quality: making sure that learners in every school actually learn to read, write and calculate well. People disagree about the best solutions, from training and supporting teachers, to providing more resources, to holding schools more accountable, and most experts agree that several of these are needed together.

## Subtopic: Skills Development and Lifelong Learning

Not every successful career goes through a university. South Africa has a serious shortage of skilled artisans such as electricians, plumbers, welders, boilermakers, motor mechanics and diesel mechanics, and these trades can lead to well-paid work or to running one's own business.

TVET colleges, which stands for Technical and Vocational Education and Training colleges, offer practical courses in engineering trades, business, hospitality, information technology, agriculture and many other fields. Learnerships and apprenticeships combine classroom learning with paid, practical work in a real workplace, so that learners gain experience and a qualification at the same time. Sector education and training authorities, called SETAs, fund many of these programmes using a skills levy paid by employers.

Other kinds of skills matter too. Digital skills, such as using a computer, spreadsheets and the internet safely, are needed in almost every job. Entrepreneurial skills, such as spotting opportunities, planning, managing money and selling, help people create their own jobs and employ others, which is important in a country with high unemployment. Soft skills, such as communication, teamwork, reliability and problem-solving, are valued by every employer.

The economy keeps changing, with new technology replacing some jobs and creating others. That means learning does not stop when school ends. Adults can study part-time, take short courses and learn on the job. Community libraries, online courses and adult education centres support lifelong learning.

Skills development fights inequality because it gives people who missed out on opportunities in the past a second chance to earn better incomes.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through one young person's journey from a village school to a skilled job, in plain words with the same facts.

## Subtopic: Sipho's Ladder Out

Sipho grows up in a village in KwaZulu-Natal. His mother sells vegetables and his father is unemployed. Nobody in his family finished school.

Sipho attends a no-fee school, so the family does not pay fees. Every day at break he gets a hot meal from the school nutrition programme, so he can concentrate in the afternoon. He works hard, especially at maths, because his teacher tells him that maths opens doors.

He passes matric. He would like to study further but the family has no money. His teacher helps him apply to NSFAS, and he gets a bursary to study electrical engineering at a TVET college. In his final year he does an apprenticeship with an electrical company, earning while he learns.

Today Sipho is a qualified electrician. He earns a good salary, helps his younger sister with school costs and has started fixing wiring for neighbours on weekends. One day he plans to run his own business and employ young people from his village.

Sipho's story shows how education and skills can break the cycle of poverty: his children will start life further ahead than he did.

## Subtopic: Your Own Skills Plan

You do not have to wait until matric to start building skills. Here is a simple plan for a Grade 7 learner.

Read every day. Books, newspapers, library books, even long articles on your phone. Strong readers do better in every subject.

Practise maths every day. Ten minutes is enough to keep improving. Maths and science open the doors to many well-paid careers.

Learn one practical or digital skill. Help an uncle who fixes cars, learn to type, make a simple spreadsheet, cook a family meal, sew or start a small project such as a vegetable garden.

Find out about opportunities. Ask your teacher or the library about bursaries, NSFAS, TVET colleges and careers. Many young people miss chances simply because nobody told them.

And remember: learning never stops. Adults go back to college, do short courses and learn new skills at work.

Education and skills are the strongest ladder out of poverty and the best long-term tool against inequality.
