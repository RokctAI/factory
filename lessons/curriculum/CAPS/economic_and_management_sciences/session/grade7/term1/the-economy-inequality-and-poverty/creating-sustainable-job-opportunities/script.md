# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines how sustainable job opportunities can be created to reduce poverty and inequality. A sustainable job is one that lasts over time, pays a fair income and does not damage the environment or the community. The governing idea is that lasting jobs come mainly from a growing economy, especially from small, medium and micro enterprises, supported by government policies, skills development and infrastructure, while temporary public works programmes provide a bridge for the unemployed. The meaning of sustainable jobs, the role of SMMEs, government programmes, green jobs and entrepreneurship are examinable, and they lead into the Term 3 work on entrepreneurship.

## Subtopic: What Makes a Job Sustainable?

A job opportunity is sustainable when it can continue over a long period, not just for a few weeks. Sustainable jobs come from businesses and activities that keep producing goods and services that people want and are willing to pay for.

A sustainable job also pays a fair and decent income, enough to help a household meet its needs. South Africa has a national minimum wage that sets the lowest hourly amount most employers may pay. The job should offer safe working conditions and fair treatment, as protected by labour laws such as the Basic Conditions of Employment Act, which covers working hours, leave and notice periods.

Sustainability also has an environmental meaning. A job that depends on destroying a natural resource, for example over-fishing until the fish disappear, cannot last, and harms future generations. Sustainable jobs use resources in a way that allows them to renew.

Some jobs are temporary by design. Government's Expanded Public Works Programme, EPWP, gives unemployed people short-term work on projects such as building roads, clearing invasive plants, cleaning communities and assisting in schools, together with some training. These jobs provide income and experience, which can act as a bridge to more permanent work, but on their own they do not solve unemployment.

To reduce unemployment permanently, the economy must grow and create jobs faster than the number of new job-seekers each year. South Africa's unemployment rate has stayed above thirty percent for years because economic growth has been slow.

## Subtopic: Small Businesses and Entrepreneurship

In most economies, small businesses create a large share of new jobs. Small, medium and micro enterprises, called SMMEs, include everything from a single person selling vegetables to a small factory employing dozens of people. In South Africa SMMEs employ a large share of workers in the private sector, and government sees them as essential for job creation.

The informal sector is made up of small businesses that are not registered for tax or with official bodies. Spaza shops, street vendors, hair salons in homes, mechanics working in yards, taxi operators and backyard tailors are often part of it. The informal sector provides income and jobs for millions of people, though often with low and uncertain pay.

Entrepreneurs are people who start businesses by spotting an opportunity and taking a risk. They combine natural resources, labour and capital to produce goods or services. Every successful entrepreneur who grows a business creates jobs for others.

SMMEs face challenges: getting finance, because banks see them as risky; lack of business skills; competition from big companies; red tape, meaning slow and complicated official procedures; crime; and unreliable electricity. Many small businesses close in their first few years.

Several organisations support SMMEs. The Department of Small Business Development, the Small Enterprise Development and Finance Agency, known as Sefa, the National Youth Development Agency, NYDA, and banks offer training, mentoring, grants and loans. Big businesses can help by buying from small local suppliers, which is part of what government calls enterprise development.

The questions for this section are with you now: the meaning of SMMEs and the informal sector, the role of entrepreneurs, and the challenges and support for small businesses.

## Subtopic: Government, Business and Green Jobs

Creating sustainable jobs needs action from several partners.

Government's role is to create conditions in which businesses can grow: a stable economy, reliable electricity, water, roads, ports and railways, good education and skills, safety, and fair, simple rules. Government can attract investment into special economic zones, support industries such as manufacturing, agriculture and tourism, and buy goods and services from local producers. It also uses the national budget to fund skills programmes and public works.

Business's role is to invest, expand and employ, and to train workers. Large companies can support small suppliers, offer learnerships and internships, and take part in programmes such as the Youth Employment Service, YES, which places young people in one-year work experience.

Communities can help by buying from local businesses, forming cooperatives, which are businesses jointly owned by their members, for example in farming or sewing, and supporting stokvels that help members save and invest.

New opportunities are growing in green jobs, which are jobs that help protect the environment. Examples include installing and maintaining solar panels and other renewable energy, recycling and waste management, clearing invasive alien plants to save water, as in the Working for Water programme, eco-tourism, organic farming and building energy-efficient houses. South Africa's sunny climate and the need for more electricity make renewable energy a promising source of jobs.

Technology also creates jobs, for example in app development, online selling, repairs of cellphones and computers, and call centres, although it can replace some routine jobs too. That is why skills that machines cannot easily replace, such as problem-solving and creativity, become more valuable.

The questions for this section are with you now: the roles of government, business and communities in job creation, and examples of green jobs.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through one small business that grows from a car wash into a job creator, in plain words with the same facts.

## Subtopic: Bongani's Car Wash

Bongani finished school but could not find a job. He noticed that the taxi rank near his home was full of dusty taxis every afternoon. With R200 he bought buckets, sponges and soap and started washing taxis for R40 each.

He kept a notebook of every rand he earned and spent. After three months he had saved enough to buy a second-hand pressure washer, which let him wash cars faster. Business grew, so he hired two friends. Now three people had incomes instead of one.

He got advice from an NYDA business mentor, who helped him register his business, open a business bank account and apply for a small grant to build a shade cover and a water-recycling tank. The recycled water cut his costs and saved water in a drought-hit area, so his business became greener and more sustainable.

Today Bongani's car wash employs five young people. They pay their families' bills, buy from local shops and some are saving to start their own businesses. One small business has created jobs that last.

Try the questions for this part: name what helped Bongani's business grow.

## Subtopic: Who Helps Create Jobs?

Bongani did not create jobs alone. Here is the team that helps.

Government builds roads and keeps the electricity and water running, so businesses can work. It funds schools, TVET colleges and support agencies such as the NYDA. It runs public works programmes that give short-term jobs and training while people look for permanent work.

Big business invests in factories, shops and farms, hires workers and trains them, and can buy from small local suppliers like Bongani.

The community supports local businesses by buying from them, and people can form cooperatives and stokvels to start projects together.

And you. Every job starts with someone's idea and someone's skill. Spotting a need, like dusty taxis, and finding a way to meet it is the start of entrepreneurship, which you will study in Term 3.

Remember: lasting jobs need a growing economy, small businesses that survive and grow, skills, and work that does not destroy the environment.
