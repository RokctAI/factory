# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines socio-economic imbalances in South Africa: the meaning of poverty and inequality, how they are measured, and their historical and present-day causes. The governing idea is that today's inequality has deep historical roots in colonial dispossession and apartheid, and is sustained by present-day factors such as unemployment, unequal access to quality education and skills, and spatial patterns that keep many people far from economic opportunities. Definitions, causes and the effects of inequality on individuals and communities are examinable, and the topic leads into the role of education, the urban-rural divide and job creation.

## Subtopic: Poverty and Inequality: Meaning and Measurement

Poverty and inequality are related but different ideas.

Poverty is the condition of not having enough income or resources to meet basic needs such as food, shelter, clothing, health care and education. Statistics South Africa measures poverty using poverty lines. The food poverty line is the amount of money a person needs each month to buy enough food to stay healthy. Someone who earns less than this cannot afford even an adequate diet. Higher poverty lines add the cost of other essentials such as clothing and transport. By these measures, a large share of South Africans, more than half on the upper-bound line in recent surveys, live in poverty.

Inequality is about the gap between people. It describes how unequally income and wealth are shared among the households in a country. A country can have little poverty but high inequality, or the other way round. South Africa has both high poverty and very high inequality.

Inequality is often measured with the Gini coefficient, a number between zero and one. Zero means everyone has exactly the same income; one means one person has all the income. South Africa's Gini coefficient for income is around 0,63, which the World Bank has described as among the highest in the world. Another way to see it: the richest ten percent of people earn more than half of all income, while the poorest half of the population earns well under a tenth.

Socio-economic imbalances are not only about income. They also show up in unequal access to land, housing, quality schooling, health care, safe neighbourhoods, water, electricity and the internet.

The questions for this section are with you now: the difference between poverty and inequality, poverty lines, and the Gini coefficient.

## Subtopic: Historical Causes: Dispossession and Apartheid

Many of the imbalances in South Africa today were created by laws and policies of the past. Understanding them helps explain why inequality in South Africa still follows patterns of race and place.

During colonial times, from the seventeenth century onwards, settlers from Europe took land from Khoekhoe, San and Bantu-speaking communities through wars, treaties and dispossession. Many African people lost the land and cattle that were the base of their wealth and were drawn into working for wages on farms and mines.

The Natives Land Act of 1913 allowed black South Africans, who were the large majority of the population, to own land in only about seven percent of the country, later increased to about thirteen percent. This pushed millions into overcrowded reserves with poor farmland and forced many to work as migrant labourers far from their families.

Under apartheid, from 1948 to 1994, these patterns were made stricter. The Group Areas Act forced people to live in separate areas by race; many black, coloured and Indian families were removed from areas such as District Six in Cape Town and Sophiatown in Johannesburg and moved to townships far from city centres and jobs. Bantu Education gave black learners a deliberately inferior education with far less spending per learner. Job reservation kept many skilled and well-paid jobs for white workers. Pass laws controlled where black people could live and work. The homelands were starved of investment.

These policies limited the ability of the majority of South Africans to build wealth, own property, gain skills and earn good incomes, and the effects passed from one generation to the next.

People and historians differ in how they weigh these causes against later factors, but there is broad agreement that colonialism and apartheid created the foundation of today's inequality.

The questions for this section are with you now: the historical laws and policies that created socio-economic imbalances, and how their effects continue.

## Subtopic: Present-Day Causes and Effects

Inequality today is kept in place by several present-day factors, many of which are linked to the past.

Unemployment is the biggest. South Africa's official unemployment rate has been above thirty percent for several years, and youth unemployment is even higher. A household with no one employed depends on grants or the support of relatives, and cannot climb out of poverty.

Unequal access to quality education and skills is another cause. Some schools have excellent facilities and results, while many, especially in poor rural areas and townships, struggle with overcrowded classes, few resources and low pass rates in subjects such as mathematics. Skilled jobs pay far more than unskilled jobs, so differences in education lead to differences in income.

Location matters. People living far from cities and economic centres have fewer jobs nearby and must spend more on transport. Many rural areas have poor roads, little infrastructure and few businesses.

Other factors include slow economic growth, which means too few new jobs are created; corruption and poor service delivery, which waste resources meant for the poor; health challenges such as HIV and TB, which can make breadwinners too ill to work; and the high cost of starting a business.

Inequality has serious effects. Poor households suffer hunger and malnutrition, illness and poor housing. Children growing up in poverty may do worse at school, which can trap the next generation. High inequality is linked to crime, social tension and protests, and it can reduce trust in institutions. It also wastes talent: many able young people never get the chance to use their abilities.

The questions for this section are with you now: present-day causes of inequality, and the effects of poverty and inequality on individuals and society.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through two learners born on the same day in the same city, in plain words with the same facts.

## Subtopic: Two Learners, One City

Lindiwe and Megan were born on the same day in the same city. They are both clever, both love netball and both want to be doctors.

Lindiwe lives in a township far from the city centre, where her grandparents were moved under the Group Areas Act. Her mother cleans offices in town and spends a big part of her wage on taxis. Her father lost his job two years ago. Lindiwe's school has fifty learners in a class and no science laboratory, and she shares one textbook with a friend.

Megan lives in a suburb near the city centre, in a house her grandparents bought decades ago. Both her parents have degrees and good jobs. Her school has thirty learners in a class, a laboratory and computers, and she has a tutor for maths.

Neither girl chose where she was born. But the history of land, schooling and jobs means they start with very different chances. That gap in income, resources and opportunity is what inequality means.

Lindiwe can still become a doctor, and many young people from backgrounds like hers do. But she will have to work harder to overcome obstacles that Megan never faces. Reducing those obstacles is the challenge for South Africa.

## Subtopic: Poor Versus Unequal

Here is how to keep the two big words apart.

Poor is about one household: it does not have enough money to meet basic needs. A family that cannot buy enough food each month is living below the food poverty line.

Unequal is about the whole country: there is a big gap between those with the most and those with the least. In South Africa the richest ten percent earn more than half of all income.

What causes the gap? Two kinds of reasons. Past causes: land taken in colonial times, the 1913 Land Act, the Group Areas Act, Bantu Education and job reservation. Present causes: very high unemployment, unequal schools, living far from jobs, slow growth and corruption.

What does the gap do? It brings hunger and illness to poor households, holds back children's learning, and is linked to crime, anger and protests. It also wastes talent, because many clever young people never get a fair chance.

Remember the two words: poor means not enough; unequal means a big gap.

Try the questions for this part: sort each cause into past or present.
