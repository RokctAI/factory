# Part 1 — Expert

This session compares urban and rural areas and the economic challenges each faces. The governing idea is that economic opportunities in South Africa are concentrated in cities, so rural areas face shortages of jobs, infrastructure and services, while cities face pressure from rapid urbanisation, informal settlements and unemployment. The meaning of urban and rural areas, the challenges of each, push and pull factors of urbanisation, and possible solutions such as rural development and better urban planning are examinable.

## Subtopic: Urban and Rural Areas

An urban area is a town or city where many people live close together, most work in secondary and tertiary industries such as factories, shops, offices and services, and there are many buildings, roads and services. Johannesburg, Cape Town, eThekwini, Tshwane, Gqeberha and Polokwane are examples. A rural area is the countryside, with farms, villages and small settlements, where people live further apart and many depend on agriculture, mining, forestry or tourism.

South Africa has become an urban country. Today about two-thirds of the population lives in urban areas, and the share is still growing. Gauteng, the smallest province by area, has the largest population because it is the economic heart of the country.

The two kinds of areas are closely linked. Rural areas grow food, supply water and raw materials, and attract tourists. Cities supply manufactured goods, services, jobs and markets for rural produce. Many families live across both: a parent works in the city and sends money home to relatives in a rural village, and returns home at holidays. These money transfers, called remittances, are an important source of income for many rural households.

Because of apartheid's homeland system, many of South Africa's rural areas, especially in the Eastern Cape, KwaZulu-Natal and Limpopo, are former homelands where people were crowded onto land with poor infrastructure and little investment. This history is part of the reason why rural poverty is so deep in these areas.

## Subtopic: Challenges in Rural Areas

Rural areas face particular challenges.

Unemployment and few jobs: there are few factories, offices or shops, so many households depend on social grants, remittances and small-scale farming. Farm work is often seasonal and low-paid.

Poor infrastructure: many rural roads are gravel and become impassable in the rain. Some villages still fetch water from rivers or communal taps, and electricity, cellphone signal and internet access may be weak or absent. This makes it hard to run a business or study online.

Access to services: clinics, hospitals, high schools, police stations, banks and government offices may be far away, so people spend a lot of time and money travelling. Rural schools often have fewer resources, and it can be hard to attract teachers and doctors.

Land and agriculture: many rural households have access to land but lack money for seeds, fertiliser, tractors and irrigation, and struggle to get produce to markets. Drought and climate change make farming riskier.

Out-migration: young and skilled people leave for the cities, which takes energy and skills out of rural communities and leaves older people and children behind.

There are efforts to address these challenges: building roads and bringing water and electricity, rural development and land reform programmes, support for small farmers, cooperatives, eco-tourism projects and community-based tourism, mobile clinics, and expanding broadband. Government's Comprehensive Rural Development Programme, for example, aims to support rural communities to develop their own areas.

## Subtopic: Challenges in Urban Areas

Urbanisation is the growth in the share of a country's people living in urban areas. People are pushed from rural areas by a lack of jobs and services, and pulled to cities by the hope of work, better schools, health care and a more exciting life. These are called push factors and pull factors.

Rapid urbanisation brings challenges for cities.

Housing: cities cannot build houses fast enough for all the new arrivals, so many people live in informal settlements, in shacks built from corrugated iron and wood, often on land without proper water, sanitation or electricity. Others rent backyard rooms. Government has built millions of RDP and BNG houses since 1994, but the housing backlog remains large.

Unemployment: there are more job-seekers than jobs, so many urban residents are unemployed or work in the informal sector, for example as street traders, car guards or casual labourers.

Services under pressure: water, sewerage, electricity and refuse systems built for fewer people become overloaded, leading to outages and spills. Illegal electricity connections can be dangerous and cause fires.

Crime and safety: overcrowding, unemployment and inequality are linked to higher crime in some areas.

Traffic and transport: long distances between townships and work, a legacy of apartheid planning, mean long and expensive commutes and congested roads.

Pollution and health: informal settlements are vulnerable to fires and floods, and crowded areas spread disease more easily.

Responses include upgrading informal settlements by installing services where people already live, building houses closer to jobs, improving public transport such as bus rapid transit systems, supporting informal traders and small businesses, and community policing.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through one family split between a village and a city, in plain words with the same facts.

## Subtopic: Thabo in the Village, Lerato in the City

Thabo lives with his gogo in a village in Limpopo. Lerato, his older sister, moved to Johannesburg to look for work.

In the village, Thabo walks five kilometres to school on a gravel road. The clinic is twenty kilometres away and the taxi is expensive. The cellphone signal drops when it rains. There are few jobs: his uncle works on a farm during harvest, and the family lives mainly on Gogo's grant and the money Lerato sends home. But there is space, the family grows vegetables, and the neighbours all help each other.

In Johannesburg, Lerato found a job at a supermarket after six months of searching. She shares a backyard room in a township. The clinic and shops are nearby, and there is electricity and running water, but the power goes off during load-shedding and the taps sometimes run dry. She spends two hours a day in traffic and a big part of her wage on taxis. Across the road is an informal settlement where people live in shacks without proper toilets.

Both places have challenges. In the village the problem is too little: too few jobs, roads and services. In the city the problem is too much: too many people for the jobs, houses and services.

## Subtopic: Push, Pull and Fix

Why did Lerato leave? Two kinds of reasons.

Push factors push people away from where they live: no jobs, poor roads, no clinic nearby, drought on the farms.

Pull factors pull people towards a new place: jobs, colleges, hospitals, shops and the hope of a better life.

When millions of people move like Lerato, cities grow quickly. That is urbanisation.

How can both places be fixed? In the village: build and maintain roads, bring water, electricity and internet, help small farmers get seeds, tractors and markets, and develop tourism, so that young people like Thabo have reasons to stay. In the city: build houses closer to jobs, upgrade informal settlements with water and toilets, improve buses and trains, and support small businesses and street traders.

Remember: rural areas often have too little, cities often have too much pressure, and developing both is part of fighting poverty and inequality.
