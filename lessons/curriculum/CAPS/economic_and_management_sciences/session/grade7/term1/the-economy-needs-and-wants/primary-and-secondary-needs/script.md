# Part 1 — Expert

This session classifies needs as primary or secondary. Primary needs, sometimes called basic or physical needs, must be satisfied for survival; secondary needs improve the quality of life once primary needs are met. The governing idea is that people satisfy primary needs first and move on to secondary needs as their income rises, so the pattern of spending tells us a great deal about a household's standard of living. The definitions, examples, and the link between income and the ability to satisfy secondary needs are examinable, and Maslow's hierarchy is introduced as a useful way of thinking about the order of needs.

## Subtopic: Primary Needs

Primary needs are the needs a person must satisfy to stay alive and healthy. They are also called basic needs or physical needs. The core primary needs are food, clean water, shelter and clothing. Many textbooks also include health care, sanitation and safety, because without them people quickly become ill or are put in danger.

Primary needs are the same for every human being, whether they live in a rural village in Limpopo or in a city flat in Cape Town, although the way they are met differs. One family may meet its need for shelter in a brick house, another in a traditional rondavel and another in an RDP house. One family may grow maize and vegetables, another buys them from the supermarket.

Primary needs must be satisfied first. A family that cannot afford enough food will not spend on music lessons. When incomes fall, for example because a parent loses a job, households cut wants and secondary needs first and protect spending on food, rent, water and electricity.

In South Africa, millions of people still struggle to meet primary needs. Some households experience hunger, and many live in informal settlements without proper sanitation. This is why government's social spending focuses heavily on primary needs: free basic water, housing subsidies, school nutrition and free primary health care at clinics.

## Subtopic: Secondary Needs

Secondary needs are needs that are not required for bare survival but that improve the quality of life and allow people to take full part in society. They are satisfied after primary needs have been met.

Examples include education beyond the basics, transport to work or school, communication such as a cellphone or internet access, electricity for appliances, furniture, recreation and sport, entertainment, and some comfort items such as a warm blanket rather than a thin one. In a modern society some of these are very important; without transport a worker cannot reach a job, and without education a person struggles to earn an income.

The line between secondary needs and wants is not always sharp. A useful way to think about it is that a secondary need improves life in a way most people in that society would regard as reasonable, while a want is a particular choice or a luxury beyond that. Transport to school is a secondary need; a private chauffeur is a want. A television for news and education may be a secondary need in many households; a second large-screen television for every bedroom is a want.

What counts as a secondary need also changes over time and between societies. Fifty years ago few South African households had a fridge or a phone; today most consider them normal. As an economy grows and incomes rise, things that were once luxuries become part of an ordinary standard of living.

## Subtopic: Income and the Ladder of Needs

How far a household can go beyond primary needs depends mainly on its income. A household with a very low income spends almost everything on food, rent or housing costs, energy and transport, and has little or nothing left for secondary needs or wants. A household with a higher income can meet its primary needs easily and spend on education, a car, furniture, entertainment and savings. This is why economists look at what share of income a household spends on food: the poorer the household, the higher the share.

A psychologist called Abraham Maslow suggested that human needs can be arranged like a pyramid. At the bottom are physical needs such as food, water, warmth and rest. Next come safety needs such as security and a stable home. Above these are the needs to belong and be loved, through family, friends and community. Then come esteem needs, such as respect and a sense of achievement. At the top is self-actualisation, becoming the best person you can be. Maslow argued that people usually focus on the lower levels before the higher ones become important. The pyramid is a model, a simplified way of thinking, and people's lives do not always follow it exactly, but it helps explain why primary needs come first.

For a country, economic growth and job creation allow more households to move up from meeting only primary needs to meeting secondary needs as well. Government also helps households climb by providing free or subsidised services, such as no-fee schools and clinics, so that even poor families can meet some secondary needs like education.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through two families and one ladder, in plain words with the same facts.

## Subtopic: Two Families, One Ladder

Picture a ladder. The bottom rungs are primary needs: food, water, shelter and clothing. The middle rungs are secondary needs: school costs, transport, a basic phone, electricity for a fridge, a warm bed. The top rungs are wants and savings: takeaways, a TV subscription, a holiday, money in the bank.

The Mokoena family has R4 000 a month. They spend most of it on maize meal, vegetables, paraffin, rent and taxi fares. They reach the bottom rungs and part of the middle, and there is almost nothing left at the top.

The Naidoo family has R30 000 a month. Their food and rent are covered easily. They pay for transport, data, a fridge, school trips and extra lessons, and they still have money for a takeaway on Friday and some savings. They reach the top of the ladder.

Both families have the same primary needs. The difference is how far up the ladder their income lets them climb. That is why a job or a better wage changes a family's whole life, and why free services like clinics and no-fee schools help families like the Mokoenas reach the middle rungs.

## Subtopic: Primary or Secondary? Quick Sort

Let us practise fast. Say primary or secondary.

Bread: primary, you need food to live. Clean water from the tap: primary. A taxi fare to get to work: secondary, it does not keep you alive directly, but it gets you to the job that pays for food. A blanket in winter: primary, you need warmth. Extra maths lessons: secondary, they improve your future but you can survive without them. A school uniform: secondary for most people, because it is needed to attend school. A roof that does not leak: primary, it is shelter.

And remember the third pile. A gold chain: neither primary nor secondary need, it is a want.

A trick to remember: primary means first, the things you need first to stay alive. Secondary means second, the things that make life better once the first are sorted.
