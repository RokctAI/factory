# Part 1 — Expert

This session introduces the central idea of economics: people have needs and wants that they try to satisfy with goods and services. It distinguishes needs, which are essential for survival and a decent life, from wants, which are desires beyond those essentials, and then examines the basic needs of individuals, families, communities and the country as a whole. The governing idea is that whether something is a need or a want depends on whether a person can live a healthy, safe and dignified life without it. The distinction between needs and wants and examples at each level are examinable, and they lead into primary and secondary needs and the problem of scarcity.

## Subtopic: Differentiating Between Needs and Wants

Every person, family and country has things it must have and things it would like to have. Economists call the first group needs and the second group wants.

A need is something a person must have to survive and to live a healthy, safe and decent life. Food, clean water, shelter, clothing, health care and safety are needs. Without them a person becomes ill, suffers or cannot take part in society.

A want is something a person desires but can live without. Wants make life more comfortable or enjoyable. Takeaway burgers, branded sneakers, the newest smartphone, a big-screen television and holidays are wants for most people.

Often a need and a want are linked. Food is a need, but a takeaway meal is a want, because cheaper food would meet the same need. Clothing is a need, but a particular brand of jeans is a want. Shelter is a need, but a house with a swimming pool is a want.

Context matters. Some things that were wants in the past have become close to needs today. For a learner who must complete schoolwork online, or for a worker who finds jobs through WhatsApp groups, a basic cellphone and some data may be necessary to take part in modern life. For a farmer, a vehicle may be essential to get produce to market, while for a city learner it is a want. Being able to explain why something is a need or a want in a particular situation is more important than memorising lists.

A useful test is to ask: what would happen if I did not have this? If the honest answer is that I would be ill, unsafe or unable to function, it is a need. If the answer is that I would be disappointed, it is a want.

The questions for this section are with you now: definitions of needs and wants, examples, and the test for telling them apart.

## Subtopic: Basic Needs of Individuals and Families

An individual's basic needs are the things every person must have. The most important are nutritious food, clean water, shelter that protects against cold, heat and rain, clothing, health care and safety. People also have needs that are not physical but are just as real: love and belonging, respect, and the chance to learn and grow. Education is a need in a modern society, because without basic literacy and numeracy a person struggles to find work and take part in daily life.

A family is a group of people who are related and often live together and share resources. A family has needs as a unit. It needs a home large enough and safe enough for its members; an income, from work, a business or grants, to buy what it cannot produce; food for every member; access to water, sanitation and energy for cooking, lighting and heating; and care for children, older members and anyone who is sick.

Children cannot meet their own needs and depend on adults. The South African Constitution recognises this: every child has the right to basic nutrition, shelter, basic health care services and social services, and to be protected from neglect and abuse. Government supports families that cannot meet their children's needs, for example through the child support grant, no-fee schools and school meals.

Families must make choices. With a limited income, a family usually pays first for food, rent, electricity, water, transport and school costs, and only then considers wants such as takeaways or entertainment.

The questions for this section are with you now: the basic needs of an individual, the needs of a family as a unit, and how families choose between needs and wants.

## Subtopic: Needs of Communities and the Country

A community is a group of people who live in the same area, such as a village, a township, a suburb or a farm settlement. Communities have needs that no single family can meet alone. A community needs clean running water and sanitation, schools and clinics, roads and transport, electricity, refuse removal, police services, and places such as libraries, parks, sports fields and community halls. Many of these are provided by municipalities and provincial governments, paid for by taxes and rates.

When a community lacks these services, every family in it is affected. If there is no clinic nearby, a sick child may have to travel for hours. If there is no safe road, a farmer cannot get produce to market and learners struggle to reach school. Communities often protest about poor service delivery because these needs are so basic.

A country also has needs. South Africa needs defence to protect its borders, police and courts to keep law and order, a reliable supply of electricity and water to run homes and businesses, ports, railways and national roads to move goods, a health system and an education system for its people, and a stable government. Above all, a country needs a growing economy that creates jobs, because jobs give families income to meet their own needs. National government provides many of these, paid for from the national budget.

The levels are connected. When the country has reliable electricity, communities have lights and businesses can operate; when businesses operate, families earn income; when families earn income, individuals can meet their needs.

The questions for this section are with you now: the needs of a community and of the country, and how needs at the different levels are linked.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through one shopping trolley and one map, in plain words with the same facts.

## Subtopic: The Trolley Test

Zanele goes shopping with her aunt and R500. Before anything goes into the trolley, her aunt asks one question: what happens if we do not buy this?

Maize meal: without it the family goes hungry. Need. Milk, eggs, vegetables and bread: the same. Needs. Soap and toothpaste: without them people get sick. Needs.

A big packet of chips and a two-litre cooldrink: without them nobody gets sick, they are just nice. Wants.

Then the shoes. Zanele's school shoes have a hole. School shoes are a need, because she must wear them to school and keep her feet safe. But the branded pair costs three times as much as the plain pair, and both do the same job. The plain pair meets the need; the brand is a want.

At the till they have money left for one treat, so Zanele chooses the chips. That is fine. Wants are not bad; they just come after needs.

Try the questions for this part: put each item through the trolley test.

## Subtopic: From Me to My Country

Now draw four circles, one inside the other.

The smallest circle is me. I need food, water, clothes, a safe place to sleep, health care and people who care about me.

The next circle is my family. We need a home, an income to buy food and pay for electricity and transport, and someone to look after the little ones and Gogo.

The next circle is my community. We need a clinic, a school, a road, clean water from a tap, refuse removal, street lights, police and a field to play on. My family cannot build these alone; the municipality and the province provide them.

The biggest circle is my country. South Africa needs electricity, defence, courts, railways and ports, and most of all jobs, so that every family can earn an income.

The circles depend on each other. If the country's electricity fails, the clinic in my community struggles, my family's food spoils and I do my homework by candlelight.

Try the questions for this part: place each need in the right circle.
