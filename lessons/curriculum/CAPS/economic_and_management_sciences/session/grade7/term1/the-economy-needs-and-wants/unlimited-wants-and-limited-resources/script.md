# Part 1 — Expert

This session introduces the basic economic problem: human wants are unlimited, while the resources available to satisfy them are limited. This mismatch is called scarcity, and it forces individuals, households, businesses and governments to make choices. The governing idea is that every choice involves giving something up, and the value of the best alternative given up is the opportunity cost. The meaning of scarcity, the types of resources, choice, priorities and opportunity cost are examinable and are the foundation of Economics in later grades.

## Subtopic: Unlimited Wants

Human wants are unlimited. As soon as one want is satisfied, another takes its place. A learner who saves for a pair of sneakers will, once she has them, want a matching tracksuit, then a new phone cover, then concert tickets. A family that buys a fridge wants a microwave; a family that gets a car wants a bigger one.

There are several reasons why wants keep growing. First, people's needs and wants change as they grow older and their situation changes: a baby needs nappies, a teenager wants data, an adult wants a home. Second, new products and technologies create new wants that did not exist before; nobody wanted a streaming subscription thirty years ago. Third, advertising and social media constantly show us things we did not know we wanted, and make us compare ourselves with others. Fourth, as incomes rise, things that were once luxuries come to feel normal, and new luxuries replace them.

Even at the level of a country, wants are unlimited. South Africa wants more schools, clinics, houses, roads, police officers, electricity and jobs, and every year the list is longer than the budget.

Unlimited wants are not a bad thing in themselves. The desire for better things drives people to work, invent and build businesses. But they create a problem when they meet the limits of the resources available to satisfy them.

The questions for this section are with you now: why human wants are unlimited, and examples of how wants grow.

## Subtopic: Limited Resources and Scarcity

Resources are the things we use to produce goods and services that satisfy needs and wants. Economists group them into factors of production. Natural resources include land, water, minerals, forests, fish and sunlight. Human resources are people's labour, skills and knowledge. Capital resources are man-made tools, machines, buildings and equipment used to produce other goods. Entrepreneurship is the ability to combine the other resources to start and run a business. For a household, the most important resources are its income, its time and its skills.

All these resources are limited. There is only so much fertile land and fresh water in South Africa, which is a water-scarce country. There are only twenty-four hours in a day. A household has a fixed income each month. Government has only the taxes it collects and what it can borrow.

Scarcity is the situation in which wants are greater than the resources available to satisfy them. Scarcity does not mean that something is rare in an absolute sense; it means there is not enough of it to satisfy everyone who wants it at no cost. Even a wealthy person faces scarcity of time, and even a wealthy country cannot provide everything for free.

Scarcity is the basic economic problem, and it is why economics exists. Because resources are scarce, every person, business and government must decide what to produce or buy, how much, and for whom.

The questions for this section are with you now: the types of resources, the meaning of scarcity, and why scarcity is called the basic economic problem.

## Subtopic: Choices, Priorities and Opportunity Cost

Because resources are limited and wants are not, we cannot have everything. We must choose. Choosing well means setting priorities: deciding what is most important and satisfying that first. For a household, priorities usually start with primary needs such as food, rent and electricity, then secondary needs such as transport and school costs, then wants.

Every choice has a cost, even when no money is spent. If you spend Saturday afternoon playing soccer, you cannot spend that same time studying or working. Economists call the value of the next best alternative that you give up the opportunity cost of your choice. If Sipho has R50 and chooses to buy a movie ticket instead of a book, the opportunity cost of the movie is the book. If a farmer uses a field to grow maize, the opportunity cost is the vegetables she could have grown there instead.

Businesses face the same problem. A small bakery with limited money must choose between buying a new oven or hiring an extra worker. Government faces it on a very large scale: money spent on building a new stadium cannot also be spent on clinics or houses, so the opportunity cost of the stadium might be several clinics.

Thinking about opportunity cost helps people make better decisions. Before you buy something, ask: what else could I do with this money or time, and is this the best use? A simple priority list, with needs at the top and wants at the bottom, is a practical tool for this.

The questions for this section are with you now: why scarcity forces choice, how to set priorities, and how to identify an opportunity cost.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through one learner, one R100 note and a very long wish list, in plain words with the same facts.

## Subtopic: R100 and a Wish List

Kabelo gets R100 for his birthday. He writes a wish list: a new soccer ball, data for the month, a calculator for maths, a burger and chips, a phone cover and a ticket to the movies. Together it all costs more than R600.

That is scarcity in one picture. The wish list is long and keeps growing; the money is only R100. He cannot have everything, so he has to choose.

His mother helps him rank the list. At the top she puts the calculator, because he needs it for his maths test next week. Next comes some data, because his class uses a WhatsApp group for homework. Then the wants: the ball, the burger, the phone cover, the movie.

The calculator costs R60 and R30 buys data. That leaves R10. The ball, the burger and the movie must wait.

Kabelo is a bit disappointed, but he has made a smart choice: needs first, wants later. Next month his wish list will have grown again, because wants never stop. That is normal; scarcity never goes away, so the choosing never goes away either.

Try the questions for this part: rank a wish list of your own.

## Subtopic: What Did You Give Up?

Every choice Kabelo makes has a hidden price tag. When he chose the calculator, the next best thing on his list was the soccer ball. The ball is what he gave up. Economists call that the opportunity cost.

It works with time too. On Saturday Kabelo can play soccer or study for the test. If he plays soccer, the opportunity cost is the study time. If he studies, the opportunity cost is the game.

And it works for whole countries. If the government spends money on a new stadium, the opportunity cost might be clinics or houses that could have been built with that money.

So before you spend money or time, ask two questions. What is the best thing I am giving up? And is what I am choosing worth more to me than that?

Remember: wants are unlimited, resources are limited, so we must choose, and every choice costs us the next best thing.

Try the questions for this part: name the opportunity cost in each choice.
