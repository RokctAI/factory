### subtopic_1 — Unlimited Wants
**Assistant:** If wants are unlimited, will people ever be satisfied?

**Tutor:** For any one want, yes; for wants as a whole, economists say no. When you finally buy the sneakers you saved for, that particular want is satisfied, and you may feel happy for a while. But new wants appear because life changes, new products are invented and we see what others have. This is not a sign that people are greedy; it is simply how human beings and modern societies work. It does, however, have important consequences. First, because wants never run out, there will always be more things people want than can be produced, so choices will always be needed. Second, people who keep chasing every new want can fall into debt, spending money they do not have. Learning to tell needs from wants, and to be content with enough, is part of being financially literate. Third, at the level of the planet, endlessly growing wants put pressure on natural resources such as water, forests and fish. That is why later in this term you will study using goods efficiently, reusing and recycling. For now, remember the economist's statement: wants are unlimited, resources are limited.

### subtopic_2 — Limited Resources and Scarcity
**Assistant:** Is water scarce in South Africa?

**Tutor:** Yes, and it is a good example of scarcity in the economic sense. South Africa receives much less rainfall than the world average, and a large share of the country is semi-arid. Most of our water comes from rivers and dams that depend on that rain, and there are only a limited number of good places to build dams. At the same time demand keeps rising, because the population is growing, cities are expanding, and farms, mines, power stations and factories all need water. In 2018 Cape Town came close to Day Zero, when taps might have been switched off, after several years of drought. Residents cut their water use dramatically, to around fifty litres per person per day, and the crisis was avoided. Water is scarce not because there is none, but because there is not enough to satisfy every want at once. That means choices must be made: how much goes to households, farms and industry, and how much it should cost. It also means each of us has a responsibility to use water carefully, fix leaks and reuse water where possible.

### subtopic_3 — Choices, Priorities and Opportunity Cost
**Assistant:** Does opportunity cost apply even when something is free?

**Tutor:** Yes, which is why economists like the saying that there is no such thing as a free lunch. Suppose a company gives away free concert tickets. You pay no money, but you still give up the evening you could have spent studying for tomorrow's test, working a shift or visiting your grandmother. The best of those alternatives is the opportunity cost of going to the concert. Free things also cost someone something. A free school meal is paid for by government from taxes, and the money used for meals cannot be used for something else, such as more textbooks. That does not mean the meal is a bad choice; school meals help learners concentrate and are a very good use of money. It means every choice, free or not, uses scarce resources that could have been used another way. Thinking about opportunity cost helps you notice the hidden cost of your time. In exam questions, always name the specific next best alternative, for example: the opportunity cost of attending the concert is the time Lindiwe could have spent preparing for her test.
