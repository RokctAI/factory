### subtopic_1 — Primary Needs
**Assistant:** Is a cellphone a primary need?

**Tutor:** Strictly, no, because a person can survive without one, and primary needs are those required for survival and health. But this is a good example of why careful reasoning matters. In South Africa today, many people use cellphones to look for work, receive grant notifications, call for help in an emergency, use banking and get school information. For those people a basic phone can be very important for meeting other needs, and some economists would describe it as a secondary need rather than simply a want. What a cellphone is not is a primary need in the same sense as food or water: nobody dies within days without a phone. So the safe exam answer is to say that food, water, shelter and clothing are primary needs; a basic phone may be a secondary need for someone who depends on it for work, safety or schooling; and an expensive latest-model smartphone is a want. Show the marker that you understand the difference between needing to communicate and wanting a particular expensive device.

### subtopic_2 — Secondary Needs
**Assistant:** Why is transport a secondary need and not a primary need?

**Tutor:** Because a person can survive without it, but in a modern economy it is very hard to live a full life or earn an income without it. Primary needs are directly about staying alive and healthy: food, water, shelter, clothing. Transport does not feed you or shelter you. But it carries you to the place where you earn the money to buy food and pay rent, and carries learners to school and patients to clinics. In South Africa, because of apartheid spatial planning, many people live far from where the jobs are, so transport is especially important. A worker in a township may spend a large share of her wage on taxi fares just to reach work in town. That is why government subsidises buses and commuter trains, and why scholar transport is provided in some rural areas. In an exam, explain that transport is a secondary need because it is not needed for survival but makes it possible to meet primary needs through work and income, and to reach services such as education and health care.

### subtopic_3 — Income and the Ladder of Needs
**Assistant:** Does Maslow's pyramid mean poor people do not care about belonging or respect?

**Tutor:** No, and that would be an unfair reading. Maslow's idea is that when a person's physical survival is under threat, those needs usually take most of their attention. A parent who does not know where the next meal is coming from has to focus on food. But people at every income level care deeply about family, friendship, dignity and respect, and many poor communities have very strong bonds of belonging, sharing and support, sometimes stronger than in wealthier areas. Stokvels and burial societies, for example, meet both financial needs and the need to belong. Critics of Maslow also point out that the order is not fixed: people sometimes give up food or comfort for the sake of their family, beliefs or community. So treat the pyramid as a helpful model rather than a law. In an exam you can use it to explain why primary needs are usually met first and why rising income allows people to give more attention to education, achievement and personal growth, while noting that all people have all of these needs.
