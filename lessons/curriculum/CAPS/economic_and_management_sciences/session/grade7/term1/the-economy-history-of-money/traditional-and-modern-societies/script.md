# Part 1 — Expert

This session opens the Grade 7 study of the economy by asking what life was like before money and why money became necessary. It compares traditional societies, in which households produced most of what they needed for themselves, with modern societies, in which people specialise in one kind of work and exchange their income for goods and services. The governing idea is that the more people specialise, the more they must exchange, and the more they exchange, the more they need a convenient medium of exchange. The comparison between traditional and modern ways of meeting needs, the meaning of self-sufficiency, specialisation and interdependence, and the problems of life without money are examinable, and they prepare the ground for the next session on bartering, coins and paper money.

## Subtopic: Life Without Money

Start with a thought experiment. Imagine waking up in a South Africa where money has disappeared overnight. There are no coins, no notes, no bank cards and no cellphone payments. You want bread for breakfast, but the spaza shop owner cannot sell it to you for money. What could you offer instead? Perhaps you could offer to sweep the shop, or swap a pencil, or promise to bring something tomorrow.

Very quickly several problems appear. First, you need to find someone who has what you want and who also wants what you have. If the shop owner does not need a pencil, there is no deal. Second, it is hard to agree on value. How many pencils is a loaf of bread worth? How many loaves is a goat worth? Third, many things cannot be divided. If a goat is worth ten loaves of bread, you cannot cut off a tenth of a live goat to buy one loaf. Fourth, it is hard to save. Bread goes stale and tomatoes rot, so you cannot easily store your wealth for later. Fifth, you can only trade with people close by, because carrying goods long distances is slow and difficult.

These problems explain why every society that grew beyond a small group eventually developed some form of money. Money is anything that people generally accept in exchange for goods and services. It makes buying and selling quicker, it gives everything a price that can be compared, it can be divided into small amounts and it can be saved.

Before money, people did not simply go without. They met their needs in other ways, and that is what the rest of this session is about.

The questions for this section are with you now: the problems people face when there is no money, and a simple definition of money.

## Subtopic: Traditional Societies: Meeting Needs Without Money

A traditional society is a community in which people meet most of their needs by producing for themselves, following customs handed down from parents and grandparents. Long before colonial times, the people of southern Africa lived in many such societies.

The San were hunter-gatherers. They lived in small groups, moved with the seasons, hunted antelope with bows and poisoned arrows, and gathered roots, berries, honey and tsamma melons. They knew their environment in great detail, and food from a successful hunt was shared among the whole group. The Khoekhoe were herders who kept cattle and fat-tailed sheep and moved to find grazing. Bantu-speaking farming communities, ancestors of many South Africans today, settled in villages, grew sorghum and millet, kept cattle, goats and sheep, worked iron and made pots.

In these societies most households were self-sufficient, which means they produced most of what they needed themselves. They built their homes from local materials such as wood, grass, mud and dung. They made clothing from animal skins. They grew or collected their own food. Work was shared out by age and by custom, and help came from the extended family and neighbours rather than from shops.

Wealth was measured in things that could be seen and used. In many farming communities, cattle were the most important store of wealth. Cattle provided milk, meat, hides and manure, and they were used in important social exchanges, for example as lobola in marriage. A family with a large herd was respected and secure.

Some trade did happen. Communities exchanged salt, iron tools, copper, beads, ivory, grain and pottery, sometimes over long distances. Mapungubwe, a powerful kingdom in the Limpopo valley about eight hundred years ago, traded gold and ivory with merchants who reached the east coast, and received glass beads and cloth from as far away as India and China. But inside most villages, daily life depended far more on producing and sharing than on buying and selling.

The questions for this section are with you now: the meaning of a traditional society, examples of how people met their needs, and the meaning of self-sufficiency.

## Subtopic: Modern Societies: Specialisation and Interdependence

A modern society is one in which most people do not produce what they need for themselves. Instead, each person specialises, which means they concentrate on one kind of work: a nurse nurses, a taxi driver drives, a farmer farms, a programmer writes software. They are paid an income in money, and they use that money to buy the goods and services that thousands of other specialists produce.

Think about your breakfast. The maize for your porridge was grown by a farmer in the Free State, milled in a factory, packed in a bag printed somewhere else, carried by truck drivers and sold by a shopkeeper. The electricity for the stove came from a power station, and the stove itself may have been made in another country. Nobody in your family did any of that work, yet the porridge is on the table. This is interdependence: in a modern society, everyone depends on everyone else.

Specialisation has big advantages. People become very skilled at the one thing they do, so they produce more and better goods. More goods mean more choice, and new inventions such as medicines and machines become possible. But specialisation also means that people cannot survive easily on their own. If you lose your income, you cannot simply grow all your own food and build your own house in a city.

Here the link with money becomes clear. In a traditional village, a few direct swaps were enough. In a modern society with millions of specialists, direct swapping would be impossible. A teacher cannot pay for bread with a lesson, because the baker may not want a lesson. Money solves this problem: the teacher is paid in money and the baker accepts money, so each can buy what they need from anyone.

South Africa today contains both ways of life side by side. Many rural households still grow some of their own food and keep livestock, while also earning wages or receiving grants and buying from shops. Learning to see both systems helps you understand the economy around you.

The questions for this section are with you now: the meaning of specialisation and interdependence, how a modern household meets its needs, and why a modern society needs money.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through one learner, one morning and one grandmother's stories, in plain words with the same facts.

## Subtopic: Gogo's Village and Your Street

Lerato lives in a flat in Johannesburg. In the holidays she visits her gogo in a village in the Eastern Cape, and Gogo tells her stories about how her own grandmother lived.

In Gogo's grandmother's time, the family grew maize and pumpkins in the field behind the house. The boys looked after the cattle and goats, the girls fetched water and helped in the field, and everyone helped to build and repair the round house with mud, poles and thatch. If the family needed something they could not make, they swapped with a neighbour: some maize for a clay pot, or a goat for help at harvest time. Cattle were the family's savings. Very little money was used.

Now look at Lerato's street. Her mother works in an office and her father drives a delivery truck. They are paid in money at the end of the month. With that money they buy maize meal, milk, bread, school shoes, electricity, data and taxi fares. They did not grow, make or build any of those things themselves. Hundreds of other people did.

So the big difference is this. In the village of long ago, families made most things themselves and swapped a little. In the city today, families make almost nothing they use and buy almost everything. And when you have to buy almost everything from strangers, you need something everybody accepts. That something is money.

Keep Gogo's village and Lerato's street in mind and try the questions for this part.

## Subtopic: One Morning With No Money

Now let us play Lerato's bad dream. She wakes up and all the money in the world has vanished.

She wants bread. She offers the shopkeeper her spare pencil. The shopkeeper says he has plenty of pencils, so no deal. She needs a taxi to school. The taxi driver needs petrol, not pencils, and the petrol station does not want pencils either. She asks what her mother's work is worth today. Nobody knows, because there is no price to compare.

That one morning shows the big problems of life without money. You must find someone who wants exactly what you have. You cannot easily agree on value. You cannot split big things into small parts. And you cannot save easily, because many goods spoil.

Then Lerato wakes up properly. Money is back. She buys bread with coins, pays the taxi with a note and her mother's salary is in the bank. Every swap is easy again, because everyone accepts money.

So remember the lesson in one line: the more people specialise and trade, the more they need money. In the next lesson you will see how people moved step by step from swapping cattle and beads to coins, paper notes and cards.

Try the questions for this part: what goes wrong without money, and how money fixes it.
