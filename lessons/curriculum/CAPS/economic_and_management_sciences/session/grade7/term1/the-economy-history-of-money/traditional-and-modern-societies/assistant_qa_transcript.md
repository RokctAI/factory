### subtopic_1 — Life Without Money
**Assistant:** If money disappeared, would people really stop trading?

**Tutor:** No. People would keep trading, because they would still have needs they cannot meet alone. What would change is how hard trading becomes. Without money, every exchange has to be a direct swap of one thing for another. That works when two neighbours each want what the other has, for example a farmer with maize and a potter with clay pots. It works badly when wants do not match. Economists call the lucky situation where both people want what the other offers a double coincidence of wants, and it is rare. People in history solved this in clever ways. Some communities agreed on one item that almost everyone valued, such as cattle, salt, beads or shells, and used it as a go-between. That item became a kind of early money even though it was not coins or notes. So the honest answer is that trade never stops; it slows down, becomes local and becomes unfair when people cannot compare value. That is exactly why money developed, and you will follow that story in the next session on bartering, coins and paper money.

### subtopic_2 — Traditional Societies: Meeting Needs Without Money
**Assistant:** Were traditional societies poor because they had no money?

**Tutor:** Not necessarily, and it is important not to judge them by modern standards alone. A traditional society can be rich in the things that matter for its way of life: enough land, healthy herds, good harvests, strong family ties and deep knowledge of the environment. The San, for example, had detailed knowledge of hundreds of plants and animals, and could survive in dry places where many modern people would struggle. Farming communities with large herds of cattle were wealthy by the standards of their time. At the same time, traditional societies faced real hardships. A drought could destroy a harvest and kill cattle, and there were no shops to buy food from and no insurance. Sickness was harder to treat. The choice of goods was small, because a household could only use what it could make or obtain nearby. So the fair answer is that traditional societies were not poor simply because they lacked money; they were vulnerable in different ways. When you compare traditional and modern societies in an exam, give advantages and disadvantages on both sides rather than calling one good and the other bad.

### subtopic_3 — Modern Societies: Specialisation and Interdependence
**Assistant:** Is specialisation always a good thing?

**Tutor:** Mostly it is, but it comes with risks. The great advantage is productivity. When people specialise, they become skilled and fast, and they can use special tools and machines. A bakery with trained bakers and big ovens can bake thousands of loaves a day, far more than if every family baked its own bread. This is why modern societies have so many more goods, services and inventions than traditional ones. The risk is dependence. A specialist needs a job to earn money, and needs the shops, trucks, power stations and water pipes to keep working. When something breaks in the chain, many people suffer at once. During load-shedding, for example, businesses that depend on electricity cannot work and people lose income. When a factory closes, the workers who specialised in that factory's work may struggle to find new jobs. In an exam, explain both sides: specialisation raises the amount and quality of goods and services, but it makes people interdependent, so they rely on money, income and a working economy to meet their needs.
