# Part 1 — Expert

This session completes the history of money with electronic banking and then draws the whole story together by examining the role of money in the economy. The governing idea is that most money today exists as bank balances recorded electronically, and it moves between accounts by card payments, electronic funds transfers and mobile payments, while still performing the same functions money has always performed. The forms of electronic banking, their advantages and risks, the functions of money and the place of money in the circular flow between households and businesses are examinable.

## Subtopic: Forms of Electronic Banking

Electronic banking means using electronic devices and networks, rather than cash or paper, to make payments and manage a bank account. Most money in South Africa today exists only as numbers in bank computers: the balance in your account is a record of what the bank owes you.

A debit card is linked to a cheque or savings account. When you pay with it, the money is taken straight out of your own account, so you can only spend what you have. A credit card lets you pay now with money borrowed from the bank, which you must pay back later, usually with interest if you do not settle the full amount on time. An automated teller machine, or ATM, lets you draw cash, check your balance and make deposits using your card and a secret PIN.

An electronic funds transfer, or EFT, moves money directly from one bank account to another. Employers pay most salaries by EFT, government pays many grants into bank accounts, and households pay rent, school fees and accounts this way. Internet banking and banking apps let you make EFTs, buy airtime and electricity, and check your statements from a computer or phone. Cellphone banking on simple phones works through USSD codes, which helps people without smartphones.

Newer forms include tap-to-pay cards, which use a small chip to pay by holding the card near the machine, phone wallets that do the same, and instant payments such as PayShap, which move money between banks within seconds using a cellphone number. Many small traders now accept card payments through small card machines linked to a phone.

The questions for this section are with you now: the meaning of electronic banking, and the difference between a debit card, a credit card and an EFT.

## Subtopic: Advantages and Risks of Electronic Banking

Electronic banking has many advantages. It is safer than carrying large amounts of cash, which can be lost or stolen. It is fast and convenient: you can pay accounts, buy electricity or send money to family in another province at any time of the day or night, without travelling to a bank. Records are kept automatically, so you can see every transaction on your statement. For businesses, card and EFT payments reduce the risk of robbery and make it easier to keep accurate records. For government, paying grants and salaries electronically is cheaper and reduces fraud.

There are also disadvantages and risks. Fraud is the biggest. Criminals try to trick people into giving away their PIN, passwords or one-time PINs. Phishing means sending fake emails, SMSs or WhatsApp messages that pretend to be from a bank and ask you to click a link or share details. A SIM swap fraud happens when a criminal takes over your cellphone number to receive your bank's security messages. At ATMs, criminals may shoulder-surf to see your PIN, or offer help and swap your card.

Other problems are practical. Bank charges, data costs and the cost of a smartphone can make electronic banking expensive for poor households. People in remote rural areas may have weak network coverage, and load-shedding or system failures can stop card machines and ATMs. Some people, especially older people, may find the technology confusing.

Simple safety rules help: never share your PIN or one-time PIN with anyone, not even someone who says they work for the bank; cover the keypad; do not click links in unexpected messages; check your statements; and report a lost card immediately.

The questions for this section are with you now: advantages and disadvantages of electronic banking, and how to protect yourself from fraud.

## Subtopic: The Role and Functions of Money

Whether it is a cow, a coin, a note or a number in a bank account, money does the same jobs. Economists list four functions of money.

First, money is a medium of exchange: it is accepted in payment for goods and services, so people do not need a double coincidence of wants. Second, money is a measure of value: every good and service can be given a price in rand, which makes it easy to compare. Third, money is a store of value: you can keep it and spend it later, so a farmer can sell a harvest in autumn and buy seed in spring. Fourth, money is a standard for deferred payment: debts can be stated in money and paid later, for example when a family buys a fridge on credit and pays it off over twelve months.

Money also plays a central role in the economy as a whole. Households own the factors of production, such as labour, and sell them to businesses. Businesses pay households wages, rent and other income in money. Households then spend that money buying goods and services from businesses. This continuous movement of money in one direction, and of goods, services and labour in the other, is called the circular flow. Without money the flow would slow to a crawl, because every exchange would be a barter.

Money does have a weakness as a store of value: when prices rise over time, which is called inflation, the same amount of money buys less. A loaf of bread that cost about R5 in 2005 costs well over R15 today. That is one reason why people save in bank accounts that pay interest.

The questions for this section are with you now: the four functions of money, and how money moves in the circular flow between households and businesses.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through one month of a family's money that hardly ever touches a hand, in plain words with the same facts.

## Subtopic: The Invisible Salary

Meet Mr Dlamini. On the twenty-fifth of the month his salary arrives. Nobody hands him an envelope of cash; his employer sends an EFT and the numbers in his account go up.

On Saturday the family buys groceries. Mr Dlamini taps his debit card on the machine, and the money moves from his account to the supermarket's account in a few seconds. At home his daughter buys prepaid electricity on the banking app and types the token into the meter. On Monday the rent goes to the landlord by EFT. His son needs R50 for a school trip, so Mr Dlamini sends it instantly to the teacher's cellphone number.

At the end of the month, Mr Dlamini has paid for almost everything without touching a coin or a note. The only cash he used was R20 for the taxi, because the taxi does not take cards yet.

This is electronic banking. The money is real, but it travels as information between bank accounts. It is fast, it is safer than a pocket full of cash and every payment is recorded on his statement.

## Subtopic: Stay Safe and Know the Four Jobs

One day Mr Dlamini gets an SMS: your account has been blocked, click this link to fix it. He nearly clicks, then remembers the golden rule. Banks never ask for your PIN, password or one-time PIN by SMS, phone or WhatsApp. He deletes the message and phones the bank on the number on his card. His account is fine. The message was phishing.

Keep three safety rules: never share your PIN or one-time PIN, never click links in unexpected messages, and cover the keypad at an ATM.

Now remember the four jobs money does, whether it is a coin or an app. We use it to buy things: a medium of exchange. We use it to compare prices: a measure of value. We use it to save: a store of value. And we use it to pay debts later: a standard for deferred payment.

And one last idea: money is a tool. A country becomes richer by producing more goods and services, not by printing more money.
