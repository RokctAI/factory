# Part 1 — Expert

This session traces the development of money from barter to commodity money, coins, promissory notes and paper money, ending with the rand. The governing idea is that each new form of money solved a problem of the form before it: barter needed a double coincidence of wants, commodity money was bulky or perishable, coins were heavy and could be stolen on long journeys, and promissory notes and banknotes were light but depended on trust. The characteristics of good money and the functions of money are examinable, and they return in Grade 8 and in Grade 10 Economics.

## Subtopic: Barter and the Double Coincidence of Wants

Barter is the direct exchange of one good or service for another, without using money. A farmer swaps a bag of maize for a clay pot; a blacksmith makes a hoe in exchange for a goat. Barter was the first way people traded, and it worked well in small communities where people knew each other and wanted similar things.

Barter has one big weakness: it needs a double coincidence of wants. This means that each person must want exactly what the other person is offering, at the same time. If the potter does not want maize, the farmer cannot get a pot, even though both would like to trade. The farmer may have to make a chain of swaps, maize for salt, salt for a hoe, hoe for a pot, wasting time and effort.

Barter has other problems too. There is no common measure of value, so every pair of goods needs its own exchange rate: how many pots for a goat, how many hoes for a cow, how many bags of maize for a hoe. Many goods cannot be divided without destroying their value; half a cow is not a cow. Many goods are perishable, so they cannot be stored for future trading. And it is difficult to carry bulky goods over long distances.

Barter has not disappeared. Neighbours still swap vegetables, children swap stickers, and some businesses swap services, for example a plumber fixing a painter's pipes in exchange for a painted room. But for a whole economy, barter is far too slow.

The questions for this section are with you now: the meaning of barter, the double coincidence of wants, and the problems of barter.

## Subtopic: Commodity Money and Coins

To escape the double coincidence of wants, communities began to use one item that almost everyone valued as a go-between in trade. This is called commodity money: money that is also a useful or valued good in itself. Across the world people used cattle, grain, salt, cowrie shells, beads, tea and cocoa beans. In southern Africa, cattle, iron hoes, copper and glass beads all served as forms of exchange and stores of wealth. In ancient Rome, soldiers were sometimes paid partly in salt, and the word salary is linked to the Latin word for salt.

Commodity money worked better than barter, but it still had problems. Cattle can get sick and die, grain can rot, and salt dissolves in rain. Different cows are not equal in value, and shells or beads can be gathered or made by anyone, which can make them lose value.

Metals solved many of these problems. Gold, silver and copper do not rot, are scarce, can be divided into small amounts and can be melted and shaped. At first, people weighed lumps of metal at every sale. Then rulers began to stamp pieces of metal with a mark that showed their weight and purity. These were the first coins, made in Lydia, in present-day Turkey, about two thousand six hundred years ago. A stamped coin could be counted instead of weighed, which made trade much faster.

But coins also had problems. Large amounts were heavy to carry, and merchants travelling long distances risked being robbed. Some people clipped or shaved metal off the edges of coins, so the coin weighed less than its stamped value.

The questions for this section are with you now: the meaning of commodity money, examples, and the advantages and problems of coins.

## Subtopic: Promissory Notes, Paper Money and the Rand

Merchants who did not want to carry heavy coins on dangerous roads began to leave their gold and silver with goldsmiths or bankers for safekeeping. The banker gave them a written receipt. This receipt was a promissory note: a written promise to pay a stated amount of money to the holder on demand. Soon people found it easier to hand the receipts to each other in trade than to fetch the coins. The paper itself began to act as money.

The first paper money was used in China more than a thousand years ago. In Europe, banks later printed banknotes that promised to pay the bearer in gold or silver. Over time governments gave a central bank the sole right to issue notes. Today notes are no longer exchanged for gold; their value depends on the law, which makes them legal tender, and on people's trust in the central bank.

In South Africa the currency is the rand, introduced in 1961, divided into one hundred cents. Only the South African Reserve Bank, known as the SARB, may issue rand notes and coins. Notes come in R10, R20, R50, R100 and R200, and coins in 10c, 20c, 50c, R1, R2 and R5. The notes carry a portrait of Nelson Mandela on the front and the Big Five animals on the back, and include security features such as a watermark, a security thread and colour-changing ink to make forgery difficult.

Good money has certain characteristics. It must be generally acceptable; durable, so it lasts; divisible into small amounts; portable, so it is easy to carry; scarce, so it keeps its value; and uniform, so each unit is the same. Money also has four functions: it is a medium of exchange, a measure of value, a store of value and a standard for deferred payment, which means it can be used to settle debts later.

The questions for this section are with you now: promissory notes, paper money, the rand and the Reserve Bank, and the characteristics and functions of money.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through one goat travelling through history, in plain words with the same facts.

## Subtopic: The Goat's Journey

Follow one goat through history. A long time ago, Sipho wants grain. He takes his goat to a farmer and swaps it for three bags of grain. That is barter. It only works because the farmer happens to want a goat.

Next, the village agrees that glass beads are valuable to everyone. Now Sipho sells his goat for ten beads and uses the beads to buy grain from anyone, even someone who does not want a goat. The beads are commodity money.

Later, metal coins arrive, stamped with their value. Sipho sells the goat for five silver coins. Coins do not rot, are easy to count and can be saved. But a bag of coins is heavy, and robbers on the road love heavy bags.

So Sipho leaves his coins with a banker and gets a paper receipt, a promissory note. He can pay with the paper, and the person who receives it can fetch the coins. Soon the paper is used as money all by itself. Today the same goat might sell for about two thousand rand at a market, paid in rand notes issued by the Reserve Bank, or straight into a bank account.

Each step fixed a problem of the step before. Keep the goat's journey in mind and try the questions for this part.

## Subtopic: What Makes Good Money?

Here is a quick test for good money. Imagine trying to use different things as money and ask six questions.

Does everybody accept it? A R10 coin, yes; a pencil, no.

Does it last? Coins and notes last for years; tomatoes last a week.

Can you carry it? A R200 note fits in your pocket; a cow does not.

Can you split it? Rand splits into cents, so you can pay R7,50 for bread; you cannot pay with half a goat.

Is it scarce? If leaves were money, anyone could pick a million and they would be worthless.

Is every unit the same? Every R5 coin is worth exactly the same; every cow is different.

The rand passes all six tests, which is why it works. And money does four jobs for us: we use it to buy things, to compare prices, to save for later and to pay debts later.

Try the questions for this part: test some items to see whether they would make good money.
