# Part 1 — Expert

This session completes the basic financial concepts for Grade 7: losses, budgets, savings, banking, financial records and transactions. The governing idea is that people and businesses manage money well when they plan before they spend, keep a record of every transaction, save part of their income, and use banks to keep money safe and make payments. The definitions of each concept, the causes of losses, the purpose of a budget, the benefits of saving and banking, and the importance of source documents and records are examinable, and they are developed in the rest of Term 2.

## Subtopic: Profit and Loss

In the previous lesson, profit was defined as income minus expenses. When expenses are greater than income for a period, the result is a loss. If a tuck shop sells snacks for R1 200 in a month but spends R1 450 on stock, packaging and transport, it makes a loss of R250.

A loss reduces owner's equity, because the business has used up more than it earned. One loss is not a disaster, but repeated losses mean the business will eventually run out of cash and close.

Common causes of losses include: selling too little, because there are too few customers or competitors are cheaper; prices that are too low to cover costs; costs that are too high, such as expensive rent or buying stock at retail prices instead of wholesale; waste, such as food that goes off or stock that is damaged; theft by outsiders or staff; and unexpected events such as load-shedding, floods or illness.

A business that makes a loss needs to find out why, and records make that possible. By looking at the income and expenses in detail, the owner can see whether sales fell, whether a particular expense rose, or whether stock is going missing. Then it can act: change prices, find cheaper suppliers, reduce waste, improve security or try new products and advertising.

Households can also make a loss in a sense: when they spend more than they earn in a month, they must use savings or borrow, and debt can build up.

The questions for this section are with you now: the meaning of a loss, its causes, and how records help an owner respond.

## Subtopic: Budgets and Savings

A budget is a plan for the future that shows the income expected and how it will be spent over a period such as a month or a year. Households, businesses, schools and governments all use budgets.

A budget helps in several ways. It makes sure that needs are paid for before wants. It shows in advance whether there will be enough money, so that a shortfall can be fixed by cutting spending or finding extra income before it happens. It helps people save for goals such as school fees, a bicycle or a new oven for the business. It also allows comparison: at the end of the month the actual income and spending can be compared with the budget to see where things went differently.

Savings are the part of income that is not spent now but kept for future use. People save for emergencies, such as a broken fridge or a medical bill; for goals, such as a school trip or a business; and for the long term, such as retirement. A useful rule is to pay yourself first: put a fixed amount aside as savings as soon as income arrives, rather than saving whatever is left at the end of the month, which is often nothing.

Savings can be kept at home, but this is risky because of theft and fire, and the money does not grow. Saving in a bank account or in a well-run community scheme such as a stokvel is safer, and banks pay interest.

Budgets and savings will be studied in more detail later in this term and in Term 4.

The questions for this section are with you now: the meaning and purpose of a budget, the meaning of savings, and reasons to save.

## Subtopic: Banking, Transactions and Financial Records

A bank is a financial institution that keeps people's money safe, makes payments for them, pays interest on savings and lends money to people and businesses, charging interest on loans. Banks offer accounts, cards, electronic transfers, cellphone banking and statements. A bank statement is a record from the bank of all the money that came into and went out of an account in a period.

A financial transaction is any event that involves the exchange of money or something with a money value: buying stock, selling cupcakes, paying wages, receiving a loan, depositing cash. Every transaction affects the business's financial position, so every one must be recorded.

The proof of a transaction is called a source document. When a business sells goods for cash it gives the customer a receipt or a cash register slip. When it pays by cheque or EFT, there is a cheque counterfoil or a payment confirmation. When it buys goods, it receives an invoice or a till slip from the supplier. Source documents prove that the transaction happened and give the details: the date, the amount, what was bought or sold and who was involved.

Financial records are the organised written or electronic records of all transactions. A small business may start with a simple cash book: a notebook with money received on one side and money paid on the other. Larger businesses use journals, ledgers and accounting software, which you will meet in Grade 8. Households can also keep records of income and spending.

Records are important because they show exactly where money came from and where it went, make it possible to calculate profit or loss, help the owner make decisions, prove income and expenses for tax and for loans, and help detect mistakes and theft.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through one school tuck shop over two months, in plain words with the same facts.

## Subtopic: The Tuck Shop's Bad Month

The Grade 7 class runs a tuck shop to raise money for a class trip. In March they sell chips, sweets and muffins for R1 200. But they spent R1 450 on stock. Expenses are bigger than income, so they made a loss of R250.

The class is upset. Their teacher says: let's look at the records. Every purchase had a till slip, and every sale was written in the cash book.

The records show the problem. They bought too many muffins, and R300 worth went off before anyone bought them. Waste caused the loss.

In April they budget before they buy. They plan to spend R900 on stock and buy only as many muffins as they can sell in two days. They put R100 aside every week as savings for the trip, in the school's bank account, which pays a little interest.

At the end of April they sell R1 400 worth of snacks with R950 of expenses: a profit of R450. The loss became a lesson.

## Subtopic: Plan, Save, Bank, Record

The tuck shop learned four habits that every household and business needs.

Plan. Make a budget before you spend: how much will come in, how much will go out and on what.

Save. Put some money aside as soon as it comes in, for emergencies and goals.

Bank. Keep the money in a bank account, where it is safe from theft and earns interest, and payments are easy.

Record. Keep every slip, receipt and invoice: those are source documents. Write every transaction in a cash book. Then you can always see where the money went and whether you made a profit or a loss.

Remember: a transaction is any money exchange; a source document proves it; records show the whole picture; a budget plans the future.

Try the questions for this part: match each habit to what it does.
