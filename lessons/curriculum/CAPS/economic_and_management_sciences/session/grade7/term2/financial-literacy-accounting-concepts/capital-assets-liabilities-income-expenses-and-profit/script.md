# Part 1 — Expert

This session introduces the basic accounting concepts that every business uses to record and understand its finances: capital, assets, liabilities, income, expenses and profit. The governing idea is that a business is separate from its owner, that what a business owns is financed either by the owner or by people it owes, and that profit is what remains when expenses are subtracted from income. The definitions, examples and the simple calculation of profit are examinable, and they are the foundation of Accounting in Grades 8 to 12.

## Subtopic: Capital, Assets and Liabilities

When a person starts a business, the business is treated as separate from the owner, even if the owner runs it alone. This lets us see clearly what belongs to the business and what belongs to the owner.

Capital is the money or other resources the owner puts into the business from his or her own pocket. If Nandi takes R3 000 from her savings and puts it into her cupcake business, the business has capital of R3 000. Capital is what the business owes the owner.

Assets are the things of value that the business owns and uses to operate. Examples are cash in the bank or in the till, equipment such as an oven or a cash register, vehicles, furniture, land and buildings, stock of goods for sale, and money that customers still owe the business, called debtors. If Nandi uses R1 800 of her capital to buy an oven, she now has an asset worth R1 800 and cash of R1 200, still R3 000 in assets altogether.

Liabilities are amounts the business owes to people or organisations other than the owner. Examples are loans from a bank or a relative, and money owed to suppliers for goods bought on credit, called creditors. If Nandi's uncle lends the business R1 000, the business has a liability of R1 000 and its cash increases by R1 000.

These ideas are linked in the accounting equation: assets equal owner's equity plus liabilities. In simple terms, everything the business owns was paid for either by the owner or by someone the business owes. After the loan, Nandi's business has assets of R4 000, the oven of R1 800 and cash of R2 200, which equals her capital of R3 000 plus the loan of R1 000.

The questions for this section are with you now: the meaning of capital, assets and liabilities, examples of each, and the accounting equation.

## Subtopic: Income, Expenses and Profit

Income is the money a business earns from its activities. For a business that sells goods, the main income is sales. For a business that provides services, it is fees for services, such as a hairdresser's fees or a mechanic's repair fees. Other income can include rent received for letting part of a building, or interest earned on money in the bank. Income increases the owner's equity, because it adds to what the business is worth for the owner.

Expenses are the costs of running the business, used up in order to earn income. Examples include wages paid to workers, rent for premises, water and electricity, telephone and data, transport, advertising, stationery, bank charges, insurance and the cost of the goods that were sold. Expenses decrease the owner's equity.

It is important to distinguish expenses from assets. Buying an oven is not an expense, because the oven stays in the business and is used for years; it is an asset. Buying flour that is used up in the cupcakes sold this month is an expense, part of the cost of sales. Paying back part of a loan is also not an expense; it reduces a liability.

Profit is what remains when total expenses are subtracted from total income for a period such as a month or a year:

Profit equals income minus expenses.

If Nandi sells cupcakes for R2 400 in a month and her expenses for ingredients, boxes, electricity and transport total R1 500, her profit is R2 400 minus R1 500, which is R900. Profit belongs to the owner and increases owner's equity. The owner may leave it in the business to grow it, or take some out as drawings.

The questions for this section are with you now: the meaning of income and expenses, examples, the difference between an expense and an asset, and calculating profit.

## Subtopic: The Effect on Owner's Equity

Owner's equity is the owner's share of the business: what the business would owe the owner if all the assets were sold and all the liabilities paid. It starts with the capital the owner contributed and then changes over time.

Four things change owner's equity. Capital contributions increase it: when the owner puts more money or equipment into the business. Profit increases it: when income is greater than expenses, the extra belongs to the owner. A loss decreases it: when expenses are greater than income. Drawings decrease it: when the owner takes money or goods out for personal use.

We can show this with Nandi's business. She starts with capital of R3 000. In the first month she makes a profit of R900, so her equity grows to R3 900. She takes R300 in drawings, so her equity is R3 600 at the end of the month.

The accounting equation must still balance. Her assets, the oven and cash, are now worth enough to cover her equity of R3 600 plus the loan from her uncle of R1 000, so her assets total R4 600.

This shows why accounting is so useful. Without keeping track, Nandi might feel she has lots of cash and spend it. With the records, she can see that her business has grown, that she owes her uncle R1 000, and that she can decide how much to take out and how much to leave in to buy more ingredients or a second oven.

The questions for this section are with you now: the meaning of owner's equity, the four things that change it, and a simple calculation.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through one month of Nandi's cupcake business, in plain words with the same facts.

## Subtopic: Nandi's Cupcake Month

Nandi loves baking. On the first of the month she takes R3 000 from her savings and puts it into a new cupcake business. That is capital: the owner's own money in the business.

She buys a small oven for R1 800. The oven belongs to the business and will be used for years, so it is an asset. Her cash is now R1 200.

She wants more cash for ingredients, so her uncle lends the business R1 000. The business owes it back, so it is a liability.

During the month she sells cupcakes at school events and to neighbours for R2 400. That is income. She spends R1 500 on flour, sugar, eggs, boxes, electricity and transport. Those are expenses.

At the end of the month she works out her profit: R2 400 income minus R1 500 expenses equals R900 profit. Well done, Nandi.

Try the questions for this part: label each thing Nandi did as capital, asset, liability, income or expense.

## Subtopic: Own, Owe, Earn, Spend

Here is a memory trick with four short words.

Own. Things the business owns are assets: cash, the oven, stock, a van.

Owe. Money the business owes to others is a liability: the uncle's loan, a bank loan, money owed to a supplier.

Earn. Money the business earns is income: sales and fees.

Spend to run. Money spent to run the business and used up is an expense: ingredients, electricity, wages, rent.

And the owner's money? Put in by the owner: capital. Taken out by the owner for personal use: drawings.

Then just remember one sum: income minus expenses equals profit. If the answer is negative, it is a loss.

Try the questions for this part: say own, owe, earn or spend for each item.
