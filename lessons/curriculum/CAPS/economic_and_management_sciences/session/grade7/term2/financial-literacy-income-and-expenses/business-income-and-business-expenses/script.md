# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines business income and business expenses in detail. The governing idea is that a business earns income by selling goods or providing services, pays expenses in order to earn that income, and must record both accurately to calculate profit and make decisions. Types of business income, types of business expenses, the difference between fixed and variable expenses, the distinction between business and personal transactions, and a simple income and expenses statement are examinable.

## Subtopic: Types of Business Income

Business income is the money a business earns from its activities over a period. The main source depends on what the business does.

A business that sells goods, such as a spaza shop, a clothing store or a bakery, earns sales. Sales can be cash sales, where the customer pays immediately, or credit sales, where the customer takes the goods and pays later. In Grade 7 we focus on cash transactions.

A business that provides services, such as a hair salon, a plumber, a taxi or a doctor, earns fees for services rendered, sometimes called current income. A salon's income is the fees charged for haircuts, braiding, colouring and manicures.

Businesses can also earn other income. If a business owns a building and lets part of it to another business, it earns rent income. If it keeps money in a bank account that pays interest, it earns interest income. A business may receive commission for selling someone else's products, for example airtime or electricity vouchers in a spaza shop.

Money that comes into a business is not always income. When the owner contributes capital, the business receives cash, but this is not income because it was not earned from trading. When the business borrows a loan, cash comes in, but this creates a liability, not income. Only money earned from the business's operations counts as income when calculating profit.

The questions for this section are with you now: types of business income, and money received that is not income.

## Subtopic: Types of Business Expenses

Business expenses are the costs a business incurs in order to earn income. Common expenses include: the cost of the goods sold, also called cost of sales, which is what the business paid for the stock it sold; wages and salaries paid to employees; rent for the premises; water and electricity; telephone and internet; transport and fuel; advertising; stationery; insurance; bank charges; repairs; and consumable materials, such as shampoo and hair products used in a salon.

Expenses can be grouped as fixed or variable.

Fixed expenses stay the same each month, regardless of how many goods are sold or customers served. Rent is the clearest example: a salon pays R4 000 a month whether it has ten customers or two hundred. Insurance, a fixed salary and a monthly internet contract are usually fixed.

Variable expenses change with the level of activity. The more customers the salon serves, the more shampoo, hair extensions and electricity it uses; the more a bakery bakes, the more flour and eggs it buys. Cost of sales, packaging and commission are variable.

Knowing which expenses are fixed matters because fixed expenses must be paid even in a quiet month. A new business must earn enough income to cover at least its fixed expenses before it can make any profit. You will use these ideas again in Term 3, when you work out costs for Entrepreneur's Day.

Remember that buying equipment, such as a hairdryer or a salon chair, is not an expense; it is an asset that will be used for a long time.

The questions for this section are with you now: types of business expenses, and the difference between fixed and variable expenses.

## Subtopic: The Income and Expenses Statement

At the end of each month, a business owner can draw up a simple income and expenses statement to see whether the business made a profit or a loss. It lists all the income for the period, then all the expenses, and subtracts the total expenses from the total income.

For example, Zola's salon, for the month of May: fees earned R18 500, sale of hair products R1 500, total income R20 000. Expenses: hair products and consumables used R4 200, wages R6 000, rent R4 000, water and electricity R1 300, telephone and data R400, advertising R300, bank charges R100, total expenses R16 300. Profit equals R20 000 minus R16 300, which is R3 700.

Accurate figures depend on keeping good records and source documents for every transaction: receipts for cash received, till slips and invoices for purchases, and bank statements.

It is essential to keep business and personal transactions separate. If Zola pays her household electricity account from the salon's money, it is not a business expense; it is drawings. If she uses business money for groceries, that is drawings too. Mixing them makes the profit figure wrong and makes it impossible to see how the business is really doing. A separate business bank account helps a great deal.

The income and expenses statement helps the owner make decisions: whether prices should change, which expenses are too high, whether she can afford to hire another stylist and how much she can safely take as drawings. In later grades you will learn the formal version, the statement of comprehensive income.

The questions for this section are with you now: drawing up a simple income and expenses statement, and why business and personal transactions must be kept separate.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through one month in Zola's salon, in plain words with the same facts.

## Subtopic: Zola's Salon in May

Zola owns a small hair salon in a shopping centre. Here is May.

Money in: customers paid R18 500 for haircuts, braids and manicures. She also sold hair products for R1 500. Total income: R20 000.

Money out to run the salon: hair products and supplies R4 200, wages for her assistant R6 000, rent R4 000, water and electricity R1 300, data R400, posters and social media adverts R300, bank charges R100. Total expenses: R16 300.

Profit: R20 000 minus R16 300 equals R3 700.

One day Zola paid for her family's groceries with R800 from the salon's cash box. That is not a salon expense; nobody's hair got done with those groceries. It is drawings: the owner taking money for personal use. It reduces what she has in the business but is not counted in the profit.

Try the questions for this part: sort each payment into income, expense or drawings.

## Subtopic: Fixed or Variable?

Now look at Zola's expenses another way. Ask: does this cost change if more customers come in?

Rent: R4 000 every month, busy or quiet. Fixed.

Her assistant's monthly wage: the same every month. Fixed.

Hair products and extensions: more customers means more products used. Variable.

Electricity: more hairdryers running means more electricity. Mostly variable.

In January the salon is quiet. Fewer customers means less spending on products. But the rent and the wage are still due in full. That is why quiet months are hard: fixed costs do not shrink.

So Zola saves some of December's profit to help pay January's fixed costs. Smart.

Remember: income is what the business earns, expenses are what it spends to earn it, fixed stays the same, variable changes with activity, and personal spending is drawings.

Try the questions for this part: say fixed or variable for each cost.
