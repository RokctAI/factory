### subtopic_1 — Types of Business Income
**Assistant:** Why is capital not counted as income?

**Tutor:** Because income measures what the business earns from its own activities, and capital is not earned. When the owner puts R5 000 of savings into the business, the business's cash increases, but nothing was sold and no service was provided; the owner simply moved her own money into the business. If capital were counted as income, profit would look R5 000 higher than it really is, and the owner might believe the business is successful when it is not. In fact, a business could appear to make a profit every month simply because the owner keeps putting in more of her own money, while it is actually losing money on trading. The same applies to loans: borrowed money must be paid back, so it is a liability, not income. Keeping these separate is one of the basic rules of accounting. In Grade 8 you will see that capital is recorded in the owner's equity section and loans in the liabilities section of the accounts, while only sales, fees, rent income, interest income and similar items appear as income in the calculation of profit.

### subtopic_2 — Types of Business Expenses
**Assistant:** Why do fixed expenses make a quiet month so dangerous for a small business?

**Tutor:** Because fixed expenses do not shrink when sales do. Imagine a salon with rent of R4 000, insurance of R300 and a receptionist's salary of R3 500: fixed expenses of R7 800 a month. In a busy month, the salon earns R20 000 in fees and spends R4 000 on variable costs such as products and extra electricity, leaving a healthy profit after the fixed expenses. In a quiet month, perhaps in January when customers have spent their money over the holidays, fees fall to R9 000 and variable costs to R2 000. Income minus variable costs is now only R7 000, which does not cover the R7 800 of fixed expenses, so the salon makes a loss of R800, even though it served many customers. Variable costs fall with activity, but fixed costs do not, so they eat into the owner's cash. That is why owners should keep cash reserves from good months, try to keep fixed costs low when starting, and plan for seasonal patterns. Understanding the difference between fixed and variable costs is one of the most practical skills in running a business.

### subtopic_3 — The Income and Expenses Statement
**Assistant:** How can the income and expenses statement help Zola decide whether to raise her prices?

**Tutor:** By showing her exactly how much each part of the business earns and costs. Suppose the statement shows that her income from braiding is high but that the cost of hair extensions has risen sharply since last year, so the profit on each braiding appointment has shrunk. She can calculate the cost of materials and time for a typical braid and compare it with her price. If the price barely covers the costs, she has a strong reason to raise it, perhaps slightly, or to find a cheaper supplier. The statement also shows her fixed expenses, so she knows how much income she needs each month just to break even. Before raising prices, she should also think about her customers and competitors: if other salons nearby charge less, a big increase could drive customers away and reduce income. She might try a small increase, offer a loyalty discount, or add a higher-priced premium service. Without the statement, Zola would be guessing. With it, she can make a decision based on evidence, and then check next month's statement to see whether it worked.
