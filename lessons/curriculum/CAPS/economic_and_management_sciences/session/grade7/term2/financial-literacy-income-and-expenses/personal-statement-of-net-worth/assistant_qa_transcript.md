### subtopic_1 — Personal Assets and Personal Liabilities
**Assistant:** Is a cellphone on contract an asset or a liability?

**Tutor:** It is both, and the two must be shown separately. The phone itself is a personal asset, because you have it and use it, and it has a value, although that value drops quickly as the phone gets older and new models appear. The contract is a liability, because you have agreed to pay the network a monthly amount for a fixed period, often twenty-four months, and part of each payment is paying off the phone. The amount still to be paid under the contract is what you owe. Early in the contract, the remaining payments may be much larger than what the phone is worth second-hand, so the phone adds less to net worth than people think, and may even reduce it. This is a useful lesson about many purchases on credit: you may have the item, but you are also carrying a debt. When you draw up a statement of net worth, list the phone at its current value under assets and the amount still owing under liabilities. Being honest about both is what makes the statement useful.

### subtopic_2 — Drawing Up the Statement of Net Worth
**Assistant:** Does a high income mean a high net worth?

**Tutor:** Not necessarily, and this surprises many people. Income is the money a person earns over a period, such as a monthly salary. Net worth is what a person has built up: everything owned minus everything owed, at a moment in time. Someone with a high salary who spends all of it, buys expensive cars and clothes on credit and has large debts may have a low or even negative net worth. Someone with a modest income who lives carefully, saves regularly, avoids unnecessary debt and pays off a house over many years may build a solid net worth. This is why financial advisers say that what you keep matters more than what you earn. It also explains why people who appear wealthy, because of what they buy, may be financially fragile, while others who live simply may be secure. The statement of net worth helps people look past appearances and see their real position. Comparing statements drawn up a year apart shows whether a person is moving forward or backward financially, which is more useful than looking at income alone.

### subtopic_3 — Growing Net Worth
**Assistant:** Is it ever wise to buy something on credit?

**Tutor:** Sometimes, but it needs careful thought. Credit means using borrowed money now and paying it back later with interest, so the item always costs more than its cash price. It can be wise when the item is a genuine need or an asset that holds its value or helps you earn more, and when the monthly repayment fits comfortably in your budget. Examples are a home loan for a reasonably priced house, a vehicle that a person needs to get to work where there is no public transport, or a loan for study or for equipment in a small business. It is usually unwise for wants and for things that lose value quickly, such as clothing, takeaways, the latest phone or a holiday, especially on store cards and credit cards with high interest rates. A useful test before using credit is to ask: Do I really need this now? What will it cost in total, including interest? Can I afford the repayments even if my income drops? Could I save up and buy it for cash instead? In South Africa many households carry heavy debt, so learning these questions early is a powerful protection.
