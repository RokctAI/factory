# Part 1 — Expert

This session applies the concepts of assets and liabilities to an individual by drawing up a personal statement of net worth. The governing idea is that a person's net worth is the value of everything they own minus everything they owe, and that net worth, not income or possessions alone, shows how financially secure a person is. The meaning of personal assets, personal liabilities and net worth, the layout of the statement, the calculation, and ways to increase net worth are examinable.

## Subtopic: Personal Assets and Personal Liabilities

Just as a business has assets and liabilities, so does every individual.

Personal assets are the things of value that a person owns. Examples are a house or land, a car or bicycle, furniture and appliances such as a fridge, stove and television, a cellphone and computer, jewellery, livestock such as cattle or goats, money in a savings account, cash and investments. Assets are valued at what they are worth now, which is often less than what was paid for them, because many goods lose value as they get older and are used. A car bought for R180 000 might be worth only R120 000 a few years later.

Personal liabilities are the amounts that a person owes to others. Examples are a home loan, called a bond, owed to a bank; a car loan; a personal loan; a credit card balance; clothing and furniture store accounts; and money borrowed from family, friends or a stokvel that must be repaid. Liabilities are valued at the amount still owing, not the original amount borrowed.

Some assets are bought with borrowed money. A family that buys a house worth R600 000 with a bond has an asset of R600 000 but also a liability equal to the amount still owed on the bond. The family does not really own the whole house until the bond is paid off.

It is important to list assets and liabilities honestly and completely. Forgetting a store account or overestimating the value of a car gives a misleading picture.

## Subtopic: Drawing Up the Statement of Net Worth

A personal statement of net worth is a list of a person's assets and liabilities on a particular date, with the net worth calculated at the bottom. It is like a financial photograph taken on that date.

The layout is simple. The heading gives the person's name and the date, for example: Statement of Net Worth of N. Mokoena on 30 June 2026. Then come two lists. Under Assets, each asset is listed with its current value, and the values are added to give total assets. Under Liabilities, each liability is listed with the amount still owing, and these are added to give total liabilities. Finally:

Net worth equals total assets minus total liabilities.

Here is an example. Mrs Mokoena owns a house valued at R450 000, a car worth R90 000, furniture and appliances worth R40 000, and has R15 000 in a savings account and R2 000 in cash. Her total assets are R597 000. She owes R280 000 on her bond, R60 000 on her car loan and R7 000 on a clothing account. Her total liabilities are R347 000. Her net worth is R597 000 minus R347 000, which is R250 000.

Net worth can be positive, when assets are greater than liabilities, or negative, when a person owes more than they own. A student who has just taken a large study loan may have a negative net worth for a while, even though the education will help them earn more later.

Notice that this is the same idea as the accounting equation for a business: assets minus liabilities equals owner's equity. A person's net worth is their personal equity.

## Subtopic: Growing Net Worth

Because net worth equals assets minus liabilities, there are two ways to increase it: increase assets, or decrease liabilities. Most people do both over time.

To increase assets, people save part of their income regularly and invest it, for example in savings accounts, fixed deposits, unit trusts or retirement funds, so that it grows with interest and returns. They may buy assets that tend to hold or increase their value, such as property or land, rather than spending everything on things that lose value quickly.

To decrease liabilities, people pay off debts, especially those with the highest interest rates, such as credit cards and store accounts. They avoid taking on new unnecessary debt, for example buying clothes or electronics on credit just because the monthly instalment looks small. Paying more than the minimum instalment on a loan reduces both the debt and the total interest paid.

Some debt can be reasonable if it helps build net worth over time: a home loan to buy a house that holds its value, or a study loan that increases future income. Debt used for wants that lose value quickly, such as an expensive phone or holiday on credit, reduces net worth.

Net worth also depends on things a person cannot always control: a car may be stolen, a property may lose value, or illness may stop someone working. That is why insurance and an emergency savings fund are part of protecting net worth.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through two neighbours and one simple subtraction, in plain words with the same facts.

## Subtopic: Own Minus Owe

Think of net worth as one simple sum: what you own minus what you owe.

Mrs Mokoena makes two lists. What she owns: her house worth R450 000, her car worth R90 000, furniture worth R40 000, savings of R15 000 and cash of R2 000. Total: R597 000.

What she owes: R280 000 still on the bond, R60 000 on the car loan and R7 000 on a clothing account. Total: R347 000.

Now subtract: R597 000 minus R347 000 equals R250 000. That is her net worth. If she sold everything and paid all her debts, she would be left with about R250 000.

Notice she did not include her salary. Salary is income that comes in each month; net worth is what she has built up on one day.

## Subtopic: The Shiny Car Trap

Mrs Mokoena's neighbour, Mr Khoza, drives a brand-new car and wears expensive clothes. Everyone thinks he is rich.

Let us check. He owns a car worth R300 000, furniture and electronics worth R80 000 and R20 000 in savings: R400 000. He owes R330 000 on the car, R60 000 on furniture and electronics accounts and R30 000 on his credit card: R420 000.

Own minus owe: R400 000 minus R420 000 equals minus R20 000. His net worth is negative. He owes more than he owns.

Mrs Mokoena drives an older car, but her net worth is R250 000. Looking rich is not the same as being rich.

How can Mr Khoza grow his net worth? Pay off the credit card first, stop buying on credit, save something every month and keep the car until the loan is paid off.
