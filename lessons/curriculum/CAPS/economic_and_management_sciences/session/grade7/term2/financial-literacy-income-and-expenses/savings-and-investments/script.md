# Part 1 — Expert

This session distinguishes saving from investing and introduces the ways in which money can grow over time. The governing idea is that money saved and invested earns interest or returns, that interest earned on interest makes savings grow faster the longer they are left, and that every investment involves a trade-off between risk, return and access. The meaning of savings and investment, simple and compound interest, types of savings and investments available in South Africa, and the relationship between risk and return are examinable.

## Subtopic: Saving and Investing

Saving and investing both mean not spending all of your income now, but they are not quite the same.

Saving means setting money aside for future use, usually somewhere safe and easy to reach, such as a savings account. People save for emergencies and for short-term goals, such as a school trip next term, a new pair of shoes or a deposit on a fridge. The main aims are safety and access: the money should be there when it is needed.

Investing means using money to buy assets that are expected to grow in value or produce income over time. Examples include fixed deposits, unit trusts, shares in companies, government bonds, retirement annuities, property and starting or buying into a business. The main aim is growth. Investments are usually for longer-term goals, such as education for a child, buying a house or retirement, and some cannot be withdrawn quickly without a penalty or loss.

Both are important. Financial planners often suggest building an emergency fund first, enough savings to cover a few months of essential expenses, and then investing for long-term goals.

Saving and investing also help the economy. Money deposited in banks is lent to households and businesses to build houses, buy equipment and expand, which creates jobs. Money invested in shares helps companies grow. A country with a low level of saving must borrow more from other countries to invest. South Africa's household savings rate is low compared with many other countries, which is why government and banks encourage saving.

## Subtopic: Interest and Compound Growth

Interest is the price paid for the use of money. When you deposit money in a bank, the bank uses it to lend to others, so it pays you interest. When you borrow money, you pay interest to the lender. Interest is usually stated as a percentage per year, called the interest rate.

Simple interest is calculated only on the original amount, called the principal. If you deposit R1 000 at 8 percent simple interest per year, you earn R80 each year. After three years you have R1 000 plus three times R80, which is R1 240.

Compound interest is calculated on the principal plus the interest already earned, so you earn interest on your interest. With R1 000 at 8 percent compounded yearly: after one year you have R1 080; in the second year you earn 8 percent of R1 080, which is R86,40, giving R1 166,40; in the third year you earn 8 percent of R1 166,40, about R93,31, giving about R1 259,71. The difference grows larger every year.

Over long periods compound growth is powerful. At 8 percent a year, money roughly doubles in about nine years. This is why starting to save early makes such a big difference: a person who starts saving R200 a month at twenty will usually end up with much more at retirement than a person who saves the same amount but starts at forty, even though the second person only missed twenty years.

The same power works against borrowers. Debt with compound interest, especially on credit cards and some loans, can grow quickly if payments are missed.

Remember too that inflation reduces what money can buy. For savings to grow in real terms, the interest rate must be higher than the inflation rate.

## Subtopic: Types of Savings and Investments, Risk and Return

South Africans can choose from many places to save and invest.

A savings account at a bank is safe and easy to access, but usually pays a low interest rate. A fixed deposit pays a higher rate if you agree to leave the money in the bank for a fixed period, such as twelve months. A notice deposit requires you to give notice, for example thirty-two days, before withdrawing. Government retail savings bonds let ordinary people lend money to government at a fixed rate for a few years.

Community schemes are very important in South Africa. A stokvel is a group of people who each contribute a fixed amount regularly, for example monthly, and take turns to receive the pool or share it at the end of the year, often for groceries in December. Burial societies help members pay for funerals. These schemes build discipline and trust, though they are less regulated than banks.

Unit trusts pool money from many investors and invest it in a mix of shares, bonds and cash, managed by professionals. Shares are part-ownership of a company listed on the Johannesburg Stock Exchange, the JSE; shareholders may receive dividends and benefit if the share price rises. Property, such as a house or flat that is rented out, can produce rental income and grow in value. Retirement funds, such as pension funds and retirement annuities, help people save for old age, often with tax benefits.

Every option involves a trade-off between risk, return and access. Risk is the chance of losing some or all of the money or of returns being lower than expected. Generally, the higher the possible return, the higher the risk. A savings account is low risk and low return; shares can earn high returns over many years but their value can fall sharply in the short term. Spreading money across different investments, called diversification, reduces risk.

Beware of schemes that promise very high, guaranteed returns with no risk; these are often scams, including pyramid and Ponzi schemes, that pay early members with later members' money until they collapse.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through two friends and two money jars over many years, in plain words with the same facts.

## Subtopic: Lebo Starts Early, Sam Starts Late

Lebo and Sam are friends. Both decide to save R200 a month in an investment that grows by about eight percent a year.

Lebo starts at twenty, when she gets her first job. Sam thinks he will start later, and begins at forty.

At sixty, both stop. Lebo saved for forty years; Sam for twenty. Lebo put in R96 000 of her own money; Sam put in R48 000. But because of compound interest, where your interest earns interest, Lebo's savings are worth roughly six times as much as Sam's, close to R700 000 compared with about R120 000.

Why such a big difference? In the early years, Lebo's savings grew slowly. But every year the interest was added and then earned interest itself, and in the later years the growth snowballed. Sam missed those first twenty years of snowballing.

The lesson: start early, even with small amounts, and let time do the work.

## Subtopic: The Safe Jar and the Growth Jar

Imagine two jars.

The safe jar is for emergencies and short goals: a savings account at the bank, or a stokvel with people you trust. The money is safe and you can get it quickly, but it grows slowly.

The growth jar is for long goals, like studying further or retirement: unit trusts, shares on the JSE, a retirement fund, maybe property one day. It can grow much faster, but its value goes up and down, so you must be patient and not need the money soon.

Fill the safe jar first, then the growth jar. And do not put everything in one place: spreading money around lowers risk.

Finally, watch for the trap jar. A WhatsApp message says: give us R1 000 and get R5 000 back next month, guaranteed! Bring your friends and earn more! That is almost certainly a scam. Real investments never guarantee huge returns. If it sounds too good to be true, walk away and ask a trusted adult.
