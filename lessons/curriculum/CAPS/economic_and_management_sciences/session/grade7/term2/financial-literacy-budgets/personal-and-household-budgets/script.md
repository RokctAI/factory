# Part 1 — Expert

This session defines a budget and applies it to individuals and households. A budget is a financial plan that estimates income and expenditure for a future period. The governing idea is that planning expected income and expenditure in advance allows a household to meet its needs, save and avoid debt, and that comparing actual figures with the budget reveals where plans must change. The definitions of budget, income, expenditure, surplus and deficit, the layout of a household budget, and the comparison of budgeted and actual amounts are examinable.

## Subtopic: Budget, Income and Expenditure

A budget is a written plan that estimates the income that will be received and the expenditure that will be made over a future period, usually a month or a year. A budget is made before the money is spent, which is what makes it a plan and not just a record.

Income in a household budget is all the money the household expects to receive in the period. Sources include salaries and wages, income from a small business or piece work, social grants such as the child support grant or the older person's grant, money sent by family members working elsewhere, rent received for a backyard room, and interest on savings.

Expenditure is all the money the household plans to spend. It includes payments for needs such as food, rent or bond repayments, electricity, water, transport and school costs; for other commitments such as insurance, funeral cover and debt repayments; for wants such as entertainment and takeaways; and the amount set aside as savings. Savings are included in the budget as a planned amount, so that they are not forgotten.

When planned income is greater than planned expenditure, the budget shows a surplus, which can be added to savings or used to pay off debt faster. When planned expenditure is greater than planned income, the budget shows a deficit or shortfall. A household with a deficit must either cut expenditure, find extra income or use savings; otherwise it will need to borrow, which leads to debt.

A balanced budget is one in which planned expenditure, including savings, equals planned income.

## Subtopic: Drawing Up a Household Budget

A household budget is drawn up in a few simple steps.

Step one: list and total all the expected income for the month. The Ndlovu household expects Mr Ndlovu's wage of R9 500, Mrs Ndlovu's income from selling vegetables of R2 000, and one child support grant of R560. Total income: R12 060.

Step two: list the needs and fixed commitments first, with realistic amounts based on past spending and current prices. Food and groceries R4 200; rent R2 500; electricity R900; water and refuse R300; transport R1 500; school costs R400; funeral policy R250; cellphone and data R350. Total for needs and commitments: R10 400.

Step three: decide on savings. The family plans to save R600 a month for emergencies and for school uniforms in January. Running total: R11 000.

Step four: allocate what remains to wants. R1 060 is left. The family allows R500 for entertainment and treats and R300 for clothing, and keeps R260 as a buffer for unexpected costs. Total expenditure: R12 060.

Step five: check. Total planned expenditure, including savings, equals total income, so the budget balances.

The layout is usually a table with the items in one column and the budgeted amounts in the next, with income at the top and expenditure below, and totals for each. Many families use a notebook; others use a spreadsheet or a budgeting app.

Realistic budgets use real figures. Looking at last month's till slips and statements helps families estimate amounts accurately, and remembering irregular costs, such as birthdays, school trips and annual fees, prevents nasty surprises.

## Subtopic: Comparing the Budget With Actual Spending

A budget is most useful when it is compared with what actually happened. At the end of the month, the household records its actual income and actual expenditure next to the budgeted amounts and calculates the difference for each item.

For example, the Ndlovus budgeted R900 for electricity but actually spent R1 150, because a cold spell meant they used a heater more. They overspent by R250. They budgeted R1 500 for transport but spent only R1 350, because Mr Ndlovu got lifts with a colleague for a week, so they underspent by R150. Mrs Ndlovu's vegetable sales were R2 300 instead of R2 000, R300 more than expected.

By studying the differences, the family learns where its plan was unrealistic and where its spending habits need to change. Some differences are once-off, such as a cold spell; others show a pattern, such as electricity always being higher than budgeted in winter, which means next winter's budget should allow more.

Keeping track during the month helps too. Writing down every purchase, keeping till slips and checking the bank balance regularly prevents the family from discovering a problem only at the end of the month.

Budgets can also be made for special events and goals, such as a birthday party, a wedding, the December holidays or January back-to-school costs. Planning these months ahead, and saving a little each month towards them, stops a big expense from causing debt.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through one learner's pocket money and one family's fridge door, in plain words with the same facts.

## Subtopic: Ayanda's R400 Plan

Ayanda gets R400 a month from her mother for school. Before, the money was gone by the middle of the month and she had to walk to school. So she makes a budget.

Income: R400.

Needs first: taxi fare to school R200, and a little extra for lunch on sports days R100. That is R300.

Savings next: she pays herself first and puts R50 into a jar for a new school bag in January.

Wants last: R50 left for airtime and the occasional treat.

Total spending including savings: R400. It balances.

At the end of the month Ayanda compares. The taxi cost R200 as planned. Lunch cost only R80, so she underspent by R20 and added it to her jar. But she spent R70 on airtime instead of R50, overspending by R20. Next month she will buy a cheaper data bundle.

That is budgeting: plan before you spend, needs first, save, then compare and adjust.

## Subtopic: The Budget on the Fridge

The Ndlovu family sticks their budget on the fridge door. Everyone can see it.

At the top: income for the month, R12 060. Below: rent, food, electricity, water, transport, school, the funeral policy, data, savings, treats. Every time something is paid, someone ticks it off and writes the real amount next to it.

In June the cold weather pushes electricity up by R250. The family sees it early on the fridge door and decides to skip one takeaway night to cover it. That is a shortfall handled by cutting a want first, not by borrowing.

In July Mrs Ndlovu sells more vegetables than expected and there is R300 extra. That is a surplus. Instead of spending it, they put it into savings for January's school uniforms.

The fridge budget turns money from something that just disappears into something the whole family controls.

Remember: a budget is a plan, income is what comes in, expenditure is what goes out, a surplus is extra, a shortfall is too little, and you compare and adjust every month.
