### subtopic_1 — Purpose of a Business Budget
**Assistant:** If the future is uncertain, how can a business budget be useful?

**Tutor:** Because a careful estimate is far better than no plan at all, and because the budget is updated as the business learns. No one knows exactly how many cars a car wash will wash next month, but the owner can make a reasonable estimate from past months, the season, school holidays, the weather and any special plans such as advertising. A budget built on that estimate shows whether the business is likely to cover its fixed costs and make a profit, and what would happen if sales were lower. Many owners prepare a cautious budget, using lower sales figures, to make sure they could still pay rent and wages in a bad month. When actual results come in, the owner compares them with the budget and adjusts both the business and the next budget. Over time the estimates become more accurate. Large businesses do exactly this, and their budgets are revised during the year. The value of a budget is not that it predicts the future perfectly, but that it forces careful thinking in advance and gives a yardstick against which to measure what actually happens.

### subtopic_2 — Estimating Business Income and Expenditure
**Assistant:** Why should a budget include the purchase of a new pressure washer if it is not an expense?

**Tutor:** Because the business still has to pay for it, and the budget must show whether there will be enough cash. When calculating profit, the purchase of equipment is not an expense; it is an asset that the business will use for years. But buying it uses cash, which then cannot be used to pay rent, wages or suppliers. If Thabo plans to buy a pressure washer for R4 000 next month and forgets to include it in his budget, he may find himself short of cash to pay his assistant at the end of the month, even though the budget showed a healthy profit. Many small businesses get into trouble this way: profitable on paper but short of cash. A complete business budget therefore shows planned purchases of assets and loan repayments as cash going out, alongside the expenses. In later grades you will learn to separate these into a projected income statement, which shows profit, and a cash budget, which shows cash coming in and going out. For now, remember that a good budget covers every rand that will move, whatever kind of transaction it is.

### subtopic_3 — Comparing Budgeted and Actual Results
**Assistant:** Should an owner be worried if costs are higher than budgeted?

**Tutor:** It depends on why, and that is the most important question. Some higher costs are good news in disguise. If Thabo washed more cars than planned, his variable costs for soap and water would be higher, but his income would be higher too, and his profit would probably rise. That unfavourable cost difference is simply the result of more business. Other higher costs are a real warning: if the price of soap rose sharply, or electricity tariffs increased, or water was being wasted by a leaking hose, then the cost per car has gone up and profit per car has fallen. That calls for action: shop around for cheaper suppliers, fix the leak, use resources more efficiently, or consider a small price increase. Some higher costs are once-off, such as an emergency repair, and may not need any long-term change. The skill is to look at costs in relation to the level of activity. Businesses often compare cost per unit, here cost per car washed, with the budgeted cost per unit. That gives a clearer picture than comparing the totals alone.
