# Part 1 — Expert

This session applies budgeting to a small business. A business budget estimates the income a business expects to earn and the expenditure it expects to incur in a future period, so that the owner can plan, control costs and judge performance. The governing idea is that a business budget is built from estimated sales and costs, shows the expected profit or loss, and becomes a tool for control when actual results are compared with it. The purpose of a business budget, estimating income from sales volume and price, fixed and variable expenditure, expected profit, and comparing budgeted with actual results are examinable.

## Subtopic: Purpose of a Business Budget

A business budget is a financial plan that estimates the income a business expects to earn and the expenditure it expects to pay over a future period, such as a month, a term or a year.

A business budget has several purposes. It helps the owner plan: how much stock to buy, how many workers are needed, whether there will be enough cash to pay rent and wages, and when to buy new equipment. It helps the owner control: by comparing actual spending with the budget, the owner can spot costs that are rising too fast and act early. It helps the owner make decisions: for example, whether a price is high enough to cover costs and leave a profit, or whether a new product is worth adding. It sets targets for the business and its workers, such as the number of sales to reach. And it is useful when the owner applies for a loan or brings in a partner, because it shows that the business has been carefully planned.

Every kind of business can budget, from a spaza shop to a large company, a school tuck shop or a stall at Entrepreneur's Day. Government departments and municipalities also have budgets, approved each year, which guide all their spending.

A business budget is different from a household budget in one main way: its income depends on sales, which are uncertain, and many of its costs change with the level of sales. So the budget must start with a careful estimate of how much the business will sell.

## Subtopic: Estimating Business Income and Expenditure

To draw up a business budget, start with income. For a business selling goods or services, budgeted income is the expected number of units sold multiplied by the selling price. Thabo's car wash expects to wash 300 cars next month at R60 each, so budgeted income is 300 times R60, which is R18 000. If he also sells air fresheners, he adds that expected income too, say 50 at R20, which is R1 000. Total budgeted income: R19 000.

Next, estimate expenditure in two groups. Fixed costs do not change with the number of cars washed: rent for the site R2 500, a monthly insurance premium R300 and a fixed wage for his assistant R4 500. Total fixed costs: R7 300.

Variable costs change with the number of cars: soap, wax and cloths cost about R8 per car, so 300 cars cost R2 400; water and electricity about R5 per car, so R1 500; air fresheners bought at R10 each, 50 of them, R500. Total variable costs: R4 400.

Total budgeted expenditure: R7 300 plus R4 400, which is R11 700.

Expected profit: R19 000 minus R11 700, which is R7 300.

The owner can also test other scenarios. If only 200 cars are washed, income from washing falls to R12 000 and variable costs for washing to R2 600, but fixed costs stay at R7 300. The budget then shows how much the profit would fall and whether the business could still cover its costs.

The budget should also include planned purchases of equipment, such as a new pressure washer, and any loan repayments, because they use cash even though they are not expenses.

## Subtopic: Comparing Budgeted and Actual Results

At the end of the month, the owner compares the budget with the actual results. Each item is listed with its budgeted amount, its actual amount and the difference.

For Thabo's car wash, the month was rainy for a week, so only 260 cars were washed instead of 300. Actual washing income was R15 600 against a budget of R18 000, an unfavourable difference of R2 400. Air fresheners sold better than expected: 70 at R20, R1 400 against R1 000, a favourable difference of R400. Variable washing costs were lower because fewer cars were washed, which is to be expected. Electricity rose because the municipality increased tariffs.

A difference is favourable when it improves profit compared with the budget: income higher than budgeted or costs lower. It is unfavourable when it reduces profit: income lower or costs higher.

The owner then asks why each difference happened and what to do. Lower sales because of rain might be an unavoidable seasonal pattern, so next year's budget for that month can be lower, or Thabo could offer a discount on sunny days after rain. Strong sales of air fresheners suggest stocking more and perhaps adding other products. Higher electricity tariffs mean he should check whether his price per wash still covers costs.

This cycle of plan, act, compare and adjust is how budgets help businesses improve. It also prepares you for Term 3, when you will draw up a budget for your own Entrepreneur's Day business.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through one Entrepreneur's Day stall planned on a single page, in plain words with the same facts.

## Subtopic: The Popcorn Stall Plan

Lindo and Musa are planning a popcorn stall for the school market day. Before they buy anything, they make a business budget.

Income: they think they can sell 120 bags of popcorn at R10 each. 120 times R10 is R1 200.

Fixed costs, which stay the same however many bags they sell: hiring the table R100, and making a sign R50. Total R150.

Variable costs, which depend on how many bags they make: kernels, oil, salt and paper bags come to about R4 per bag. For 120 bags that is R480.

Total expected costs: R150 plus R480 equals R630.

Expected profit: R1 200 minus R630 equals R570.

They also check a bad day: if they sell only 80 bags, income is R800, variable costs are R320, fixed costs still R150. Profit would be R330. Still positive, so the plan is safe.

## Subtopic: How Did the Stall Really Do?

After market day, Lindo and Musa compare their plan with what really happened.

They sold 150 bags instead of 120. Actual income: R1 500 instead of R1 200. That is R300 better than planned: favourable.

But oil was more expensive than they expected, so each bag cost R5 instead of R4. For 150 bags that is R750 in variable costs. Costs were higher than planned: unfavourable. Fixed costs stayed at R150.

Actual profit: R1 500 minus R750 minus R150 equals R600. A bit better than the R570 they budgeted.

What did they learn? People loved the popcorn, so next time they can make more. And they should compare oil prices at different shops, or buy a bigger bottle, to bring the cost per bag down.

That is the budget cycle: plan, sell, compare, improve. You will do exactly this for your own Entrepreneur's Day business next term.
