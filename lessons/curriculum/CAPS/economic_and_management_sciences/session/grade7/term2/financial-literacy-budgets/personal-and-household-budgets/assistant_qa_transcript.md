### subtopic_1 — Budget, Income and Expenditure
**Assistant:** Why make a budget if income is so small that it is all spent anyway?

**Tutor:** Because a budget is most useful exactly when money is tight. A household with a large income can afford mistakes; a household with a small income cannot. Without a budget, money tends to be spent on whatever comes first: a takeaway on payday, airtime, a small treat, and by the twentieth of the month there is nothing left for electricity or taxi fare, so the family borrows from a neighbour or a mashonisa and starts the next month in debt. With a budget, the household decides in advance that the essential needs come first: food, rent, electricity, transport and school costs, and only then sees what is left. It may reveal that the money really does not cover everything, which is valuable information too: the family can plan to buy maize meal in bulk, walk instead of taking a taxi for short trips, or look for extra income before the crisis arrives. A budget also makes it possible to save even a small amount, which over time creates a cushion. People with low incomes are often very skilled budgeters; writing the plan down simply makes those skills stronger.

### subtopic_2 — Drawing Up a Household Budget
**Assistant:** What should a family cut first if its budget shows a deficit?

**Tutor:** Usually wants first, then look for savings within needs, and only as a last resort cut savings or borrow. Start with the clearly optional items: takeaways, entertainment, new clothes that are not urgent, extra data bundles, subscriptions. These can be reduced or postponed without harming anyone's health or safety. Next, look at needs for cheaper ways to meet them: buying maize meal, rice and soap in bulk or on special, cooking at home, switching off appliances to save electricity, walking short distances, sharing lifts, or changing to a cheaper cellphone package. Check commitments too: are there insurance policies or accounts the family does not need, or debts that could be consolidated at a lower interest rate? At the same time, look at the income side: could someone earn extra from a small business, selling vegetables, or part-time work? Is the family receiving every grant it qualifies for? Cutting savings should come late, because savings protect the family in emergencies. Borrowing to cover a regular monthly deficit is dangerous, because the repayments make next month's deficit even larger. A budget deficit is a signal to act early, not a reason to panic.

### subtopic_3 — Comparing the Budget With Actual Spending
**Assistant:** Is it a failure if the actual figures do not match the budget?

**Tutor:** No. Almost no household's actual figures match its budget exactly, because a budget is an estimate of the future and the future is uncertain. Prices change, the weather changes, children need things unexpectedly and income can be higher or lower than expected. The point of comparing actual with budgeted figures is not to judge, but to learn. Small differences are normal. Large differences are useful signals. If the family always overspends on food, perhaps the budget amount is unrealistic given current prices and needs to be increased, with something else reduced. If they overspend on takeaways, perhaps it is a habit worth changing. If an underspent amount appears, such as lower transport costs, the family can decide whether to move the extra into savings. Businesses and governments do exactly the same: they compare actual results with budgets and explain the differences, which accountants call variances. The real failure would be never to look at the figures at all. Over time, comparing and adjusting makes each month's budget more accurate, and the family's money more under its control.
