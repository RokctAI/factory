### subtopic_1 — Manufacturing and Adding Value
**Assistant:** Why does South Africa want more manufacturing rather than just exporting raw materials?

**Tutor:** Because most of the value, and many of the jobs, are created when raw materials are turned into products. South Africa is rich in raw materials such as iron ore, platinum, chrome, coal, wool, timber and fruit. If we export iron ore and then import steel products, other countries do the processing, employ the workers and earn most of the value. If the ore is turned into steel here, and the steel into car parts, machines and building materials, far more jobs are created here, from semi-skilled factory work to engineering and design, and more income stays in the country. Manufacturing also supports many other businesses: suppliers, transport, packaging and services. That is why government programmes promote local manufacturing and beneficiation, which means adding value to minerals before export, and why campaigns encourage consumers to buy South African-made products. Manufacturing faces challenges, including competition from countries with lower costs, electricity supply problems and the need for skills. In an exam, explain that manufacturing adds value, creates jobs and earns more from exports than raw materials alone.

### subtopic_2 — Costs of Production and Cost per Unit
**Assistant:** Why should a small entrepreneur include the value of her own time in her costs?

**Tutor:** Because otherwise she cannot tell whether the business is really rewarding her. Suppose Naledi spends ten hours a week making bracelets and does not count her time. If her materials cost R160 and she sells the bracelets for R200, she might think she made R40 profit. But for ten hours of work, R40 is only R4 an hour, far less than the national minimum wage, and less than she might earn doing something else. Valuing her labour, say at R25 an hour, adds R250 to her costs and reveals that she is actually making a loss when her time is counted, so her price is far too low. Including labour also helps her plan to grow: if she later hires a helper, she will have to pay wages, and her price must already allow for that. It also helps her compare options: perhaps a different product would give a better return on her time. Many small manufacturers underprice their work because they forget their own labour, and then wonder why the business never seems to get ahead. A realistic cost per unit includes materials, labour and overheads.

### subtopic_3 — Pricing and Profit in Manufacturing
**Assistant:** How can a small manufacturer charge more than mass-produced competitors?

**Tutor:** By offering something the mass-produced product does not. Large factories can usually make standard products more cheaply because of economies of scale, so a small manufacturer rarely wins on price alone. Instead it can compete on uniqueness, quality and personal service. Handmade products, such as beadwork, pottery or clothing, can carry cultural meaning, local designs or the maker's story, which many customers value, including tourists. Customisation is powerful: a bracelet with a person's name, school colours or a design chosen by the customer is something a factory cannot easily provide. Quality matters: products that last longer or use better materials justify a higher price. Freshness is a strong advantage for food: a home bakery's bread or cakes, baked that morning, can beat factory products. Small makers can also respond quickly to trends and to customer feedback. They must still keep costs under control and communicate their advantages clearly, with good packaging, photos and descriptions. In an exam, mention uniqueness, quality, customisation, freshness and local or cultural value as reasons customers may pay more.
