# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines manufacturing as a way of making profit. A manufacturer converts raw materials into finished products, adding value through labour, equipment and skill, and sells them at a price above the total cost of production. The governing idea is that the manufacturer must calculate the full cost of producing each unit, including materials, labour and overheads, and set a selling price that covers these and leaves a profit. The meaning of manufacturing, raw materials and finished goods, the costs of production, cost per unit, pricing and the differences between manufacturing and trading are examinable.

## Subtopic: Manufacturing and Adding Value

Manufacturing is the process of turning raw materials or parts into finished products that people want to buy. A business that does this is a manufacturing business, or a producer in the secondary sector.

Raw materials are the basic materials used to make a product: flour, sugar and eggs for a baker, fabric and thread for a dressmaker, wood for a furniture maker, beads and wire for a jewellery maker. Some manufacturers use components, parts made by other manufacturers, such as a car factory using tyres, glass and engines.

The finished product is worth more than the raw materials used to make it. The difference is the value added by the manufacturer's labour, skill, equipment and creativity. A piece of fabric that costs R80 may become a dress that sells for R400.

Manufacturing happens at every scale. A home industry, such as a woman baking and selling cakes, a man welding burglar bars in his yard or a learner making beaded jewellery, is small-scale manufacturing. Medium businesses include bakeries, clothing workshops and furniture makers. Large-scale manufacturers include car plants in the Eastern Cape and Gauteng, food factories and steel mills, which use expensive machines and employ thousands.

Manufacturing is important to the economy because it adds value to South Africa's raw materials, creates many jobs, including for semi-skilled workers, and produces goods for export. Government encourages local manufacturing, for example through campaigns to buy products made in South Africa, so that more of the value is added and more jobs are created in the country.

## Subtopic: Costs of Production and Cost per Unit

To set a price that makes a profit, a manufacturer must know how much it costs to make each unit of the product. The costs of production fall into three groups.

Direct materials are the raw materials and components that go into each product. For a beaded bracelet, these are the beads, wire and clasp.

Direct labour is the cost of the work done to make the product, such as the wages of the people who assemble it. A small entrepreneur who makes products herself should also value her own time, or she will not know whether the business is really worth her effort.

Overheads, also called indirect costs, are the other costs of running the production: rent for the workspace, electricity, the wear and tear on equipment, packaging and transport. Some overheads are fixed, such as rent; others vary with production.

To find the cost per unit, add up all the costs for a batch and divide by the number of units made. Suppose Naledi makes 20 bracelets in a week. Beads, wire and clasps cost R160. She values her labour at R100 for the week. Packaging and transport cost R40. Total cost: R300. Cost per unit: R300 divided by 20 is R15 per bracelet.

Making more units often lowers the cost per unit, because fixed costs are spread over more products and materials can be bought in bulk at a discount. This is called an economy of scale. If Naledi makes 40 bracelets and her fixed costs stay the same, each bracelet costs less.

## Subtopic: Pricing and Profit in Manufacturing

Once the cost per unit is known, the manufacturer sets a selling price above it. A common method is to add a mark-up to the cost. If Naledi's bracelets cost R15 each and she adds 100 percent, the selling price is R30. Her profit per unit is R30 minus R15, which is R15, and if she sells all 20, her profit is 20 times R15, which is R300.

But the price must also make sense to customers. She checks what similar bracelets sell for at the market and online, and what her customers, mostly learners and young adults, can afford. If similar bracelets sell for R25, she may need to lower her price, lower her costs, or make her bracelets special enough, for example with custom names or school colours, that customers will pay more.

She must also sell what she makes. Unsold stock ties up money in materials, so a manufacturer plans production to match expected demand.

Manufacturing and trading are both ways to make profit, but they differ. A trader buys finished goods and resells them unchanged; a manufacturer makes the goods. Trading usually needs less equipment and skill to start, and profit can come quickly, but the mark-up is often small and competition high. Manufacturing needs skills, equipment, more planning and more time, but can add more value and create a unique product that customers cannot buy elsewhere. Some businesses do both: a bakery makes bread and cakes and also sells cooldrinks bought from a wholesaler.

For Entrepreneur's Day, you will choose whether to trade, by buying and reselling, or to manufacture, by making your own product, and you will need to calculate costs and prices either way.

The questions for this section are with you now: setting a selling price and calculating profit for a manufacturer, and the differences between manufacturing and trading.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through one learner and one batch of bracelets, in plain words with the same facts.

## Subtopic: Naledi's Bracelet Batch

Naledi loves beading. She decides to make bracelets to sell.

First she adds up her costs for one batch of 20 bracelets. Beads, wire and clasps: R160. Her own time, which she values at R100 for the week: R100. Small paper bags and a taxi to the bead shop: R40. Total: R300.

Cost per bracelet: R300 divided by 20 equals R15.

She checks the market. Similar bracelets sell for R25 to R35. She chooses R30, because hers can have the buyer's name spelled in letter beads, which most others do not offer.

Profit per bracelet: R30 minus R15 equals R15. If she sells all 20: R300 profit.

The beads alone were worth R160. The finished bracelets are worth R600. Naledi added R440 of value with her skill, time and design. That is manufacturing.

## Subtopic: Make It or Buy It?

For Entrepreneur's Day, Naledi's friend Tumi must choose: make it or buy it?

Option one, trading: buy packets of sweets from a wholesaler and sell them one by one. Easy to start, no special skills, quick. But everybody else might be selling sweets too, and the mark-up is small.

Option two, manufacturing: bake muffins at home and sell them. Tumi needs ingredients, an oven, a recipe and time, and he must work out the cost per muffin carefully. But freshly baked muffins are special, few others will sell them, and he can add more value.

Tumi works out both. Sweets: buy at R1, sell at R2, R1 profit each. Muffins: cost R4 each to make, sell at R10, R6 profit each, but he can make only 40.

He chooses muffins, but also buys a few cooldrinks to sell alongside. Like many real businesses, he does a bit of both.

Remember: manufacturers make goods and add value; traders buy and resell; both must know their costs and set prices that customers will pay.
