# Part 1 — Expert

This session opens the Term 3 study of entrepreneurship by defining an entrepreneur and examining the characteristics and skills that help entrepreneurs succeed. The governing idea is that an entrepreneur identifies an opportunity, combines the factors of production to meet a need or want, and accepts the risk of loss in the hope of profit, and that the characteristics and skills required can be developed through practice. The definition, the role of the entrepreneur in the economy, the characteristics and the skills of entrepreneurs are examinable and prepare learners for Entrepreneur's Day.

## Subtopic: What Is an Entrepreneur?

An entrepreneur is a person who identifies a need or an opportunity in the market, comes up with an idea to meet it, and takes the risk of starting and running a business in the hope of making a profit. The word comes from French and means someone who undertakes, or takes on, a task.

Entrepreneurship is one of the four factors of production, together with natural resources, labour and capital. The entrepreneur is the one who brings the other three together. A person who opens a bakery finds premises, which is land; hires bakers, which is labour; buys ovens and mixers, which is capital; and organises them all to produce bread that customers want. The reward for entrepreneurship is profit; the risk is that the business may make a loss and the entrepreneur may lose the money invested.

Entrepreneurs exist at every scale. A learner who buys sweets in bulk and sells them at break, a woman who runs a spaza shop from her home, a young man who starts a cellphone-repair stall at a taxi rank and a team that builds a company employing thousands are all entrepreneurs.

Entrepreneurs are important to the economy. They create jobs, which South Africa badly needs; they produce goods and services that satisfy needs and wants; they bring new ideas, products and technologies; they increase competition, which can lower prices and improve quality; and they pay taxes that fund government services. Many famous South African businesses began as small ventures started by one or two determined people.

Some entrepreneurs start businesses because they see an exciting opportunity; others start because they cannot find a job and need an income. Both kinds contribute to the economy.

The questions for this section are with you now: the definition of an entrepreneur, the entrepreneur as a factor of production, and the importance of entrepreneurs in the economy.

## Subtopic: Characteristics of Successful Entrepreneurs

No two entrepreneurs are the same, but research and experience show that successful ones tend to share certain characteristics, which are personal qualities.

They are creative and innovative: they see new ways of doing things and new products or services that people might want. They are alert to opportunities and notice problems that could become businesses.

They are willing to take calculated risks. A calculated risk is one that has been thought through: the entrepreneur weighs up what could go wrong and how likely it is, and plans to reduce the risk, rather than gambling blindly.

They are determined, hard-working and resilient. Starting a business takes long hours, and setbacks are common. Resilient people do not give up after a failure; they learn from it and try again. Many famous entrepreneurs failed several times before they succeeded.

They are self-motivated and show initiative: they do not wait to be told what to do. They are self-confident enough to believe in their ideas and persuade others, while still listening to advice.

They are responsible and honest. Customers, suppliers, workers and lenders must be able to trust them. An entrepreneur who cheats customers or does not pay suppliers soon loses business.

They are goal-oriented, setting clear targets and working towards them, and flexible, willing to change plans when circumstances change. They are often good at working with people and leading a team.

These characteristics are not fixed at birth. Many can be developed through practice, for example by taking on responsibility at school, joining clubs, running a small project or helping in a family business.

The questions for this section are with you now: the characteristics of successful entrepreneurs, and the meaning of a calculated risk.

## Subtopic: Skills of an Entrepreneur

Characteristics are personal qualities; skills are abilities that are learned and improved through practice. Entrepreneurs need a range of skills, and those they lack they can learn or find in partners and employees.

Financial skills: drawing up budgets, keeping records of income and expenses, working out costs and setting prices that cover costs and make a profit, and managing cash so that bills can be paid. Many small businesses fail because of poor financial management, even when customers like their products.

Communication skills: explaining the product clearly to customers, listening to their needs and complaints, writing simple adverts and messages, and speaking confidently to suppliers, lenders and workers.

Negotiation skills: agreeing on prices with suppliers, terms with landlords and deals with customers in a way that benefits both sides.

Planning and organising skills: setting goals, deciding on the steps to reach them, organising stock, equipment and people, and managing time so that the most important tasks are done first.

Marketing and selling skills: understanding who the customers are, what they want and how to reach them, and persuading them to buy.

Problem-solving and decision-making skills: finding solutions when things go wrong, such as a supplier running out of stock, and making good decisions with the information available.

People and leadership skills: hiring, motivating and treating workers fairly, and building good relationships with customers and the community.

Technical skills: the specific skills of the trade, such as baking, hairdressing, coding or repairing phones.

Increasingly, digital skills matter too: using a phone or computer for records, online payments and social media marketing.

The questions for this section are with you now: the difference between characteristics and skills, and the main skills entrepreneurs need.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through one learner who turns a problem at school into a small business, in plain words with the same facts.

## Subtopic: Kagiso Spots a Gap

Kagiso notices that learners at his school keep running out of pens, pencils and exercise books, and the nearest shop is far away. Every week someone begs to borrow a pen.

That is a need, and Kagiso sees an opportunity. His idea: buy stationery in bulk from a wholesaler and sell it at break time for a little more than he paid, but still less than the far-away shop charges.

He takes a risk: he uses R300 of his own savings to buy the first stock. If nobody buys, he loses his money. He asks the principal for permission and gets it.

He brings the factors of production together: a small table at the school gate, which is his place; his own time and a friend's help, which is labour; a cash box and a tub to carry stock, which is capital; and his idea and organisation, which is entrepreneurship.

After a month he has sold most of his stock, paid back his R300, and made a profit of R180. His classmates are happy because they no longer go without pens.

That is what an entrepreneur does: spot a need, have an idea, take a risk, organise resources and make a profit by meeting the need.

Try the questions for this part: find the need, the idea, the risk and the reward.

## Subtopic: Kagiso's Toolkit

What helped Kagiso succeed? Two kinds of tools.

His characteristics, who he is: he was creative enough to see an idea, brave enough to risk his savings after thinking it through, determined enough to keep going when sales were slow in the first week, and honest, so classmates trusted him to give the right change.

His skills, what he learned to do: he worked out the cost of each pen and set a fair price; he kept a notebook of every sale and purchase; he spoke politely to customers and made a small sign; he negotiated a bulk discount with the wholesaler; and he planned which items to buy based on what sold best.

In the second week, his supplier ran out of blue pens. Kagiso solved the problem by finding another supplier: problem-solving.

Remember: characteristics are who you are, and you can grow them; skills are what you can do, and you can learn them. Both make an entrepreneur.
