# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines trading as a way of making profit. A trader buys goods from a producer or wholesaler and sells them to customers without changing them, earning a profit from the difference between the cost price and the selling price. The governing idea is that the trader's mark-up must cover the business's other expenses and leave a profit, while still offering customers a price and convenience they are willing to pay for. The meaning of trading, cost price, selling price, mark-up, gross profit and net profit, and simple calculations are examinable.

## Subtopic: Trading: Buying and Selling

Trading means buying goods and selling them again, usually without changing them, in order to make a profit. A business that trades is called a trading business. It does not manufacture anything; it adds value by making goods available where and when customers want them, in quantities they can afford.

Goods usually pass through a distribution chain. A manufacturer, such as a factory making soap or a mill producing maize meal, sells large quantities to a wholesaler. A wholesaler buys in bulk, stores the goods in a warehouse and sells them in large quantities to retailers. A retailer sells goods in small quantities to the final consumer. Supermarkets, spaza shops, clothing stores, pharmacies and street vendors are all retailers. Some retailers buy directly from manufacturers, and some wholesalers, such as cash-and-carry stores, also sell to the public.

Traders provide useful services. They break bulk: a wholesaler buys pallets of soap, and a spaza shop sells one bar at a time. They provide convenience: a spaza shop in the street saves people a long taxi trip to town. They offer choice, stock goods so that customers can buy when they need them, and sometimes provide credit to regular customers.

Trading is one of the most common kinds of business in South Africa, from huge retail chains to the hundreds of thousands of spaza shops and street traders in townships and villages. Informal trading, such as selling fruit, sweets and airtime on the street, is often the first business people start, because it needs little capital and no special equipment.

## Subtopic: Cost Price, Selling Price and Mark-up

To make a profit from trading, a trader needs to know three amounts.

The cost price is the amount the trader paid for the goods, including transport to bring them to the shop if that is counted per item. If a spaza shop owner buys a loaf of bread from a bakery for R14, the cost price is R14.

The selling price is the amount the customer pays the trader. If she sells the loaf for R17, the selling price is R17.

The mark-up is the amount added to the cost price to arrive at the selling price. Mark-up equals selling price minus cost price: R17 minus R14 equals R3. On one loaf, R3 is the gross profit.

Mark-up is often expressed as a percentage of the cost price: mark-up divided by cost price, multiplied by one hundred. R3 divided by R14, times one hundred, is about 21 percent. Traders often decide their selling price by choosing a mark-up percentage. If a trader buys a packet of chips for R8 and uses a mark-up of 50 percent, the mark-up is R4 and the selling price is R12.

For a whole batch, gross profit equals the total sales minus the cost of the goods that were sold. If the shop buys 40 loaves at R14, costing R560, and sells all 40 at R17, sales are R680 and gross profit is R120.

If some goods are not sold, for example if 5 loaves go stale and are thrown away, the cost of those 5 loaves, R70, is lost. Sales are then only 35 loaves, which is R595, while the cost of all 40 loaves was still R560, so gross profit falls to just R35. Waste can wipe out profit, so traders must buy carefully.

## Subtopic: What Affects a Trader's Prices and Profit

A trader cannot simply choose any selling price. Several factors limit how much mark-up is possible and how much profit the business will make.

Competition: if three spaza shops in the same street sell bread, a shop that charges much more than the others will lose customers. Large supermarket chains can buy in huge quantities at lower prices, so small traders must compete on convenience, friendly service, opening hours and credit rather than on price alone.

Customers: the prices must be affordable for the people the shop serves. In a low-income area, a high mark-up on basic foods means fewer sales.

Costs: the trader's expenses must be covered. Transport to the wholesaler, rent, electricity for fridges, security and wages all add to costs. Buying in bulk or with other traders, for example through a buying cooperative, can lower the cost price.

Type of goods: perishable goods such as bread, milk and vegetables carry the risk of waste, so traders may add a higher mark-up to cover losses, or buy smaller quantities more often. Items that sell quickly, such as airtime, may have a small mark-up but a large volume of sales.

Location: a shop in a busy place, such as near a taxi rank or school, sells more; a shop in a remote area with no competition may charge more but also faces higher transport costs.

Good traders keep records of what sells, how fast and at what profit, and adjust their stock and prices. Many spaza shop owners have built successful businesses by knowing their customers well, keeping popular items in stock and offering a friendly, trusted service.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through one spaza shop and one crate of cooldrinks, in plain words with the same facts.

## Subtopic: Mama Thembi's Cooldrinks

Mama Thembi runs a spaza shop. On Monday she goes to the cash-and-carry and buys a tray of 24 cans of cooldrink for R216. That is R9 per can: her cost price.

She puts the cans in her fridge and sells each one for R13: her selling price.

Her mark-up on each can is R13 minus R9, which is R4.

By Friday she has sold all 24 cans. Her sales are 24 times R13, which is R312. Her gross profit on the tray is R312 minus R216, which is R96.

She did not make the cooldrink. She did not change it. She brought it from the cash-and-carry to her street, kept it cold and sold it one can at a time when people wanted it. That convenience is what customers pay the extra R4 for.

But her R96 is not all profit. She paid R40 for the taxi to the cash-and-carry, and her fridge uses electricity. Those costs come out of the gross profit before she knows what she really earned.

## Subtopic: The Price Is Not Just Up to Her

Could Mama Thembi just charge R20 a can and get rich? No. Look at what limits her price.

The shop next door sells the same cooldrink for R12. If she charges R20, her customers will walk next door. That is competition.

Her neighbours do not earn much. If the price is too high, they buy water instead. That is what customers can afford.

She must cover her costs: the taxi to the cash-and-carry, electricity for the fridge and the cans that sometimes get dropped and break.

So she looks for the price that customers will pay and that still covers her costs and leaves a profit. She decides to charge R12, the same as next door, but to buy two trays at a time with a neighbour to share the taxi cost. And she stays open later than the other shop, which brings her extra customers.

Remember: trading means buying and selling without changing the goods; mark-up is selling price minus cost price; competition, customers and costs decide what price works.
