# Part 1 — Expert

This session applies costing and budgeting to an Entrepreneur's Day business. The governing idea is that the entrepreneur must know the fixed costs, which do not change with the number of units produced, and the variable costs, which change with each unit, in order to calculate the cost price per unit, set a profitable selling price and estimate the profit. The meaning of fixed cost, variable cost, cost price and selling price, simple cost calculations, the break-even idea and a complete budget for Entrepreneur's Day are examinable and form part of the term project.

## Subtopic: Fixed Costs and Variable Costs

For Entrepreneur's Day, every cost the team will pay falls into one of two groups.

Fixed costs stay the same no matter how many units are made or sold. Examples for a school stall are the fee for hiring a table or stall space, the cost of making a banner and posters, hiring or buying equipment such as a cooler box or a gas burner, and a float of change if counted as a cost of borrowing. Whether the team sells 20 muffins or 200, these costs do not change. In a real business, rent, insurance and fixed salaries are fixed costs.

Variable costs change with the number of units produced. Each additional unit adds to the cost. For muffins, the variable costs are the ingredients, such as flour, sugar, eggs, butter, milk and chocolate chips, and the paper cups and serviettes. Make twice as many muffins, and the variable costs roughly double. In a real business, raw materials, packaging and sales commission are variable costs.

Some costs are a little of both, such as electricity for baking, which increases with each batch; for a simple school budget, it is usually treated as variable or estimated per batch.

Separating fixed and variable costs matters because they behave differently. Fixed costs must be paid even if very little is sold, so they are the main risk. Variable costs are only paid for what is made, so making too much creates waste. Knowing both lets the team calculate the cost of each unit and decide how many to make.

## Subtopic: Cost Price per Unit and Selling Price

The cost price per unit is the total cost of producing one unit. For a school stall, it is calculated by adding all the costs and dividing by the number of units planned.

Here is an example. The team plans to bake 80 chocolate-chip muffins. Variable costs: ingredients R240 and paper cups and serviettes R40, total R280, which is R3,50 per muffin. Fixed costs: table hire R50, posters R30 and the gas to bake, estimated at R40, total R120. Total cost: R280 plus R120, which is R400. Cost price per muffin: R400 divided by 80, which is R5.

Another way to see it: the variable cost per muffin is R3,50, and the fixed costs spread over 80 muffins add R1,50 each, so the full cost price is R5.

The selling price is the price customers pay. It must be higher than the cost price for the stall to make a profit. The team can add a mark-up: with a mark-up of 100 percent on R5, the selling price would be R10. Before deciding, they check what customers will pay and what competitors charge. If cupcakes at another stall are R8, muffins at R10 may still sell if they are bigger and fresher, or the team might choose R9.

Profit per unit equals selling price minus cost price: R10 minus R5 is R5. If all 80 sell, the profit is 80 times R5, which is R400. Another way: sales of 80 times R10, which is R800, minus total cost of R400, gives R400.

Notice that if the team bakes more muffins, the fixed costs are spread over more units, so the cost price per unit falls. If it bakes fewer, each muffin carries more of the fixed costs.

## Subtopic: Break-Even and the Full Budget

The break-even point is the number of units that must be sold for total income to equal total costs. Below it, the stall makes a loss; above it, a profit.

Each muffin sold for R10 has a variable cost of R3,50, so it contributes R6,50 towards paying the fixed costs. The fixed costs are R120. Units to break even equal fixed costs divided by the selling price minus the variable cost per unit: R120 divided by R6,50 is about 18,5, so the team must sell 19 muffins to break even. Every muffin sold after that adds R6,50 of profit. If they sell all 80, the profit is 80 times R6,50, which is R520, minus R120 of fixed costs, which gives R400, the same answer as before.

Note one difference from the earlier calculation: once the muffins are baked, the ingredients have been bought, so unsold muffins still cost money. The break-even calculation assumes the team only makes what it sells, which is why planning the right quantity matters.

A complete Entrepreneur's Day budget sets all this out clearly: the product and planned quantity; the expected selling price and total expected income; the fixed costs listed and totalled; the variable costs listed and totalled; the total costs; the cost price per unit; the expected profit; and the break-even number of units. Some teams also show where the starting money comes from, such as contributions from team members, which is their capital, and plan to repay it from sales.

After the day, the team records the actual income and costs, compares them with the budget, notes favourable and unfavourable differences and explains why, just as you did in Term 2.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through one team's shopping list and one page of sums, in plain words with the same facts.

## Subtopic: The Muffin Shopping List

The muffin team writes its shopping list and splits it into two columns.

Fixed costs, which stay the same whether they sell 10 muffins or 80: table hire R50, posters R30, gas for baking R40. Total fixed: R120.

Variable costs, which grow with every muffin: ingredients for 80 muffins R240, paper cups and serviettes R40. Total variable: R280. That is R3,50 per muffin.

Total cost: R120 plus R280 equals R400.

Cost price per muffin: R400 divided by 80 equals R5.

Now the selling price. Cupcakes at another stall cost R8, but theirs are smaller. The team chooses R10 for big, fresh chocolate-chip muffins.

Profit per muffin: R10 minus R5 equals R5. If all 80 sell: R800 income minus R400 costs equals R400 profit.

## Subtopic: How Many Before We're Safe?

The team worries: what if it rains and we don't sell all 80? How many do we need to sell just to not lose money?

Each muffin sells for R10 and its ingredients and cup cost R3,50. So each muffin sold gives R6,50 to help pay the fixed costs.

Fixed costs are R120. R120 divided by R6,50 is about 18,5. So they need to sell 19 muffins to break even.

Nineteen out of eighty: that feels safe. After the 19th muffin, every sale is profit.

On the day, they sell 72 muffins. At the end of second break they drop the price of the last 8 to R6, still more than R3,50 each, and sell them all.

They compare with the budget: income R768 instead of R800, a small unfavourable difference because of the discount, but they sold out and made a profit of R368. Next time they might bake 90.

Remember: fixed costs stay the same, variable costs grow with each unit, cost price is total cost divided by units, selling price must be higher, and the break-even point tells you how many sales make you safe.
