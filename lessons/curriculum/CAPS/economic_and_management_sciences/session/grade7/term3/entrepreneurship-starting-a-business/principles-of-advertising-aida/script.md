# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines the principles of advertising through the AIDA model. The governing idea is that an effective advertisement leads a potential customer through four stages: it attracts Attention, holds Interest, creates Desire for the product and prompts Action, and that each part of an advert can be designed to achieve one of these stages. The meaning of each stage, the techniques used at each stage, analysing adverts using AIDA and designing an AIDA advert for Entrepreneur's Day are examinable.

## Subtopic: Attention and Interest

AIDA is a model that describes the steps a person goes through before buying, and it guides how adverts are designed. The letters stand for Attention, Interest, Desire and Action.

Attention is the first step. People see and hear hundreds of messages every day and ignore most of them, so an advert must first make them notice it. Techniques include a bold, short headline; bright colours and strong contrast; an eye-catching picture or video; a surprising question or statement; humour; a famous or relatable person; music or a jingle on radio and television; and placing the advert where people will see it. On social media, the first one or two seconds of a video must grab attention before people scroll past.

Interest is the second step. Once someone notices the advert, it must hold their attention long enough for them to learn more. The advert does this by telling them something relevant to them: what the product is, what problem it solves and why it is different. Interesting facts, a short story, clear information and a focus on the target market's needs all help. For example, a poster for vetkoek on a cold day might say: Cold outside? Warm up with fresh, hot vetkoek made from Gogo's secret recipe.

An important principle is to focus on benefits, not just features. A feature is a fact about the product, such as this bag holds twenty litres. A benefit is what the feature does for the customer, such as carry all your school books and sports kit in one bag. Customers are more interested in benefits.

## Subtopic: Desire and Action

Desire is the third step. Interest is not enough; the advert must turn I see what it is into I want it. Techniques include showing the product being enjoyed by people like the target market; describing how good it will feel to own or use it; showing quality, for example with close-up pictures of fresh food; using testimonials, which are statements from satisfied customers, such as the best vetkoek I have ever had; showing that others are buying it, for example over 500 sold!; and creating a sense of value or urgency with special offers, such as two for R15 today only, or limited stock.

Action is the final step. The advert must tell the customer exactly what to do next and make it easy. This is called a call to action. Examples include: buy now; visit our stall at the school gate at first break; order on WhatsApp at this number; call today; scan the code; while stocks last. The advert should give the information needed to act: the price, where to buy, when, and how to pay. An advert that creates desire but leaves people unsure where or when to buy loses sales.

A complete AIDA advert therefore has a part for each stage. For example: Attention: a big picture of steaming vetkoek and the headline COLD? Interest: Fresh, hot vetkoek with mince, made from Gogo's secret recipe. Desire: Last year's fair sold out in 20 minutes! Two for R15. Action: Find us at the gate, first and second break, Friday 14 September. Bring cash.

The model is simple, but checking an advert against each of the four letters is a powerful way to improve it.

## Subtopic: Analysing and Designing AIDA Adverts

AIDA can be used in two ways: to analyse existing adverts and to design new ones.

To analyse an advert, look for each stage. What grabs attention: a picture, a headline, a sound? What holds interest: what information does it give, and is it relevant to the target market? What creates desire: benefits, testimonials, offers? What is the call to action, and does the advert give enough information to act? Then judge whether any stage is missing or weak. Many weak adverts grab attention with a clever picture but never say what the product is or where to buy it; others are full of information but too dull for anyone to notice.

To design an advert, start with the target market and the medium. A poster on a busy wall needs a very strong attention-grabber and few words. A radio advert needs a catchy sound and must repeat the key information. A WhatsApp image needs to be clear on a small phone screen.

Then plan one element for each stage. Attention: headline and image. Interest: a sentence or two on what it is and why it is good, focused on benefits. Desire: a reason to want it now, such as a testimonial, special offer or mouth-watering description. Action: clear instructions including price, place and time.

Keep the design simple and uncluttered, use large, readable text and colours that stand out, check spelling and make sure all claims are honest.

Finally, test the advert on a few people from the target market before using it. Ask them what they noticed first, what they understood the product to be, whether they want it, and what they would do next. If their answers do not match your AIDA plan, improve the advert.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

## Subtopic: Building the Vetkoek Poster

Sizwe has a blank poster and four letters to fill: A, I, D, A.

Attention: he draws a big, steaming vetkoek in bright colours and writes one huge word at the top: COLD? Learners walking past in July will stop and look.

Interest: underneath, in a short line: Warm up with fresh, hot vetkoek filled with mince, made from Gogo's secret recipe. Now they know what it is and why it is good for them on a cold day.

Desire: in a bright star he writes: Sold out in 20 minutes last year! And: 2 for R15, Friday only. Now they want it, and they want it soon.

Action: at the bottom, in clear letters: Find us at the school gate, first and second break, Friday 14 September. Bring cash!

Sizwe checks each letter. Would someone notice it? Understand it? Want it? Know what to do? Yes, yes, yes, yes.

## Subtopic: Fixing a Weak Advert

Now let us fix two weak posters from other teams.

Team one made a beautiful poster with a huge picture of a cupcake and the word YUM! Lots of people looked at it. But it does not say the price, where the stall is or when. People wanted cupcakes but did not know where to go. It has attention and desire but no action. Fix: add Cupcakes R8, Room 12, second break, Friday.

Team two wrote a poster full of small writing: We are selling homemade juice made with oranges and water and sugar at R6 a cup at the netball court on Friday at break. All the information is there, but nobody stopped to read it. It has action but no attention. Fix: add a big picture of a cold orange drink and a headline: THIRSTY?

So use AIDA as a checklist. Attention: will they notice? Interest: will they understand why it is good for them? Desire: will they want it? Action: will they know exactly what to do?
