# Part 1 — Expert

This session introduces the SWOT analysis as a tool for evaluating a business idea or an existing business. The governing idea is that strengths and weaknesses are internal factors the business can control, while opportunities and threats are external factors in the environment that it cannot control but must respond to, and that a good plan builds on strengths, fixes weaknesses, uses opportunities and prepares for threats. The meaning of each element, the difference between internal and external factors, completing a SWOT grid and using it to make decisions are examinable and form part of the Entrepreneur's Day project.

## Subtopic: Strengths and Weaknesses: Looking Inside

SWOT stands for Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities and Threats. The first two look inside the business and are called internal factors, because the owner can control and change them.

Strengths are the things the business does well or the advantages it has. Examples include skilled and reliable team members, a good product or recipe, low costs, a convenient location, a good reputation, loyal customers, useful equipment, enough capital, good financial records and strong relationships with suppliers. For a Grade 7 vetkoek stall, strengths might be that one team member's grandmother has an excellent recipe, the team members are hard-working and the cost of ingredients is low.

Weaknesses are the things the business lacks or does badly, which put it at a disadvantage. Examples include lack of money, lack of experience or skills, poor location, unreliable equipment, high costs, weak marketing, poor record-keeping and disorganised teamwork. For the vetkoek stall, weaknesses might be that nobody has run a stall before, the team has only one frying pot, and vetkoek must be eaten hot, so they cannot make large batches in advance.

Identifying weaknesses honestly is just as important as identifying strengths. A weakness that is recognised can often be fixed or reduced: the team can practise beforehand, borrow a second pot and plan a schedule for frying in small batches.

When listing strengths and weaknesses, be specific. Instead of writing good product, write vetkoek made fresh from a family recipe, which customers at the school fair last year praised. Specific points lead to specific actions.

## Subtopic: Opportunities and Threats: Looking Outside

Opportunities and threats look outside the business, at its environment. They are called external factors, because the business cannot control them, but it can prepare for them and respond to them.

Opportunities are favourable conditions or events in the environment that the business could use to its advantage. Examples include a growing number of customers in the area, a gap in the market where nobody sells a product people want, a special event such as a sports day or festival, a new road or taxi rank bringing more people past, changes in fashion or trends, cheaper suppliers, new technology such as cheap card machines or social media, and government support programmes. For the vetkoek stall, opportunities might be that Entrepreneur's Day falls in winter, when people want hot food, and that no other team is planning to sell hot savoury food.

Threats are unfavourable conditions or events in the environment that could harm the business. Examples include competitors, especially new ones or ones with lower prices; rising prices of ingredients or fuel; bad weather; load-shedding; crime; new laws or regulations; changes in customers' tastes; and economic hard times when customers have less money. For the vetkoek stall, threats might be that another team decides to sell hot chips, that oil prices rise before the day, or that rain moves the market indoors where frying is not allowed.

Because threats cannot be controlled, the business plans how to reduce their effect: for example, buying oil early, preparing a backup product that does not need frying, or agreeing with teachers in advance on a safe indoor space.

A useful test to tell internal from external factors is to ask: would this still exist if our business did not? Competitors, weather and prices exist regardless, so they are external. Our skills and equipment belong to us, so they are internal.

## Subtopic: Using the SWOT Grid to Plan

A SWOT analysis is usually drawn as a grid of four boxes: strengths at the top left, weaknesses at the top right, opportunities at the bottom left and threats at the bottom right. The top row is internal; the bottom row is external. The left column is helpful; the right column is harmful.

Filling in the grid is only the first step. The real value comes from using it to make decisions. A simple approach is to ask four questions.

How can we use our strengths to take advantage of the opportunities? The vetkoek team's family recipe and low costs, together with winter and the lack of other hot food, suggest they should advertise hot, fresh vetkoek as the warm treat of the day.

How can we fix or reduce our weaknesses? Borrow a second pot, practise frying at home and plan who does what.

How can we use our strengths to defend against threats? If another team sells chips, the family recipe and fillings can make the vetkoek special.

How can we prepare for threats that could hit our weaknesses? Buy oil early, check the indoor rules with the teacher and prepare a cold backup product in case frying is not allowed.

The result is a list of actions that make the business plan stronger. Real businesses, from small shops to large companies, use SWOT analyses when starting a business, launching a new product or reviewing their performance. You will include a SWOT analysis in your Entrepreneur's Day project.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through one team's four-box plan for a vetkoek stall, in plain words with the same facts.

## Subtopic: The Vetkoek Team's Four Boxes

Sizwe, Lerato and Musa want to sell vetkoek on Entrepreneur's Day. Their teacher asks them to draw four boxes.

Strengths, inside and good: Sizwe's gogo has a famous vetkoek recipe. Flour and oil are cheap. The team works hard and gets along.

Weaknesses, inside and bad: they have only one frying pot. Nobody has run a stall before. Vetkoek must be fried fresh, which is slow.

Opportunities, outside and good: the day is in July, so everyone will want hot food. No other team is selling hot savoury food. The school allows stalls near the gate where parents pass.

Threats, outside and bad: another team might decide to sell hot chips. Oil prices could go up. If it rains, the market moves indoors where frying might not be allowed.

Top boxes are about them; bottom boxes are about the world around them.

Try the questions for this part: put each point in the right box.

## Subtopic: From Boxes to Action

Four boxes are only useful if they lead to action. The team writes one action for each box.

Strength: put Gogo's famous recipe on the poster: hot vetkoek, Gogo's recipe!

Weakness: borrow a second pot from Lerato's aunt, and practise frying at home on Saturday so they are faster.

Opportunity: set up near the gate where parents pass, and add a mince filling for a warm, filling winter snack.

Threat: buy the oil this week before prices rise, ask the teacher where they can fry if it rains, and prepare cold muffins as a backup.

On Entrepreneur's Day it does rain. Because they asked in advance, they fry under the covered walkway, and the muffins sell too. They sell out by the end of second break.

Remember: S and W are inside, and you can change them. O and T are outside, and you can only prepare for them. Then turn every box into an action.

Try the questions for this part: match each action to the box it comes from.
