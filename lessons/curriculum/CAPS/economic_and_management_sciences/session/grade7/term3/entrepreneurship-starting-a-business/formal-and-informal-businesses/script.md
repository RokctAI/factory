# Part 1 — Expert

This session begins the planning of a business by distinguishing formal from informal businesses and linking business ideas to needs and wants. The governing idea is that formal businesses are registered and comply with laws on tax, labour and trading, while informal businesses operate outside most of these systems, and that both kinds succeed only if they satisfy customers' needs and wants. The characteristics, examples, advantages and disadvantages of formal and informal businesses, the importance of the informal sector, and how needs and wants generate business opportunities are examinable.

## Subtopic: Formal Businesses

A formal business is one that is officially registered and operates within the law's requirements for businesses. In South Africa, companies are registered with the Companies and Intellectual Property Commission, the CIPC. Formal businesses register with the South African Revenue Service, SARS, to pay income tax and, if their turnover is large enough, VAT. They register their employees for the Unemployment Insurance Fund, UIF, follow labour laws on wages, working hours and safety, and may need licences from the municipality, for example to sell food or alcohol.

Formal businesses usually have fixed premises such as shops, offices or factories, keep proper financial records, have business bank accounts and often employ several people. Examples include supermarket chains, banks, mines, car dealerships, registered restaurants, pharmacies and registered small businesses such as a plumbing company or a hair salon in a shopping centre.

Being formal has advantages. A formal business can get loans from banks more easily, apply for government tenders and contracts, sell to large companies that require registered suppliers, protect its name and brand, and give customers confidence. Its workers are protected by labour laws and UIF.

There are also disadvantages and costs. Registration, licences, accounting and tax compliance take time and money. Formal businesses must pay minimum wages and follow regulations, which can be difficult for very small businesses, and they face inspections. Some people call the paperwork red tape.

## Subtopic: Informal Businesses

An informal business is one that is not officially registered and operates outside most formal systems of tax, labour and business regulation. Many are run by one person or a family, from home, from a street corner, a taxi rank or a market stall. They often deal in cash and keep few written records.

Examples in South Africa include street vendors selling fruit, vegetables, sweets and snacks; many spaza shops and shebeens; home hair salons and nail technicians; backyard mechanics and panel beaters; informal car washes; dressmakers and shoe repairers working from home; vetkoek and kota sellers; and people who sell second-hand clothes. Some informal traders do have municipal trading permits, so the line between formal and informal is not always sharp.

Informal businesses have advantages. They are easy to start, need little capital and no paperwork, and the owner can begin quickly. They are flexible: they can change products, move to where customers are, and open whenever there is demand. They often provide convenient, affordable goods and services close to where people live.

They also have disadvantages. Incomes are often low and uncertain. Owners struggle to get bank loans and cannot bid for large contracts. Workers have little protection, no UIF and no guarantee of minimum wages. Traders may face harassment, confiscation of goods or eviction from trading spots, and are vulnerable to crime and bad weather. Without records, owners may not know whether they are really making a profit.

The informal sector is very important in South Africa. It provides work and income for millions of people who might otherwise have none, and supplies many households with daily goods. Government and municipalities increasingly try to support informal traders with permits, trading stalls, training and links to formal suppliers, while also encouraging them to formalise as they grow.

## Subtopic: Business Ideas From Needs and Wants

Every successful business, formal or informal, exists because it satisfies people's needs or wants, as you learned in Term 1. Customers spend money only on goods and services that meet a need or a want, so the starting point for any business idea is to ask: what do people need or want that they are not getting, or not getting conveniently, cheaply or well enough?

Business ideas can come from many places. Problems people complain about: long queues, no shop nearby, broken phones, dirty taxis. Your own skills and interests: baking, braiding hair, fixing bicycles, drawing, coding. Changes in the community: a new school or clinic brings people who need food, stationery and transport. Seasons and events: school uniforms in January, warm drinks and blankets in winter, gifts in December. Waste that could become a product: recycled materials made into crafts.

Businesses that meet needs, such as food, water, basic clothing and transport, tend to have steady demand, because people must buy them even when money is short. But competition is often strong and prices low. Businesses that meet wants, such as treats, fashion, entertainment and decorations, can charge more and be more creative, but demand may fall when customers have less money.

Before starting, an entrepreneur should test the idea. Who exactly are the customers? How many are there? How much would they pay? Who else already sells this, and at what price? Asking potential customers, watching what sells and starting small are cheap ways to test an idea. The next lessons show tools for this: SWOT analysis, setting goals and advertising.

The questions for this section are with you now: how business ideas come from needs and wants, and how to test an idea.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through two businesses on the same street corner, in plain words with the same facts.

## Subtopic: The Supermarket and the Fruit Stand

On one corner of a busy street there is a big supermarket. Right outside its door, Mr Sithole sells fruit and vegetables from a table.

The supermarket is a formal business. It is registered with the CIPC and SARS, pays tax, has a bank account, employs fifty people who are registered for UIF, and has a licence from the municipality. It can borrow money from the bank to open a new branch. But it spends a lot on accountants, rent, permits and following rules.

Mr Sithole's stand is an informal business. He did not register anything. He started with R500 and a table, buys fruit at the market each morning and sells it for cash. He can move his table to the taxi rank on busy days and close when it rains. But he cannot get a bank loan, he has no UIF, and some days the municipal officials tell him to move.

Both businesses meet the same need: people want fresh fruit and vegetables. The supermarket offers choice and a roof; Mr Sithole offers low prices and convenience right where people walk past.

## Subtopic: Start From a Need

How did Mr Sithole decide to sell fruit? He noticed that taxi passengers walking home had no time to go into the supermarket and wanted a quick, cheap apple or banana. That is a need, and he met it.

You can find a business idea the same way.

See a problem: learners at your school want something cold to drink on hot days, and the tuck shop runs out.

Ask: will people pay, and how much? Ask ten classmates. If most say yes at R5, that is a good sign.

Check: who else sells it? If the tuck shop sells cooldrinks for R12, your frozen juice pops at R5 might win.

Start small and listen: make 30 juice pops, sell them, ask what people think, and improve.

Needs give steady sales. Wants give room to be creative. Either way, the customer decides.

Remember: formal businesses are registered and follow the rules; informal ones are not registered but are easy to start; and every business begins with a need or want.
