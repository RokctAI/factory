# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines how entrepreneurs and individuals set and achieve goals using the SMARTER framework. The governing idea is that clearly defined goals give direction and motivation, and that goals which are specific, measurable, achievable, relevant and time-bound, and which are evaluated and reviewed, are far more likely to be achieved. The meaning of a goal, the difference between short-term and long-term goals, each element of SMARTER, rewriting vague goals as SMARTER goals and planning steps to reach them are examinable and form part of the Entrepreneur's Day project.

## Subtopic: Why Goals Matter

A goal is a specific result that a person or a business plans to achieve in the future. Goals give direction, so that effort and resources are focused on what matters most. They motivate people, because progress towards a clear target is encouraging. They help with decisions, because options can be judged by whether they help reach the goal. And they make it possible to measure success.

Goals can be short-term or long-term. A short-term goal can be achieved in a fairly short time, such as days, weeks or a few months: selling 100 muffins on Entrepreneur's Day, saving R200 by the end of the term, or improving a maths mark in the next test. A long-term goal takes much longer, often years: passing matric with good marks, qualifying as an electrician, or growing a small business into one that employs ten people. Long-term goals are usually reached through a series of short-term goals.

Businesses set many kinds of goals: sales targets, profit targets, numbers of customers, quality standards, opening a new branch, or reducing waste. Successful entrepreneurs often have a clear vision, a long-term picture of what they want the business to become, and break it down into specific goals.

People also set personal goals for their studies, health, finances and relationships. The same skills apply.

Many goals fail not because people do not work hard, but because the goals are vague, unrealistic or forgotten. That is why business planners use frameworks such as SMART and SMARTER.

The questions for this section are with you now: the meaning of a goal, why goals matter, and the difference between short-term and long-term goals.

## Subtopic: The SMARTER Framework

SMARTER is a checklist for writing strong goals. Each letter stands for a quality the goal should have.

Specific: the goal states clearly what is to be achieved. Not do well on Entrepreneur's Day, but sell freshly baked muffins on Entrepreneur's Day.

Measurable: progress and success can be measured with a number or a clear test. Sell 120 muffins and make a profit of at least R400.

Achievable: the goal is realistic given the time, skills, money and resources available. If the team can bake only 80 muffins in one oven the night before, a goal of 500 is not achievable.

Relevant: the goal matters and fits the bigger purpose. Selling muffins is relevant to a goal of raising money for a class trip; spending the profit on a party may not be.

Time-bound: the goal has a deadline. By the end of the second break on Entrepreneur's Day, 14 September.

Evaluated: progress is checked regularly along the way. The team checks during the first break how many muffins have sold and whether to lower the price slightly for the last batch.

Reviewed: after the deadline, the result is reviewed to see what worked, what did not, and what to change next time. Some versions say re-adjusted: the goal and plan are updated based on what was learned.

Some versions of the framework use slightly different words for some letters, for example attainable instead of achievable, or realistic instead of relevant, but the ideas are the same.

A full SMARTER goal reads: Our team will sell 120 home-baked muffins at R10 each by the end of second break on Entrepreneur's Day, making a profit of at least R400 for our class trip; we will check sales at first break and review our results the following day.

The questions for this section are with you now: the meaning of each letter of SMARTER and how to write a SMARTER goal.

## Subtopic: Planning Steps and Tracking Progress

Once a SMARTER goal is set, the next step is an action plan: a list of the tasks needed to achieve it, with who will do each task and by when.

For the muffin goal, the action plan might be: by Friday 30 August, choose the recipe and calculate the cost per muffin, Lerato; by Wednesday 4 September, do a SWOT analysis and draw up the budget, the whole team; by Friday 6 September, test-bake a small batch and ask ten classmates for feedback, Musa; by Monday 9 September, design and put up posters, Sizwe; on Thursday 13 September, buy ingredients and packaging, Lerato and Musa; that evening, bake 120 muffins, the whole team at Musa's house; on the day, set up the stall at 07:30 and keep a record of sales, Sizwe.

Breaking a goal into tasks makes it manageable, shows whether the timeline is realistic and makes each person responsible for something specific.

Tracking progress keeps the plan on course. A simple chart with each task and a tick when it is done, displayed where the team can see it, works well. If a task falls behind, the team discusses how to catch up or whether to adjust the goal.

When the goal is reached, it is worth celebrating, because success motivates people for the next goal. When it is not reached, the review should focus on learning rather than blame.

Goal-setting skills apply far beyond business: studying for exams, saving money, getting fit or learning a new skill all work better with SMARTER goals and an action plan.

The questions for this section are with you now: action plans, assigning responsibility and deadlines, and tracking progress.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through one vague wish that becomes a goal a team can actually hit, in plain words with the same facts.

## Subtopic: From Wish to SMARTER Goal

The team's first idea for a goal is: we want to make lots of money on Entrepreneur's Day. That is a wish, not a goal. How much is lots? Selling what? By when?

Let us make it SMARTER, letter by letter.

Specific: sell home-baked muffins.

Measurable: 120 muffins and at least R400 profit.

Achievable: two ovens at two houses can bake 120 the night before. Yes.

Relevant: the profit goes to the class trip, which is why we are doing this.

Time-bound: by the end of second break on Entrepreneur's Day.

Evaluated: at first break we count how many are sold and decide whether to change anything.

Reviewed: the next day we sit together and write down what worked and what to do better.

Now the goal is: we will sell 120 home-baked muffins by the end of second break on Entrepreneur's Day and make at least R400 profit for our class trip, checking sales at first break and reviewing the next day.

Try the questions for this part: rewrite a vague wish as a SMARTER goal.

## Subtopic: The Muffin Countdown Chart

A goal needs a plan. The team draws a countdown chart on a big sheet of paper.

Friday: choose the recipe and work out the cost per muffin. Lerato. Tick.

Wednesday: do the SWOT analysis and the budget. Everyone. Tick.

Friday: test-bake 12 muffins and ask ten classmates what they think. Musa. Tick, and they say add chocolate chips.

Monday: posters up around the school. Sizwe. Tick.

Thursday: buy ingredients, then bake 120 muffins at two houses. Everyone.

On the day: set up at 07:30, record every sale.

At first break they have sold 70. On track. By the end of second break, they have sold all 120 and made R430. Goal achieved. They celebrate, and the next day they review: next time, bake a few more, because they sold out too early.

Remember: SMARTER means specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, time-bound, evaluated, reviewed. Then break it into steps with names and dates, and tick them off.

Try the questions for this part: put the steps in order and say who is responsible.
