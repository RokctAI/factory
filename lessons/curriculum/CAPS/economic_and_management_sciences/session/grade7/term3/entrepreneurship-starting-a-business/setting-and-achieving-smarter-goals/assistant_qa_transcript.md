### subtopic_1 — Why Goals Matter
**Assistant:** Is it better to set a big goal or a small one?

**Tutor:** Both have a place, and the best approach usually combines them. A big, long-term goal, such as becoming a doctor or building a successful business, gives a sense of purpose and keeps you motivated through hard times. But a big goal on its own can feel overwhelming and far away, and it is hard to know what to do today. Small, short-term goals turn the big goal into steps you can actually take: passing this term's science test, joining a study group, finishing a holiday project. Each small success builds confidence and keeps you moving. The key is that each small goal should be achievable but still require effort; goals that are too easy do not help you grow, while goals that are impossible lead to discouragement. Psychologists have found that challenging but realistic goals tend to produce the best results. For Entrepreneur's Day, a team might have a big goal of being the most profitable stall in the grade, broken into smaller goals: finalise the product by Friday, test a batch next week, make 120 units by the morning of the event. Big goals give direction; small goals give momentum.

### subtopic_2 — The SMARTER Framework
**Assistant:** Why add evaluated and reviewed to the usual SMART goal?

**Tutor:** Because setting a good goal is only the beginning; most goals succeed or fail in how they are followed up. The original SMART framework focuses on how a goal is written. The extra E and R focus on what happens afterwards. Evaluating means checking progress regularly while working towards the goal, so that problems are noticed early and the plan can be adjusted. A team that sees at first break that only twenty muffins have sold can move the stall, lower the price or advertise more loudly, while there is still time to reach the target. Without evaluation, they might only discover the problem at the end of the day. Reviewing means looking back after the deadline to learn: did we reach the goal, why or why not, and what will we do differently next time? This turns every goal, whether achieved or not, into a lesson. Businesses do this all the time, comparing actual results with targets and budgets, as you saw in Term 2. Many people set goals at New Year and forget them by February; building in evaluation and review is what keeps goals alive.

### subtopic_3 — Planning Steps and Tracking Progress
**Assistant:** What should I do if I realise halfway that my goal is unrealistic?

**Tutor:** Adjust it, and do not see that as failure. Evaluation exists precisely to catch this. Suppose your team planned to sell 120 muffins, but at the test bake you discover that your oven can only bake 24 at a time and each batch takes 25 minutes, so 120 muffins would take over two hours of baking, which you do not have on the evening before. You have several options. You can change the plan, for example by baking at two houses, starting earlier or baking some the day before if they keep well. You can change the goal to something achievable, such as 80 muffins, and perhaps raise the price slightly or add a second product to reach a similar profit. What you should not do is keep an unrealistic goal and hope for a miracle, because that usually leads to stress and a worse result. The R in SMARTER is sometimes read as re-adjusted for exactly this reason. Being willing to change a goal based on evidence is a sign of good planning, not weakness, and it is how successful businesses operate.
