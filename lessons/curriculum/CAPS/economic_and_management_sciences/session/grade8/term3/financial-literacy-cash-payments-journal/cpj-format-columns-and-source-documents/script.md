# Part 1 — Expert

This session introduces the cash payments journal, CPJ, of a service business: why it exists, how its columns are laid out and used, and which source documents supply its entries. The anchor is the appliance repair business you met in Term 2, in April. Its bookkeeper has already completed the April cash receipts journal; now she turns to the other pile on her desk: EFT payment confirmations for spare parts, wages, rent and new tools, a till slip for stationery, and the bank statement showing bank charges and an insurance debit order. Every one of those documents records money that left the business, and every one becomes a line in the CPJ. The governing idea is that the CPJ is the subsidiary journal in which all money paid out by a business is recorded in date order, and that its columns are designed so that each payment is entered once in the bank column, for the credit to the bank account, and once again in the column that names the account to be debited. The purpose of the CPJ, the meaning and use of each column, and the source documents used are examinable, and the CPJ is used in every grade of Accounting to Grade 12.

## Subtopic: The Purpose of the Cash Payments Journal

The cash payments journal is a book of first entry in which all money paid out by the business is recorded, in the order in which it was paid, from the source documents. In Grade 8 we assume that all payments are made from the business bank account, by electronic funds transfer, debit order, card or bank charge.

A service business pays money for a limited number of purposes. It pays expenses, such as wages, rent, water and electricity, telephone, stationery, insurance, repair materials and bank charges. It buys assets, such as equipment and vehicles. It repays loans. And the owner withdraws money or goods for private use, called drawings. Some of these, especially wages and materials, happen several times a month; others happen once a month or rarely.

The CPJ has the same three purposes as the CRJ. It records every payment in one place, in date order, with a reference to its source document. It analyses payments, separating them by the account to be debited, so the owner can see how much went on wages, how much on materials and how much on other items. And it saves posting work, because analysis columns are totalled and posted as totals at the end of the month.

The two cash journals are partners. Money in goes to the CRJ, money out goes to the CPJ, and together they record every movement in the bank account. Only when the expenses from the CPJ are set against the income from the CRJ can profit be calculated.

A CPJ is numbered and headed in the same way as the CRJ: for example, cash payments journal of the repair business for April 2026, CPJ 4. The journal number is used as a folio reference in the ledger.

The questions for this section are with you now: the purpose of the CPJ, the kinds of payment it records and its link with the CRJ.

## Subtopic: The Columns of the CPJ

A CPJ for a service business usually has the following columns, from left to right.

The document column, Doc, shows the number of the source document: the EFT number, such as EFT 101, or B/S for an item taken from the bank statement. In older books this column was headed cheque number, because businesses used to pay by cheque; South African banks stopped processing cheques at the end of 2020, but you will still meet the heading in older textbooks and past papers.

The day column shows the day of the month on which the payment was made.

The name of payee column shows who was paid: the supplier, the landlord, the municipality, the employees, the owner or the bank. The payee is the person or business receiving the payment.

The folio column next to the payee is generally left blank, because individual payments are not posted from it.

The bank column shows the amount of each payment. Unlike the CRJ, the CPJ does not need an analysis of payments column for grouping, because each payment leaves the bank as a separate transaction. The total of the bank column is credited to the bank account in the ledger.

The analysis columns are for payments that occur often. Almost every service business has a wages column. A repair business also has a column for repair parts or materials, and other businesses may have columns for stationery, fuel or consumables, depending on what they pay for regularly. The total of each analysis column is debited to the account of the same name.

The sundry accounts section has three sub-columns, amount, folio and details, for payments that do not belong in an analysis column, such as rent expense, water and electricity, insurance, bank charges, equipment, loan repayments and drawings. The details sub-column names the account to be debited, and each sundry amount is posted individually.

As in the CRJ, every payment appears twice: once in the bank column and once in an analysis or sundry column. That reflects the double entry: debit the account named, credit bank.

The questions for this section are with you now: the name and use of each CPJ column, and why every payment appears twice.

## Subtopic: Source Documents for the CPJ

Every entry in the CPJ must be supported by a source document.

The EFT payment confirmation, or proof of payment, is printed or downloaded from the business's banking app after an electronic funds transfer. It shows the date, the amount, the account paid and a reference, and it is the main source document for payments to suppliers, landlords, employees and the owner. The business numbers its EFTs in sequence, and that number goes in the document column.

The original invoice or cash slip from the supplier is the document the supplier gives the business, showing what was bought and its price. When a business pays immediately, the supplier's invoice or till slip is filed with the EFT confirmation as evidence of what the payment was for. The supplier keeps the duplicate; the business keeps the original.

The bank statement is the source document for payments that the bank deducts directly, without the business initiating an EFT: bank charges, interest on overdrafts, debit orders such as insurance premiums, and card payments. These are entered with B/S in the document column, usually when the statement arrives.

A payslip and the wages record support payments of wages, showing each employee's gross pay, deductions and net pay. In Grade 8, wages are entered at the amount paid.

The cheque counterfoil was the stub kept in the cheque book when a cheque was written. It was once the main CPJ source document. It is now historical, but it still appears in some examination questions.

Matching documents to entries is an important control. Every EFT number should appear in sequence, every payment should be supported by evidence of what was bought, and at month-end the bank statement is compared with the CPJ to find payments not yet recorded, such as bank charges and debit orders.

## Subtopic: Analysing Payments into Columns

The repair business's April payments, with its CPJ analysis columns for wages and repair parts, are entered as follows.

Second of April: EFT 101 to a spares supplier for repair parts, two thousand three hundred and forty rand. Bank two thousand three hundred and forty; repair parts two thousand three hundred and forty.

Fifth of April: EFT 102 to the landlord for rent of the workshop, four thousand five hundred rand. Bank four thousand five hundred; sundry four thousand five hundred, details rent expense.

Seventh of April: EFT 103 to the employees for wages, three thousand rand. Bank and wages three thousand.

Tenth of April: EFT 104 to a stationery shop, six hundred and eighty rand, supported by the till slip. Bank six hundred and eighty; sundry six hundred and eighty, details stationery.

Fourteenth of April: EFT 105 for more repair parts, one thousand eight hundred and sixty rand. Bank and repair parts one thousand eight hundred and sixty.

Twenty-first of April: EFT 106, wages, three thousand rand. Bank and wages three thousand.

Twenty-fourth of April: EFT 107 to the owner for private use, two thousand five hundred rand. Bank two thousand five hundred; sundry, details drawings.

Twenty-fifth of April: EFT 108 to the municipality for water and electricity, one thousand three hundred and eighty rand. Sundry, details water and electricity.

Twenty-eighth of April: EFT 109 to a tool supplier for a new diagnostic tool, five thousand two hundred rand. Sundry, details equipment.

Thirtieth of April, from the bank statement: bank charges, two hundred and forty-five rand, sundry, details bank charges; and an insurance debit order, six hundred and twenty rand, sundry, details insurance.

Notice the judgement calls. Drawings are not an expense of the business; they reduce owner's equity, but they are a payment, so they go in the CPJ under sundry. Equipment is an asset, not an expense, because it will be used for years; it still goes in the CPJ, because money was paid. And the way the money left, by EFT or debit order, does not change the account debited.

## Subtopic: Totalling and Checking the CPJ

At the end of the month the CPJ is closed off in the same way as the CRJ.

Each column is added. The wages column totals three thousand plus three thousand, six thousand rand. The repair parts column totals two thousand three hundred and forty plus one thousand eight hundred and sixty, four thousand two hundred rand. The sundry column totals four thousand five hundred, six hundred and eighty, two thousand five hundred, one thousand three hundred and eighty, five thousand two hundred, two hundred and forty-five and six hundred and twenty: fifteen thousand one hundred and twenty-five rand. The bank column totals twenty-five thousand three hundred and twenty-five rand.

The cross-check, or cross-cast, is that the bank column total equals the sum of all the other column totals: six thousand plus four thousand two hundred plus fifteen thousand one hundred and twenty-five is twenty-five thousand three hundred and twenty-five rand. It agrees, so the CPJ has been added correctly.

The totals are written on the last line, double-ruled underneath, and ready for posting in Term 4: the bank total credited to the bank account, the wages total debited to wages, the repair parts total debited to repair parts, and each sundry amount debited to its own account.

Comparing the April CPJ with the April CRJ, the business received thirty-one thousand three hundred and thirty rand and paid out twenty-five thousand three hundred and twenty-five rand, so its bank balance rose by six thousand and five rand during the month. That is not the profit, because some receipts, such as capital, are not income, and some payments, such as drawings and equipment, are not expenses.

The questions for this section are with you now: totalling, cross-casting and the change in the bank balance.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The CRJ was the notebook for money in. The CPJ is its twin: the notebook for money out.

## Subtopic: A Notebook for Money Out

Picture the repair shop's bookkeeper with a new notebook. On the cover: cash payments journal, April. Every time money leaves the bank, she writes one line.

Each line has the same lanes. Doc: the EFT number, like EFT 101, or B/S if it came off the bank statement. Day: the date. Payee: who got the money. Bank: how much left the account. Then she writes the amount a second time, in the lane where it belongs.

Some lanes have their own name because the shop pays for them all the time: wages, and repair parts. Everything else goes in the sundry lane, with a word saying what it was: rent, stationery, insurance, drawings, equipment.

Why write it twice? Because every payment has two sides. Money left the bank, and something was paid for. The bank lane shows the first side; the named lane shows the second.

At the end of April she adds up each lane. Wages six thousand. Repair parts four thousand two hundred. Sundry fifteen thousand one hundred and twenty-five. Add those three: twenty-five thousand three hundred and twenty-five. And the bank lane? Also twenty-five thousand three hundred and twenty-five. They match, so her adding is right.

## Subtopic: The Payment Pile

Now the pile of papers on her desk. Which ones go in this notebook?

EFT payment confirmations: the proof from the banking app that money was sent to a supplier, the landlord, the workers or the owner. These are the main money-out documents.

Supplier slips and invoices: they show what was bought, so she staples them to the EFT proof.

The bank statement: it shows things the bank took by itself, like bank charges and the insurance debit order. She did not send those, so she only finds them when the statement arrives. That is why she must always check it.

Payslips: they show what each worker was paid.

And in old textbooks you may see cheque counterfoils, the stubs from a cheque book. Banks stopped cheques in 2020, but the word still turns up in tests.

Three traps to remember. The owner taking money for himself is drawings, not wages, and not an expense. A new tool is equipment, an asset, not an expense. And paying by EFT or debit order does not change what the money was for.

Here is a quick test you can do with any document. Ask two questions. First: did money go out of the business bank account? If yes, it belongs in the CPJ. Second: what was the money for? The answer tells you the lane. Spare parts go to the repair parts lane, the workers' pay goes to the wages lane, and anything else goes to sundry with its proper name written next to it.

Now try the practice questions: name the columns, match each document, and place each payment in the right lane.
