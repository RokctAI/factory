### subtopic_1 — The Purpose of the Cash Payments Journal
**Assistant:** Why does the CPJ not need an analysis of payments column like the CRJ?

**Tutor:** Because payments leave the bank one at a time, while receipts are often banked together in one deposit. In the CRJ, a business may receive a customer's payment and a day's cash register takings and deposit them together on one deposit slip. The bank records a single deposit, so the CRJ's bank column must show that single total to agree with the bank, while the analysis of receipts column shows the separate amounts that make it up. In the CPJ, each payment is a separate transaction on the bank statement. When the repair business pays its spares supplier by EFT, the bank records that one payment; when it pays the landlord, the bank records another. Since each line in the CPJ already matches one transaction on the bank statement, the bank column can show each amount directly, and there is nothing to group. Historically, the same was true of cheques: each cheque was presented to the bank and cleared separately. The bank statement is therefore easy to compare with the CPJ line by line, which is an important control at month-end. Some businesses pay several suppliers in one batch of EFTs, but the bank still lists each payment separately, so the CPJ still records each one on its own line. The general principle is that a journal's bank column should match what the bank actually records, so that the two can be reconciled. In the CRJ that means deposits; in the CPJ that means individual payments. The analysis columns that do appear in the CPJ, such as wages and repair parts, are for analysing payments by account, not for grouping them.

### subtopic_2 — The Columns of the CPJ
**Assistant:** Why are drawings recorded in the CPJ if they are not an expense?

**Tutor:** Because the CPJ records all money paid out of the bank, whatever it is for, and drawings are money paid out. A journal is organised by what happens to the bank: the CRJ for money in and the CPJ for money out. It does not matter whether the money paid out is an expense, the purchase of an asset, a loan repayment or a withdrawal by the owner; if it leaves the bank account, it belongs in the CPJ. What changes is the column, and later the ledger account, in which the other side is recorded. Drawings are entered in the sundry column with the details drawings, and the total is debited to the drawings account. Drawings are not an expense because they were not incurred to earn income for the business. When the owner transfers two thousand five hundred rand to his personal account to pay school fees, the business gains nothing in return; the owner is simply taking back some of his investment or profit. That is why drawings reduce owner's equity directly rather than through profit. In the accounting equation, assets decrease because the bank balance falls, and owner's equity decreases by the same amount. Expenses also reduce owner's equity, but only through the calculation of profit in the income statement. The distinction matters because profit measures how well the business performed. If drawings were treated as expenses, the business would appear less profitable than it really was. The same reasoning applies to equipment: it is recorded in the CPJ because cash was paid, but it is an asset, because it will be used for years.

### subtopic_3 — Source Documents for the CPJ
**Assistant:** How does the bookkeeper know about bank charges before entering them?

**Tutor:** She usually does not know the exact amount until the bank statement arrives, which is why the bank statement is itself a source document for the CPJ. Bank charges are fees the bank deducts from the account for services such as monthly account fees, EFT fees, card transaction fees and cash deposit fees. The business does not make a payment to initiate them; the bank simply takes them. The same applies to debit orders, which the business has authorised in advance, for example for insurance premiums or equipment rentals; the amount leaves the account on the agreed date without a new EFT. These items appear on the bank statement, and many banks also show them immediately in the banking app. At month-end, the bookkeeper compares the bank statement line by line with the CPJ and the CRJ. Any payment on the statement that is not yet in the CPJ, such as the two hundred and forty-five rand bank charges and the six hundred and twenty rand insurance debit order in April, is entered in the CPJ with B/S in the document column. This process is the beginning of a bank reconciliation, which you will study in later grades. If the bookkeeper forgot to check the statement, the CPJ would omit those payments. The business's records would then show more money in the bank than it actually has, and expenses would be understated, so profit would be overstated. Many businesses reduce surprises by downloading transaction lists from their banking apps every week and recording bank charges and debit orders promptly, rather than waiting for the month-end statement.

### subtopic_4 — Analysing Payments into Columns
**Assistant:** Why do businesses choose different analysis columns in their CPJ?

**Tutor:** Because analysis columns are meant for payments that happen often, and each business pays for different things often. The purpose of an analysis column is to save posting work and give useful information. Payments that occur many times in a month are totalled in their own column and posted to the ledger as one amount, instead of being posted line by line. Almost every business with employees pays wages several times a month, so the wages column is nearly universal. Beyond that, the choice depends on the business. The repair business buys spare parts several times a month, so a repair parts column is efficient. A taxi business would choose a fuel column. A printing business might choose a paper and ink column. A tutoring business might choose stationery. Payments that occur once a month or less, such as rent, water and electricity, insurance, bank charges, equipment purchases and drawings, go in the sundry column, where each amount is posted individually to its own account. Examination questions normally tell learners which analysis columns the CPJ has, and learners must then decide for each payment whether it fits an analysis column or belongs in sundry, naming the account in the details. A common error is to put a payment in an analysis column because it seems similar, for example putting stationery in the repair parts column; that would post stationery to the wrong account. Another is to leave the sundry details blank, which makes posting impossible. The design of the journal should always serve the business: if a sundry item starts appearing every week, it may deserve its own column.
