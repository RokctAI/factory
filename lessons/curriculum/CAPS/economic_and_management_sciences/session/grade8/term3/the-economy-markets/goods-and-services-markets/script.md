# Part 1 — Expert

This session turns from factor markets to goods and services markets, the markets in which households spend the income they earn. The anchor is the trade in tomatoes. A farmer in Limpopo sends boxes of tomatoes to a large fresh produce market in Johannesburg. Before sunrise, buyers for supermarkets, greengrocers and street traders bid for them. By midday the same tomatoes are on sale in a supermarket, in a spaza shop and on a pavement stall, and some are ordered online for home delivery. Every step is a market, and in every market the price is shaped by how much buyers want and how much sellers offer. The governing idea is that in goods and services markets, businesses sell final goods and services to households, government and other businesses; that these markets come in many kinds, local and international, physical and online, formal and informal; that the price is set by the interaction of demand and supply; and that consumers have rights that are protected by law. The kinds of goods and services, the types of markets, the basic laws of demand and supply, equilibrium price, surpluses and shortages, and consumer protection are examinable, and they lead into Grade 10 Economics and Business Studies.

## Subtopic: Goods and Services Markets

Goods and services markets, also called product markets, are markets in which final products are bought and sold. Businesses are the sellers. Households are the main buyers, but government and other businesses also buy in these markets.

Goods are physical products that can be seen and touched, such as tomatoes, school shoes and fridges. Services are activities performed for someone, which cannot be stored or touched, such as a haircut, a taxi ride, a doctor's consultation or a cellphone data bundle.

Consumer goods are usually grouped by how long they last. Non-durable goods are used up quickly, usually once: food, toothpaste, petrol and electricity. Semi-durable goods last for a while, perhaps a year or two, but wear out: clothing, shoes, school bags and kitchen utensils. Durable goods last for many years: furniture, fridges, cars and houses.

The distinction matters to businesses and households. Households buy non-durable goods every week, so spending on them is steady. They can postpone buying durable goods, such as a new fridge or car, so spending on durables falls sharply when times are hard and rises when incomes grow. Businesses that sell durables therefore feel the ups and downs of the economy more strongly than businesses that sell bread.

Goods are also divided into consumer goods, bought by households for their own use, and capital goods, bought by businesses to produce other goods, such as the bakery's oven or a farmer's tractor. The same item can be either: a car bought by a family is a consumer good, while the same car bought by a delivery business is a capital good.

The questions for this section are with you now: goods and services, durable, semi-durable and non-durable goods, and consumer and capital goods.

## Subtopic: Kinds of Goods and Services Markets

Goods and services markets can be classified in several ways.

By area. Local markets serve a town or suburb, such as a spaza shop or a hair salon. National markets serve the whole country, such as a chain of supermarkets or a cellphone network. International markets cross borders: South Africa exports citrus, wine, cars and minerals and imports fuel, machinery and electronics.

By how buyers and sellers meet. Physical markets have a place where buyers and sellers meet: the fresh produce market, a mall, a taxi rank. Online, or virtual, markets operate through websites and apps, where buyers order and pay electronically and goods are delivered.

By level of trade. In wholesale markets, large quantities are sold to other businesses that will resell them. The fresh produce market is a wholesale market: a greengrocer buys twenty boxes of tomatoes, not two kilograms. In retail markets, goods are sold in small quantities to final consumers.

By formality. The formal market consists of registered businesses that pay tax, keep records and follow labour and safety laws: supermarket chains, franchises and registered shops. The informal market consists of small, usually unregistered businesses, such as street vendors, spaza shops and home-based traders. The informal sector provides income for millions of South Africans who cannot find formal jobs, offers convenience and small quantities to customers, and is an important starting point for entrepreneurs, but its workers often have no job security, no benefits and irregular income.

By competition. Some markets have many small sellers, so no single seller can control the price, such as the many stalls at a fresh produce market. Others have only a few large sellers, such as the cellphone network market, or even a single seller, a monopoly, such as the national supplier of electricity for most of South Africa's history.

One business can sell in several kinds of market at once. A farmer might sell most of the crop on the wholesale fresh produce market, supply a local school directly, and export the best tomatoes to a neighbouring country. Knowing the market it serves helps a business decide on its prices, packaging and advertising.

The questions for this section are with you now: classifying markets by area, by how buyers and sellers meet, by level of trade, by formality and by competition.

## Subtopic: Demand, Supply and Price

In a market economy, the price of most goods and services is set by the interaction of demand and supply.

Demand is the quantity of a product that buyers are willing and able to buy at different prices. The law of demand states that, other things being equal, the higher the price, the smaller the quantity demanded, and the lower the price, the larger the quantity demanded. When tomatoes are expensive, people buy fewer or switch to other vegetables; when they are cheap, people buy more.

Supply is the quantity of a product that sellers are willing and able to sell at different prices. The law of supply states that, other things being equal, the higher the price, the larger the quantity supplied. A higher price makes it more profitable to send tomatoes to market, so farmers send more.

At the fresh produce market one morning, the demand and supply for five-kilogram boxes of tomatoes are as follows. At forty rand a box, buyers want five hundred boxes and farmers offer one hundred. At fifty rand, buyers want four hundred and farmers offer two hundred. At sixty rand, buyers want three hundred and farmers offer three hundred. At seventy rand, buyers want two hundred and farmers offer four hundred. At eighty rand, buyers want one hundred and farmers offer five hundred.

The equilibrium price is the price at which the quantity demanded equals the quantity supplied. Here it is sixty rand a box, and the equilibrium quantity is three hundred boxes. At that price, every buyer who is willing to pay sixty rand gets tomatoes, and every farmer willing to sell at sixty rand finds a buyer. The market clears.

The schedule can also be drawn as a graph, with price on the vertical axis and quantity on the horizontal axis. Plotting the quantity demanded at each price gives the demand curve, which slopes downwards from left to right, because buyers want more at lower prices. Plotting the quantity supplied gives the supply curve, which slopes upwards, because sellers offer more at higher prices. The point where the two curves cross is the equilibrium: read across to the price axis for sixty rand, and down to the quantity axis for three hundred boxes. In Grade 10 Economics you will use these graphs to analyse many markets.

The questions for this section are with you now: the law of demand, the law of supply, and finding the equilibrium price and quantity from a schedule.

## Subtopic: Surpluses, Shortages and Changing Prices

If the price is not at equilibrium, market forces push it there.

Suppose the market opens at seventy rand a box. Farmers offer four hundred boxes, but buyers want only two hundred. There is a surplus of two hundred boxes. Tomatoes go soft quickly, so sellers lower their prices to avoid being left with unsold stock, and the price falls towards sixty rand.

Suppose instead the price is fifty rand. Buyers want four hundred boxes, but farmers offer only two hundred. There is a shortage of two hundred boxes. Buyers compete for the scarce boxes and offer more, and the price rises towards sixty rand.

The equilibrium itself changes when demand or supply changes. Supply changes when production conditions change. A hailstorm in Limpopo that destroys part of the crop reduces supply, so at every price fewer boxes are offered, and the equilibrium price rises. A bumper harvest increases supply and lowers the price. Higher costs of fuel, fertiliser or electricity reduce supply and push prices up.

Demand changes when buyers' circumstances change. If incomes rise, people buy more of most goods, so demand increases and prices rise. Changes in tastes and fashions, the prices of related products, the number of buyers and the season all shift demand. In December, when many people travel and entertain, the demand for many foods rises.

These price changes are signals. A high price tells producers that buyers want more and encourages them to produce more; a low price tells them to produce less or switch to something else. In this way markets coordinate the decisions of millions of buyers and sellers without anyone being in charge.

The questions for this section are with you now: surpluses and shortages, how they correct themselves, and the causes of changes in demand and supply.

## Subtopic: Consumers in the Market

Consumers are the final buyers in goods and services markets, and in a market economy their choices largely decide what is produced, an idea called consumer sovereignty. Businesses that do not offer what consumers want, at a price they will pay, lose sales.

But consumers are often weaker than sellers. They may have less information about the product, they may be pressured by advertising, and an individual has little power against a large company. For this reason, South Africa protects consumers by law.

The Consumer Protection Act of 2008 gives consumers rights that include: the right to fair and honest dealing, without misleading claims; the right to information in plain and understandable language, including the total price; the right to choose, including the right to cancel certain agreements; the right to safe, good-quality goods, with the right to return defective goods within six months for a repair, replacement or refund; the right to fair contract terms; and the right to privacy, including the right to refuse unwanted direct marketing. The National Consumer Commission enforces the Act, and consumers can complain to the relevant ombud or to the Consumer Goods and Services Ombud.

The Competition Act of 1998 protects consumers indirectly by keeping markets competitive. The Competition Commission investigates businesses that fix prices together, divide markets between them or abuse a dominant position, because such behaviour raises prices and reduces choice. Firms found guilty can be fined heavily.

The Act also gives a cooling-off period. If you sign an agreement because of direct marketing, such as a salesperson at your door or an unexpected phone call, you may cancel it in writing within five business days without giving a reason. Prices displayed on goods are binding: if two different prices are shown, the consumer may pay the lower one.

Consumers also have responsibilities: to compare prices and quality, to read contracts before signing, to keep receipts, to use products as instructed, and to report unfair practices.

The questions for this section are with you now: consumer sovereignty, the main consumer rights, and the roles of the National Consumer Commission and the Competition Commission.

Follow one box of tomatoes from the farm to a family's table, and you will pass through several markets on the way.

## Subtopic: A Morning at the Fresh Produce Market

It is four in the morning in Johannesburg. Trucks full of tomatoes from Limpopo are lined up. Buyers walk the floor: a supermarket buyer, a greengrocer, a woman who runs a pavement stall.

If the price is forty rand a box, everyone wants tomatoes: five hundred boxes. But farmers will only send one hundred at that price. Too few tomatoes, too many buyers. Buyers start offering more.

If the price is eighty rand, farmers happily offer five hundred boxes, but only one hundred are wanted. Tomatoes pile up and start to go soft. Sellers drop the price.

Somewhere in the middle, at sixty rand, buyers want three hundred boxes and farmers offer three hundred boxes. Everyone who wants to buy at sixty finds a seller, and everyone who wants to sell at sixty finds a buyer. That is the equilibrium price.

Next week a hailstorm flattens half the crop. Fewer boxes arrive, buyers compete for them, and the price rises above sixty. The higher price tells farmers elsewhere: send more tomatoes. Nobody gave an order. The market sent the message.

## Subtopic: From the Stall to the Table

By midday the tomatoes are everywhere. On a supermarket shelf, which is the formal, retail market. At a spaza shop and on the pavement stall, which is the informal market, where you can buy just three tomatoes. And on a shopping app, which is an online market with home delivery.

Tomatoes are non-durable: they are eaten within days. The school shoes on the next shelf are semi-durable. The fridge the tomatoes go into is durable.

Now imagine the fridge breaks after two months. The Consumer Protection Act says faulty goods can be returned within six months for a repair, a replacement or a refund. The shop must also have told you the full price in plain language before you bought it.

And if the few big companies that sell fridges secretly agreed to keep prices high? That is illegal, and the Competition Commission can fine them.

Online shopping is convenient, but take care. Buy from websites and apps you can trust, check the total price including delivery, read the returns policy, and never share your banking passwords. The same consumer rights apply whether you buy in a shop or online.

So you have power as a consumer: compare prices, keep your receipts, read before you sign, and use your rights.
