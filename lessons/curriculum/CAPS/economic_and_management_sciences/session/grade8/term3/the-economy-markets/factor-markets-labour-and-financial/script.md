# Part 1 — Expert

This session introduces markets, and then focuses on two of the most important factor markets: the labour market and the financial market. The anchor is a growing bakery in Polokwane. To bake more bread, the owner needs two more assistant bakers, so she enters the labour market, where workers offer their skills and employers offer wages. She also needs a bigger oven, so she enters the financial market, where people who have saved money and businesses that need money are brought together by banks and other institutions, at a price called interest. At the same time, the households of her workers, and many other households, are selling their labour and saving part of their income in those same markets. The governing idea is that a market is any arrangement that brings buyers and sellers together so that they can exchange at a price; that in factor markets households sell the factors of production to businesses; that the price of labour is the wage and the price of borrowed money is interest; and that each market has role players and rules that shape what happens in it. The meaning of a market, the difference between factor markets and goods and services markets, the labour market and its role players, and the money and capital markets are examinable, and they lead into Grade 10 Economics and Business Studies.

## Subtopic: What a Market Is

In everyday speech, a market is a place: a fresh produce market, a flea market or a shopping centre. In economics, a market is any arrangement that brings buyers and sellers together so that they can exchange goods, services or factors of production at a price. It does not need a building. A market exists when a job is advertised online and people apply, when shares are traded electronically, or when a farmer phones a buyer to agree a price for maize.

Every market has buyers, who create demand, sellers, who create supply, a product or factor being exchanged, and a price, which is what the buyer gives up to obtain it.

Economists divide markets into two broad groups. In goods and services markets, also called product markets, businesses sell final goods and services to households, government and other businesses: bread, cellphones, haircuts and taxi rides. In factor markets, households sell the factors of production to businesses: natural resources, labour, capital and entrepreneurship. Households own the factors; businesses need them to produce.

The two groups of markets are linked in the circular flow of income. Households supply factors of production to businesses in the factor markets and receive income in return: rent, wages and salaries, interest and profit. Households then spend that income in the goods and services markets, buying what businesses produce. The money that businesses receive from sales is used to pay for factors again. Money flows in one direction around the circle, while factors, goods and services flow in the other.

The questions for this section are with you now: the meaning of a market, the elements of every market, and the difference between factor markets and goods and services markets.

## Subtopic: The Labour Market

The labour market is the market in which workers sell their labour, their physical and mental effort, and employers buy it. The demand for labour comes from employers: businesses, government and non-profit organisations that need people to produce goods and services. The supply of labour comes from households: all the people who are willing and able to work.

The price in the labour market is the wage or salary. A wage is usually paid per hour, day or week, often to workers whose hours vary; a salary is usually a fixed amount per month or year. Like any price, wages are influenced by demand and supply. Where many people have a skill and few employers need it, wages tend to be low. Where a skill is scarce and in high demand, such as experienced artisans, engineers or medical specialists, wages tend to be high.

The demand for labour is a derived demand. The bakery does not want assistant bakers for their own sake; it wants them because customers want more bread. If bread sales fall, the bakery needs fewer workers. This is why the labour market weakens in a recession and strengthens when the economy grows.

Labour is not all the same. In Term 2 you met unskilled, semi-skilled and skilled labour. Each forms its own part of the labour market, and workers can move to a better-paid part by gaining education, training and experience. The bakery's two assistants need semi-skilled labour: some training in hygiene, the use of mixers and ovens, and the bakery's recipes.

The bakery offers each assistant six thousand rand a month. For two assistants, that is twelve thousand rand a month, or one hundred and forty-four thousand rand a year, before any other costs of employment. The owner must be sure that the extra bread they make will bring in more than that.

Wages are not set by demand and supply alone. The national minimum wage puts a floor under the lowest wages. Collective agreements negotiated by trade unions can raise wages across a whole industry. Productivity matters too: a worker who produces more each hour, because of better skills, tools or organisation, is worth more to the employer and can be paid more. And wages differ by region, because the cost of living and the number of job seekers differ between, for example, a metro such as Johannesburg and a small rural town.

The labour market also has a time dimension. Young people entering it for the first time often lack experience, which employers value, so they find it harder to get a first job. Programmes such as learnerships, internships and the Youth Employment Service try to give young people that first experience.

The questions for this section are with you now: the meaning of the labour market, who demands and who supplies labour, wages as the price of labour, and derived demand.

## Subtopic: Role Players and Problems in the Labour Market

The labour market is not left entirely to employers and workers. Several role players influence it.

Employers and employers' organisations represent businesses. They decide how many people to hire and negotiate wages and conditions.

Workers and trade unions represent employees. A trade union is an organisation of workers that negotiates with employers on behalf of its members, a process called collective bargaining, about wages, working hours, safety and benefits. Trade unions may join federations, such as COSATU.

Government makes and enforces labour laws. The Labour Relations Act of 1995 protects the right to join a union and to strike, and created the Commission for Conciliation, Mediation and Arbitration, the CCMA, which helps settle disputes between employers and workers. The Basic Conditions of Employment Act of 1997 sets minimum conditions, such as maximum working hours, leave and notice periods. The National Minimum Wage Act of 2018 sets a minimum hourly wage that almost all employers must pay, adjusted every year. The Employment Equity Act of 1998 prohibits unfair discrimination and promotes fair representation in the workplace. The Unemployment Insurance Fund pays temporary benefits to contributors who lose their jobs.

South Africa's labour market has serious problems. The most important is unemployment. By the official definition, about one in three people in the labour force who want work cannot find it, and the rate is much higher among young people. Causes include slow economic growth, a mismatch between the skills that workers have and the skills employers need, the legacy of apartheid in education and where people live, and the distance and transport cost between townships and jobs. Many people work in the informal sector, in small unregistered businesses, often with low and irregular incomes.

Solutions involve all the role players: faster economic growth, better education and training through learnerships and apprenticeships under the Skills Development Act, support for small businesses, and cooperation between employers, unions and government.

The questions for this section are with you now: the role players in the labour market, the main labour laws, and the causes of unemployment in South Africa.

## Subtopic: Financial Markets

Financial markets are the markets in which money is lent and borrowed, and in which financial assets such as shares and bonds are bought and sold. They bring together savers, who have more money than they need right now, and borrowers, who need more money than they have.

The price in the financial market is interest. A borrower pays interest for the use of someone else's money, and a saver earns interest as a reward for postponing spending. Interest is usually expressed as a percentage per year.

The bakery owner wants to borrow one hundred and twenty thousand rand for a new oven, at twelve percent interest per year. If she repays the loan at the end of one year, the interest is twelve percent of one hundred and twenty thousand, which is fourteen thousand four hundred rand, so she repays one hundred and thirty-four thousand four hundred rand in total. In reality, most loans are repaid in monthly instalments, so the interest is calculated on a balance that gets smaller over time.

Financial markets are divided into two parts by how long the money is borrowed for.

The money market deals in short-term funds, borrowed and lent for periods of less than one year. Examples are overdrafts, short-term deposits, and short-term loans between banks and from the central bank. Businesses use the money market to manage cash flow, for example to pay suppliers before customers have paid them.

The capital market deals in long-term funds, for periods of more than one year. Examples are long-term loans such as the bakery's oven loan, home loans, bonds issued by government and large companies, and shares. Businesses use the capital market to finance buildings, equipment and expansion.

The questions for this section are with you now: the meaning of financial markets, interest as the price of borrowing, and the difference between the money market and the capital market.

## Subtopic: Role Players in Financial Markets

Several kinds of institution make the financial markets work.

Commercial banks are financial intermediaries: they accept deposits from savers and lend that money to borrowers. They pay depositors a lower rate of interest than they charge borrowers, and the difference, called the interest margin, covers their costs and profit. A household that keeps six thousand rand in a savings account for a year at five percent earns three hundred rand interest; the bank might lend the same money to the bakery at twelve percent.

The South African Reserve Bank is the central bank. It sets the repo rate, the rate at which it lends to commercial banks, which influences all other interest rates. It also issues banknotes and coins and, through its Prudential Authority, supervises banks so that depositors' money is safe.

The Johannesburg Stock Exchange, the JSE, founded in 1887, is the market where shares and bonds of listed companies are bought and sold. When a public company issues new shares, it raises capital; when existing shares are traded, ownership passes from one investor to another.

Insurance companies, pension funds and unit trusts collect money from many households and invest it in shares, bonds and property, which channels household savings to businesses and government. Development finance institutions, such as the Small Enterprise Finance Agency, lend to small and emerging businesses that commercial banks may consider too risky.

Regulators protect the public. The Financial Sector Conduct Authority supervises how financial institutions treat their customers, and the National Credit Act protects borrowers from reckless lending.

Households take part in financial markets every time they save, borrow, take out insurance or contribute to a pension fund. Their savings provide the funds that businesses borrow to invest, which creates jobs and growth.

The questions for this section are with you now: the role of banks, the Reserve Bank, the JSE and other institutions, and how household savings reach businesses.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

A market is simply a meeting of buyers and sellers. Let us follow one bakery as it goes shopping, not for flour, but for people and for money.

## Subtopic: Hiring a Baker

The bakery in Polokwane is busy. Customers queue every morning and the bread runs out by ten. The owner needs two more assistant bakers.

She puts up a notice and posts the job online. That is her side of the labour market: she is a buyer of labour. Twenty people apply. They are the sellers: each is offering time, effort and skill.

The price of labour is the wage. She offers six thousand rand a month each. Two assistants cost twelve thousand a month, one hundred and forty-four thousand a year.

Why does she want them? Not for their own sake. She wants them because customers want more bread. If customers stopped buying, she would not need them. That is what derived demand means.

The rules matter too. She must pay at least the national minimum wage, give fair working hours and leave, and not discriminate. If there is a dispute, the CCMA helps sort it out. The workers may join a trade union, which bargains for all its members together.

And twenty people applying for two jobs? That shows South Africa's biggest labour problem: too many people looking for work, too few jobs.

## Subtopic: Where the Oven Money Comes From

Now the bakery needs a bigger oven costing one hundred and twenty thousand rand. The owner does not have that much. Where does the money come from?

Think of all the households in town. A worker's family saves five hundred rand a month in the bank. After a year, that is six thousand rand, and at five percent it earns three hundred rand interest.

The bank collects savings like that from thousands of families and lends them to people who need money, like the bakery owner. She pays twelve percent. On one hundred and twenty thousand, that is fourteen thousand four hundred rand interest for a year. The bank keeps the difference between what it charges and what it pays, to cover its costs and make a profit.

So interest is the price of money: savers earn it, borrowers pay it.

A loan for a few weeks or months is the money market. A loan for years, like the oven loan, is the capital market. Big companies can also raise money by selling shares on the JSE.

So the bakery shops in two factor markets: the labour market for people and the financial market for money. And the households selling their labour and their savings are the same households that come back each morning to buy bread.
