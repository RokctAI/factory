### subtopic_1 — Goods and Services Markets
**Assistant:** Why do people buy fewer tomatoes when the price goes up?

**Tutor:** There are two main reasons, and both come from the fact that every household has a limited income and many things it wants to buy. The first is the income effect. When the price of tomatoes rises, a household's money buys less than before. If a family budgets fifty rand a week for vegetables and tomatoes double in price, the same fifty rand buys fewer tomatoes, so the family buys fewer simply because it can afford fewer. The second is the substitution effect. When tomatoes become more expensive compared with other foods, some buyers switch to substitutes: tinned tomatoes, onions, cabbage or a different recipe altogether. A restaurant might use less tomato in its salads. The higher the price climbs, the more buyers decide that something else is better value. Together these effects give the law of demand: other things being equal, a higher price means a smaller quantity demanded. The phrase other things being equal is important. It means we are looking only at the effect of the price, while incomes, tastes, the prices of other goods and the number of buyers stay the same. If incomes rise at the same time as the price, people might buy just as many tomatoes as before, but that would be because of the change in income, not in spite of the law. Not every product reacts equally. For necessities with few substitutes, such as bread or electricity, people cut back only a little when prices rise. For products with many substitutes or that people can do without, they cut back a lot. Economists will teach you to measure this in Grade 11 as elasticity.

### subtopic_2 — Kinds of Goods and Services Markets
**Assistant:** Is the informal market good or bad for the economy?

**Tutor:** It has important benefits and real weaknesses, so the most accurate answer is that it is valuable but should be supported to become stronger and safer. On the positive side, the informal market provides income for millions of South Africans who cannot find jobs in the formal sector. With unemployment around one in three, a pavement stall or a home-based business can be the difference between a household having food and going hungry. Informal traders also serve customers well: they are close to where people live, they sell in small quantities that suit tight budgets, such as three tomatoes or a single cigarette lighter, and they open early and close late. The informal market is also a training ground. Many successful entrepreneurs started by selling from a table or a car boot and grew into registered businesses. On the negative side, informal workers usually have no employment contract, no paid leave, no unemployment insurance and no pension, and their incomes are irregular. Informal businesses often cannot get bank loans because they lack records and security, which limits their growth. They may not meet health and safety standards, and because they do not pay income tax, government collects less revenue to spend on services, although informal traders still pay VAT on most of the goods they buy. Government and municipalities try to support the sector through trading permits, market stalls with shelter, water and toilets, training and small-business finance, while encouraging traders to register as they grow. The aim is not to destroy informal trade but to help it become more secure and productive.

### subtopic_3 — Demand, Supply and Price
**Assistant:** How can a hailstorm in Limpopo change the price of tomatoes in Cape Town?

**Tutor:** Because the tomato market is a national market, connected by trucks, wholesalers and supermarket chains, so a change in supply in one place affects prices everywhere the tomatoes are sold. Limpopo is one of the main tomato-growing areas in South Africa. When a hailstorm destroys part of the crop, fewer boxes are sent to the fresh produce markets. At the old equilibrium price of sixty rand a box, buyers still want three hundred boxes, but perhaps only two hundred arrive. There is a shortage. Buyers for supermarkets and greengrocers compete for the scarce boxes and offer higher prices, and the price rises until the quantity demanded falls to match the smaller supply, perhaps at seventy or eighty rand a box. Supermarket chains buy for stores across the country, including Cape Town, so their higher costs are passed on to shoppers everywhere. Traders in Cape Town who usually buy from Western Cape farmers also see prices rise, because buyers from other regions now compete for Western Cape tomatoes too. This is the price mechanism at work. The higher price performs two useful jobs. It rations the smaller supply, because people buy fewer tomatoes or switch to substitutes, so the tomatoes go to those who value them most. And it signals to farmers in other regions, and to those with tomatoes in storage or in tunnels, that it is now worth sending more to market, which gradually increases supply and brings the price down again. When the next harvest is normal, prices usually return to around their previous level. Weather shocks are one reason food prices can change sharply from month to month.

### subtopic_4 — Surpluses, Shortages and Changing Prices
**Assistant:** If a shop has a no-refunds sign, does that cancel my rights under the Consumer Protection Act?

**Tutor:** No. A shop cannot use a sign or a contract term to take away rights that the Consumer Protection Act gives you, so a no-refunds sign does not apply to goods that are defective. Under the Act, goods must be of good quality, in good working order and free of defects, and they must be reasonably suitable for the purpose for which they are usually used. If a product turns out to be faulty within six months of delivery, you may return it, at the supplier's risk and expense, and choose whether the supplier repairs it, replaces it or refunds your money. This is called the implied warranty of quality, and it applies regardless of what any sign says. There are some limits. The right covers defects, not a change of mind. If you simply decide you do not like the colour of a shirt, the shop does not have to take it back, although many shops do so voluntarily, under their own returns policy. Damage you caused through misuse is not covered. And if the shop clearly told you before the sale that an item was sold as is, for example a marked-down item with a described defect, you cannot later complain about that particular defect. To use your rights, keep your receipt, return the item promptly, and explain the fault calmly. If the supplier refuses, you can complain to the Consumer Goods and Services Ombud or to the National Consumer Commission. Knowing these rights makes you a more confident consumer, and businesses that respect them build trust and repeat customers.
