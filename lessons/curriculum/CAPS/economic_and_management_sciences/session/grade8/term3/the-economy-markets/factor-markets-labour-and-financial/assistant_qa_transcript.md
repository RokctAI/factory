### subtopic_1 — What a Market Is
**Assistant:** If a market does not need a building, how do I know when a market exists?

**Tutor:** A market exists whenever buyers and sellers are able to come into contact with each other and agree to exchange something at a price. The place is not what matters; the contact and the exchange are. A fresh produce market in a city is a market in the everyday sense, with stalls and trucks and crowds. But a job advertised on a website, with applicants sending their CVs and an employer choosing whom to hire at an agreed salary, is just as much a market, the labour market, even though the buyer and sellers may never stand in the same room until the interview. When someone in Durban buys shares in a mining company through an app, and the seller is a pension fund in Cape Town, the trade happens through the electronic system of the Johannesburg Stock Exchange, which is the market. When a farmer and a mill agree a price for maize over the phone, they have created a market for that maize. To check whether a market exists, ask four questions. Who are the buyers? Who are the sellers? What is being exchanged? How is the price set? If you can answer all four, you have identified a market. This way of thinking helps you see markets everywhere, including in places people do not usually call markets: the market for school places, the market for rental housing, and the market for electricity. It also explains why economists group markets by what is traded, goods and services or factors of production, rather than by where they happen.

### subtopic_2 — The Labour Market
**Assistant:** Why does the bakery's demand for workers depend on its customers?

**Tutor:** Because the bakery does not want workers for their own sake; it wants them for the bread they help to produce, and bread is only worth producing if customers will buy it. Economists call this derived demand: the demand for a factor of production is derived from the demand for the goods and services it helps to make. Suppose the bakery's customers buy an extra four hundred loaves a day. The owner needs more labour to mix, shape, bake and pack those loaves, so she hires two assistants. If a new supermarket opens nearby and her sales fall back, she no longer needs the extra hands, and she might reduce their hours or not replace someone who leaves. The same idea operates across the whole economy. When people have more money to spend, businesses sell more and hire more, so unemployment tends to fall. During a recession, when spending falls, businesses sell less and the demand for labour falls with it, so unemployment rises. Derived demand also explains why the demand for particular skills changes over time. As more people buy online, the demand for delivery drivers and warehouse workers rises, while the demand for some kinds of shop assistants falls. As mines become more mechanised, the demand for unskilled underground labour falls and the demand for skilled technicians rises. So when you think about jobs, always look past the employer to the customers behind the employer. Policies that help businesses sell more, at home or abroad, are among the most effective ways to create jobs.

### subtopic_3 — Role Players and Problems in the Labour Market
**Assistant:** What is the difference between the money market and the capital market?

**Tutor:** The difference is the length of time for which the money is borrowed or invested: the money market deals in short-term funds, for less than one year, and the capital market deals in long-term funds, for more than one year. They are two parts of the financial market, and they serve different needs. Businesses and households use the money market to manage short-term timing problems. The bakery might use an overdraft to pay its flour supplier on the first of the month before its customers' monthly accounts come in, then repay it within weeks. Banks lend to each other overnight to balance their accounts. Government borrows for a few months by issuing treasury bills. Savers who will need their money soon can put it in short-term deposits. The capital market is for long-term investment. The bakery's oven loan, repayable over several years, is a capital market transaction because the oven will be used for years. Home loans, often over twenty years, are in the capital market. So are bonds, which are long-term loans to government or companies, and shares, which have no repayment date at all because they make the investor a part-owner. The JSE is the best-known part of South Africa's capital market. The rule of thumb is to match the finance to the use. Short-term needs, such as stock and wages, should be financed short-term. Long-term assets, such as buildings and equipment, should be financed long-term, because they take years to pay for themselves. A business that finances a building with an overdraft can be forced to repay before the building has earned its keep.

### subtopic_4 — Financial Markets
**Assistant:** Why should a bank pay savers less interest than it charges borrowers? Is that unfair?

**Tutor:** It is not unfair in itself, because the difference, called the interest margin, pays for real services that the bank provides, although the size of the margin can be questioned. A bank acts as a financial intermediary between thousands of savers and thousands of borrowers who would otherwise struggle to find each other. Imagine a household that has saved six thousand rand and the bakery owner who needs one hundred and twenty thousand. Without a bank, she would have to find twenty such households, persuade each to lend to her, and agree terms with each one, and each household would carry the risk that she might not repay. The bank solves these problems. It pools many small deposits into large loans. It checks borrowers' ability to repay and asks for security, reducing the risk. It spreads its lending across many borrowers, so that one bad loan does not ruin the savers. It allows savers to withdraw their money when they need it, while lending for years. And it must keep capital and reserves, as the Reserve Bank's Prudential Authority requires, to protect depositors. All of this costs money: staff, systems, security, regulation, and losses on loans that are not repaid. If the bank pays savers five percent and charges the bakery twelve percent, the margin of seven percentage points must cover those costs, and what remains is profit for the bank's shareholders. Competition between banks, and alternatives such as savings co-operatives and development finance institutions, help keep margins in check, and the National Credit Act limits the interest that lenders may charge on many kinds of credit.
