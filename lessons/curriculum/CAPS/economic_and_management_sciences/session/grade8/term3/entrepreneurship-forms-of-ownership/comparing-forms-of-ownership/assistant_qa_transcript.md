### subtopic_1 — The Criteria for Comparison
**Assistant:** Why is the partnership often called the most risky form of ownership?

**Tutor:** Because partners carry unlimited liability for debts that other people may have caused. A sole trader also has unlimited liability, but she is responsible only for her own decisions; she knows every debt the business takes on because she takes it on herself. In a partnership, each partner can usually bind the partnership to contracts in the ordinary course of business, so one partner can order stock, sign a lease or take on a debt that the others did not know about. Yet every partner is jointly and severally liable for the partnership's debts once its assets are exhausted: a creditor can claim the full amount from any one partner, who must then try to recover the others' shares. If one partner is careless, dishonest or simply unlucky, the others may lose their personal possessions. Partnerships are also vulnerable to disagreements, which can paralyse decisions, and they end when a partner leaves or dies, which can force the sale of assets to pay out the departing partner's share. None of this means partnerships are bad. Many successful professional firms operate as partnerships, and the form combines capital and skills cheaply. But the risk explains why careful partners choose each other with great care, sign a detailed partnership agreement, require joint signatures for large commitments, keep proper accounts that all partners can see, take out insurance and, as the business grows, often convert to a private company or a personal liability company.

### subtopic_2 — Owners, Legal Personality and Liability
**Assistant:** If public companies can raise the most capital, why are most businesses not public companies?

**Tutor:** Because most businesses do not need that much capital, and the costs and obligations of being a public company would outweigh the benefits. A car wash, a hair salon, a plumbing business or even a successful medium-sized factory can usually finance itself from the owners' savings, retained profits and bank loans. Becoming a public company is expensive: it requires at least three directors, an audit committee, a company secretary, an annual audit by a registered auditor and an annual general meeting. Offering shares to the public needs a prospectus prepared with lawyers and accountants. Listing on the JSE adds listing fees, strict continuing obligations and public reporting of results. All of this can cost millions of rand a year, which only makes sense for a large business raising large sums. There are also non-financial costs. A public company's results and plans are visible to competitors. The founders may lose control as shares are sold to outsiders, and they face pressure from investors to deliver profits every half year. Decisions become slower and more formal. Many owners value control and privacy more than access to outside capital, so they keep their businesses as private companies, even very large ones; some of South Africa's biggest businesses are privately owned. The forms of ownership are therefore not a ladder that every business should climb. Each form is a tool, and the best choice is the simplest form that meets the business's needs for capital, protection and continuity.

### subtopic_3 — Capital, Management and Profit
**Assistant:** How should a learner justify a choice of form of ownership in the term project?

**Tutor:** By identifying the business's specific needs and linking each one to a characteristic of the chosen form, then briefly explaining why the other forms are less suitable. A weak answer simply names a form and lists its general advantages. A strong answer starts with the business. Describe it: what it does, how many people will own it, how much capital it needs, what risks it faces and what the owners want, such as control, privacy or the ability to pass it on. Then choose the form and connect each need to a characteristic. For example, a learner proposing a school uniform business owned by three friends might write: the business needs about eighty thousand rand, which none of us has alone, so pooling capital as partners or shareholders is necessary; we will sign contracts with schools and suppliers, so we want limited liability to protect our families' homes; we want to decide who may join as an owner; therefore a private company suits us. The learner should then consider the alternatives: a sole trader cannot raise enough capital and would carry all the risk; a partnership would raise the capital but leave us with unlimited liability; a public company would be far too expensive and complex for our size. Finally, the learner should acknowledge the disadvantages of the chosen form, such as registration costs and annual returns, and explain how they will be managed. Using the correct terms, such as unlimited liability, legal personality, continuity and Memorandum of Incorporation, accurately throughout shows understanding and earns marks.

### subtopic_4 — Continuity, Formation and Regulation
**Assistant:** Can a business change its form of ownership as it grows?

**Tutor:** Yes, and many successful businesses do exactly that, moving to a form that fits their new size and needs. The usual path begins with a sole trader, because it is cheapest and simplest. When the owner needs more capital or skills, she may bring in partners and form a partnership, or bring in shareholders through a private company. When debts and risks grow, owners often convert to a private company to gain limited liability and continuity. A very large private company may later convert to a public company and list on a stock exchange to raise capital from the public. Changing form is a legal process. A sole trader or partnership does not convert automatically: the owners register a new company with CIPC, and the company buys or takes over the assets, liabilities and contracts of the old business, after which the old business ceases. Contracts, bank accounts, licences and tax registrations must be transferred or redone in the company's name. A private company converts to a public company by amending its Memorandum of Incorporation and meeting the additional requirements, and listing requires approval from the JSE. Businesses can also move the other way: a listed public company can be bought out and delisted, becoming private again, which happens when owners want to escape the costs and pressures of public listing. The key lesson is that the form of ownership is chosen to suit the business at a particular stage, and it should be reviewed as the business grows and its needs change.
