# Part 1 — Expert

This session closes the topic of forms of ownership by asking what each form contributes to the wider economy, in particular to creating lasting jobs and to using natural resources responsibly. The anchor is a small town in Limpopo, at the edge of a dry river valley, where four businesses operate side by side: a sole trader who runs a tyre repair stall at the taxi rank, a partnership of two brothers who farm tomatoes on irrigated land, a private company that owns the local brick factory, and a large listed public company that mines platinum on the hills outside town. Each employs people, each uses natural resources, and each affects the town's future in a different way. The governing idea is that every form of ownership can create jobs and use resources sustainably or unsustainably, that small businesses create many of the new jobs South Africa needs while large companies create large numbers of jobs and carry large environmental responsibilities, and that sustainable businesses balance people, the planet and profit. The role of the forms of ownership in sustainable job creation and the use of natural resources is examinable, and it links to the Term 3 project and to Grade 10 Business Studies and Economics.

## Subtopic: Why Job Creation Matters

South Africa has one of the highest unemployment rates in the world. In recent years, roughly a third of people in the labour force who want work have not been able to find it, and the rate among young people aged fifteen to twenty-four has been far higher, around sixty percent. Unemployment means lost income for households, poverty, hunger, and lost skills and confidence for the unemployed, especially the young. For the economy it means wasted labour, a factor of production that could be producing goods and services, lower tax revenue, and higher spending on social grants.

Government cannot employ everyone. The National Development Plan set a target of creating eleven million jobs by 2030, and it expected about ninety percent of those jobs to come from small and expanding firms. That makes entrepreneurs and the businesses they form central to solving unemployment.

A sustainable job is one that lasts, pays a fair wage, offers decent working conditions and allows the worker to develop skills. A job that disappears after a few months, or that harms the worker's health, does not solve the problem. So the question for each form of ownership is not only how many jobs it creates, but how secure and decent those jobs are.

The questions for this section are with you now: why unemployment matters, the role of businesses in creating jobs and what makes a job sustainable.

## Subtopic: Small Businesses and Jobs

Sole traders, partnerships and small private companies, often grouped together as small, medium and micro enterprises, or SMMEs, play a large role in job creation.

They are easy and cheap to start, so they are often the first step for people who cannot find a job: the tyre repair stall began when its owner was retrenched from a mine and used his payout to buy a compressor and tools. That one stall now employs two young assistants. Small businesses are often labour-intensive, using many workers rather than expensive machinery, so each rand of capital creates more jobs. They operate in townships, villages and small towns where large companies seldom invest, and they supply goods and services close to where people live.

The informal sector, made up mainly of unregistered sole traders, provides a living for millions of South Africans, from street vendors to home-based childminders. These jobs are valuable, but many are insecure, low paid and without benefits such as unemployment insurance.

Small businesses also face serious obstacles: limited capital, difficulty obtaining loans, lack of business skills, crime, unreliable electricity and the cost of complying with regulations. Many fail within their first few years. Government and private organisations support them through finance, training, mentorship and business hubs, and through preferential procurement, in which government and large companies buy from small and black-owned suppliers.

The tomato-farming brothers show both sides. Their partnership employs twelve permanent workers and thirty seasonal pickers at harvest time. The seasonal jobs are important income, but they last only a few months each year.

The questions for this section are with you now: why SMMEs create many jobs, the informal sector, and the obstacles small businesses face.

## Subtopic: Large Companies and Jobs

Private and public companies, especially large ones, create jobs in a different way.

Because they can raise large amounts of capital, large companies can build factories, mines, shopping centres and telecommunications networks that employ hundreds or thousands of people. The platinum mine outside town employs about three thousand people directly. Large companies also create many indirect jobs: the mine buys from suppliers of equipment, transport, catering, security and cleaning, many of them local small businesses, and its workers spend their wages in the town's shops, which then employ more people.

Jobs in large companies are often more secure and better paid, with benefits such as medical aid, pension funds, skills training and the protection of labour laws and trade unions. Companies also train apprentices and graduates, building skills that the whole economy uses.

But large companies are often capital-intensive: they use machinery and technology that raise productivity but employ fewer workers for each rand invested. When commodity prices fall or companies restructure, they may retrench large numbers of workers at once, which can devastate a town that depends on them.

Continuity matters too. Because companies continue beyond the life of any owner, they can offer long-term employment and invest in training over many years, while a sole trader's business, and the jobs in it, may end when the owner retires or dies.

The questions for this section are with you now: direct and indirect jobs, labour-intensive and capital-intensive production, and the role of continuity.

## Subtopic: Forms of Ownership and Natural Resources

Every business uses natural resources: land, water, energy and raw materials. The way it uses them decides whether those resources will still be available for future generations.

Sustainable use of natural resources means meeting the needs of the present without reducing the ability of future generations to meet their own needs. It includes using renewable resources, such as water, soil and forests, no faster than they can be replaced; using non-renewable resources, such as minerals and fossil fuels, efficiently; reducing waste and pollution; and restoring land after it has been used.

The tomato-farming brothers depend completely on the river and the soil. If they pump too much water, the river dries up for the farms downstream; if they overuse fertiliser and pesticides, the soil degrades and the river is polluted. Drip irrigation, crop rotation and careful use of chemicals let them farm the same land for decades.

The brick factory, a private company, digs clay and burns coal in its kilns, producing smoke and leaving open pits. It can use cleaner kiln technology, restore the clay pits and recycle broken bricks.

The platinum mine, a public company, uses enormous amounts of water and energy, moves millions of tonnes of rock and produces waste that can pollute water for decades. South African law requires mines to obtain environmental authorisation, to set aside money for rehabilitation of the land when mining ends, and to submit social and labour plans that commit them to developing their workers' skills and the surrounding communities.

Larger businesses usually have larger environmental impacts, but also more capital to invest in cleaner technology, and as public companies they are more visible: investors, the media and communities scrutinise their environmental records. Small businesses have smaller impacts individually, but together they matter a great deal, and they often lack the money and knowledge to improve.

The questions for this section are with you now: what sustainable use means, and how farms, factories and mines can use resources responsibly.

## Subtopic: Laws, Incentives and Responsibility

Businesses are guided towards sustainable behaviour by laws, by taxes and incentives, and by their own sense of responsibility.

Laws set minimum standards. The Constitution gives everyone the right to an environment that is not harmful to their health or well-being. The National Environmental Management Act requires environmental impact assessments before large projects such as mines, factories and dams can proceed, and holds polluters responsible for cleaning up. Water use is licensed, and labour laws such as the Basic Conditions of Employment Act set minimum standards for working hours, leave and wages.

Taxes and incentives change costs. South Africa introduced a carbon tax in 2019, which makes businesses pay for some of the greenhouse gases they emit, encouraging them to use cleaner energy. Government programmes have bought electricity from private renewable energy producers, creating an industry of solar and wind farms. Tax incentives and preferential procurement reward businesses that train workers and support small suppliers.

Responsibility goes beyond the law. Many businesses follow the idea of the triple bottom line: they measure their success not only by profit, but also by their effect on people, such as workers and communities, and on the planet. Large companies report on their environmental and social performance, and many spend part of their profits on corporate social investment, such as funding schools, clinics and bursaries. Co-operatives and non-profit companies are often formed specifically to meet social or community needs.

The questions for this section are with you now: environmental and labour laws, the carbon tax and incentives, and the triple bottom line.

## Subtopic: Comparing the Contributions

Bringing the four businesses together shows that each form of ownership contributes in its own way.

The sole trader creates a few jobs quickly and cheaply, often for people with few other options, but the jobs are vulnerable if the owner fails, and its environmental impact is small but often unmanaged, such as old tyres dumped or burned.

The partnership creates more jobs, including seasonal ones, and its survival depends directly on how well the partners look after their land and water, which gives them a strong reason to farm sustainably.

The private company creates steady, longer-term jobs and has continuity, but needs to invest in cleaner production and land rehabilitation.

The public company creates thousands of direct and indirect jobs, invests heavily in skills and pays large amounts of tax, but has the largest environmental footprint and the greatest power to harm or help a whole town.

A sustainable local economy needs all of them: small businesses that create jobs close to home, and large companies that bring capital, skills and markets, all using natural resources in a way that leaves the valley productive for the next generation.

The questions for this section are with you now: how each form contributes to sustainable jobs and resource use.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

One small town, four businesses, two big questions: are they creating lasting jobs, and are they looking after the valley?

## Subtopic: Who Makes the Jobs?

Our town has a big problem: too many people cannot find work. In South Africa, about a third of people who want a job do not have one, and for young people it is even worse.

Look at the tyre repair stall at the taxi rank. One man, a sole trader, started it with his retrenchment payout. Now two young people work with him. Small, cheap to start, right where people need it. Lots of small businesses like this create many jobs together.

The two brothers farming tomatoes, a partnership, employ twelve people all year and thirty extra pickers at harvest. Good jobs, but the seasonal ones end when the harvest ends.

The brick factory, a private company, employs sixty people steadily, year after year. Because a company carries on even when owners retire, those jobs can last.

The platinum mine, a public company, employs three thousand people, and buys from local caterers, transport firms and security companies, which creates even more jobs. But if platinum prices crash, it could retrench hundreds at once.

Notice the difference between a direct job and an indirect job. The miner who drills the rock has a direct job at the mine. The woman who cooks lunch for the miners works for a catering company, and the driver who brings diesel works for a transport firm. They have indirect jobs: they exist because the mine exists. And when miners buy bread, clothes and airtime in town, the shop assistants who serve them owe their jobs partly to the mine too. One big employer can keep a whole chain of jobs going, which is wonderful while it lasts, and frightening if it ever closes.

So small businesses create jobs close to home; big companies create big numbers and training. A town needs both.

## Subtopic: Who Looks After the Valley?

Every one of these businesses uses nature.

The tyre stall should send old tyres for recycling instead of burning them. The brothers need the river and the soil: drip irrigation saves water, and careful use of chemicals keeps the river clean. If they ruin the soil, they ruin their own farm. The brick factory burns coal and digs pits: it can use cleaner kilns and fill in old pits. The mine uses huge amounts of water and energy and leaves waste: the law makes it plan and pay for repairing the land when mining ends.

Laws help. Big projects need an environmental impact assessment first. Companies that pollute must pay to clean up. A carbon tax makes polluting more expensive.

The best businesses think about three things, not one: people, planet and profit. That is called the triple bottom line. A business that makes profit but ruins the river and the workers' health is not really successful.

So, to finish: every form of ownership can create jobs and every form uses natural resources. The sustainable ones create jobs that last and leave the valley healthy for the next generation.

Here is one last way to picture it. Imagine visiting the same town in thirty years. If the tyre stall grew into a workshop, the brothers' children still farm healthy soil, the brick factory replanted its old pits, and the mine's land was restored when the platinum ran out, then those businesses were sustainable. If instead the river is dry, the land is scarred and the jobs are gone, then the profits of today were paid for by the town's future.

Now try the practice questions on the four businesses in the valley: who creates the jobs, and who protects the resources.
