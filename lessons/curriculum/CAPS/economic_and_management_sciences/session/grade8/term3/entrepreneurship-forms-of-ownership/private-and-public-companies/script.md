# Part 1 — Expert

This session continues forms of ownership with companies, the forms that give owners limited liability and continuity. The anchor is the Soweto bakery from the previous lesson, now run by two partners who want to open three more shops and supply supermarkets. They need far more capital, they worry about unlimited liability, and they want the business to outlive them. Their accountant suggests registering a private company, and, if the business grows very large one day, perhaps a public company that sells shares to the public on the stock exchange. The governing idea is that a company is a separate legal person, owned by shareholders and managed by directors, whose owners have limited liability, and that private and public companies differ mainly in whether they may offer shares to the public. The characteristics, advantages and disadvantages of private and public companies, and the meaning of shares, shareholders, directors and dividends, are examinable, and they lead into Grade 10 Business Studies.

## Subtopic: What a Company Is

A company is a business that is registered under the Companies Act of 2008 with the Companies and Intellectual Property Commission, CIPC. Registration is called incorporation, and it creates a new legal person, separate from the people who own it.

Because a company has legal personality, it owns its own assets, enters into contracts in its own name, borrows in its own name, and can sue and be sued. The company, not the owners, owes its debts. That is the source of limited liability: shareholders can lose only the money they paid for their shares, and creditors cannot claim their personal possessions. Limited liability does have exceptions; for example, banks often ask the directors of small companies to sign personal guarantees for loans, and directors who run a company recklessly or fraudulently can be held personally liable.

A company also has continuity. Because it is a separate person, it continues to exist when shareholders sell their shares, retire or die. Ownership simply passes to new shareholders.

The owners of a company are its shareholders. The capital of the company is divided into units called shares, and each shareholder owns a number of them. A shareholder who owns three hundred of a company's one thousand shares owns thirty percent of it.

The company is managed by a board of directors, elected by the shareholders. In a small company, the shareholders and the directors are often the same people. In a large company, thousands of shareholders elect a board of directors, who appoint managers to run the business.

The founding document of a company is the Memorandum of Incorporation, known as the MOI. It sets out the company's rules, such as the rights attached to shares and how directors are appointed. To register, the founders file the MOI and a notice of incorporation with CIPC, choose a name and pay a fee.

The questions for this section are with you now: incorporation, legal personality, limited liability, continuity, shares, shareholders and directors.

## Subtopic: The Private Company

A private company is a company whose MOI prohibits it from offering its shares to the public and restricts the transfer of its shares. Its name ends with the words Proprietary Limited, usually shortened to (Pty) Ltd.

The characteristics of a private company are these. It needs at least one director. It may have one or more shareholders; the old maximum of fifty shareholders no longer applies under the 2008 Act. Its shares cannot be offered to the public, and a shareholder who wants to sell usually has to offer the shares to the existing shareholders first, so the owners keep control of who joins. It is a separate legal person, with limited liability and continuity. It must keep proper accounting records, file an annual return with CIPC and pay company income tax, which is twenty-seven percent of taxable profit. Depending on its size and public interest score, its financial statements may need to be audited or independently reviewed, but many small private companies are not required to have a full audit.

The advantages of a private company are limited liability for the shareholders, continuity, the ability to raise more capital than a partnership by bringing in more shareholders, and control: the owners decide who may become a shareholder. It is also more credible to banks and large customers than a sole trader or partnership, and its financial affairs are not published to the public.

The disadvantages are that it costs more to start and run than a sole trader or partnership, because of registration fees, accountants' fees and annual returns. There are more legal requirements. Capital is still limited, because shares cannot be sold to the public. And it may be hard for a shareholder to sell shares, because there is no open market for them.

Most family businesses, many small and medium businesses and many subsidiaries of large groups in South Africa are private companies. The bakery partners could form Soweto Bakes (Pty) Ltd, issue themselves shares in the ratio of their contributions, become its directors and run it much as before, but now with limited liability and continuity.

The questions for this section are with you now: the characteristics, advantages and disadvantages of a private company.

## Subtopic: The Public Company

A public company is a company that may offer its shares to the public. Its name ends with the word Limited, shortened to Ltd.

The characteristics of a public company are these. It must have at least three directors, and it must appoint an audit committee and a company secretary. It may invite the public to buy its shares by publishing a prospectus, a document that gives investors detailed information about the company. Its shares are freely transferable: shareholders can sell them to anyone. Many public companies are listed on a stock exchange, the main one in South Africa being the Johannesburg Stock Exchange, the JSE, where shares are bought and sold every trading day. A public company must have its annual financial statements audited by an independent registered auditor and must make them available to shareholders, and listed companies must publish them. It must hold an annual general meeting, or AGM, where shareholders elect directors and receive reports. Like a private company, it has legal personality, limited liability and continuity, and pays company income tax.

The advantages of a public company are its ability to raise very large amounts of capital from thousands of investors, limited liability for shareholders, continuity, the easy sale of shares on the stock exchange, and the ability to employ highly skilled professional managers. Large public companies can achieve economies of scale: producing on a large scale lowers the cost of each unit.

The disadvantages are that it is expensive and complex to establish and run, with strict legal and stock exchange requirements. Its financial results are public, so competitors can see them. Ownership is separated from control: shareholders own the company, but directors and managers run it, and their interests may differ. Decision-making can be slow because of its size. And because shares are freely traded, the original founders can lose control if others buy enough shares.

Many of the best-known South African businesses, including large retailers, banks, mining houses and mobile network operators, are public companies listed on the JSE.

The questions for this section are with you now: the characteristics, advantages and disadvantages of a public company, the prospectus, the JSE and the AGM.

## Subtopic: Shares, Dividends and Limited Liability in Numbers

Shareholders invest in a company in the hope of two kinds of reward: dividends and growth in the value of their shares.

A dividend is a share of the company's profit paid to shareholders. The directors decide how much of the profit after tax to pay out and how much to keep in the business, called retained profit, to finance growth. The dividend is paid per share. If a company has one thousand shares and declares a total dividend of one hundred thousand rand, each share receives one hundred rand, so a shareholder with three hundred shares receives thirty thousand rand. Dividends paid to individuals are subject to dividends tax, generally twenty percent, which the company withholds and pays to SARS.

Limited liability can also be shown in numbers. Suppose an investor buys one hundred shares in a public company at fifty rand each, paying five thousand rand. If the company later fails with large debts, the investor loses the five thousand rand, but no more; creditors cannot claim her home or savings. If the company does well and the share price rises to eighty rand, her shares are worth eight thousand rand, a capital gain of three thousand rand, as well as any dividends she has received.

Compare the bakery as a partnership and as a private company. If the partnership fails owing two hundred thousand rand, the partners' personal possessions are at risk. If Soweto Bakes (Pty) Ltd fails owing the same amount, the company's creditors can claim only the company's assets, and the shareholders lose only what they invested, unless they signed personal guarantees.

The questions for this section are with you now: dividends per share, retained profit, and limited liability in rand.

## Subtopic: Other Kinds of Companies

The Companies Act of 2008 recognises more kinds of companies than the private and public company, and it is useful to recognise their name endings, even though Grade 8 concentrates on the four main forms of ownership.

A personal liability company has a name ending in Incorporated, shortened to Inc. It is a kind of private company, but its directors, past and present, are jointly and severally liable with the company for debts incurred while they were directors. Professionals such as attorneys, accountants and doctors often use this form, because their professional rules require them to remain personally responsible to their clients while still gaining the continuity of a company.

A state-owned company has a name ending in SOC Ltd. It is owned by the national, provincial or local government. Examples in South Africa include the electricity utility, the national freight rail and ports operator, and the national passenger airline. These companies provide services that government considers important for the economy, and their performance affects the national budget you studied in Term 1, because government sometimes has to support them with loans, guarantees or bailouts.

A non-profit company has a name ending in NPC. It is formed for a public benefit or a cultural or social purpose, such as running a community clinic, a school feeding scheme or a sports development programme. It may earn income and make a surplus, but it may not pay that surplus to its members or directors as profit; any surplus must be used for its purpose.

Co-operatives are a separate form of business, registered under the Co-operatives Act rather than the Companies Act. A co-operative is owned and controlled democratically by its members, each usually with one vote regardless of how much capital he or she has contributed, and it exists to meet the members' common needs. Farming, savings and burial co-operatives are common examples in South Africa, and some textbooks list them alongside the other forms of ownership.

Each of these forms answers the same questions as before: who owns the business, who controls it, who carries the debts, and what happens to the profit.

The questions for this section are with you now: personal liability companies, state-owned companies, non-profit companies and co-operatives.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Companies sound grand, but the main idea is simple: the business becomes its own person.

## Subtopic: The Bakery Becomes a Person

The two bakers want to open three more shops. That is a lot of money and a lot of risk. Their accountant says: register a company.

When you register a company with CIPC, something strange and useful happens. The business becomes a separate person in law. It has its own name, Soweto Bakes (Pty) Ltd. It owns the ovens. It signs the shop leases. It owes the debts.

So if the company cannot pay, the creditors can take the company's things, but not the bakers' houses. The most the owners can lose is the money they put into the company. That is limited liability. And if one baker retires, the company carries on, because the company is the person, not the bakers. That is continuity.

The owners are called shareholders. The company is cut into shares, like slices of a cake. Own three hundred slices out of a thousand and you own thirty percent of the company. The people who run it are the directors. In a small company, the shareholders and the directors are often the same people.

Proprietary Limited, or (Pty) Ltd, means a private company. It cannot sell its shares to the public, and the owners decide who may join. It needs at least one director.

## Subtopic: Selling Slices to the Public

Imagine the bakery grows into a giant, with shops in every province. Now it might become a public company. Its name ends in Ltd, without the Pty. A public company can sell shares to anyone, and many are listed on the Johannesburg Stock Exchange, the JSE, where shares are bought and sold every day.

That means it can raise huge amounts of money from thousands of people. But it must follow stricter rules: at least three directors, an independent audit every year, an annual general meeting for shareholders, and its results open for everyone, including competitors, to read.

Why would you buy a share? For two rewards. First, dividends: a slice of the profit. If the company pays out one hundred thousand rand on a thousand shares, that is one hundred rand a share, so three hundred shares earn thirty thousand rand. Second, growth: buy a share for fifty rand, sell it later for eighty rand.

And the risk? Buy one hundred shares at fifty rand, and the most you can lose is five thousand rand, even if the company collapses. That is limited liability in action.

So here are the four forms of ownership in one line each. A sole trader is one owner with unlimited liability. A partnership is two or more owners with unlimited liability. A private company, Pty Ltd, is its own legal person with limited liability, whose shares are not sold to the public. A public company, Ltd, is its own legal person with limited liability that can sell its shares to anyone.

Now try the practice questions on the bakery company: who owns it, who runs it, who gets the dividends and how much a shareholder can lose.
