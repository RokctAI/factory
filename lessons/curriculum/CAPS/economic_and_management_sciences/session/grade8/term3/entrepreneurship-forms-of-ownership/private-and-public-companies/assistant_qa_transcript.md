### subtopic_1 — What a Company Is
**Assistant:** If a company is a separate person, who actually makes its decisions?

**Tutor:** The company acts through people, mainly its board of directors, while the shareholders make the biggest decisions at meetings. A company cannot think or sign for itself, so the law gives authority to particular people. The shareholders, as owners, hold the ultimate power: they elect and remove directors, approve changes to the Memorandum of Incorporation and vote on major matters such as selling the whole business. Each share usually carries one vote, so a shareholder with more shares has more say. The directors, elected by the shareholders, manage the company's business and make the important decisions between meetings: approving budgets, borrowing, opening new branches and deciding on dividends. Directors have legal duties under the Companies Act: they must act in good faith, in the best interests of the company, with reasonable care and skill, and must not use their position for personal gain. Directors who break those duties can be held personally liable. The directors usually appoint managers, such as a chief executive officer, to run the business from day to day, and those managers employ the staff. In the bakery company, the two former partners might be the only shareholders and the only directors, so in practice they make every decision themselves, just as before, but now in their roles as directors of Soweto Bakes (Pty) Ltd, signing contracts on the company's behalf. In a large public company, ownership and management are separated: thousands of shareholders own it, a board of perhaps a dozen directors oversees it, and professional managers run it.

### subtopic_2 — The Private Company
**Assistant:** Why would founders of a successful private company choose to become a public company?

**Tutor:** Mainly to raise large amounts of capital for growth and to allow the founders and early investors to sell some of their shares. A private company can raise capital only from its existing shareholders, from a few new private investors, from retained profits and from loans. When the business wants to expand rapidly, for example by opening shops in every province, building a factory or buying a competitor, those sources may not be enough. By converting to a public company and listing on the JSE, the company can offer shares to the public through a prospectus and raise money from thousands of investors, including pension funds and unit trusts that manage the savings of millions of ordinary South Africans. Listing also creates a market for the shares. In a private company, a founder who wants to retire or diversify may struggle to find a buyer for her shares; once the company is listed, she can sell them on the stock exchange at the market price. A listing can also raise the company's profile and credibility with customers, suppliers and banks. But the decision has costs. The company must meet strict JSE listing requirements, have its statements audited, publish its results, hold annual general meetings and comply with governance codes such as the King Code, all of which are expensive. The founders may lose control if they sell too many shares, and the company becomes exposed to public scrutiny and short-term pressure from investors. Many successful companies choose to remain private for exactly these reasons.

### subtopic_3 — The Public Company
**Assistant:** Is limited liability fair to the people a company owes money to?

**Tutor:** It shifts some risk to creditors, but the law and the market include protections that make it broadly fair. Without limited liability, few people would invest in businesses they do not run themselves, because they could lose their homes if the directors made poor decisions. Limited liability makes it possible for millions of people, including workers saving through pension funds, to own small parts of large companies, which channels savings into investment, jobs and growth. Creditors know the rules: when a supplier sells to a company on credit or a bank lends to it, they know that only the company's assets stand behind the debt. They can protect themselves by checking the company's financial statements and credit record, charging interest that reflects the risk, asking for security over assets, or asking directors of small companies to sign personal guarantees, which makes the directors personally liable for that debt. The Companies Act also protects creditors. Directors must not trade recklessly, with gross negligence or with intent to defraud creditors, and directors who do can be made personally liable for the company's debts. A company may pay dividends only if it passes a solvency and liquidity test, so owners cannot strip out money that creditors need. And when a company is wound up, creditors are paid from its assets before shareholders receive anything. So limited liability is not a licence to escape debts; it is a rule that limits the losses of passive owners, balanced by duties on directors and tools for creditors.

### subtopic_4 — Shares, Dividends and Limited Liability in Numbers
**Assistant:** Why is a public company's information made public?

**Tutor:** Because the public is invited to invest in it, and investors need reliable information to make decisions and to protect themselves. A public company can raise money from anyone, including people who will never meet the directors or visit the business. Those investors are trusting the directors with their savings. If the company could hide its results, dishonest directors could exaggerate profits to attract investors, sell shares at inflated prices, or conceal losses until the company collapsed. The law therefore requires a prospectus when shares are offered to the public, giving detailed information about the business, its risks, its directors and its finances. Each year, the financial statements must be audited by an independent registered auditor, who checks that they fairly present the company's position, and an audit committee of the board oversees the process. Listed companies must also publish interim and annual results and announce important events promptly on the stock exchange news service, so that all investors receive the same information at the same time. These rules support trust in the market. When investors trust the information, they are more willing to invest, and companies can raise capital more cheaply. South Africa has seen what happens when that trust breaks: a large listed retailer revealed serious accounting irregularities in 2017, its share price collapsed and pension funds and ordinary investors lost billions of rand. Strong disclosure and auditing rules are meant to prevent such failures, and their cost is part of the price of being able to raise money from the public.
