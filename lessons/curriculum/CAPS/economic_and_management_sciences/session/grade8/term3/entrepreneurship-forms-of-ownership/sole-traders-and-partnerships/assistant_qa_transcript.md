### subtopic_1 — What a Form of Ownership Is
**Assistant:** Why do so many South African businesses stay sole traders despite unlimited liability?

**Tutor:** Because for a small business, the advantages of simplicity, low cost and full control usually outweigh the risk, especially when the business has little debt. Starting a sole trader business requires no registration under the Companies Act, no founding documents and no audit, so a person can begin selling the same day with whatever capital she has. That suits informal and survivalist businesses, such as spaza shops, street vendors and home bakers, where the owner often has very few assets to protect anyway and where the cost of registering and maintaining a company would eat a large share of a small profit. The owner also keeps all the profit and makes quick decisions, which matters when margins are thin. In practice, the risk of unlimited liability is greatest when a business borrows heavily or signs large contracts. Many sole traders buy stock for cash, have no loans and owe little, so their real exposure is limited. The problem appears as the business grows: when the baker wants a shop lease, an expensive oven on credit or large orders from suppliers, her personal possessions become exposed. That is usually the point at which owners consider a partnership to share the load, or a private company to gain limited liability. There are also costs to converting: a company must register with CIPC, keep proper records, submit annual returns and may pay tax at the company rate. So staying a sole trader is often a sensible choice, not ignorance, as long as the owner keeps debts under control.

### subtopic_2 — The Sole Trader
**Assistant:** What does jointly and severally liable mean for partners?

**Tutor:** It means that each partner can be held responsible for the whole of the partnership's debts, not only for his or her own share, once the partnership's assets have been used up. Suppose the bakery partnership owes a supplier forty thousand rand and has no money left. The partners have agreed to share profits and losses equally, so in fairness each owes twenty thousand rand. But the supplier does not have to collect twenty thousand from each. Under joint and several liability, the supplier may claim the full forty thousand rand from either partner, usually the one with more assets. That partner must pay and then try to recover twenty thousand rand from the other partner under their agreement. If the other partner cannot pay, the first partner bears the whole loss. The rule protects creditors, who lend to the partnership trusting that all the partners stand behind it. For partners, it is the strongest reason to choose partners carefully. You are trusting your partner not only with the business but with your house and savings. A partnership agreement cannot prevent creditors from claiming against any partner, but it can set out how partners must compensate each other, limit which decisions a single partner may make alone, and require both signatures for large purchases or loans. Partners should also keep proper accounts, so that each can see what the business owes at any time. Professional partnerships, such as law firms, often take out insurance for this reason, and some convert to personal liability companies or private companies as they grow.

### subtopic_3 — The Partnership
**Assistant:** Why does a partnership end when a new partner joins?

**Tutor:** Because a partnership is not a separate legal person; it is simply an agreement between particular people. The partnership agreement is a contract between the baker and the pastry chef, setting out what each contributes, how profits are shared and how decisions are made. If a third person, say a cake decorator, wants to join, the old contract between the two cannot simply be stretched to include her, because she was not a party to it and the terms will change: the capital contributions, the profit-sharing ratio and the duties of each partner must all be agreed again. So in law, the old partnership is dissolved and a new partnership between three people is formed under a new agreement. The same happens when a partner dies, retires or leaves: the agreement between the original partners can no longer operate, so that partnership ends. In practice, this does not always mean the shop closes. A well-written agreement can provide for the business to continue under the remaining partners, for example by setting out how the departing partner's share will be valued and paid out, and the new partnership can take over the assets, the premises and the customers. But the change is a legal break, and it can cause disputes, delays and costs, especially if the departing partner's share must be paid in cash. This lack of continuity is one of the main differences from a company, which is a separate legal person and continues to exist when shareholders sell their shares or die.

### subtopic_4 — Choosing Between the Two
**Assistant:** Should the profit-sharing ratio always match the capital each partner contributed?

**Tutor:** No. The ratio is whatever the partners agree, and a fair agreement usually considers both capital and work. Capital is the most obvious contribution: if the pastry chef puts in fifty thousand rand and the baker contributes equipment and goodwill worth thirty thousand rand, a capital ratio of five to three seems natural. But partners also contribute their time, skills, customers and reputation, and those can matter as much as money. If the baker works full time in the shop while the chef only works weekends, sharing in the capital ratio would underpay the baker. Partners can handle this in several ways. They can share profits equally if they believe their total contributions are roughly equal. They can pay one or both partners a salary, often called a partner's salary, for work done, and then share the remaining profit in an agreed ratio. They can also allow interest on capital, so that a partner who contributes more money receives a return on it first, before the rest is shared. For example, if the bakery makes one hundred and sixty thousand rand, they could allow interest on capital, pay the full-time partner a salary, and share whatever remains equally. In Grade 8 you only need to share a profit in a given ratio: one hundred and sixty thousand rand in the ratio three to five gives sixty thousand rand and one hundred thousand rand. The important lesson is that the ratio must be agreed in writing before the partnership starts, because arguments about money are one of the most common reasons partnerships fail.
