# Part 1 — Expert

This session opens the study of forms of ownership with the two simplest ways to own a business: the sole trader and the partnership. The anchor is a home baker in Soweto who starts by selling vetkoek, scones and birthday cakes from her kitchen as a sole trader, and who two years later is asked by a friend, a trained pastry chef with savings, to go into partnership and open a small bakery shop. Should she stay on her own or share the business? The answer depends on the characteristics of each form of ownership: who owns it, who manages it, who carries the debts, how it is started and what happens when the owner leaves or dies. The governing idea is that the form of ownership decides who controls a business, who shares the profits and who is personally responsible for its debts, and that sole traders and partnerships are easy to start but expose their owners to unlimited liability. The characteristics, advantages and disadvantages of sole traders and partnerships are examinable, and they lead into Grade 10 Business Studies, where all the forms of ownership are studied in more depth.

## Subtopic: What a Form of Ownership Is

A form of ownership is the legal way in which a business is owned and organised. It answers a set of practical questions: how many owners there are, who makes the decisions, who provides the capital, how the profits are shared, who is responsible for the debts, and whether the business continues if an owner leaves or dies.

Several key ideas run through every form of ownership, and it helps to learn them first.

Legal personality means that the business exists in law as a separate person from its owners. A business with legal personality can own property, enter into contracts, sue and be sued in its own name. A business without legal personality is legally the same as its owner or owners.

Liability is responsibility for the debts of the business. Unlimited liability means the owners are personally responsible for all the debts of the business, so creditors can claim the owners' private possessions, such as a house or a car, if the business cannot pay. Limited liability means the owners can lose only the money they invested in the business; their private possessions are protected.

Continuity means whether the business continues to exist when an owner dies, retires or leaves. A business with continuity carries on; a business without it may have to close or be restarted.

Capital, management and profit sharing complete the picture: how much money the owners can raise, who runs the business day to day, and how the rewards are divided.

In South Africa, the Companies Act of 2008 governs companies, which must be registered with the Companies and Intellectual Property Commission, known as CIPC. Sole traders and partnerships are not registered under the Companies Act, although they must still register with the South African Revenue Service for tax and may need a trading licence or permit from the municipality.

## Subtopic: The Sole Trader

A sole trader, also called a sole proprietor, is a business owned by one person, who provides the capital, makes the decisions, keeps all the profit and carries all the risk. Sole traders are by far the most common form of ownership in South Africa: spaza shops, hairdressers, plumbers, tutors, taxi owners, street vendors and home bakers are usually sole traders.

The characteristics of a sole trader are these. There is one owner, although the owner may employ workers. There are no legal formalities to start, apart from tax registration and any licence the activity needs, so it is quick and cheap to establish. The business has no legal personality: the owner and the business are the same person in law. The owner has unlimited liability for all the debts of the business. Capital is limited to what the owner can save or borrow. The owner manages the business personally. The owner pays personal income tax on the profit; the business itself is not taxed separately. And there is no continuity: when the owner dies or stops trading, the business ends, although someone else may buy its assets and start again.

The advantages follow from those characteristics. A sole trader is easy and cheap to start. The owner makes quick decisions without consulting anyone. The owner keeps all the profit, which is a strong motivation to work hard. The owner has close contact with customers and can give personal service. Affairs are private, because a sole trader does not publish financial statements.

The disadvantages are serious too. Unlimited liability means the owner can lose personal possessions if the business fails. Capital is limited, so growth is difficult. The owner carries the whole workload and may lack skills in areas such as accounting or marketing. Illness or holiday can stop the business. And because there is no continuity, the business may close when the owner dies.

The baker in Soweto is a typical sole trader. She started with an oven, baking tins and a few bags of flour, sold to neighbours and on social media, and keeps every rand of profit. But if a supplier she owes is not paid, her own savings and furniture are at risk.

## Subtopic: The Partnership

A partnership is an agreement between two or more people who each contribute capital, skills or both to a business, with the aim of making a profit that they share. Partnerships are common among professionals such as doctors, attorneys, accountants and architects, and among small businesses where friends or family combine resources. Older law limited most partnerships to twenty partners; since the Companies Act of 2008 that limit no longer applies, but most partnerships remain small.

The characteristics of a partnership are these. There are at least two partners. A partnership is formed by agreement, which can be verbal, but a written partnership agreement is strongly advised. The agreement should set out each partner's capital contribution, how profits and losses will be shared, each partner's duties, how decisions will be made and what happens when a partner leaves or dies. Like a sole trader, a partnership has no legal personality. The partners have unlimited liability, and in South African law they are jointly and severally liable for the partnership's debts once the partnership's own assets are used up: a creditor can claim the full amount from any one partner, who must then recover the other partners' shares from them. Each partner is taxed personally on his or her share of the profit. And a partnership has no continuity: when a partner dies, retires or a new partner joins, the old partnership ends and a new agreement must be made.

The advantages of a partnership are that it can raise more capital than a sole trader, because several people contribute. Partners can bring different skills: in the bakery, one partner knows baking and the other knows marketing and bookkeeping. Workload and risk are shared, so a partner can take leave while the others continue. Decisions benefit from more than one point of view. And, like a sole trader, a partnership is easy to start and its affairs are private.

The disadvantages are that liability is unlimited and shared, so one partner's poor decision can cost the others their personal possessions. Disagreements between partners can paralyse or destroy the business. Profits must be shared. Capital is still limited compared with a company. And the partnership ends whenever a partner leaves, which can disrupt the business.

The pastry chef offers fifty thousand rand and her training; the baker offers her customers, recipes and equipment. Together they could open a shop that neither could afford alone, but each would then be responsible for debts the other incurs on behalf of the business.

## Subtopic: Choosing Between the Two

The choice between staying a sole trader and forming a partnership depends on what the business needs and on the people involved.

Staying a sole trader makes sense when the business is small, the owner has the skills and capital it needs, and the owner values independence and quick decisions. Forming a partnership makes sense when the business needs more capital or skills than one person has, and when the partners trust each other and can agree on how to run it.

Because both forms carry unlimited liability, the owners must be careful about how much debt the business takes on. A partnership also requires trust, because each partner can usually bind the partnership to contracts in the ordinary course of business. That is why the partnership agreement matters so much: it reduces misunderstandings by putting the important rules in writing before problems arise.

Profit sharing is usually agreed in a ratio. If the baker contributes equipment and customers worth thirty thousand rand and the chef contributes fifty thousand rand in cash, they might agree to share profits in the ratio of their capital, three to five, or equally, if they value their work equally. If the bakery makes a profit of one hundred and sixty thousand rand in a year and they share in the ratio three to five, the baker receives sixty thousand rand and the chef receives one hundred thousand rand. If they share equally, each receives eighty thousand rand.

Owners of both forms can reduce their risk even though liability stays unlimited. They can avoid unnecessary debt and buy stock for cash where possible. They can take out insurance against fire, theft and claims by customers. They can keep the business bank account separate from their personal account, so that they always know what the business owns and owes. They can keep proper records, using the journals you have studied this year, so that problems are seen early. And partners can agree that large purchases or loans need the signatures of all partners, which prevents one partner from committing the others without their knowledge.

Sole traders and partnerships also matter greatly to the economy. Most new businesses in South Africa start in one of these two forms, because they cost so little to establish. They provide jobs and incomes in townships, villages and small towns where large companies seldom operate, and they supply goods and services close to where people live. Government agencies such as the Small Enterprise Development and Finance Agency, which brought together earlier support bodies, offer training, advice and finance to help such businesses grow.

A business can also change its form as it grows. Many companies began as sole traders or partnerships. When the risks and the need for capital grow, owners often convert the business into a private company to gain limited liability and continuity, which is the subject of the next lesson.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Two shapes of business, side by side: one person on her own, or a few people sharing.

## Subtopic: One Baker, One Kitchen

Picture the baker in Soweto. She bakes scones and birthday cakes in her own kitchen and sells them to the neighbours and on her phone. She owns the business alone. That makes her a sole trader.

Starting was easy. No forms at a government office for the business itself, just her oven, her tins and a few bags of flour. She decides everything: what to bake, what to charge, when to work. And every rand of profit is hers.

But here is the catch. In law, she and the business are the same person. So if the business owes the flour supplier money it cannot pay, the supplier can come after her own savings, her furniture, even her car. That is called unlimited liability. No limit on what she could lose.

And if she gets sick, nobody bakes. If she stops, the business stops. That is what we mean by no continuity.

So a sole trader is quick, simple and free, but it rests entirely on one person's shoulders.

## Subtopic: Two Bakers, One Shop

Now her friend, a trained pastry chef with fifty thousand rand saved, says: let's open a proper shop together. If they agree, they become a partnership.

Two people means more money, more skills and more hands. The chef bakes the fancy pastries; the baker brings her loyal customers and runs the social media. One can take a holiday while the other keeps the shop open.

But they must share the profit. If the shop makes one hundred and sixty thousand rand and they agree to share it equally, each gets eighty thousand. If they agree to share three to five, the baker gets sixty thousand and the chef gets one hundred thousand.

And the risk is shared too, in an uncomfortable way. A partnership still has unlimited liability. If the business cannot pay its debts, a creditor can ask either partner for the whole amount. So if one partner orders an expensive oven the shop cannot afford, the other could end up paying.

That is why partners should write down a partnership agreement before they start: who puts in what, how profits are shared, who does which job, and what happens if one wants to leave. And remember, if a partner leaves or dies, the partnership ends and a new agreement is needed.

Here is the quick comparison to carry with you. Sole trader: one owner, keeps all the profit, carries all the risk. Partnership: two or more owners, share the profit, share the risk. Both are easy to start. Both have unlimited liability. Neither is a separate person in law. And neither carries on automatically when an owner leaves.

Think about the businesses on your own street. The hairdresser who works alone is a sole trader. The two brothers who run the panel-beating workshop together may well be partners. The doctors who share a practice in the shopping centre are often a partnership too. Once you know the questions to ask, how many owners, who carries the debts, what happens if one leaves, you can name the form of ownership almost anywhere you look.

Now try the practice questions on the baker and her friend: one owner or many, who keeps the profit, and who pays when things go wrong.
