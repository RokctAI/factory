# Part 1 — Expert

This session brings the four forms of ownership together and compares them point by point: the sole trader, the partnership, the private company and the public company. The anchor is a business advice desk at a township enterprise hub, where four very different people arrive on the same morning. A young man wants to start a car wash on his own. Three qualified electricians want to go into business together. A family that has run a successful furniture factory for twenty years wants to protect its members' homes and plan for the next generation. And a large grocery chain wants to raise two billion rand to build stores across Southern Africa. Each needs a different form of ownership. The governing idea is that the four forms differ in a consistent set of characteristics, namely the number of owners, legal personality, liability, capital, management, profit sharing, continuity, formation and regulation, and that the right choice depends on the size, risk and needs of the business. Comparing the four forms, and choosing and justifying a suitable form for a given business, is examinable and is the basis of the Term 3 project; it leads into Grade 10 Business Studies.

## Subtopic: The Criteria for Comparison

To compare forms of ownership fairly, we use the same set of questions for each one. These criteria appear again and again in tests and projects, so it is worth learning them as a list.

Number of owners: how many people may own the business. Formation: what must be done to start it, and how much that costs. Legal personality: whether the business is a separate legal person. Liability: whether the owners have limited or unlimited liability for its debts. Capital: how the business raises money, and how much it can realistically raise. Management: who makes the decisions and runs the business. Profit sharing: who receives the profit and how. Continuity: whether the business continues when an owner leaves or dies. Regulation and disclosure: which rules the business must follow and what information it must make public. Taxation: whether the profit is taxed in the hands of the owners or of the business.

Using a table with the four forms across the top and these criteria down the side is the clearest way to compare them. In an examination, a well-organised table earns marks quickly, but each entry should be a full, accurate statement, not a single word.

The questions for this section are with you now: the criteria used to compare forms of ownership.

## Subtopic: Owners, Legal Personality and Liability

The first group of criteria concerns who owns the business and who carries its risks.

A sole trader has one owner. A partnership has two or more partners. A private company has one or more shareholders, and a public company usually has many shareholders, often thousands, because anyone may buy its shares.

Sole traders and partnerships have no legal personality: in law, the business and its owners are the same. Private and public companies are separate legal persons, created by incorporation with CIPC.

That difference drives liability. The owners of sole traders and partnerships have unlimited liability: creditors can claim the owners' personal possessions if the business cannot pay, and partners are jointly and severally liable. Shareholders in private and public companies have limited liability: they can lose only what they paid for their shares, although directors who give personal guarantees or trade recklessly can be held personally liable.

For the car wash owner with little debt, unlimited liability is a small risk. For the furniture family, whose factory owes suppliers and banks millions of rand, it is a major risk to every member's home.

The questions for this section are with you now: the number of owners, legal personality and liability of each form.

## Subtopic: Capital, Management and Profit

The second group concerns money, control and reward.

Capital. A sole trader relies on the owner's savings and whatever the owner can borrow, so capital is the most limited. A partnership pools the capital of several partners and can borrow more, but is still limited. A private company can raise capital from its shareholders, bring in new private shareholders and borrow more easily because lenders find it more credible, but it cannot offer shares to the public. A public company can raise the largest amounts by selling shares to the public and listing on a stock exchange.

Management. The sole trader manages the business alone. Partners usually share management, each with a say according to the agreement. A private company is managed by its directors, who in small companies are often the shareholders themselves. A public company is managed by a board of at least three directors, who appoint professional managers, so ownership and control are separated.

Profit. The sole trader keeps all the profit. Partners share profit according to their agreement. Company shareholders receive dividends declared by the directors, in proportion to the shares they own, and the company retains the rest. Profit in a sole trader or partnership is taxed as the owners' personal income; a company pays company income tax of twenty-seven percent, and dividends paid to individuals are subject to dividends tax.

The questions for this section are with you now: how each form raises capital, who manages it and how profit is shared and taxed.

## Subtopic: Continuity, Formation and Regulation

The third group concerns starting, running and ending the business.

Formation. A sole trader and a partnership are easy and cheap to start: no registration under the Companies Act, only tax registration and any licence the activity needs, and in a partnership a written agreement. A private company must be registered with CIPC with a Memorandum of Incorporation, which costs more and takes longer. A public company is the most complex and expensive to form, especially if it issues a prospectus and lists on the JSE.

Continuity. A sole trader ends when the owner dies or stops trading. A partnership ends when a partner leaves, dies or joins, and a new agreement must be made. Both private and public companies have continuity: they continue regardless of changes in shareholders.

Regulation and disclosure. Sole traders and partnerships face the fewest rules and keep their affairs private. A private company must keep records, file annual returns with CIPC, and may need an audit or independent review depending on its size. A public company faces the strictest rules: an annual audit, an audit committee, a company secretary, an annual general meeting and, if listed, the JSE's listing requirements and public reporting.

There is a pattern across the four forms. Moving from the sole trader to the public company, the business gains limited liability, continuity and access to capital, but loses simplicity, privacy and the owner's direct control, and its costs and legal duties increase.

The questions for this section are with you now: formation, continuity and regulation of each form, and the overall pattern.

## Subtopic: Choosing and Justifying a Form

Choosing a form of ownership means matching the characteristics of the form to the needs of the business, and justifying the choice with those characteristics.

The young man starting a car wash on his own needs little capital, wants full control and has little debt. A sole trader suits him: it is cheap and quick to start, he keeps all the profit, and his unlimited liability risk is small. If the business grows and takes on debt, he can later register a private company.

The three electricians each bring skills and some savings, and want to share the work and decisions. A partnership suits them, with a carefully written agreement, because it combines their capital and skills cheaply. But electrical work carries risks of damage claims, so they might instead form a private company, or a personal liability company if their professional body requires it, to protect their personal assets. Either choice can be justified if the reasons are given.

The furniture family needs limited liability to protect their homes, continuity so the business passes to the next generation, and control over who becomes an owner. A private company suits them: the MOI can restrict share transfers to family members, and the company continues when the founders retire.

The grocery chain needs two billion rand, far more than any family or bank would provide alone. A public company suits it: it can issue shares to the public, list on the JSE and attract institutional investors such as pension funds, accepting strict regulation and public disclosure as the price.

In every case, a good justification names the business's needs and links each one to a specific characteristic of the chosen form.

## Subtopic: Comparing Advantages and Disadvantages

Examinations often ask learners to compare the forms through their advantages and disadvantages rather than through a table of characteristics. The two approaches are linked: every advantage or disadvantage comes from a characteristic, and naming that characteristic makes an answer stronger.

The sole trader's main advantages are simplicity, low cost, full control, quick decisions, keeping all the profit and privacy. Its main disadvantages are unlimited liability, limited capital, dependence on one person's skills and health, and no continuity.

The partnership's main advantages are more capital than a sole trader, a combination of skills, shared workload and risk, and easy formation. Its main disadvantages are unlimited and joint and several liability, possible conflict between partners, shared profit and no continuity.

The private company's main advantages are limited liability, continuity, greater credibility with lenders and customers, the ability to raise capital from more shareholders, and control over who may become an owner. Its main disadvantages are the cost and formality of registration, annual returns and record-keeping, possible audit or review requirements, and capital still limited by the ban on public share offers.

The public company's main advantages are access to very large amounts of capital, limited liability, continuity, freely transferable shares and professional management. Its main disadvantages are high costs, strict regulation, public disclosure of results, slower decisions, the separation of ownership from control and the risk that founders lose control.

Notice that the same feature can be an advantage for one business and a disadvantage for another. Privacy suits a family business that does not want competitors to see its results, but it may make banks less willing to lend. Strict regulation is a cost for the company, but it gives investors confidence and makes it easier to raise capital. That is why the best answers always connect the advantages and disadvantages to a particular business in a particular situation.

The questions for this section are with you now: the main advantages and disadvantages of each form, and how one feature can help one business and hurt another.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Four forms, one table, and a simple way to remember which is which.

## Subtopic: The Big Table in Plain Words

Line them up from small to big: sole trader, partnership, private company, public company.

Owners: one; two or more; one or more shareholders; usually many shareholders.

Separate person in law? No, no, yes, yes.

Liability: unlimited, unlimited, limited, limited. So for the first two, your house can be at risk. For the companies, you can lose only what you put in.

Money: the sole trader has the least. Partners pool their money. A private company can bring in shareholders, but not from the public. A public company can sell shares to anyone, so it can raise the most.

Who runs it: the owner; the partners; the directors; a board of directors with professional managers.

Does it carry on if an owner dies? No, no, yes, yes.

Easy to start? Very easy, easy, harder, hardest.

Here is the pattern to remember. As you move from the sole trader to the public company, you gain protection, money and continuity, but you lose simplicity, privacy and direct control, and you gain rules and costs.

Here is a memory trick. Think of the four forms as four ways to travel. The sole trader is a bicycle: cheap, you steer it yourself, but if you crash, you get hurt. The partnership is a tandem bicycle: two people pedalling, more power, but if your partner steers into a ditch, you both fall. The private company is a family car with seatbelts and airbags: it costs more to buy and to licence, but it protects the people inside, and it can be passed on to the children. The public company is a passenger aeroplane: it carries thousands, needs huge investment and strict safety inspections, and the passengers do not fly it themselves; professional pilots do.

## Subtopic: Four Visitors, Four Answers

Four people visit a business advice desk in the same morning.

A young man wants to start a car wash alone. Small, cheap, little debt. Best fit: sole trader. Easy to start, and he keeps all the profit.

Three electricians want to work together. They want to pool their tools, savings and skills. Best fit: partnership, with a written agreement. Or, because a wiring mistake could lead to big claims, a private company to protect their homes. Either answer can be right, if you explain why.

A family furniture factory, twenty years old, owes suppliers and the bank a lot of money and wants to pass the business to the children. Best fit: private company. Limited liability protects their homes, continuity lets it pass to the next generation, and they can keep shares inside the family.

A grocery chain needs two billion rand to build new stores. No family has that much. Best fit: public company, selling shares to the public on the JSE.

So when a question asks you to choose a form of ownership, do two things. Choose the form. Then give reasons using its characteristics: liability, capital, control, continuity, cost. The reasons earn the marks.

A good way to practise is to use businesses you already know. The spaza shop on the corner, the panel beater with two brothers, the family transport company, the big supermarket at the mall: for each one, ask how many owners it has, whether their homes are at risk, where the money comes from and what would happen if an owner died. Your answers will point you straight to the right form of ownership.

Now try the practice questions: compare the forms and match each business to the right one.
