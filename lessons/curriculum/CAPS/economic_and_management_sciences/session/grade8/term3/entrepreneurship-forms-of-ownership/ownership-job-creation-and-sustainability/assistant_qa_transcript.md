### subtopic_1 — Why Job Creation Matters
**Assistant:** Why do small businesses create so many jobs if each one employs only a few people?

**Tutor:** Because there are so many of them, they are cheap to start, and they tend to use more labour for each rand of capital than large firms. A single spaza shop or tyre stall may employ only two or three people, but South Africa has hundreds of thousands of small businesses, formal and informal, so together they employ millions. Starting one requires little capital and few legal formalities, so people who cannot find a job can create one for themselves and often for a few others. Small businesses also tend to be labour-intensive. The tyre repair stall uses a compressor and hand tools, so most of its output comes from human effort; a large factory may spend millions on automated machinery that does the work of many people. Each rand invested in a small business therefore tends to create more jobs, although those jobs are often less productive and less well paid. Small businesses also operate in places large firms avoid, such as villages and townships, and they grow: a successful sole trader may become a partnership or a private company employing dozens of people. That is why the National Development Plan expected about ninety percent of new jobs to come from small and expanding firms. The weakness is that many small businesses fail within a few years, and many informal jobs offer low pay, no benefits and no security. Supporting small businesses with finance, training, reliable infrastructure and access to markets is therefore one of the most important ways to turn their potential into lasting jobs.

### subtopic_2 — Small Businesses and Jobs
**Assistant:** Why might a public company look after the environment better than a small business?

**Tutor:** Because it often has more money, more expertise and more people watching it, although its impact is also much larger. A large listed company can afford to employ environmental scientists, install water treatment plants and invest in cleaner technology. It can spread these costs over a large output, so the cost per unit is small. It is also under constant scrutiny. As a public company, it must publish its results, and listed companies are expected to report on their environmental and social performance. Investors, especially large pension funds, increasingly refuse to invest in companies with poor environmental records, because pollution brings fines, lawsuits, clean-up costs and damaged reputations. Communities, journalists and non-governmental organisations monitor large mines and factories and can take them to court. Regulators also focus on large operations, which need environmental authorisations and water-use licences. A small business, such as a tyre repair stall, usually has none of these pressures and little money or knowledge to invest in better practices, so it may burn old tyres or dump waste without thinking about the harm. However, it would be wrong to conclude that large companies are always better for the environment. Their impact is far larger: one mine can affect a whole catchment area for decades, and history includes many large companies that polluted rivers and left unrehabilitated land and abandoned mines that are still dangerous. The fair conclusion is that larger businesses have greater capacity to protect the environment and greater responsibility to do so, while small businesses need education and support to improve.

### subtopic_3 — Large Companies and Jobs
**Assistant:** Can a business be both profitable and sustainable?

**Tutor:** Yes, and in the long run the most successful businesses usually are, because sustainable practices often reduce costs and protect the resources the business depends on. The tomato-farming brothers are a clear example. Drip irrigation costs money to install, but it uses far less water than flooding the fields, which lowers pumping costs and protects the river they depend on. Rotating crops and using fertiliser carefully keep the soil productive, so they can farm profitably for decades instead of exhausting the land in a few years. The brick factory can save money by using less coal in more efficient kilns, and the carbon tax makes that saving larger. Reducing waste means buying fewer raw materials. Treating workers fairly reduces staff turnover and training costs, and raises productivity. Many customers and investors also prefer sustainable businesses, which can bring higher sales and cheaper capital. That said, sustainability sometimes does reduce profit in the short term: cleaner equipment, land rehabilitation and fair wages cost money now, while the benefits come later or go to other people, such as communities downstream. That is why laws and taxes are needed to stop businesses from simply passing environmental costs on to society, and why the triple bottom line asks businesses to measure their success by their effect on people and the planet as well as profit. A business that makes large profits by destroying the resources and communities around it is not truly successful, because it is using up the conditions it needs to survive.

### subtopic_4 — Forms of Ownership and Natural Resources
**Assistant:** What happens to a town when a large mine closes?

**Tutor:** The town can suffer severe and long-lasting harm unless the closure is planned years in advance. A mine that employs three thousand people supports far more jobs indirectly: suppliers of equipment and services, transport operators, caterers, and the shops, schools and clinics where workers spend their wages. When it closes, thousands of workers lose their income at once. They spend less, so local businesses lose customers and also retrench workers or close. Many workers came from other provinces or countries as migrant labourers, and their families back home, who depended on the money they sent, also suffer. The municipality collects less in rates and service charges, so it struggles to maintain roads, water and electricity. Young people leave to look for work elsewhere, and the town may decline into a so-called ghost town. There are environmental legacies too: open pits, waste dumps and polluted water that can harm health and farming for decades if the land is not rehabilitated. South African law tries to reduce this damage. Mining companies must set aside money for rehabilitation before they mine and must restore the land when mining ends. Their social and labour plans must include skills training that workers can use elsewhere and local economic development projects, such as support for small businesses, agriculture and infrastructure, that can continue after the mine. The best approach is for the town to diversify while the mine is still operating, building other industries such as farming, tourism and manufacturing, so that it does not depend on a single employer.
