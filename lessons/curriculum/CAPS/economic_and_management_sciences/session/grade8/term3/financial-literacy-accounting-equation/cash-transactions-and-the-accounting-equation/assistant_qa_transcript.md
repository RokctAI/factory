### subtopic_1 — The Equation and Its Rules
**Assistant:** Why does paying an expense reduce owner's equity?

**Tutor:** Because an expense reduces profit, and profit belongs to the owner. Owner's equity is the owner's claim on the business: what would be left for the owner if all the assets were sold and all the liabilities paid. It starts with the capital the owner contributed and grows when the business makes profit, because the profit belongs to the owner, not to anyone else. Profit is income minus expenses. When the business earns income, profit rises and so does owner's equity. When it incurs an expense, profit falls and so does owner's equity. In the repair business, paying wages of three thousand rand means the business has three thousand rand less in the bank and nothing lasting to show for it: the workers' time has been used up in producing repairs. So the business's net worth, and therefore the owner's claim, is three thousand rand smaller. In the equation, assets decrease because bank decreases, and owner's equity decreases because an expense was incurred. Liabilities are not affected, because the business does not owe anyone more or less. The same logic applies to every expense, from rent to bank charges. In Grade 8 we show the effect of each expense directly on owner's equity, which is a shortcut: strictly, expenses are first collected in expense accounts, and only at the end of the year is the net profit transferred to owner's equity. The shortcut is useful because it shows immediately how each transaction affects the owner. It also explains why owners watch expenses closely: every rand of unnecessary expense is a rand less of their own wealth.

### subtopic_2 — Payments That Decrease Owner's Equity
**Assistant:** If buying equipment does not change owner's equity, why does the owner care about the cost?

**Tutor:** Because although the purchase itself does not change the owner's equity, the way the asset is used and the cash it ties up affect the business deeply. When the repair business pays five thousand two hundred rand for a diagnostic tool, it exchanges one asset, money in the bank, for another, equipment. On the day of purchase the business is no richer or poorer, so the equation shows equipment up and bank down by the same amount, with no effect on owner's equity. But the owner still cares, for several reasons. First, cash is gone. The business now has five thousand two hundred rand less available to pay wages, rent and suppliers, and if it runs short of cash it may struggle to pay its bills even though its total assets are unchanged. Second, equipment loses value over time as it is used and becomes outdated. In later grades you will record this as depreciation, an expense that gradually reduces profit and owner's equity over the life of the asset. Third, the purchase is only worthwhile if the tool helps the business earn more. If the diagnostic tool lets the technician find faults faster and complete more repairs each week, it will increase current income and owner's equity in future months. If it sits unused, it will lose value and earn nothing. So buying assets is an investment decision, and the accounting equation shows only the immediate effect. Owners compare the cost of an asset with the extra income it will help generate, and consider whether the business can afford to give up the cash.

### subtopic_3 — Payments That Change Other Assets or Liabilities
**Assistant:** Why is a month's profit different from the increase in the bank balance?

**Tutor:** Because the bank balance changes with every receipt and payment, while profit includes only income and expenses. In April the repair business's bank balance rose by six thousand and five rand, but its profit was three thousand seven hundred and five rand. The difference comes from transactions that affect the bank but are not income or expenses. On the receipts side, the owner contributed ten thousand rand of additional capital. That money increased the bank balance, but it was not earned; it was the owner's own investment, so it is not income and does not form part of profit. A loan received would be similar: more money in the bank, but a liability, not income. On the payments side, the owner's drawings of two thousand five hundred rand reduced the bank balance but are not an expense. The purchase of equipment for five thousand two hundred rand also reduced the bank balance, but equipment is an asset, not an expense. A loan repayment would also reduce the bank without being an expense. To reconcile the two figures: start with profit of three thousand seven hundred and five, add capital of ten thousand, which increased bank without being income, and subtract drawings of two thousand five hundred and equipment of five thousand two hundred, which decreased bank without being expenses. Three thousand seven hundred and five plus ten thousand minus two thousand five hundred minus five thousand two hundred equals six thousand and five rand, the change in bank. In later grades this kind of reconciliation becomes the cash flow statement, which explains why profit and cash differ.

### subtopic_4 — Analysing the April Payments
**Assistant:** How should I show an exchange of assets in an accounting equation table?

**Tutor:** Show both changes in the assets column, so the marker can see that one asset increased and another decreased by the same amount, and name both accounts in the reason. In a typical Grade 8 table, each transaction has a row with columns for assets, owner's equity and liabilities, and sometimes for the reason or the accounts affected. For the purchase of equipment for five thousand two hundred rand, you would write plus five thousand two hundred and minus five thousand two hundred in the assets column, often on two lines or as plus and minus in the same cell, with the reason that equipment increased and bank decreased. The owner's equity and liabilities columns show no effect, often written as zero or a dash. Some teachers accept a net effect of zero in the assets column, but showing both effects is safer, because it demonstrates that you understand what happened; a single zero could also mean you thought nothing happened at all. The same approach applies to other exchanges, such as receiving payment from a debtor, where bank increases and debtors decrease. For transactions that affect two sides, such as paying an expense, show minus in the assets column and minus in the owner's equity column, and give the reason for the owner's equity effect, for example expense incurred. For a loan repayment, show minus in assets and minus in liabilities, with the reason that the loan decreased. A quick check after every row: add the left side and the right side separately; they must change by the same total. If they do not, look for a missing or wrong sign.
