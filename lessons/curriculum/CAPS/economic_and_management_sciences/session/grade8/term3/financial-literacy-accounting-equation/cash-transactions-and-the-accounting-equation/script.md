# Part 1 — Expert

This session completes the accounting equation for cash transactions. In Term 2 you analysed money received; now you add money paid out, so that every transaction in the cash receipts journal and the cash payments journal can be shown in the equation. The anchor is April at the appliance repair business: the receipts you entered in the April CRJ and the payments you entered in the April CPJ. Paying wages, buying a diagnostic tool, repaying part of a loan and the owner's drawings each affect assets, owner's equity and liabilities in a different way, yet after every one of them the equation still balances. The governing idea is that the accounting equation, assets equal owner's equity plus liabilities, must balance after every transaction; that cash payments always decrease the asset bank; and that the other side of each payment decides whether owner's equity decreases, a liability decreases, or another asset increases. Analysing the effect of cash receipts and payments on the accounting equation, giving reasons, and summarising a month's effect are examinable, and the skill is used throughout Grade 9 to Grade 12 Accounting.

## Subtopic: The Equation and Its Rules

The accounting equation is assets equal owner's equity plus liabilities, often written A equals OE plus L.

Assets are the possessions and amounts owed to the business that it controls and that will bring future benefits: bank, equipment, vehicles, land and buildings, and debtors. Owner's equity is the owner's interest in the business: the capital the owner contributed, plus profits, less losses and drawings. Liabilities are the debts the business owes to outsiders, such as loans and creditors.

The equation always balances because every asset has been financed either by the owner or by outsiders. Each transaction has at least two effects, and those effects keep the equation in balance. The possible patterns are these. Both sides increase by the same amount. Both sides decrease by the same amount. One asset increases and another asset decreases by the same amount, so the total is unchanged. Or, more rarely, one item on the right increases while another on the right decreases.

Four rules about owner's equity must be remembered. Capital contributed increases owner's equity. Income, such as current income, rent income and interest income, increases owner's equity, because it increases profit. Expenses decrease owner's equity, because they decrease profit. Drawings decrease owner's equity directly.

In Term 2 you saw that receipts increase the asset bank. When the owner contributes capital, assets and owner's equity increase. When the business earns income, assets and owner's equity increase. When it borrows, assets and liabilities increase.

## Subtopic: Payments That Decrease Owner's Equity

Most cash payments of a service business are expenses, and every expense paid has the same effect on the equation.

When the repair business pays wages of three thousand rand by EFT, the asset bank decreases by three thousand rand, because money has left the account. Owner's equity decreases by three thousand rand, because wages are an expense, and expenses reduce profit, which belongs to the owner. Liabilities are not affected. The equation balances: minus three thousand on the left and minus three thousand on the right.

The same pattern applies to every expense: repair parts, rent expense, stationery, water and electricity, insurance and bank charges. In each case, assets decrease and owner's equity decreases by the same amount.

Drawings follow the same pattern, but for a different reason. When the owner transfers two thousand five hundred rand to his personal account, bank decreases by two thousand five hundred rand, and owner's equity decreases by two thousand five hundred rand. Drawings are not an expense; they decrease owner's equity directly, because the owner is taking back part of his interest in the business.

When asked to give a reason for an effect on owner's equity, the reason must name the cause: owner's equity decreases because an expense was incurred, or because the owner made drawings.

The questions for this section are with you now: the effect of expenses and drawings on the equation, and the correct reasons.

## Subtopic: Payments That Change Other Assets or Liabilities

Some payments do not affect owner's equity at all.

Buying an asset for cash is an exchange of one asset for another. When the repair business pays five thousand two hundred rand for a diagnostic tool, the asset equipment increases by five thousand two hundred rand and the asset bank decreases by five thousand two hundred rand. Total assets do not change, owner's equity does not change and liabilities do not change. The equation still balances, because nothing changed in total. The tool is not an expense, because it will be used for several years; it is not consumed in the month it is bought.

Repaying a loan decreases a liability. Suppose the business repays one thousand five hundred rand of its bank loan, ignoring interest. The asset bank decreases by one thousand five hundred rand, and the liability loan decreases by one thousand five hundred rand. Owner's equity is not affected, because repaying a debt is neither income nor an expense. If the payment included interest on the loan, the interest portion would be an expense and would decrease owner's equity, while only the capital portion would reduce the loan.

These two patterns show why it is important to identify what each payment was for. The bank always decreases on a payment; the question is which other item changes.

So the CPJ contains three kinds of payment: expenses and drawings, which reduce owner's equity; purchases of assets, which swap one asset for another; and loan repayments, which reduce liabilities.

The questions for this section are with you now: buying assets for cash and repaying loans in the equation.

## Subtopic: Analysing the April Payments

The April CPJ can be analysed transaction by transaction in a table with the columns: transaction, assets, owner's equity, liabilities, and reason.

Repair parts, two thousand three hundred and forty rand: assets minus two thousand three hundred and forty, bank; owner's equity minus two thousand three hundred and forty, expense; liabilities no effect.

Rent of the workshop, four thousand five hundred rand: assets minus four thousand five hundred; owner's equity minus four thousand five hundred, expense.

Wages, three thousand rand, paid twice: each time, assets minus three thousand and owner's equity minus three thousand, expense.

Stationery, six hundred and eighty rand: assets and owner's equity minus six hundred and eighty, expense.

Repair parts, one thousand eight hundred and sixty rand: assets and owner's equity minus one thousand eight hundred and sixty, expense.

Drawings, two thousand five hundred rand: assets minus two thousand five hundred; owner's equity minus two thousand five hundred, drawings.

Water and electricity, one thousand three hundred and eighty rand: assets and owner's equity minus one thousand three hundred and eighty, expense.

Equipment, five thousand two hundred rand: assets plus five thousand two hundred, equipment, and minus five thousand two hundred, bank; no effect on owner's equity or liabilities.

Bank charges, two hundred and forty-five rand, and insurance, six hundred and twenty rand: assets and owner's equity decrease by each amount, expense.

In the assets column, an exchange of assets is often shown as plus and minus the same amount, or written as a net zero, with both accounts named in the reason. Markers usually expect both effects to be shown.

## Subtopic: The Whole Month in the Equation

Combining the April CRJ and CPJ shows the month's total effect on the equation.

Receipts: current income of eighteen thousand six hundred and fifty rand, capital of ten thousand rand, rent income of two thousand five hundred rand and interest income of one hundred and eighty rand, a total of thirty-one thousand three hundred and thirty rand. All increased bank and owner's equity.

Payments: expenses of seventeen thousand six hundred and twenty-five rand, being wages six thousand, repair parts four thousand two hundred, rent four thousand five hundred, stationery six hundred and eighty, water and electricity one thousand three hundred and eighty, bank charges two hundred and forty-five and insurance six hundred and twenty; drawings of two thousand five hundred rand; and equipment of five thousand two hundred rand. The total paid was twenty-five thousand three hundred and twenty-five rand.

Effect on assets: bank increased by thirty-one thousand three hundred and thirty minus twenty-five thousand three hundred and twenty-five, which is six thousand and five rand, and equipment increased by five thousand two hundred rand, a total increase of eleven thousand two hundred and five rand.

Effect on owner's equity: plus ten thousand capital, plus twenty-one thousand three hundred and thirty income, minus seventeen thousand six hundred and twenty-five expenses, minus two thousand five hundred drawings, a total increase of eleven thousand two hundred and five rand.

Liabilities did not change in April. So assets increased by eleven thousand two hundred and five rand and owner's equity plus liabilities increased by the same amount: the equation balances.

The profit for April is income minus expenses: twenty-one thousand three hundred and thirty minus seventeen thousand six hundred and twenty-five, which is three thousand seven hundred and five rand. Notice that the profit is not the same as the increase in the bank balance, because capital is not income, and drawings and equipment are not expenses.

The questions for this section are with you now: the month's total effect on assets and owner's equity, and the difference between profit and the change in bank.

## Subtopic: Common Errors and How to Avoid Them

Learners make a small number of predictable errors when analysing cash payments, and recognising them in advance prevents most lost marks.

The first error is treating drawings as an expense. The effect on the equation is the same, assets and owner's equity both decrease, but the reason is different. Writing expense as the reason for drawings is marked wrong. Drawings decrease owner's equity because the owner took money for private use.

The second error is treating the purchase of an asset as an expense. Learners sometimes write that buying equipment decreases owner's equity. It does not; it is an exchange of assets. Ask whether the item will be used up this month or used for years. If it will last, it is an asset.

The third error is forgetting that repaying a loan affects liabilities, not owner's equity. Paying a debt does not make the owner poorer; it reduces what the business owes.

The fourth error is getting the direction wrong for receipts and payments together. A useful habit is to begin every analysis with the bank: a receipt means bank increases, a payment means bank decreases. Then find the second effect.

The fifth error is ignoring the balance check. After every row, the change on the left must equal the change on the right. If assets decrease by six hundred and eighty rand, either another asset increases by six hundred and eighty, or owner's equity or liabilities decrease by six hundred and eighty.

The questions for this section are with you now: the common errors and the checks that prevent them.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The equation is a balance scale. Every payment takes something off one side, and something must come off, or go on, somewhere else.

## Subtopic: Three Kinds of Money Out

Think of the equation as a scale. On the left: assets, everything the business has. On the right: owner's equity, what belongs to the owner, plus liabilities, what is owed to outsiders. The scale must always balance.

Every payment takes money out of the bank, so assets go down on the left. Then ask: what else changed?

Kind one: an expense or drawings. The shop pays wages of three thousand rand. Bank down three thousand. The owner is poorer by three thousand, because expenses reduce profit. So owner's equity down three thousand. Both sides down, still balanced. Drawings work the same way: the owner takes money out, so his share goes down.

Kind two: buying an asset. The shop pays five thousand two hundred rand for a diagnostic tool. Bank down five thousand two hundred, equipment up five thousand two hundred. Both changes are on the left, so the left side's total does not move. Owner's equity does not change. A swap, not a loss.

Kind three: repaying a loan. The shop pays one thousand five hundred rand back to the bank. Bank down on the left, loan down on the right. Both sides down, balanced, and the owner is no poorer, because paying a debt is not an expense.

So the bank always goes down. The question is always: which other item changes, and why?

## Subtopic: April on the Scale

Now put the whole of April on the scale.

Money in: thirty-one thousand three hundred and thirty rand. Money out: twenty-five thousand three hundred and twenty-five. So the bank went up by six thousand and five rand. Add the new tool, five thousand two hundred, and the shop's assets went up by eleven thousand two hundred and five rand.

Now the right side. The owner put in ten thousand capital. The shop earned twenty-one thousand three hundred and thirty in income. It spent seventeen thousand six hundred and twenty-five on expenses. The owner took out two thousand five hundred in drawings. Ten thousand, plus twenty-one thousand three hundred and thirty, minus seventeen thousand six hundred and twenty-five, minus two thousand five hundred: eleven thousand two hundred and five. Exactly the same. Balanced.

And the profit? Income minus expenses: twenty-one thousand three hundred and thirty minus seventeen thousand six hundred and twenty-five, which is three thousand seven hundred and five rand. That is less than the six thousand and five rand the bank grew by, because the owner's capital was not earned income, and the tool and the drawings were not expenses.

Here is a checklist to use with any transaction in a test. First, which asset changed, and did it go up or down? Second, did owner's equity change, and if so, was it because of capital, income, an expense or drawings? Third, did a liability change? Fourth, add up the effects on both sides and make sure they match. If they do not match, one of your answers is wrong, so go back and look for it before you move on.

Now try the practice questions: show each payment on the scale, give a reason, and check that it balances.
