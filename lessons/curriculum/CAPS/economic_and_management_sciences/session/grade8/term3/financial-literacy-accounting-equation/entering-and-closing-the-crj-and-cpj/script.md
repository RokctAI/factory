# Part 1 — Expert

This session brings the two cash journals together. Until now you have entered receipts in the cash receipts journal and payments in the cash payments journal as separate exercises. In a real business, the source documents arrive mixed together, day by day, and the bookkeeper must sort each one into the correct journal, enter it in the correct columns, close off both journals at month-end and then explain the month's effect on the accounting equation. The anchor is the first month of a garden service business owned by a sole trader: June, with capital from the owner, a lawnmower and other equipment, a bank loan, fees from customers, wages, fuel, advertising, drawings, a loan repayment and the bank's own charges and interest. The governing idea is that every cash transaction belongs in exactly one of the two journals, depending on whether money came into or went out of the bank; that each journal is closed off by totalling, cross-casting and ruling off; and that together the two journals explain the change in the bank balance and the effect of the month on assets, owner's equity and liabilities. Entering combined transactions, closing off both journals, calculating the bank balance and analysing the month in the equation are examinable, and they prepare you for posting to the general ledger in Term 4.

## Subtopic: Sorting Documents into the Two Journals

The first decision for every source document is which journal it belongs in, and the rule is simple: money into the bank goes in the CRJ, and money out of the bank goes in the CPJ.

Receipts are supported by duplicate receipts, cash register rolls, deposit slips and the bank statement for EFTs received and interest earned. Payments are supported by EFT payment confirmations, the original invoices or till slips from suppliers, and the bank statement for bank charges, debit orders and card payments.

The bank statement needs special care, because it contains both kinds of entry. Every line on the statement that increases the balance, such as an EFT from a customer or interest earned, belongs in the CRJ, marked B/S in the document column. Every line that decreases the balance, such as bank charges or a debit order, belongs in the CPJ, also marked B/S.

The word in the transaction can mislead. A loan received is entered in the CRJ, because money came in, even though the business will later pay it back. A loan repayment is entered in the CPJ, because money went out. Interest earned on the bank balance is a receipt; interest charged on a loan or overdraft is a payment.

The garden service uses these columns. The CRJ has document, day, details, analysis of receipts, bank, current income, and sundry accounts with amount, folio and details. The CPJ has document, day, name of payee, folio, bank, wages, fuel, and sundry accounts with amount, folio and details. Fuel has its own analysis column because the lawnmowers and the bakkie need fuel every week.

A useful habit is to sort the month's documents into two piles before writing anything, then to enter each pile in date order. That way the journals stay in date order and no document is entered twice.

The questions for this section are with you now: deciding which journal each source document belongs in, and why the bank statement feeds both journals.

## Subtopic: Entering the June Receipts

The garden service's June receipts are entered in the CRJ as follows.

First of June: receipt 001, the owner contributed capital of fifteen thousand rand. Document 001, day one, details the owner, bank fifteen thousand, sundry fifteen thousand with details capital.

Third of June: receipt 002 for one thousand two hundred rand from a customer for clearing a garden, and the cash register roll for eight hundred and fifty rand. Both were deposited together. Line one: document 002, details the customer, analysis of receipts one thousand two hundred, current income one thousand two hundred. Line two: document CRR, details cash sales, analysis of receipts eight hundred and fifty, current income eight hundred and fifty, and bank two thousand and fifty, the total deposited.

Twelfth of June: the bank statement showed a loan of ten thousand rand from a bank. Document B/S, details the bank, bank ten thousand, sundry ten thousand with details loan. A loan is not income, so it never goes in the current income column.

Fifteenth of June: the cash register roll showed two thousand three hundred rand. Document CRR, cash sales, bank and current income two thousand three hundred.

Twenty-sixth of June: the bank statement showed an EFT of one thousand six hundred and fifty rand from a customer for monthly garden maintenance. Document B/S, details the customer, bank and current income one thousand six hundred and fifty. This is current income, because it was earned from the business's services; the EFT is only the method of payment.

Thirtieth of June: the bank statement showed interest earned of forty rand. Document B/S, details the bank, bank forty, sundry forty with details interest income.

Notice the judgement calls. Capital and the loan both increase the bank, but neither was earned, so both go in sundry with different account names. Current income contains only fees for garden services. Interest is income, but not from the business's main activity, so it is entered under sundry with its own account name.

The questions for this section are with you now: entering each June receipt, the combined deposit on the third, and why capital and the loan are sundry receipts.

## Subtopic: Entering the June Payments

The garden service's June payments are entered in the CPJ as follows.

Second of June: EFT 01 to a garden equipment supplier for a lawnmower and a brush cutter, six thousand eight hundred rand. Bank six thousand eight hundred; sundry six thousand eight hundred, details equipment. Equipment is an asset, because it will be used for several years.

Fifth of June: EFT 02 to the employees for wages, one thousand eight hundred rand. Bank and wages one thousand eight hundred.

Eighth of June: EFT 03 to a filling station for fuel, six hundred and forty rand, supported by the till slip. Bank and fuel six hundred and forty.

Nineteenth of June: EFT 04, wages, one thousand eight hundred rand. Bank and wages one thousand eight hundred.

Twentieth of June: EFT 05, fuel, seven hundred and ten rand. Bank and fuel seven hundred and ten.

Twenty-second of June: EFT 06 to a local newspaper for advertising, four hundred and fifty rand. Bank four hundred and fifty; sundry, details advertising.

Twenty-fourth of June: EFT 07 to the owner for private use, one thousand five hundred rand. Bank one thousand five hundred; sundry, details drawings.

Twenty-eighth of June: EFT 08 to the bank, a repayment of one thousand rand on the loan, with no interest included. Bank one thousand; sundry, details loan.

Thirtieth of June, from the bank statement: bank charges of ninety-five rand. Document B/S, payee the bank, bank ninety-five; sundry, details bank charges.

Notice that the loan account appears in both journals: credited from the CRJ when the loan was received, and debited from the CPJ when part of it was repaid. Equipment and drawings appear in the CPJ because money left the bank, even though neither is an expense.

The questions for this section are with you now: entering each June payment, choosing between the wages, fuel and sundry columns, and why the loan appears in both journals.

## Subtopic: Closing Off Both Journals

At the end of June both journals are closed off, using the same three steps: total every money column, cross-cast, and rule off.

The CRJ. The current income column totals one thousand two hundred plus eight hundred and fifty plus two thousand three hundred plus one thousand six hundred and fifty, which is six thousand rand. The sundry column totals fifteen thousand plus ten thousand plus forty, which is twenty-five thousand and forty rand. The bank column totals fifteen thousand plus two thousand and fifty plus ten thousand plus two thousand three hundred plus one thousand six hundred and fifty plus forty, which is thirty-one thousand and forty rand. Cross-cast: six thousand plus twenty-five thousand and forty is thirty-one thousand and forty. It agrees.

The CPJ. The wages column totals three thousand six hundred rand. The fuel column totals six hundred and forty plus seven hundred and ten, which is one thousand three hundred and fifty rand. The sundry column totals six thousand eight hundred plus four hundred and fifty plus one thousand five hundred plus one thousand plus ninety-five, which is nine thousand eight hundred and forty-five rand. The bank column totals fourteen thousand seven hundred and ninety-five rand. Cross-cast: three thousand six hundred plus one thousand three hundred and fifty plus nine thousand eight hundred and forty-five is fourteen thousand seven hundred and ninety-five. It agrees.

Each journal is ruled off with a single line above the totals and a double line below, and the totals are dated the thirtieth of June.

The bank balance can now be calculated. The business started June with nothing in the bank, because it is new. Total receipts thirty-one thousand and forty minus total payments fourteen thousand seven hundred and ninety-five gives a favourable balance of sixteen thousand two hundred and forty-five rand at the end of June. In an established business, the opening balance is added first: opening balance plus receipts minus payments equals the closing balance. That figure should agree with the bank statement once every B/S item has been entered; if it does not, the difference must be investigated.

The questions for this section are with you now: totalling and cross-casting both journals, and calculating the bank balance at the end of the month.

## Subtopic: The Month in the Accounting Equation

The two closed journals together explain June's effect on the accounting equation.

Assets. Bank increased by sixteen thousand two hundred and forty-five rand. Equipment increased by six thousand eight hundred rand. Total assets increased by twenty-three thousand and forty-five rand.

Owner's equity. Capital added fifteen thousand rand. Income added six thousand and forty rand: current income of six thousand and interest income of forty. Expenses subtracted five thousand four hundred and ninety-five rand: wages three thousand six hundred, fuel one thousand three hundred and fifty, advertising four hundred and fifty and bank charges ninety-five. Drawings subtracted one thousand five hundred rand. The net increase in owner's equity is fourteen thousand and forty-five rand.

Liabilities. The loan received added ten thousand rand, and the repayment subtracted one thousand rand, a net increase of nine thousand rand.

Check: owner's equity plus liabilities increased by fourteen thousand and forty-five plus nine thousand, which is twenty-three thousand and forty-five rand, exactly the increase in assets. The equation balances.

The profit for June is income minus expenses: six thousand and forty minus five thousand four hundred and ninety-five, which is five hundred and forty-five rand. That is far smaller than the increase in the bank balance, because most of June's receipts were financing, capital and a loan, not income. A new business often looks rich in its first month for exactly this reason.

An exam question may ask for the analysis of a single line from either journal. Start with the bank: up for a CRJ line, down for a CPJ line. Then use the column or the sundry details to find the second effect and the reason.

The questions for this section are with you now: the month's total effect on assets, owner's equity and liabilities, and the June profit.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Two notebooks, one bank account. One notebook for money in, one for money out, and at the end of the month they must tell the same story as the bank.

## Subtopic: Two Notebooks, One Bank

Picture the garden service's desk at the end of June, with a pile of papers. Receipts, till rolls, EFT slips and a bank statement, all mixed up.

Ask one question of every paper: did money come in, or go out? In goes on the left pile, for the cash receipts journal. Out goes on the right pile, for the cash payments journal.

The bank statement is tricky because it has both. The customer's EFT and the interest earned go to the in pile. The bank charges go to the out pile.

Now the money-in notebook. Each line has the money in the bank lane, and then the same money again in a lane that says what it was. Fees for gardening go in current income. Everything else, like the owner's fifteen thousand capital, the ten thousand loan and the forty rand interest, goes in sundry with a name tag.

The money-out notebook works the same way. Bank lane first, then the lane that says what the money was for: wages, fuel, or sundry with a name tag, like equipment, advertising, drawings, loan or bank charges.

Notice the loan. It appears in both notebooks. In the money-in notebook when the bank lent ten thousand, and in the money-out notebook when the business paid one thousand back. Same account, opposite directions.

## Subtopic: Does June Balance?

At month-end, add up every lane.

Money-in notebook: current income six thousand, sundry twenty-five thousand and forty, bank thirty-one thousand and forty. Six thousand plus twenty-five thousand and forty is thirty-one thousand and forty. The lanes agree.

Money-out notebook: wages three thousand six hundred, fuel one thousand three hundred and fifty, sundry nine thousand eight hundred and forty-five, bank fourteen thousand seven hundred and ninety-five. They agree too.

So the bank: in thirty-one thousand and forty, out fourteen thousand seven hundred and ninety-five, left sixteen thousand two hundred and forty-five rand.

Now the scale. The business has sixteen thousand two hundred and forty-five more in the bank and six thousand eight hundred of new equipment: twenty-three thousand and forty-five more in assets. On the other side, the owner's share went up by fourteen thousand and forty-five, and the loan by nine thousand. Fourteen thousand and forty-five plus nine thousand: twenty-three thousand and forty-five. Balanced.

And the profit? Only five hundred and forty-five rand. Most of the money in the bank was the owner's savings and the bank's loan. The business is only starting to earn its own way.

Here is the checklist for any month. Sort the papers into in and out. Enter each in date order, in the bank lane and one other lane. Add every lane, check that the lanes agree with the bank lane, and rule off. Work out the bank balance. Then put the month on the scale.

Now try the practice questions: sort, enter, close off, and check that June balances.
