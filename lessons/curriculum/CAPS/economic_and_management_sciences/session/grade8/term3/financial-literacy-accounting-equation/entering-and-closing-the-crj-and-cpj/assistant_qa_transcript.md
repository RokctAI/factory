### subtopic_1 — Sorting Documents into the Two Journals
**Assistant:** How do I decide which journal a bank statement item goes in?

**Tutor:** Look at whether the item increased or decreased the balance on the statement, because that tells you whether money came into or went out of the business's account. A bank statement is written from the bank's point of view, and it usually shows each transaction with a date, a description and an amount in either a debit or credit column, or with a minus sign for money taken out. Learners are often confused because the bank's words seem back to front: the bank calls money paid into your account a credit, because from the bank's side it owes you more. So do not rely on the words debit and credit on a bank statement. Instead, ask what happened to the balance. If the balance went up, the business received money: an EFT from a customer, interest earned, or a loan paid into the account. Those go in the CRJ, with B/S in the document column. If the balance went down, the business paid money: bank charges, a debit order for insurance, a card payment, interest on an overdraft, or a loan instalment taken directly by the bank. Those go in the CPJ, also with B/S. In the garden service's June statement, the loan of ten thousand rand, the customer's EFT of one thousand six hundred and fifty rand and interest of forty rand increased the balance, so all three are CRJ entries, while the bank charges of ninety-five rand decreased it, so they are a CPJ entry. Then decide the column in the usual way, from what the money was for.

### subtopic_2 — Entering the June Receipts
**Assistant:** Why does the loan appear in both journals, and does that cancel it out?

**Tutor:** It appears in both because two different transactions happened, one in each direction, and it does not cancel out completely, because the amounts are different. On the twelfth of June the bank lent the garden service ten thousand rand. Money came into the business's account, so the transaction is a receipt and belongs in the CRJ, under sundry, with the details loan. In the ledger the loan account will be credited with ten thousand rand, which records the increase in the liability. On the twenty-eighth the business repaid one thousand rand. Money went out, so the transaction is a payment and belongs in the CPJ, under sundry, with the details loan. In the ledger the loan account will be debited with one thousand rand, which records the decrease in the liability. When the loan account is balanced at the end of June, the credit of ten thousand and the debit of one thousand leave a credit balance of nine thousand rand, the amount the business still owes. So the two entries partly offset each other, and that is exactly what should happen: the debt started at ten thousand and was reduced by one thousand. The same pattern happens with other accounts. Equipment could appear in the CPJ when it is bought and, much later, in the CRJ if the business sells an old item. What matters is not the account name but the direction of the money. Every time you see the same account in both journals, check that one entry increases it and the other decreases it.

### subtopic_3 — Entering the June Payments
**Assistant:** What should I do if the cross-cast of one journal does not agree?

**Tutor:** Treat it as a clue that something specific is wrong, and search for it systematically before you go any further, because an error in the totals will be carried into the ledger and the trial balance. The cross-cast checks that the bank column total equals the sum of all the other column totals. In the garden service's CPJ, wages of three thousand six hundred plus fuel of one thousand three hundred and fifty plus sundry of nine thousand eight hundred and forty-five must equal fourteen thousand seven hundred and ninety-five rand. If it does not, first calculate the difference. If the difference equals one transaction, such as six hundred and forty, look for a payment that was entered in the bank column but not in an analysis or sundry column, or the reverse. If the difference is double one amount, an amount may have been entered twice in one column. If the difference is divisible by nine, suspect a transposition, such as seven hundred and ten written as one hundred and seventy, a difference of five hundred and forty, which is nine times sixty. If no single transaction explains it, re-add each column, preferably in the opposite direction, because addition errors are common. In the CRJ, also check combined deposits: the bank column should show the deposit total only once, while each individual amount appears in the analysis of receipts and current income columns. Once the error is found, correct it neatly and re-total. Remember that an agreeing cross-cast does not prove the journal is correct: an amount entered in the wrong column, or left out entirely, still cross-casts, so each line must also be checked against its source document.

### subtopic_4 — Closing Off Both Journals
**Assistant:** Why is the June profit so much smaller than the money in the bank?

**Tutor:** Because most of the money that came into the bank in June was not earned. The garden service received thirty-one thousand and forty rand in June, but fifteen thousand was the owner's capital and ten thousand was a bank loan. Those twenty-five thousand rand were financing, money provided so that the business could buy equipment and operate while it found customers. Only six thousand and forty rand was income, made up of six thousand rand of current income from garden services and forty rand of interest. Expenses were five thousand four hundred and ninety-five rand, so the profit was five hundred and forty-five rand. On the payments side, two large items reduced the bank without being expenses: equipment of six thousand eight hundred rand, which is an asset that will be used for years, and drawings of one thousand five hundred rand, which are the owner taking money for private use. The loan repayment of one thousand rand reduced a liability, not profit. You can reconcile the two figures: start with the profit of five hundred and forty-five, add capital of fifteen thousand and the loan of ten thousand, and subtract equipment of six thousand eight hundred, drawings of one thousand five hundred and the loan repayment of one thousand. The result is sixteen thousand two hundred and forty-five rand, the bank balance. This is typical of a business's first month. The owner should not feel rich because the bank balance is healthy; the real question is whether income will grow enough in the coming months to cover the expenses, repay the loan and reward the owner for the risk.
