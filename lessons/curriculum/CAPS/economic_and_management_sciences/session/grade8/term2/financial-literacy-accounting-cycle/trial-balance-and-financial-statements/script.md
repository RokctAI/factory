# Part 1 — Expert

This session gives an overview of the second half of the accounting cycle: the trial balance, the income statement and the balance sheet. The anchor is the same appliance repair business at the end of March, with its ledger accounts posted and balanced: seventy-four thousand one hundred and eighty rand in the bank, equipment worth eighteen thousand rand, a loan of twenty-five thousand rand, and a month of repair income, rent income, wages and other expenses. From those balances the bookkeeper produces one page that checks the arithmetic, one statement that tells the owner whether the month was profitable, and one statement that shows what the business owns and owes. The governing idea is that the trial balance lists every ledger balance to test that total debits equal total credits, and that the financial statements summarise those balances into the two pictures every owner, banker and investor needs: performance, in the income statement, and financial position, in the balance sheet. The purpose and layout of the trial balance, the classification of balances as debit or credit, the structure of the two financial statements and the link between profit and owner's equity are examinable, and they are developed fully in Grade 9 and Grade 10 Accounting.

## Subtopic: The Trial Balance

A trial balance is a list of the balances of all the accounts in the general ledger on a particular date, with debit balances in one column and credit balances in the other. Its purpose is to check the arithmetical accuracy of the double entry: because every transaction was recorded with equal debits and credits, the total of all debit balances must equal the total of all credit balances.

The heading states the name of the business and the date, for example trial balance of the repair business on the thirty-first of March. The columns are the account name, the folio number, the debit amount and the credit amount. Balance sheet accounts are listed first, then nominal accounts.

Which accounts have debit balances? Use the rule from Term 1: assets, expenses and drawings increase on the debit side, so they normally have debit balances. Capital, liabilities and income increase on the credit side, so they normally have credit balances.

The repair business's trial balance at the end of March is as follows. Debit balances: bank seventy-four thousand one hundred and eighty rand, equipment eighteen thousand rand, drawings three thousand rand, wages six thousand rand, stationery seven hundred and fifty rand, water and electricity one thousand four hundred rand, and bank charges two hundred and twenty rand. Their total is one hundred and three thousand five hundred and fifty rand. Credit balances: capital sixty thousand rand, loan twenty-five thousand rand, current income fifteen thousand nine hundred rand, rent income two thousand five hundred rand and interest income one hundred and fifty rand. Their total is also one hundred and three thousand five hundred and fifty rand. The trial balance balances.

A trial balance that balances is evidence that the arithmetic of the double entry is correct, but it is not proof that the records are free of errors. Some errors leave the totals equal: a transaction left out completely, an amount recorded wrongly on both sides, or an amount posted to the wrong account on the correct side, such as wages posted to stationery. If the trial balance does not balance, there is definitely an error, perhaps an amount posted to one side only, posted twice, or a balance calculated incorrectly, and it must be found before the financial statements are prepared.

The questions for this section are with you now: the purpose of the trial balance, which accounts have debit and credit balances, totalling the March trial balance, and errors that do and do not affect its balance.

## Subtopic: The Income Statement

The income statement, also called the statement of comprehensive income, shows the financial performance of a business over a period: the income it earned, the expenses it incurred, and the resulting profit or loss. Its heading must state the period, for example for the month ended thirty-first March, because it describes activity over time.

It is prepared from the nominal accounts in the trial balance. First the income items are listed and totalled; then the expense items are listed and totalled; then total expenses are subtracted from total income.

For the repair business in March, income is current income fifteen thousand nine hundred rand, rent income two thousand five hundred rand and interest income one hundred and fifty rand, a total of eighteen thousand five hundred and fifty rand. Expenses are wages six thousand rand, stationery seven hundred and fifty rand, water and electricity one thousand four hundred rand and bank charges two hundred and twenty rand, a total of eight thousand three hundred and seventy rand. Net profit is eighteen thousand five hundred and fifty minus eight thousand three hundred and seventy, which is ten thousand one hundred and eighty rand.

Three items in the trial balance do not appear on the income statement, and knowing why is essential. Equipment is an asset, not an expense, so it goes to the balance sheet. Capital and loan are sources of finance, not income, so they also go to the balance sheet. Drawings are a withdrawal by the owner, not an expense, so they reduce owner's equity on the balance sheet.

The income statement answers the owner's first question: did the business make money? It also shows where the money came from and where it went, so the owner can see, for example, that wages are the largest expense or that rent income is a useful extra source. In Grade 10 the income statement will include the cost of sales for trading businesses and more detailed expense items.

The questions for this section are with you now: the purpose of the income statement, why its heading states a period, calculating March's profit, and which trial balance items do not belong on it.

## Subtopic: The Balance Sheet

The balance sheet, also called the statement of financial position, shows the financial position of a business at a particular date: what it owns, its assets, and how those assets are financed, by owner's equity and liabilities. Its heading states a single date, for example at thirty-first March, because it is a snapshot at one moment. It is the accounting equation, A equals OE plus L, written as a statement.

The assets section lists non-current assets first, such as equipment and vehicles, and then current assets, such as bank and cash. The equity and liabilities section lists owner's equity first, then non-current liabilities, such as a long-term loan, and then current liabilities, such as creditors.

Owner's equity on the balance sheet is calculated as capital plus net profit minus drawings. For the repair business at the end of March: capital sixty thousand rand plus net profit ten thousand one hundred and eighty rand minus drawings three thousand rand, which is sixty-seven thousand one hundred and eighty rand.

Now complete the statement. Assets: equipment eighteen thousand rand, a non-current asset, and bank seventy-four thousand one hundred and eighty rand, a current asset; total assets ninety-two thousand one hundred and eighty rand. Equity and liabilities: owner's equity sixty-seven thousand one hundred and eighty rand, and loan twenty-five thousand rand, a non-current liability; total ninety-two thousand one hundred and eighty rand. The two totals are equal, as the accounting equation requires.

The balance sheet answers different questions from the income statement. Can the business pay its debts? Here, the bank balance of seventy-four thousand rand easily covers the loan of twenty-five thousand rand. How much of the business is financed by the owner and how much by borrowing? Here, about seventy-three percent by owner's equity and twenty-seven percent by the loan. A bank considering a further loan would study exactly these figures.

The questions for this section are with you now: the purpose of the balance sheet, why its heading states a date, calculating owner's equity, completing the March balance sheet, and what it tells a bank.

## Subtopic: How the Statements Connect

The trial balance and the two financial statements are linked, and following the links shows that the whole cycle fits together.

All the trial balance accounts end up in one of the two statements. Nominal accounts, income and expenses, go to the income statement. Balance sheet accounts, assets, liabilities, capital and drawings, go to the balance sheet.

The net profit is the bridge between the two statements. It is calculated on the income statement and then carried to the balance sheet, where it is added to capital in owner's equity. That makes sense: profit belongs to the owner, so it increases the owner's claim on the assets. A net loss would be subtracted.

A useful check shows the connection. Total debits in the trial balance equal total credits. Remove the income statement items from both sides: debit expenses of eight thousand three hundred and seventy rand and credit income of eighteen thousand five hundred and fifty rand. The difference, ten thousand one hundred and eighty rand, is the profit. What remains on the debit side is assets plus drawings; on the credit side, capital plus liabilities. Moving drawings and profit into owner's equity turns the remaining trial balance into the balance sheet. The arithmetic must work, because both statements come from the same balanced set of accounts.

The statements also connect over time. The balance sheet at the end of one month is the starting position for the next month. The bank balance of seventy-four thousand one hundred and eighty rand becomes the opening balance in April, and owner's equity of sixty-seven thousand one hundred and eighty rand becomes the starting owner's equity.

Different users read the statements for different reasons. The owner wants to know about profit and whether to expand. A bank wants to know whether loans can be repaid. SARS uses the profit to assess income tax. A possible buyer or partner wants to know what the business is worth.

The questions for this section are with you now: which accounts go to which statement, net profit as the bridge, and who uses the financial statements and why.

## Subtopic: Common Errors in the Second Half of the Cycle

Exam questions often include deliberate errors, so it pays to know the most common ones.

In the trial balance: placing a balance in the wrong column, for example listing drawings as a credit or current income as a debit. Use the DEAD rule to check. Forgetting an account, especially bank charges or interest income recorded from the bank statement. Copying a balance incorrectly, especially transposing digits, such as writing seventy-four thousand eight hundred and ten instead of seventy-four thousand one hundred and eighty; a transposition error always creates a difference divisible by nine, a useful clue when hunting for errors.

In the income statement: including capital or a loan as income, including the purchase of equipment as an expense, including drawings as an expense, and leaving the period out of the heading.

In the balance sheet: forgetting to add the profit to capital, forgetting to subtract drawings, placing a loan under assets, and using a period instead of a date in the heading.

Here is a check routine. After the trial balance, confirm that debits equal credits. After the income statement, confirm that only income and expense items appear. After the balance sheet, confirm that total assets equal total owner's equity and liabilities, and that owner's equity equals capital plus profit minus drawings. If the balance sheet does not balance, work backwards: check the profit figure first, because an error in the income statement flows into owner's equity.

The questions for this section are with you now: errors in each statement, the transposition clue, and a routine for checking the second half of the cycle.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through one checklist, one report card and one photograph, plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: The Checklist

At the end of March the repair shop's ledger folders are full. Before anyone writes a report, the bookkeeper makes a checklist: one page, every folder's balance, folders with left-side balances in the left column and folders with right-side balances in the right column. That page is the trial balance.

Left column, the things the business owns, spends or the owner takes: bank seventy-four thousand one hundred and eighty, equipment eighteen thousand, drawings three thousand, wages six thousand, stationery seven hundred and fifty, water and electricity one thousand four hundred, bank charges two hundred and twenty. Add them up: one hundred and three thousand five hundred and fifty.

Right column, the owner's money, the money owed and the money earned: capital sixty thousand, loan twenty-five thousand, current income fifteen thousand nine hundred, rent income two thousand five hundred, interest one hundred and fifty. Add them up: one hundred and three thousand five hundred and fifty.

Same total. That means every rand that went into a folder on one side also went into a folder on the other side. The checklist passes.

But a passing checklist is like a soccer team with eleven players on the field: it is the right number, but it does not prove they are wearing the right jerseys. If wages were put in the stationery folder by mistake, both totals would still match. So a balanced trial balance means the adding is right, not that everything is right.

Keep the checklist in mind and try the questions for this part: left column or right column, and does balancing prove everything?

## Subtopic: The Report Card and the Photograph

From the checklist, the bookkeeper makes two reports.

The first is the report card for the month: the income statement. It answers one question: did the shop make money in March? Money earned: repairs fifteen thousand nine hundred, storeroom rent two thousand five hundred, interest one hundred and fifty, together eighteen thousand five hundred and fifty. Money used up to earn it: wages, stationery, water and electricity, bank charges, together eight thousand three hundred and seventy. Profit: ten thousand one hundred and eighty rand. A report card covers a period, so its heading says for the month ended thirty-first March.

The second is a photograph: the balance sheet. It shows what the shop looks like on one day, the thirty-first of March. On one side, what it owns: equipment eighteen thousand and bank seventy-four thousand one hundred and eighty, together ninety-two thousand one hundred and eighty. On the other side, who it all belongs to: the owner's share and the bank's loan. The owner's share is her capital sixty thousand, plus the profit ten thousand one hundred and eighty, minus the three thousand she took home: sixty-seven thousand one hundred and eighty. Add the loan of twenty-five thousand: ninety-two thousand one hundred and eighty. Both sides match, just like the scale from Term 1.

See how the profit jumped from the report card into the photograph? That is the bridge between them. A good month makes the owner's share bigger.

Keep the report card and the photograph in mind and try the questions for this part: which report, period or date, and where the profit goes.
