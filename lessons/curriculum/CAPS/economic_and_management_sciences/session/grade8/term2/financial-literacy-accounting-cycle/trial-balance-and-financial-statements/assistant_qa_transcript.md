### subtopic_1 — The Trial Balance
**Assistant:** If a trial balance balances, why might the records still be wrong?

**Tutor:** Because the trial balance only tests whether the debits and credits are equal, and several kinds of error keep them equal. The first is an error of omission: if a transaction is never recorded at all, for example a cash register roll for one day is lost and never entered, neither the debit nor the credit is posted, so the totals still agree, but income and bank are understated. The second is an error of commission: an amount is posted to the correct side but the wrong account of the same type, such as wages of six thousand rand posted to the stationery account. Both are expense accounts on the debit side, so the totals are unaffected, but the income statement shows the wrong breakdown. The third is an error of principle: a transaction is recorded in the wrong type of account, such as the purchase of equipment recorded as an expense. The trial balance still balances, but profit is understated and assets are understated. The fourth is an original entry error: the wrong amount is taken from the source document, for example one thousand eight hundred rand entered as one thousand two hundred rand, and posted on both sides, so both totals are wrong by the same amount. The fifth is a compensating error, where two separate mistakes cancel each other out. That is why accountants check entries against source documents, compare the bank account with the bank statement, and review the statements for figures that look unusual, instead of relying only on the trial balance.

### subtopic_2 — The Income Statement
**Assistant:** Why does the income statement cover a period but the balance sheet a single date?

**Tutor:** Because they measure different things: one measures a flow and the other a stock. Income and expenses are flows. They build up over time, like water running into a bucket. To say the repair business earned fifteen thousand nine hundred rand in current income only means something if you say over what period: a day, a month or a year. So the income statement heading must state the period, for the month ended thirty-first March or for the year ended twenty-eighth February. Assets, liabilities and owner's equity are stocks. They are the amount in the bucket at a particular moment. The bank balance is seventy-four thousand one hundred and eighty rand at the close of business on the thirty-first of March; on the first of April, after the first transaction, it will be different. So the balance sheet heading must state a date, at thirty-first March. A useful image is a video and a photograph. The income statement is a video of the business's activities over the month. The balance sheet is a photograph taken at the end of the video. The profit links them: the activity on the video changes what appears in the photograph, by increasing owner's equity. Getting the heading right is not just a formality; exam memoranda allocate marks for it, and a heading that says for the month on a balance sheet shows the learner has not understood what the statement measures.

### subtopic_3 — The Balance Sheet
**Assistant:** Why do drawings appear on the balance sheet but not the income statement?

**Tutor:** Because drawings are not an expense of the business; they are a reduction of the owner's interest in it. The income statement measures the performance of the business: the income it earned from its activities and the costs it incurred to earn that income. Wages, stationery, water and electricity and bank charges are all used up in running the repair business, so they belong there. When the owner takes three thousand rand to pay for her family's groceries, that money does nothing to help the business repair appliances. Including it as an expense would understate the profit and give a false picture of how well the business performed. But the money has left the business, so it must be recorded somewhere. It reduces owner's equity, because the owner has taken back part of what she owned in the business. On the balance sheet, owner's equity is therefore calculated as capital plus net profit minus drawings: sixty thousand plus ten thousand one hundred and eighty minus three thousand, sixty-seven thousand one hundred and eighty rand. In Grade 10 you will see this calculation set out in a separate note to the balance sheet, sometimes called the owner's equity note, showing the opening capital, the profit and the drawings. The general principle is that transactions between the business and its owner, such as capital contributions and drawings, affect owner's equity directly and are kept off the income statement.

### subtopic_4 — How the Statements Connect
**Assistant:** What would a bank look for in this balance sheet before lending more money?

**Tutor:** A bank wants to know two things: whether the business can repay, and whether there is enough security if things go wrong. From the balance sheet, it would first look at liquidity, the ability to pay debts as they fall due. The repair business has seventy-four thousand one hundred and eighty rand in the bank and no current liabilities, so it is very liquid at this moment. Second, the bank would look at solvency and gearing, which compare debt with owner's equity. Total liabilities are twenty-five thousand rand and owner's equity is sixty-seven thousand one hundred and eighty rand, so the owner has financed most of the business herself; about twenty-seven percent of the assets are financed by borrowing. A low proportion of debt reassures a lender, because the owner has her own money at risk and the business is not already heavily indebted. Third, it would look at the assets available as security, such as equipment, though a bank knows that second-hand equipment may sell for much less than its book value. The bank would not rely on the balance sheet alone. It would also study the income statement, to see whether profits are large and steady enough to cover the extra interest and repayments: a net profit of ten thousand one hundred and eighty rand in one month is promising, but one month is too short to judge. It would ask for several months or years of statements, bank statements and perhaps a cash flow forecast. You will calculate these relationships as financial indicators in Grade 10 to 12 Accounting.
