# Part 1 — Expert

This session gives an overview of the first half of the accounting cycle: transactions, source documents, subsidiary journals and the general ledger. The anchor is one month in the life of an appliance repair business run by a sole trader: in March the owner deposits capital, borrows from a bank, repairs fridges and washing machines for customers, sublets a storeroom, buys equipment and pays wages. Every one of those events travels the same road, from the event itself, to a piece of paper, to a line in a journal, to a total posted in the ledger. The governing idea is that accounting is a cycle of steps repeated every financial period, that each step summarises the one before it, and that the general ledger is where all the information from the journals is gathered into accounts, one for each asset, liability, owner's equity, income and expense item. The steps of the cycle, the link between each source document and journal, the structure of the general ledger and its two sections, and the idea of posting are examinable, and the full cycle is the backbone of Grade 10 Accounting.

## Subtopic: The Accounting Cycle

The accounting cycle is the sequence of steps a business follows to record and report its financial activities for a financial period. It is called a cycle because, when one period ends, the same steps begin again for the next.

The steps are these. First, a transaction takes place. Second, a source document is issued or received as evidence. Third, the transaction is recorded from the source document in a subsidiary journal, the book of first entry. Fourth, at the end of the month the journals are closed off and their totals are posted to the general ledger, the book of final entry. Fifth, the balances of all the ledger accounts are listed in a trial balance to check that total debits equal total credits. Sixth, from the trial balance the financial statements are prepared: the income statement, which shows profit or loss, and the balance sheet, which shows the financial position. The last two steps are the subject of the next session.

Each step summarises the step before it. Hundreds of source documents become a few dozen journal lines; journal columns become a handful of totals; ledger accounts become one balance each; balances become two financial statements. The cycle turns a mass of detail into information an owner can use.

A financial period is the time covered by the cycle. Journals are usually closed off monthly, while a full set of financial statements is usually prepared annually, at the end of the financial year. Many South African businesses use a financial year ending on the last day of February, to match the tax year for individuals, but a business may choose any year-end.

A useful memory aid for the order is T, S, J, L, T, F: transaction, source document, journal, ledger, trial balance, financial statements.

The questions for this section are with you now: the six steps in order, the meaning of a financial period, and why each step summarises the previous one.

## Subtopic: From Source Document to Journal

The repair business's March transactions show how documents feed journals. Money received is recorded in the cash receipts journal, CRJ; money paid is recorded in the cash payments journal, CPJ.

In the CRJ: on the first of March the owner deposited sixty thousand rand as capital, recorded from duplicate receipt 001. On the third, cash from repairs of four thousand two hundred rand was recorded from the cash register roll. On the fifth, a loan of twenty-five thousand rand from a bank was recorded from the bank statement. On the tenth, a customer paid one thousand eight hundred rand for a fridge repair, recorded from duplicate receipt 002. On the fifteenth, the cash register roll showed three thousand six hundred rand and receipt 003 showed nine hundred rand for a repair. On the twentieth, rent income of two thousand five hundred rand was received for the storeroom, receipt 004. On the twenty-eighth, the cash register roll showed five thousand four hundred rand. On the thirty-first, interest of one hundred and fifty rand earned on the bank account appeared on the bank statement.

In the CPJ, recorded from EFT confirmations and the bank statement: equipment eighteen thousand rand, wages six thousand rand, stationery seven hundred and fifty rand, water and electricity one thousand four hundred rand, bank charges two hundred and twenty rand, and drawings by the owner three thousand rand.

Each journal has a bank column, analysis columns for frequent items such as current income or wages, and a sundry accounts column for occasional items, each with the name of the account.

At month-end the columns are totalled. In the CRJ, current income totals four thousand two hundred plus one thousand eight hundred plus three thousand six hundred plus nine hundred plus five thousand four hundred, fifteen thousand nine hundred rand. Sundry accounts total capital sixty thousand plus loan twenty-five thousand plus rent income two thousand five hundred plus interest income one hundred and fifty, eighty-seven thousand six hundred and fifty rand. The bank column totals one hundred and three thousand five hundred and fifty rand, which equals current income plus sundry accounts, a check that the journal has been totalled correctly. In the CPJ, the bank column totals twenty-nine thousand three hundred and seventy rand.

The questions for this section are with you now: which document supports each March transaction, which journal it goes to, and the column totals and cross-check.

## Subtopic: The General Ledger

The general ledger is the book of final entry. It contains an account for every item that the business needs to track: each asset, liability, owner's equity item, income and expense. Each account is drawn as a T-account, with the account name and a folio number at the top, the debit side on the left and the credit side on the right.

The general ledger is divided into two sections. The balance sheet accounts section contains the accounts that appear on the balance sheet: owner's equity accounts such as capital and drawings, liability accounts such as loan, and asset accounts such as equipment, vehicles and bank. The nominal accounts section contains the income and expense accounts, such as current income, rent income, interest income, wages, stationery, water and electricity, and bank charges. Nominal accounts are temporary: at the end of the financial year their balances are transferred to work out profit, as you will see in Grade 9 and 10.

Each account has a folio number to identify it, such as B1 for capital or N1 for current income, with B for balance sheet accounts and N for nominal accounts. The folio column in the journals and in the ledger shows where each amount was posted from or to, so that any entry can be traced backwards and forwards. This cross-referencing is part of the audit trail.

The bank account is special. All cash receipts are debited to it, as one total from the CRJ, and all cash payments are credited to it, as one total from the CPJ. Its balance shows how much money the business has in the bank according to its own records.

The ledger exists because journals are organised by date and type of transaction, while owners need information organised by item. The journal can tell you every receipt on the fifteenth of March; only the ledger can tell you, in one place, the total wages paid this year or the balance owed on the loan.

The questions for this section are with you now: the purpose of the general ledger, its two sections and the accounts in each, folio numbers, and why the ledger is needed as well as journals.

## Subtopic: Posting from the Journals to the Ledger

Posting is the transfer of amounts from the journals to the ledger accounts. In the cash journals, totals of analysis columns are posted, while sundry amounts are posted individually, because each belongs to a different account.

The double-entry principle decides the sides. From the CRJ, the bank column total is debited to the bank account, because money received increases the asset bank. The other side of every receipt is a credit: the current income total of fifteen thousand nine hundred rand is credited to current income, and each sundry amount is credited to its own account, capital sixty thousand rand, loan twenty-five thousand rand, rent income two thousand five hundred rand and interest income one hundred and fifty rand. Total debits, one hundred and three thousand five hundred and fifty rand, equal total credits, fifteen thousand nine hundred plus eighty-seven thousand six hundred and fifty.

From the CPJ, the bank column total of twenty-nine thousand three hundred and seventy rand is credited to the bank account, because money paid reduces the asset bank. The other side of each payment is a debit: equipment eighteen thousand rand, wages six thousand rand, stationery seven hundred and fifty rand, water and electricity one thousand four hundred rand, bank charges two hundred and twenty rand, and drawings three thousand rand.

The details column in each ledger account names the contra account, the account where the other side of the entry was made. In the bank account, the CRJ total is shown with the details total receipts and the CPJ total with total payments; in the current income account, the credit entry shows bank as its contra account.

After posting, each account is balanced. The bank account has debits of one hundred and three thousand five hundred and fifty rand and credits of twenty-nine thousand three hundred and seventy rand, so its debit balance is seventy-four thousand one hundred and eighty rand. That is the business's bank balance at the end of March, and it should agree with the bank statement after any differences are explained.

The questions for this section are with you now: what posting means, which side each CRJ and CPJ total is posted to, contra accounts, and balancing the bank account.

## Subtopic: Manual and Computerised Accounting

The repair business can keep its records by hand, in printed journals and a ledger book, or on a computer using accounting software or a cloud accounting app. Many small South African businesses now use software, and a growing number of spaza and informal businesses use simple phone apps.

The cycle is exactly the same in both systems. A transaction still needs a source document; it is still captured once, in a journal; it is still posted to ledger accounts; a trial balance is still produced; and financial statements are still prepared. What changes is who does the repetitive work. In a computerised system the bookkeeper captures each document, often by scanning or importing the bank statement, and the software posts to the ledger, balances the accounts and prints the trial balance and statements automatically.

Computerised systems have clear advantages: they are fast, they add correctly every time, they can produce reports at any moment instead of only at month-end, and they can link directly to the bank, to the business's invoicing and to SARS eFiling for VAT returns. They also have risks: if a transaction is captured wrongly, the software will post the error faithfully to every report; data can be lost without backups; and passwords and access rights must be protected against fraud and cyber attacks.

That is why learning the manual cycle matters even in a digital world. A bookkeeper who understands debits, credits, journals and posting can check that the software's output makes sense, find and correct capturing errors, and explain the reports to the owner. The rule garbage in, garbage out applies: the quality of the financial statements depends entirely on the accuracy of what was captured at the first steps of the cycle.

The questions for this section are with you now: how the cycle is the same in manual and computerised systems, two advantages and two risks of software, and why the manual method still matters.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

## Subtopic: The Road Every Rand Travels

Think of the accounting cycle as a road with stops, and every rand the repair shop earns or spends must travel the whole road.

Stop one: something happens. A customer pays to fix her fridge. That is the transaction.

Stop two: paper. The shop writes a receipt and keeps the copy. That is the source document.

Stop three: the diary. The bookkeeper writes the receipt into the cash receipts journal, in date order, with the date, receipt number, who paid and how much. That is the journal, the first book.

Stop four: the filing cabinet. At month-end, instead of copying every receipt again, the bookkeeper adds up each column and files the totals into the right folders: one folder for bank, one for current income, one for capital, one for the loan. Those folders are the ledger accounts, and the cabinet is the general ledger.

Stop five: the check. Every folder's balance is listed on one page to see whether the left side and right side add up to the same total. That is the trial balance.

Stop six: the report card. From that page, two reports are made: one shows whether the shop made a profit, the other shows what it owns and owes. Those are the financial statements.

Six stops: transaction, source document, journal, ledger, trial balance, financial statements. T, S, J, L, T, F. And when the year ends, the road starts again.

Keep the road in mind and try the questions for this part: put the six stops in order.

## Subtopic: Diary and Folders

Here is why a business needs both a diary and folders.

The journal is a diary. It tells the story in the order it happened: the first of March, capital; the third, repairs; the fifth, a loan. If you want to know what happened on the fifteenth, the diary is perfect.

But what if the owner asks: how much do we owe the bank? Or: how much have we spent on wages since January? The diary cannot answer quickly; you would have to read every page. So at month-end everything gets sorted into folders, one folder per item. Open the loan folder and you see the loan straight away. Open the wages folder and you see all the wages. Those folders are the ledger accounts.

Moving the totals from the diary into the folders is called posting. Every rand goes into two folders, once on the left and once on the right, because every transaction has two sides. Money received: into the bank folder on the left, and into the income or capital or loan folder on the right. Money paid: out of the bank folder on the right, and into the wages or stationery or equipment folder on the left.

At the end of March the repair shop's bank folder has one hundred and three thousand five hundred and fifty rand on the left, all the money that came in, and twenty-nine thousand three hundred and seventy rand on the right, all the money that went out. Take one from the other: seventy-four thousand one hundred and eighty rand left in the bank.

Keep the diary and the folders in mind and try the questions for this part: journal or ledger, and which side.
