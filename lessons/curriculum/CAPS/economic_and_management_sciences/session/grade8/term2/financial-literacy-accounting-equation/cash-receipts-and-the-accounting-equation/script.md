# Part 1 — Expert

This session applies the accounting equation, A equals OE plus L, to one family of transactions: cash receipts, every way in which money comes into a business. The anchor is a photography studio run by a sole trader in its first month: the owner deposits capital, a bank grants a loan, clients pay for photo shoots, a neighbouring business rents a corner of the studio, the bank pays interest, an old camera is sold, and a client who owed money settles the account. Every one of these increases the bank, yet they affect the right-hand side of the equation in three different ways. The governing idea is that every cash receipt increases the asset bank, and that the other half of the transaction decides the rest: an increase in owner's equity if the money is capital or income, an increase in liabilities if it is borrowed, or a decrease in another asset if it is the conversion of something the business already owned into cash. Analysing each type of receipt in the equation, naming the account debited and credited, giving the reason for any change in owner's equity, and totalling the effects are examinable, and they prepare directly for the cash receipts journal later this term.

## Subtopic: Every Receipt Increases Bank

A cash receipt is any money received by the business, by any method: notes and coins deposited, card payments, EFTs or direct deposits. In Grade 8 all receipts are banked, so the first effect of every cash receipt is the same: the asset bank increases, and bank is debited.

Because the equation must balance, an increase in an asset must be matched by one of three changes. The right-hand side can increase, through owner's equity or through liabilities, or another asset on the left-hand side can decrease by the same amount. That gives three patterns for cash receipts.

Pattern one: bank increases and owner's equity increases. This applies to capital contributed by the owner and to income earned: current income for services, rent income, interest income and commission income.

Pattern two: bank increases and liabilities increase. This applies to loans received from a bank or other lender.

Pattern three: bank increases and another asset decreases. This applies when the business turns an asset into cash: selling equipment or another asset at its book value, or receiving payment from a debtor who owed the business money.

Recognising the pattern is the whole skill. Ask: what was this money for? Is the owner investing, has the business earned it, has the business borrowed it, or has the business converted something it already owned into money? The answer tells you which account is credited and what happens to the equation.

The questions for this section are with you now: why every receipt debits bank, and the three patterns for the other side.

## Subtopic: Receipts that Increase Owner's Equity

Two kinds of receipt increase owner's equity: capital and income.

Capital. On the first day of the month, the owner deposits forty thousand rand as capital. Bank is debited and capital is credited. Assets increase by forty thousand rand; owner's equity increases by forty thousand rand, with the reason capital contribution; liabilities are not affected. Later in the month the owner contributes a further five thousand rand: the effect is the same, assets plus five thousand, owner's equity plus five thousand, reason capital contribution.

Current income. A client pays three thousand five hundred rand for a family photo shoot. Bank is debited and current income is credited. Assets increase by three thousand five hundred; owner's equity increases by three thousand five hundred, reason income earned. The reason matters: income increases owner's equity because it increases profit, which belongs to the owner.

Rent income. A neighbouring hair salon rents a corner of the studio for storage and pays one thousand two hundred rand. Bank is debited and rent income is credited. Assets plus one thousand two hundred; owner's equity plus one thousand two hundred, reason income earned.

Interest income. The bank statement shows interest of eighty-five rand earned on the business account. Bank is debited and interest income is credited. Assets plus eighty-five; owner's equity plus eighty-five, reason income earned.

Notice that capital and income both increase owner's equity, but in different ways. Capital is the owner putting her own money in; income is the business earning money from others. On the financial statements, capital goes straight to owner's equity on the balance sheet, while income goes first to the income statement, becomes part of profit, and then reaches owner's equity. That is why the reason column must distinguish them.

The questions for this section are with you now: the effect of capital, current income, rent income and interest income on the equation, and the different reasons given for each.

## Subtopic: Loans and Conversions of Assets

Loan received. A bank grants the studio a loan of thirty thousand rand, paid into the business account, to be repaid over three years. Bank is debited and loan is credited. Assets increase by thirty thousand; liabilities increase by thirty thousand; owner's equity is not affected. This is not income, because the money must be repaid; the business has more cash but also a debt of the same amount. Treating a loan as income is one of the errors examiners look for most.

Sale of an asset at cost. The studio sells an old camera, recorded in the books at two thousand four hundred rand, for exactly that amount, and the buyer pays by EFT. Bank is debited and equipment is credited. Assets increase by two thousand four hundred, bank, and decrease by two thousand four hundred, equipment. The total of assets is unchanged, and owner's equity and liabilities are not affected. In Grade 8 we sell assets at their book value, so there is no profit or loss on the sale; in Grade 10 you will deal with selling an asset for more or less than its book value.

Receipt from a debtor. A client had a photo shoot last month and was allowed to pay later, so she is a debtor for one thousand five hundred rand. She now pays. Bank is debited and debtors control, the account for amounts owed by customers, is credited. Assets increase by one thousand five hundred, bank, and decrease by one thousand five hundred, debtors. No income is recorded now, because the income was recorded when the service was provided on credit last month; recording it again would double-count it.

These two conversion receipts are where many learners make mistakes, because money has come in, which feels like earning. Ask the question: did the business earn something new, or did it simply turn something it already owned into money? If the second, owner's equity does not change.

The questions for this section are with you now: the effect of a loan, the sale of an asset at cost and a receipt from a debtor, and why none of them is income.

## Subtopic: The Full Analysis

Bring the studio's first month together in an analysis table, with the effect on assets, owner's equity and liabilities and the reason for owner's equity changes.

Capital forty thousand rand: assets plus forty thousand, owner's equity plus forty thousand, capital contribution. Photo shoot three thousand five hundred rand: assets plus three thousand five hundred, owner's equity plus three thousand five hundred, income. Loan thirty thousand rand: assets plus thirty thousand, liabilities plus thirty thousand. Rent income one thousand two hundred rand: assets plus, owner's equity plus, income. Interest income eighty-five rand: assets plus, owner's equity plus, income. Additional capital five thousand rand: assets plus, owner's equity plus, capital contribution. Camera sold at cost two thousand four hundred rand: assets plus and minus two thousand four hundred. Debtor paid one thousand five hundred rand: assets plus and minus one thousand five hundred.

Now total. The bank increased by forty thousand plus three thousand five hundred plus thirty thousand plus one thousand two hundred plus eighty-five plus five thousand plus two thousand four hundred plus one thousand five hundred, which is eighty-three thousand six hundred and eighty-five rand. But two of those receipts reduced other assets by a total of three thousand nine hundred rand, so the net increase in total assets is seventy-nine thousand seven hundred and eighty-five rand.

Owner's equity increased by forty thousand plus three thousand five hundred plus one thousand two hundred plus eighty-five plus five thousand, which is forty-nine thousand seven hundred and eighty-five rand. Liabilities increased by thirty thousand rand. Forty-nine thousand seven hundred and eighty-five plus thirty thousand is seventy-nine thousand seven hundred and eighty-five. The equation balances.

Of the owner's equity increase, forty-five thousand rand is capital and four thousand seven hundred and eighty-five rand is income: three thousand five hundred plus one thousand two hundred plus eighty-five.

The questions for this section are with you now: completing the analysis table for all eight receipts, and totalling the effects to check that the equation balances.

## Subtopic: From the Equation to the Journal

The analysis of receipts in the equation leads straight into the cash receipts journal, which you will study in the coming weeks. Every receipt analysed in this session would be entered in the CRJ, because money was received. The bank column would show every amount, because bank is debited for every receipt.

The other column used depends on the account credited, which you have already identified in the analysis. Current income has its own analysis column, because it occurs often. Every other credit, capital, loan, rent income, interest income, equipment and debtors control, would be entered in the sundry accounts column with the account name, because these occur less often.

So the question you ask for the equation, what was this money for, is the same question that decides the column in the journal. If you can analyse a receipt in the equation, you can enter it in the journal.

The source documents also follow from the analysis. Capital and rent income are supported by duplicate receipts, the photo shoot by a duplicate receipt or cash invoice, interest and the loan by the bank statement, the camera sale by a duplicate receipt or the EFT shown on the bank statement, and the debtor's payment by a duplicate receipt.

This is the method you will use for the whole of Grade 8 financial literacy: identify the document, analyse the transaction in the equation, record it in the journal, and post it to the ledger. Each skill builds on the one before.

The questions for this section are with you now: the column each receipt would use in the CRJ, and its source document.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through one photography studio and three kinds of money, plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Three Kinds of Money In

Every time money lands in the studio's bank account, ask one question: what is this money?

Kind one: the owner's money or earned money. When the owner puts in forty thousand rand to start, that is her money, so her share goes up. When a family pays three thousand five hundred rand for a photo shoot, the studio earned it, and earned money belongs to the owner too, so her share goes up. Same for rent from the hair salon next door and the little bit of interest the bank pays. Bank up, owner's share up.

Kind two: borrowed money. The bank lends thirty thousand rand. The studio has more money, but it has to pay every cent back. So the bank account goes up, and the amount owed goes up by exactly the same. The owner's share does not move at all. Borrowed money is never income.

Kind three: swapped money. The owner sells an old camera for what it is worth in the books, two thousand four hundred rand. The bank goes up, but the camera is gone. A client who already owed one thousand five hundred rand finally pays. The bank goes up, but the amount owed to the studio goes down. Nothing new was earned; something the studio already owned just turned into money. Bank up, another asset down, the total stays the same.

So: owner's or earned money, the owner's share up. Borrowed money, the debts up. Swapped money, nothing up on the right, just a swap on the left.

Why does this matter so much? Imagine the owner looks at her bank app at the end of the month and sees eighty-three thousand rand. She might think the studio is booming. But most of that is her own money and the bank's money. Only a few thousand rand was actually earned from taking photographs. If she spends the loan money thinking it is profit, she will have nothing left when the repayments start. Knowing the three kinds of money keeps her from fooling herself, and keeps her books honest for the bank and SARS.

Keep the three kinds of money in mind and try the questions for this part: which kind is it?

## Subtopic: The Studio's Month on the Scale

Put the studio's month on the kitchen scale from Term 1.

Left pan, the assets. Right pan, the owner's share and the debts.

The owner's forty thousand: left up forty thousand, right up forty thousand. Level. The photo shoot: both up three thousand five hundred. Level. The loan: left up thirty thousand, right up thirty thousand on the debts side. Level. The rent and the interest: both up. The owner's extra five thousand: both up.

Now the camera. Left pan: money up two thousand four hundred, camera out two thousand four hundred. The left pan weighs the same as before; the right pan does nothing. Level. The client who owed: money up one thousand five hundred, amount owed to the studio down one thousand five hundred. Again the left pan just swaps. Level.

Count it all at the end. Money that came into the bank: eighty-three thousand six hundred and eighty-five rand. But two of those were swaps worth three thousand nine hundred rand together, so the left pan really grew by seventy-nine thousand seven hundred and eighty-five. The owner's share grew by forty-nine thousand seven hundred and eighty-five, and the debts by thirty thousand. Add those: seventy-nine thousand seven hundred and eighty-five. Level.

And here is a bonus. Of the owner's share growth, forty-five thousand was her own money and only four thousand seven hundred and eighty-five was earned. That earned part is the beginning of her profit.

Keep the scale in mind and try the questions for this part: which pan moves, and by how much?
