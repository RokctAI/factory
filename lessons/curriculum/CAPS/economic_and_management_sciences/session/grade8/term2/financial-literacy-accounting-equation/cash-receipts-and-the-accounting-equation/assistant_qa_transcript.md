### subtopic_1 — Every Receipt Increases Bank
**Assistant:** Why is a debtor's payment not recorded as income when the money comes in?

**Tutor:** Because the income was already recorded when the service was provided, and recording it again would count the same income twice. Think about what happened in two steps. Last month the photography studio did a photo shoot for a client who was allowed to pay later. At that moment the studio had earned the income: it had done the work and the client owed the money. So the studio recorded income of one thousand five hundred rand and created a debtor, an asset, for the same amount. In the equation: assets plus one thousand five hundred, debtors; owner's equity plus one thousand five hundred, income. This month the client pays. The studio's bank increases by one thousand five hundred, and the debtor's balance decreases to zero, because she no longer owes anything. In the equation: assets plus one thousand five hundred, bank, and minus one thousand five hundred, debtors. Owner's equity does not change. If the studio recorded income again, its profit would be overstated by one thousand five hundred rand, and the debtors account would still show the client owing money she has already paid. This principle, that income is recognised when it is earned, not when the cash arrives, is called the accrual basis of accounting. You will meet it fully in Grade 9, when you record credit transactions in the debtors journal, and in Grade 10 with adjustments. For now, remember: a debtor paying is a swap of one asset, debtors, for another, bank.

### subtopic_2 — Receipts that Increase Owner's Equity
**Assistant:** Is extra capital from the owner treated differently from the first capital?

**Tutor:** No, the effect on the equation is the same, but it is worth recording carefully because it tells an important story. Whenever the owner contributes money or assets to the business, bank or the asset contributed is debited and capital is credited, and owner's equity increases with the reason capital contribution. That applies to the forty thousand rand at the start and the five thousand rand later in the month. In the ledger both amounts go to the same capital account, so at the end of the month the capital account shows forty-five thousand rand. On the financial statements, however, accountants show additional capital separately from the opening capital in the note to owner's equity, so that a reader can see how much of the increase in owner's equity came from the owner putting in more money and how much came from profit. That distinction matters to anyone judging the business. If owner's equity rose by fifty thousand rand during a year, that sounds impressive, but if forty-five thousand rand of it was the owner topping up her investment, the business itself only earned five thousand rand. Additional capital is also a signal. An owner who regularly has to put in more money may be covering losses or a shortage of cash, while an owner who adds capital to buy new equipment for growth is investing. Capital can also be contributed in a form other than money, such as equipment or a vehicle, which is recorded at its fair value, and in that case it is not a cash receipt and goes to the general journal.

### subtopic_3 — Loans and Conversions of Assets
**Assistant:** Why does selling the camera at its book value not create a profit?

**Tutor:** Because profit on a sale only arises when the business receives more than the amount at which the asset is recorded in its books, and here it received exactly that amount. The book value, also called the carrying value, is the amount at which the asset is shown in the accounting records. When the studio sells the old camera recorded at two thousand four hundred rand for exactly two thousand four hundred rand, it gives up an asset worth two thousand four hundred rand in its books and receives cash of the same amount. Its total assets are unchanged, and nothing has been earned. In the equation it is a swap on the assets side: bank plus two thousand four hundred, equipment minus two thousand four hundred. Owner's equity does not move. If the camera had been sold for three thousand rand, the extra six hundred rand would be a profit on the sale, an income item that increases owner's equity. If it had been sold for two thousand rand, the shortfall of four hundred rand would be a loss on the sale, which decreases owner's equity. Those cases appear in Grade 10, together with depreciation, the process of reducing an asset's book value each year as it wears out. In Grade 8, assets are sold at their book value, so you only need to record the swap. Learners often record the whole selling price as income. That is the error to avoid: the money from selling a camera is not earned from the studio's normal business of taking photographs.

### subtopic_4 — The Full Analysis
**Assistant:** How can I check my analysis table quickly in an exam?

**Tutor:** Use three checks, each taking seconds. First, check each line on its own. For every transaction, the change on the left, assets, must equal the total change on the right, owner's equity plus liabilities. If a line shows assets plus three thousand five hundred and owner's equity plus three thousand five hundred, it balances. If a line shows assets plus and minus the same amount and nothing on the right, it also balances, because the net change on the left is zero. Any line where the two sides differ contains an error. Second, check the bank. Every cash receipt must include bank plus the amount received. If any receipt line does not increase assets, you have missed the bank. Third, check owner's equity reasons. Every time owner's equity changes, there must be a reason: capital contribution or income for receipts. If owner's equity changes on a loan, a sale of an asset or a debtor's payment, that is wrong, because none of those increase owner's equity. After the line checks, total the columns. In the studio example, the net change in assets of seventy-nine thousand seven hundred and eighty-five rand equals owner's equity forty-nine thousand seven hundred and eighty-five plus liabilities thirty thousand. If your totals do not agree, go back to the line checks to find which line is wrong. Finally, read the question carefully: some ask only for the effect on owner's equity, or for the accounts debited and credited, and marks are lost for answering a different question.
