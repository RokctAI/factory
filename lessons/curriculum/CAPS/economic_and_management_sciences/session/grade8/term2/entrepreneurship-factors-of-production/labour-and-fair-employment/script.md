# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines labour, the human factor of production: the levels of skill workers bring, the role workers play in a business, and the laws that make employment fair in South Africa. The anchor is a busy restaurant in a shopping centre. The dishwasher, the waiters, the chef, the bookkeeper and the manager all supply labour, but with very different skills and pay, and every one of them is protected by the same set of employment laws: a maximum number of ordinary hours, paid leave, a national minimum wage, the right to join a union and protection against unfair dismissal and discrimination. The governing idea is that labour is the physical and mental effort people supply in exchange for wages and salaries, that its value depends largely on skill, and that fair employment practices, set out in law and in good management, protect workers while helping businesses keep productive and motivated staff. Unskilled, semi-skilled and skilled labour, the roles and rights of workers, the main labour laws, and wage and overtime calculations are examinable, and they lead into Grade 10 Business Studies and Economics.

## Subtopic: Labour and Levels of Skill

Labour is the physical and mental effort that people put into producing goods and services. It is supplied by households to businesses and government in exchange for remuneration in the form of wages, usually paid per hour or per week, or salaries, usually a fixed monthly amount.

Labour is classified by level of skill, which depends on education, training and experience.

Unskilled labour needs little or no formal training or education. The work can be learned quickly on the job: cleaning, dishwashing, packing shelves, carrying goods, general labouring on a building site. Because many people can do it, unskilled labour is usually paid the least.

Semi-skilled labour needs some training or experience, often a short course or a period of on-the-job learning, but not a professional qualification: waiters, shop assistants, machine operators, drivers, call-centre agents and security guards.

Skilled labour needs a high level of education or specialised training, often a qualification from a university, a TVET college or an apprenticeship: chefs with professional training, electricians, plumbers, nurses, teachers, accountants, engineers and doctors. Some textbooks separate a fourth level, highly skilled or professional labour, for occupations that need many years of study, such as doctors and engineers.

In the restaurant, the dishwasher is unskilled, the waiters semi-skilled, and the qualified chef and the bookkeeper skilled.

Skill levels matter for wages and for the economy. Skilled workers are scarcer, so they earn more; unskilled workers are plentiful, so they earn less and are more likely to be unemployed. South Africa has a shortage of skilled workers, such as artisans, engineers and teachers in mathematics and science, alongside high unemployment among unskilled and young workers. This mismatch is structural unemployment, which you met in Term 1, and it is why education and skills training are so important for both individuals and the economy.

The questions for this section are with you now: the definition of labour, its remuneration, and the three levels of skill with examples.

## Subtopic: The Role of Workers

Workers are not simply a cost to the business; they are the people who turn the other factors of production into goods and services. In the restaurant, the best location, kitchen equipment and ingredients produce nothing without the people who cook, serve and clean.

Workers have several roles. They produce the goods and services the business sells. They deal with customers, so their attitude and skill shape the business's reputation. They operate and look after the business's equipment and stock. They share knowledge and ideas, often noticing problems and improvements the owner cannot see. And as employees they have responsibilities as well as rights.

The responsibilities of workers include doing the work they were employed to do, diligently and to an acceptable standard; arriving on time and working the agreed hours; obeying reasonable and lawful instructions; acting honestly, not stealing or misusing the employer's property and keeping business information confidential; following health and safety rules; and treating customers and colleagues with respect.

Productivity, the amount produced per worker per hour, is the link between the worker's role and the business's success. Productivity rises with skills and training, good equipment, good management and motivation. Motivated workers produce more and stay longer, which saves the cost of recruiting and training replacements. Workers are motivated by fair pay, but also by fair treatment, recognition, safe working conditions, training and the chance of promotion.

Workers can also organise themselves. A trade union is an organisation of workers that negotiates with employers on their behalf about wages and conditions, a process called collective bargaining. Unions in South Africa, many grouped in federations such as COSATU, played a central role in the struggle against apartheid and remain important in protecting workers' rights.

The questions for this section are with you now: the roles and responsibilities of workers, productivity and motivation, and the purpose of trade unions.

## Subtopic: Fair Employment Laws

South Africa's Constitution, in section 23, gives everyone the right to fair labour practices, and several laws give that right detail.

The Basic Conditions of Employment Act, BCEA, sets minimum conditions for most employees. The maximum ordinary working time is forty-five hours a week, which is nine hours a day for a five-day week or eight hours a day for a six-day week. Overtime must be agreed, may not exceed ten hours a week, and must be paid at one and a half times the normal wage, or double on Sundays and public holidays if the employee does not usually work then. Employees are entitled to annual leave of twenty-one consecutive days a year, which is fifteen working days for someone working five days a week; sick leave of thirty days in each three-year cycle for a five-day week; four consecutive months of maternity leave; and family responsibility leave. Employers must give each employee a written contract or written particulars of employment and a payslip. Children under fifteen may not be employed.

The National Minimum Wage Act sets the lowest hourly wage that may be paid. From the first of March 2025, the national minimum wage is twenty-eight rand seventy-nine cents per hour for most workers. It is adjusted every year.

The Labour Relations Act protects the right to join a trade union, to bargain collectively and to strike, and protects workers against unfair dismissal. Disputes can be referred to the Commission for Conciliation, Mediation and Arbitration, the CCMA, which helps settle them without going to court.

The Employment Equity Act prohibits unfair discrimination on grounds such as race, gender, disability, religion, HIV status or pregnancy, and requires larger employers to take affirmative action measures to make their workforce representative.

The Occupational Health and Safety Act requires employers to provide a safe workplace. The Unemployment Insurance Fund, UIF, to which employers and employees each contribute one percent of earnings, pays benefits during unemployment, illness and maternity leave. The Compensation for Occupational Injuries and Diseases Act provides for workers injured at work. The Skills Development Act funds training through sector education and training authorities, SETAs, and learnerships.

The questions for this section are with you now: section 23 of the Constitution, the BCEA conditions, the national minimum wage, and the purpose of the LRA, the EEA, the OHSA and the UIF.

## Subtopic: Wages, Overtime and Payslips

Labour law becomes practical in wage calculations, which appear often in exams.

At the national minimum wage of twenty-eight rand seventy-nine cents an hour, a worker on the maximum ordinary week of forty-five hours earns forty-five times twenty-eight rand seventy-nine, which is one thousand two hundred and ninety-five rand fifty-five cents a week.

Overtime: a waiter earns forty rand an hour for a forty-five-hour week and works five hours overtime on weekdays. Ordinary pay is forty-five times forty rand, one thousand eight hundred rand. Overtime is paid at one and a half times the hourly rate, sixty rand an hour, so five hours earn three hundred rand. Total pay for the week is two thousand one hundred rand.

From gross pay, the employer makes deductions: PAYE income tax if the employee earns above the tax threshold, and the employee's UIF contribution of one percent. The employer adds its own UIF contribution of one percent, which is not deducted from the worker. Net pay, the amount the employee actually receives, is gross pay minus deductions. On a gross monthly wage of five thousand rand, below the tax threshold, the employee's UIF contribution is fifty rand, so net pay is four thousand nine hundred and fifty rand, and the employer also pays fifty rand of its own to the UIF.

A payslip must show the employer's name, the employee's name and job, the period, the wage rate, the hours worked including overtime, gross pay, each deduction and the net pay. It allows the worker to check that they have been paid correctly and is proof of income when applying for credit or accommodation.

For the business, wages are an expense. Paying fair wages on time, with correct deductions paid over to SARS and the UIF, is a legal duty, and it builds the trust that keeps good workers.

The questions for this section are with you now: weekly pay at the minimum wage, overtime at one and a half times, gross and net pay, UIF deductions, and the contents of a payslip.

## Subtopic: Fair Employment as Good Business

Fair employment practices go beyond obeying the law. They are choices that good employers make because they also make business sense.

Fair recruitment means advertising jobs openly, choosing the best person for the job on merit, and not discriminating. Fair pay means paying at least the minimum wage, paying equally for work of equal value, and explaining how pay is set. Fair treatment means respectful supervision, no harassment, clear rules and a fair disciplinary process in which a worker is told what they are accused of and given the chance to respond before any action is taken. Training and development means helping workers gain skills and progress. Safe and healthy conditions mean protective equipment, safe machinery, clean facilities and reasonable hours.

The benefits for the business are real. Workers who are treated fairly are more motivated and productive, take fewer days off, and are less likely to leave, which saves the costs of recruiting and training. Fair employers attract better applicants. Customers increasingly prefer businesses with good reputations. And fair practices reduce the risk of costly disputes at the CCMA, strikes and legal penalties.

Unfair practices do the opposite. Paying below the minimum wage, forcing unpaid overtime, firing workers without a fair hearing, or ignoring safety may save money in the short term, but they lead to high staff turnover, poor service, disputes, fines and damaged reputation.

The entrepreneur who starts a small business should therefore plan for fair employment from the first worker: a written contract, a payslip, registration with the UIF and SARS where required, and clear, fair rules.

The questions for this section are with you now: examples of fair employment practices and how they benefit both workers and the business.

## Subtopic: Everyone in the Restaurant

Walk through a restaurant on a Friday night. At the back, a young man washes a mountain of plates. He learned the job in an afternoon: that is unskilled labour. Out front, the waiters carry five plates at once, remember twelve orders and keep the customers happy. They learned with some training and practice: semi-skilled labour. In the kitchen, the chef runs the stove like an orchestra. She studied for years at a culinary school: skilled labour. In the office, the bookkeeper with her accounting diploma pays the bills: skilled labour too.

Everyone's work is needed. Without the dishwasher there are no clean plates; without the waiters no food reaches the tables; without the chef there is no food worth eating. But the pay is different, because some skills are rarer and take longer to learn. More people can wash dishes than can run a professional kitchen.

Every worker has a job to do well: arrive on time, work honestly, follow safety rules, treat customers well and look after the restaurant's things. And every worker has rights.

That is why education and training matter so much. The dishwasher who takes a cooking course and practises can become a cook, then a chef. Each step up in skill usually means a step up in pay.

And if the workers want to speak with one voice about wages and conditions, they can join a trade union, which negotiates with the employer for all of them.

Keep the restaurant in mind and try the questions for this part: unskilled, semi-skilled or skilled, and what workers owe the business.

## Subtopic: The Rules on the Wall

In the restaurant's back passage there is a poster on the wall. The law says it must be there: it is a summary of the Basic Conditions of Employment Act. Here is what it says, in plain words.

You may not be made to work more than forty-five ordinary hours a week. If you work extra, that is overtime, and it must be paid at one and a half times your normal rate. A waiter earning forty rand an hour who works five extra hours gets sixty rand for each extra hour: three hundred rand on top of the one thousand eight hundred rand for the normal week. Total: two thousand one hundred rand.

Nobody may be paid less than the national minimum wage, which from March 2025 is twenty-eight rand seventy-nine cents an hour. A full forty-five-hour week at that rate is one thousand two hundred and ninety-five rand fifty-five cents.

You get paid leave every year, sick leave when you are ill, and four months of maternity leave. You must get a payslip showing what you earned, what was taken off, like one percent for the UIF, and what you actually received.

You may not be treated unfairly because of your race, your gender, your religion or a disability. If you are fired, there must be a fair reason and a fair hearing. If there is a dispute, the CCMA can help settle it.

And the kitchen must be safe: guards on the slicers, mats on wet floors, a first-aid kit.

Why does a smart owner follow these rules happily? Because happy workers stay, work harder and treat customers better.

Keep the poster in mind and try the questions for this part: hours, overtime, minimum wage and fair treatment.
