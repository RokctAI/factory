### subtopic_1 — The Four Factors of Production
**Assistant:** Why is profit called the reward for entrepreneurship if it is not guaranteed?

**Tutor:** Because the entrepreneur's contribution is precisely to take on the uncertainty that the other factors avoid. The owner of the land agrees a rent in advance and is paid it whether the coffee business does well or badly. The barista agrees a wage and is paid it every week. The bank agrees an interest rate and expects its instalments regardless of sales. The entrepreneur is the person who promises to pay all of them before knowing whether customers will come. In exchange, she claims whatever is left after those fixed payments: the residual. If the business is well run and customers love the coffee, the residual is large, and profit rewards her for spotting the opportunity, organising the resources and carrying the risk. If customers do not come, the residual is negative, and she bears the loss out of her own capital. That is why profit is a reward in a special sense: it is the payment for risk-taking and for the skill of combining resources well. Economists sometimes say that the possibility of profit is what persuades people to start businesses at all, because without it no one would take on the risk. In South Africa, where unemployment is high, entrepreneurship matters greatly: each successful new business creates income for its owner and wages for its workers. That is also why government programmes try to reduce the risks small entrepreneurs face, through training, mentorship and affordable finance.

### subtopic_2 — What Capital Means
**Assistant:** If own capital has no interest, why is it not free?

**Tutor:** Because using money in one place always means giving up the chance to use it somewhere else, and that lost alternative is a real cost even if no one sends a bill. Economists call it the opportunity cost. If the coffee entrepreneur left her thirty thousand rand in a savings account or a fixed deposit, it might earn several percent interest a year with almost no risk. By putting it into the business instead, she gives up that interest, and she also takes on the risk that the money may be lost if the business fails. So her own capital has a hidden cost: the safe return she could have earned elsewhere, plus a reward she should expect for bearing the risk. A sensible entrepreneur asks whether the business is likely to earn more on her money than the safe alternative; if not, she would be better off keeping her savings in the bank. The same reasoning applies to retained profits. When she leaves profit in the business instead of taking it as drawings, she gives up spending or saving it privately, so retained profit also has an opportunity cost. Accountants do not record opportunity cost in the books, because no transaction occurs, which is why accounting profit can look healthier than it really is. When you study Economics in Grade 10, you will see that economists include opportunity cost when they judge whether a business is truly worthwhile.

### subtopic_3 — Own Capital
**Assistant:** Why do banks demand collateral before lending to a small business?

**Tutor:** Because lending to a small, new business is risky, and collateral reduces the bank's loss if the borrower cannot repay. When a bank lends sixty thousand rand, it is lending other people's money: the deposits of its customers. It must expect to get that money back with interest. A new coffee business has no track record of profits, its owner may have a short credit history, and many small businesses fail in their first few years. Collateral is an asset that the borrower pledges to the bank: if the loan is not repaid, the bank can take and sell the asset to recover what it is owed. For vehicle and equipment finance, the asset bought, such as the trailer, is the collateral. For larger loans, banks may ask for property, such as the owner's house, or a personal guarantee, which means the owner is personally liable for the debt. This is especially serious for a sole trader, who already has unlimited liability. Collateral is also why people without assets find it hardest to borrow, a problem that falls heavily on young and previously disadvantaged entrepreneurs. Government agencies such as the Small Enterprise Finance Agency and the National Empowerment Fund, and some credit guarantee schemes, exist partly to fill this gap by sharing the risk with banks or lending with less collateral. Banks also look at the business plan, cash flow forecasts and the owner's own capital, because an owner who has invested her own savings is more committed to making it work.

### subtopic_4 — Borrowed Capital
**Assistant:** Is it ever a good idea to borrow from a microlender to start a business?

**Tutor:** It can be, but only rarely and with great care, because microloans are usually small, short-term and expensive. Microlenders provide small loans to people who may not qualify at a bank, often quickly and with little paperwork. That access can help someone buy a first stock of goods or a small piece of equipment. But the cost is high. Interest rates and fees on short-term microloans are much higher than on bank business loans, and when you add initiation fees and monthly service fees, the total cost of a small loan over a few months can be a large share of the amount borrowed. For a business, that means the profit on the goods or service must be high and quick enough to cover the cost, or the business will be working mainly to pay the lender. Several protections exist. The National Credit Act sets maximum interest rates and fees, requires lenders to be registered with the National Credit Regulator, and prohibits reckless lending, meaning lending to someone who clearly cannot afford to repay. Unregistered lenders, often called mashonisas, may charge far more, keep bank cards or identity documents as security, which is illegal, and use threats to collect. A safer path is to start smaller with own capital, use supplier credit where possible, join a stokvel that lends to members at agreed terms, or approach development finance agencies and enterprise development programmes. If borrowing is necessary, calculate the total cost in rand, compare offers, and make sure the business's realistic sales can repay it.
