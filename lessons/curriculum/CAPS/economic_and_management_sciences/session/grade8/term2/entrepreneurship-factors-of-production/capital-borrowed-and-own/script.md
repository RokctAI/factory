# Part 1 — Expert

This session opens the study of the factors of production with capital, and in particular the two ways an entrepreneur can obtain it: own capital and borrowed capital. The anchor is a young entrepreneur who wants to open a mobile coffee business, a converted trailer with an espresso machine, parked outside a taxi rank and a college. She needs about one hundred thousand rand. She has thirty thousand rand saved, her aunt offers ten thousand rand, a bank will lend her sixty thousand rand at interest, and a coffee supplier will let her pay for beans thirty days after delivery. Each source has a different cost, a different risk and a different set of strings attached. The governing idea is that capital is one of the four factors of production, that a business can finance its capital from the owner's own resources or by borrowing, and that the choice between them balances the cost of interest against the control and risk carried by the owner. The four factors of production, the meanings of capital, the sources of own and borrowed capital, their advantages and disadvantages, and simple interest calculations are examinable, and they lead into Grade 10 Business Studies and Economics.

## Subtopic: The Four Factors of Production

Every good or service is produced by combining resources, and economists group these resources into four factors of production.

Natural resources, sometimes called land, are all the gifts of nature used in production: land itself, water, minerals, forests, fish, sunlight and wind. Labour is the human effort, physical and mental, used in production: the work of employees, from a cleaner to an engineer. Capital is the man-made resources used to produce other goods and services: buildings, machinery, equipment, tools and vehicles, as well as the money used to acquire them. Entrepreneurship is the ability and willingness to combine the other three factors, take risks and start or run a business, in the hope of making a profit.

Each factor earns its own reward, called remuneration. Natural resources earn rent, labour earns wages and salaries, capital earns interest, and the entrepreneur earns profit. Profit is different from the other rewards because it is not guaranteed: the entrepreneur receives what is left after paying rent, wages and interest, which may be a loss.

In the coffee business, the factors are easy to see. The space outside the taxi rank, the water and the coffee beans grown on farms come from natural resources. The barista who makes the coffee supplies labour. The trailer, the espresso machine and the money used to buy them are capital. And the young woman who spotted the opportunity, organised everything and carries the risk is the entrepreneur.

The factors of production are scarce. There is only so much land, so many skilled workers and so much capital in an economy, and every business competes for them. That scarcity is the reason they must be paid for and the reason entrepreneurs must use them efficiently.

The questions for this section are with you now: the four factors of production, the reward of each, and why profit differs from the other rewards.

## Subtopic: What Capital Means

The word capital is used in two related ways, and it helps to keep them apart.

In economics, capital means physical capital: man-made goods that are used to produce other goods and services, rather than being consumed. The espresso machine is capital because it is used to make coffee for sale; the coffee beans are not, because they are used up. Physical capital is divided into fixed capital, long-lasting items such as buildings, vehicles, machinery and equipment, and working capital, the money and short-term resources needed to run the business from day to day, such as cash to buy stock, pay wages and cover expenses until customers pay.

In business and accounting, capital usually means financial capital: the money used to acquire the physical capital and keep the business running. In Term 1 you met capital in a narrower accounting sense: the amount the owner contributes, shown as owner's equity. In this topic, capital includes all the money a business uses, whether it comes from the owner or from lenders.

Capital is important because it multiplies the productivity of labour. A barista with a professional espresso machine can make far more coffee, of better quality, than one with a kettle. A farmer with a tractor can plough far more land than one with a hand hoe. Economies that invest more in capital, such as roads, factories and machinery, generally produce more per worker and have higher incomes.

The coffee entrepreneur's estimate of one hundred thousand rand includes fixed capital, the trailer and espresso machine and a grinder, and working capital, the first stock of beans, milk and cups, the vendor's permit and enough cash to pay the barista until the business earns enough.

The questions for this section are with you now: physical and financial capital, fixed and working capital, and why capital raises productivity.

## Subtopic: Own Capital

Own capital is money provided by the owner or owners of the business, which does not have to be repaid and on which no interest is charged.

Sources of own capital for a small business include the owner's personal savings; money from family and friends given as a contribution to the business rather than a loan; selling personal assets, such as a car, to raise money; retained profits, meaning profits the owner leaves in the business instead of taking them as drawings; and, for companies, selling shares to new owners, which you will study in Term 3.

Own capital has important advantages. It does not have to be repaid, so it puts no pressure on the business's cash flow. No interest is payable, so it is cheaper in cash terms than a loan. The owner keeps full control, without a lender setting conditions. And a business with a large share of own capital is seen as less risky, so it finds it easier to borrow later if needed.

It also has disadvantages. Most people have limited savings, so own capital may not be enough to start or grow the business. The owner's savings are at risk: if the business fails, the money is lost. Using savings for the business means giving up other uses of the money, such as earning interest in a savings account, which is the opportunity cost of own capital. And if family members contribute, they may expect a say in decisions or a share of profits, which can strain relationships.

For the coffee entrepreneur, own capital is her thirty thousand rand of savings and, if it is a contribution and not a loan, her aunt's ten thousand rand: forty thousand rand in total, forty percent of the one hundred thousand rand she needs.

The questions for this section are with you now: sources of own capital, its advantages and disadvantages, and its opportunity cost.

## Subtopic: Borrowed Capital

Borrowed capital, also called debt or loan capital, is money obtained from outsiders that must be repaid, usually with interest.

Sources of borrowed capital include bank loans, repaid in instalments over a fixed period; a bank overdraft, which allows the business to pay out more than it has in its account up to an agreed limit, useful for short-term working capital; vehicle and equipment finance, where the item itself is security for the loan; trade credit, where suppliers deliver goods now and allow payment later, such as the coffee supplier's thirty days; loans from government development agencies, such as the Small Enterprise Finance Agency and the National Empowerment Fund, which offer finance and support to small and emerging businesses; and loans from family or friends that must be repaid. Microlenders also lend small amounts, often at high interest rates.

Borrowed capital allows an entrepreneur to start or grow a business sooner and bigger than own capital alone would allow, and the owner keeps ownership and all the profit after interest. Interest is also a deductible expense for tax.

Its disadvantages are serious. Interest must be paid whether the business makes a profit or not. Instalments must be repaid on time, which strains cash flow, especially in the early months. Lenders require security, called collateral, such as property or the asset being financed, and a business that cannot pay may lose it. Banks also need proof of the owner's ability to repay: a business plan, a credit record and often some own capital, which is why a person with no savings finds it hardest to borrow.

Interest is calculated as a percentage of the amount borrowed. Simple interest is principal times rate times time. If the coffee entrepreneur borrows sixty thousand rand at twelve percent a year for one year, the interest is sixty thousand times twelve percent, which is seven thousand two hundred rand, and she must repay sixty-seven thousand two hundred rand. Over two years at simple interest, the interest would be fourteen thousand four hundred rand.

The questions for this section are with you now: sources of borrowed capital, collateral, its advantages and disadvantages, and simple interest.

## Subtopic: Choosing the Mix

Most businesses use a combination of own and borrowed capital. The balance between them is a key decision for the entrepreneur.

Compare the two. Own capital is not repaid, carries no interest, and leaves full control with the owner, but is limited in amount and puts the owner's savings at risk. Borrowed capital can be larger and keeps ownership with the owner, but must be repaid with interest regardless of profit and requires security.

The proportion of borrowed capital to own capital is called gearing. A business financed mostly by borrowing is highly geared; one financed mostly by its owner is lowly geared. The coffee business, with forty thousand rand of own capital and sixty thousand rand of borrowed capital, is financed sixty percent by debt.

Higher gearing increases both the possible reward and the risk. If the business does well, the owner earns profit on a business bigger than her own money could have built. If it does badly, the interest and repayments must still be paid, and losses fall on a small amount of own capital. A sensible rule for a small business is to borrow only for assets that will generate the income to repay the loan, to keep the instalments affordable from realistic monthly profits, and to keep an emergency reserve of cash.

Before borrowing, an entrepreneur should compare offers by the total cost of the loan, not only the monthly instalment, read the conditions carefully, and avoid lenders who are not registered with the National Credit Regulator. A cheaper alternative is often to start smaller, with more own capital, and grow from retained profits.

The questions for this section are with you now: comparing own and borrowed capital, the meaning of gearing, and rules for borrowing wisely.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through one coffee trailer and one hundred thousand rand, plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: The Coffee Trailer

A young woman stands outside the taxi rank at six in the morning and counts the people: hundreds, all tired, many of them buying sweet coffee from a flask at the corner. She thinks: a proper coffee trailer, good espresso, quick service. That thinking, spotting the chance and being willing to take the risk, is the fourth factor of production: entrepreneurship.

To build her dream she needs the other three. The spot outside the rank, the water, the coffee beans grown on a farm somewhere: natural resources. A barista to help her in the rush: labour. And the trailer, the espresso machine, the grinder, and the money to buy them: capital.

Each factor gets paid. The landowner gets rent for the spot. The barista gets wages. Whoever lends her money gets interest. And she, the entrepreneur, gets profit, whatever is left after everyone else is paid. If nothing is left, she carries the loss.

Now the money. The trailer and machine cost about seventy thousand rand: that is fixed capital, things she will use for years. She also needs about thirty thousand for the first beans, milk, cups, her trading permit and the barista's first wages before the customers pay enough: that is working capital, the money that keeps the business running day to day. Together, one hundred thousand rand.

Where will it come from? Her own savings, her aunt, the bank, or the coffee supplier. Each one is a different kind of money with a different price.

Keep the coffee trailer in mind and try the questions for this part: the four factors, their rewards, and fixed versus working capital.

## Subtopic: My Money or Borrowed Money

She opens her savings: thirty thousand rand. Her aunt gives her ten thousand as a gift towards the business. That is forty thousand rand of own capital. It is hers. Nobody will knock on the door asking for it back, and no interest is charged. But it is also everything she has saved, and if the trailer fails, it is gone.

She still needs sixty thousand. The bank offers a loan at twelve percent a year. That is borrowed capital. She can have the money now, but she must pay it back, plus interest. Twelve percent of sixty thousand is seven thousand two hundred rand a year. And here is the scary part: the bank wants its instalment every month, whether it rained and nobody bought coffee or not. If she cannot pay, the bank can take the trailer, because the trailer is the security for the loan.

The coffee supplier offers something else: deliver the beans now, pay in thirty days. That is borrowed too, a short-term kind called trade credit, and if she pays on time it costs nothing extra.

So her mix is forty thousand of her own, sixty thousand borrowed. More than half is borrowed. If business booms, she owns a bigger business than her savings alone could buy. If business is slow, the loan payments still come.

What would a wise friend say? Borrow only what the coffee sales can repay. Keep a little money aside for bad weeks. Compare loans by the total cost, not just the monthly payment. And never borrow from someone who is not registered.

There is one more path many South Africans know well: the stokvel. A group of people each pay in a fixed amount every month, and the pot goes to one member at a time, or is lent to members at an agreed rate. If she joined a business stokvel, her turn at the pot could become own capital, or a cheaper loan from people who know her. It works because members trust each other and keep each other honest.

Keep the trailer in mind and try the questions for this part: own or borrowed, and how much the loan really costs.
