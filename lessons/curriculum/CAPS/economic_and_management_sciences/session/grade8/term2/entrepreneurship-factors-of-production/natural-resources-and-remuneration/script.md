# Part 1 — Expert

This session completes the factors of production with natural resources, and then brings all four factors together through their remuneration: rent, wages and salaries, interest and profit. The anchor is a family farm in the Free State that grows maize and sunflowers. The farm uses land, rain and borehole water, sunshine and soil, the natural resources; it employs farm workers and a mechanic, the labour; it uses a tractor, a planter and a silo bought partly with a bank loan, the capital; and it is run by an owner who decides what to plant and carries the risk of drought and low prices, the entrepreneur. At harvest, the money from selling the crop is divided between the four factors, and what is left over is the farmer's profit, or loss. The governing idea is that natural resources are the gifts of nature used in production, that they are scarce and must be used sustainably, and that each factor of production receives its own form of remuneration, which together make up the income flowing from businesses to households in the economy. Natural resources, renewable and non-renewable, South Africa's resources, the four forms of remuneration and the calculation of profit as the residual are examinable, and they lead into Grade 10 Economics and Business Studies.

## Subtopic: Natural Resources

Natural resources, also called land in economics, are all the gifts of nature that are used in the production of goods and services. They include the land surface itself, on which farms, factories and houses stand; the soil; water in rivers, dams and underground; minerals and energy sources such as gold, platinum, coal, iron ore and natural gas; plants and forests; animals and fish; and climate, including sunlight and wind.

Natural resources are classified as renewable or non-renewable. Renewable resources can be replaced naturally within a human timescale if they are used wisely: forests that regrow, fish stocks that reproduce, water that is replenished by rain, sunlight and wind. Non-renewable resources exist in fixed amounts and cannot be replaced once used: minerals and fossil fuels such as coal, oil and gas. Even renewable resources can be exhausted if they are overused faster than they recover, as happens with overfishing or with groundwater pumped faster than rain refills it.

Natural resources have three special features. Their supply is limited: there is only so much land and water. They are not evenly distributed: some regions have rich soil, minerals or rainfall, others do not. And they are not made by people, although their usefulness depends on human effort, skill and capital to extract, grow or process them.

South Africa is rich in some natural resources and poor in others. It has some of the world's largest reserves of platinum group metals, manganese and chrome, as well as gold, coal, iron ore and diamonds, which made mining the foundation of the modern economy. It has excellent sunshine and good wind resources for renewable energy, a long coastline with fisheries, and diverse farming regions. But it is a water-scarce country, with low and unreliable rainfall in many areas, and only a small share of its land is suitable for growing crops; much of the rest is suited only to grazing.

The questions for this section are with you now: the definition of natural resources, renewable and non-renewable resources, their special features, and South Africa's resource strengths and weaknesses.

## Subtopic: Using Natural Resources Sustainably

Because natural resources are scarce, businesses must use them efficiently, and because many cannot be replaced, they must use them sustainably, so that future generations can also meet their needs.

On the farm, sustainable use means protecting the soil from erosion by ploughing along contours and keeping crop residues on the field; rotating crops so the soil is not exhausted; using fertilisers and pesticides carefully so they do not pollute rivers; using water efficiently through drip or pivot irrigation scheduled by soil moisture; and protecting wetlands and natural veld.

In mining, it means rehabilitating land after mining, managing waste dumps and treating mine water to prevent acid mine drainage. In fishing, it means quotas that limit catches to what stocks can replace, as South Africa applies to species such as hake and abalone. In energy, it means a gradual shift from coal towards renewable sources such as solar and wind.

Businesses have strong reasons to use resources sustainably beyond obeying the law. Resources that are wasted must be paid for, so efficiency lowers costs. A farm whose soil is eroded becomes less productive and less valuable. Customers, investors and banks increasingly prefer businesses with good environmental records. And a business that depends on a resource, such as a fishery or a water supply, destroys its own future if it exhausts that resource.

Government regulates the use of natural resources through laws on mining rights, water use licences, environmental impact assessments and fishing quotas, and through taxes and levies such as the carbon tax. Under the Constitution, mineral and water resources are held by the state as custodian on behalf of all South Africans, and businesses must obtain rights or licences to use them.

The questions for this section are with you now: examples of sustainable use on farms, in mines, in fishing and in energy, why it makes business sense, and how government regulates resource use.

## Subtopic: Remuneration of the Factors of Production

Each factor of production receives a reward, called remuneration, for its contribution to production.

Natural resources earn rent. Rent is the payment for the use of land or other natural resources owned by someone else: the farmer who leases an extra field from a neighbour pays rent; a mining company pays royalties to the state for the minerals it extracts, which is a form of rent; a shop pays rent for its premises. If the farmer owns the land, the rent it could have earned is an opportunity cost.

Labour earns wages and salaries. Wages are usually paid per hour, day or week, often to workers whose hours vary, such as farm workers during harvest. Salaries are usually a fixed monthly amount, such as the farm manager's salary.

Capital earns interest. Interest is the payment for the use of money or capital goods supplied by someone else: the farmer pays interest on the bank loan for the tractor. A household that saves money in a bank earns interest because the bank lends it to businesses.

Entrepreneurship earns profit. Profit is what remains of the business's income after paying rent, wages and salaries, interest and all other costs. It rewards the entrepreneur for organising production and bearing risk. It is not guaranteed and may be a loss.

Consider the farm's year. Income from selling maize and sunflowers is five hundred thousand rand. The farmer pays rent of sixty thousand rand for a leased field, wages and salaries of two hundred and twenty thousand rand, and interest of thirty thousand rand on the loan, and has no other costs in this simplified example. Profit is five hundred thousand minus sixty thousand minus two hundred and twenty thousand minus thirty thousand, which is one hundred and ninety thousand rand. In a drought year, if income fell to two hundred and eighty thousand rand while rent, wages and interest stayed the same, the farmer would make a loss of thirty thousand rand: the other factors are still paid, and the entrepreneur bears the loss.

## Subtopic: Remuneration in the Circular Flow

The remuneration of the factors of production connects businesses and households in the circular flow of the economy, which you met in Term 1.

Households own the factors of production. Individuals own their own labour; households own land and buildings, savings that become capital through banks and shares, and the entrepreneurial ability of their members. Businesses need these factors to produce. So in the factor market, households supply natural resources, labour, capital and entrepreneurship to businesses, and businesses pay households rent, wages and salaries, interest and profit. That is a real flow of factors from households to businesses, and a money flow of factor income from businesses to households.

Households then spend that income in the goods and services market, buying the products businesses make. That is a real flow of goods and services from businesses to households, and a money flow of consumer spending from households to businesses. The money that businesses receive pays for the factors again, and the circle continues.

This explains why the remuneration of the factors is the same thing as household income. The total income of all households in the economy is, in effect, the total rent, wages, interest and profit paid by producers. When businesses produce more, they pay more factor income, and households can spend more.

The share of income going to each factor matters for inequality. In South Africa, a large share of national income goes to the owners of capital and to highly skilled workers, while many households own no capital and have no employment, so they receive little factor income at all and depend on grants. Broadening the ownership of capital and land, and raising skills and employment, are ways of widening the share of factor income that ordinary households receive.

The questions for this section are with you now: households as owners of the factors, factor income in the circular flow, and why the distribution of factor income affects inequality.

## Subtopic: What Nature Gives the Farm

Stand in a maize field in the Free State in summer. Look down: soil, dark and deep. Look up: sun and, if the farmer is lucky, rain clouds. Look at the edge of the field: a borehole pumping water from underground. All of that came from nature, not from a factory. That is the first factor of production: natural resources.

Some of nature's gifts come back by themselves, if you are careful: the sun shines again tomorrow, the rain comes again next summer, a field that is looked after grows crops year after year. Those are renewable. Other gifts are used up for good: the coal under the next farm, the gold deep below Johannesburg. Once they are dug up, they are gone. Those are non-renewable.

But even renewable things can be ruined. If the farmer ploughs straight down a slope, the rain washes the topsoil away, and soil that took thousands of years to form is lost in one storm. If he pumps the borehole too hard, it runs dry. So a smart farmer ploughs across the slope, rotates his crops, waters carefully and leaves the wetland alone. That is sustainable use: using nature today without stealing it from tomorrow.

South Africa got a mixed bag from nature: lots of gold, platinum, coal and sunshine, but not much rain and not much land good for crops. That makes water and good soil especially precious here.

## Subtopic: Splitting the Harvest Cheque

Harvest time. The farmer sells his maize and sunflowers and gets a cheque, well, an EFT, for five hundred thousand rand. Now he has to share it, because four factors of production helped grow that crop, and each one gets paid.

The neighbour whose field he leased gets rent: sixty thousand rand. That is the reward for natural resources.

The farm workers and the mechanic get wages and salaries: two hundred and twenty thousand rand. That is the reward for labour.

The bank that lent money for the tractor gets interest: thirty thousand rand. That is the reward for capital.

And the farmer, who decided what to plant, took the risk and worked it all out? He gets what is left over. Five hundred thousand minus sixty thousand minus two hundred and twenty thousand minus thirty thousand: one hundred and ninety thousand rand. That is profit, the reward for entrepreneurship.

Now imagine a drought. The crop is small, and the cheque is only two hundred and eighty thousand rand. The neighbour still wants his rent, the workers still need their wages, the bank still wants its interest: three hundred and ten thousand rand altogether. The farmer is short by thirty thousand rand. That is a loss, and he carries it alone. That is the risk the entrepreneur takes.

Here is a handy way to remember the pairs. Picture four people around the kitchen table at harvest time. The landowner holds out his hand for rent, because he supplied the field, the soil and the water that falls on it. The workers hold out their hands for wages, because they supplied their time, their strength and their skill: ploughing, planting, fixing the tractor and driving the trucks to the silo. The bank holds out its hand for interest, because it supplied the money that bought the tractor and the seed before a single cob was harvested. And the farmer waits until last, because he supplied the idea, the planning and the courage to risk everything on one season. Land and rent. Labour and wages. Capital and interest. Entrepreneurship and profit. Say them in pairs until they stick.

Notice something important about the order. Rent, wages and interest are agreed before the season begins. The landowner signs a lease, the workers agree their pay, the bank sets its interest rate. They know what they will get. Only the farmer does not know. If the rains are good and prices are high, his share grows. If the rains fail or prices fall, his share shrinks, and it can even disappear completely. That is why we say profit is a reward for risk. Nobody would take that risk unless there was a chance of a good reward at the end.

Nature also needs looking after if the farm is to keep paying everybody year after year. If the farmer ploughs too deeply, the topsoil blows away in the wind. If he pumps too much borehole water, the water table drops and the neighbours' boreholes run dry. If he sprays too many chemicals, the river below the farm gets polluted. Good farmers rotate their crops, leave stubble on the fields to protect the soil, and measure the water they use. That way the natural resources keep producing, and the rent, wages, interest and profit keep flowing for the next generation too.

And where does all that money go? The rent, the wages, the interest and the profit go home to households, the neighbour's, the workers', the savers' and the farmer's, and they spend it at the shops in town. The money goes round and round. That is the circular flow.

Keep the harvest cheque in mind and try the questions for this part: rent, wages, interest, profit, and who carries the loss.
