### subtopic_1 — Labour and Levels of Skill
**Assistant:** Why does a chef earn more than a dishwasher if both work hard?

**Tutor:** Because wages are set mainly by the supply of and demand for each kind of skill, not by how hard the work feels. Washing dishes for eight hours is physically demanding, and a dishwasher may work just as hard as a chef. But the skill can be learned quickly, so many people are able and willing to do it. With a large supply of possible workers, employers do not need to offer high wages to fill the job. A trained chef has skills that took years of study and practice: planning menus, managing a kitchen, controlling food costs, keeping hygiene standards and producing consistent quality under pressure. Fewer people have those skills, and a restaurant's success depends heavily on them, so restaurants compete for good chefs by offering higher pay. Economists call the extra earnings that come from education and training a return on human capital. There are also other factors. Jobs with more responsibility, such as supervising staff or handling money, usually pay more. Some jobs pay more to compensate for unpleasant hours or risk. And historically in South Africa, apartheid laws reserved skilled jobs for white workers and denied quality education to the majority, which is one reason wage differences still follow racial lines today, and why the Employment Equity Act and skills development programmes exist. The national minimum wage sets a floor so that unskilled work cannot be paid below a basic level, while training and education offer the most reliable path to higher earnings.

### subtopic_2 — The Role of Workers
**Assistant:** Does the national minimum wage cause job losses?

**Tutor:** Economists disagree, and the honest answer is that it depends on how high the minimum wage is set relative to what businesses can afford and what workers produce. The argument for a minimum wage is that it protects the lowest-paid workers from exploitation, reduces poverty and inequality, and increases spending in the economy, because low-paid workers spend most of what they earn. It also encourages employers to invest in training to raise productivity. The argument against is that if the minimum wage is higher than the value some workers produce for small businesses, employers may hire fewer workers, cut hours, invest in machines instead, or operate informally and illegally. Small businesses and farms in poorer regions may find it hardest to pay. When South Africa introduced its national minimum wage in 2019, it was set after long negotiation between government, business, labour and community representatives at NEDLAC, the National Economic Development and Labour Council, and it was set below the wages already paid in many sectors to limit job losses. Some sectors, such as domestic work and farm work, were initially given lower rates that have since been brought up to the full rate. Each year the National Minimum Wage Commission recommends an increase, considering inflation, the cost of living, the ability of businesses to pay and the effect on employment. Research on South Africa's experience so far has generally found modest effects on employment, but the debate continues, especially given high unemployment. For an exam answer, give both sides.

### subtopic_3 — Fair Employment Laws
**Assistant:** What makes a dismissal fair or unfair?

**Tutor:** Under the Labour Relations Act, a dismissal is fair only if there is a fair reason and a fair procedure. Both are needed. There are three kinds of fair reason. The first is the employee's conduct, meaning serious misconduct such as theft, assault, gross dishonesty or repeated breaking of workplace rules after warnings. The second is incapacity, meaning the employee cannot do the job because of poor performance despite training and support, or because of long-term ill health or injury, after the employer has considered alternatives. The third is operational requirements, meaning the business must cut jobs for economic, technological or structural reasons, such as a downturn in sales, which is called retrenchment and has its own consultation rules. A fair procedure for misconduct means the employee is told what he or she is accused of, is given time to prepare, has a chance to answer at a hearing with a representative such as a union official or colleague, and is told the outcome and the reasons. For poor performance, the employee must be told what standard is expected and given guidance, training and time to improve. Some dismissals are automatically unfair, such as dismissing a worker for joining a union, for being pregnant, or on discriminatory grounds. A worker who believes he or she was unfairly dismissed can refer the dispute to the CCMA within thirty days. If the dismissal is found unfair, the remedy can be reinstatement or compensation.

### subtopic_4 — Wages, Overtime and Payslips
**Assistant:** Why do both the worker and the employer pay into the UIF?

**Tutor:** Because unemployment insurance works like any insurance: many people pay small regular contributions into a fund, so that the few who suffer a loss in a particular period can be supported. Each month, the employer deducts one percent of the worker's earnings and adds another one percent of its own, and pays both to the Unemployment Insurance Fund, up to an earnings ceiling. If the worker later loses the job through no fault of his or her own, such as retrenchment or the end of a contract, the worker can claim benefits for a limited period, calculated on a sliding scale of the previous earnings. The fund also pays benefits during long illness, maternity leave, adoption leave and parental leave, and to the dependants of a contributor who dies. During the COVID-19 lockdowns, the UIF paid a special benefit called TERS to millions of workers whose employers could not pay them. Sharing the contribution spreads the cost: workers contribute because they benefit directly, and employers contribute because a stable, protected workforce benefits the business and the economy, and because it is part of their responsibility as employers. Employers must register with the UIF and pay over contributions every month; failing to do so is an offence and can leave workers without protection when they most need it. That is why the UIF deduction must appear on every payslip, and why a worker should check that it is being paid over, which can be done through the Department of Employment and Labour.
