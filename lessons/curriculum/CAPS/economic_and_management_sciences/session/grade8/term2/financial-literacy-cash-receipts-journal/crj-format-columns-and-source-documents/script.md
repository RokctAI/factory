# Part 1 — Expert

This session introduces the cash receipts journal, CRJ, of a service business: what it is for, how its columns are laid out and used, and which source documents supply its entries. The anchor is the appliance repair business from the accounting cycle, whose bookkeeper sits down at the end of each day with a small pile of documents: duplicate receipts from the receipt book, the cash register roll from the counter, a deposit slip from the bank and, at month-end, the bank statement. Every one of those documents contains money received, and every one ends up as a line in the CRJ. The governing idea is that the CRJ is the subsidiary journal in which all money received by a business is recorded in date order, and that its columns are designed so that each receipt is entered once, in the bank column for the double entry with the bank account, and once again in the column that names the account to be credited. The purpose of the CRJ, the meaning and use of each column, and the source documents used are examinable, and the CRJ is used in every grade of Accounting to Grade 12.

## Subtopic: The Purpose of the Cash Receipts Journal

The cash receipts journal is a book of first entry in which all money received by the business is recorded, in the order in which it was received, from the source documents. In Grade 8 we assume that all money received is deposited in the business bank account on the same day, so the CRJ records receipts into the bank.

A service business receives money from a limited number of sources: current income for services rendered, capital contributed by the owner, loans received, rent income, interest income and, occasionally, the sale of an asset or payments from debtors. Some of these, especially current income, happen many times a month; others happen rarely.

The CRJ has three purposes. First, it records every receipt in one place, in date order, with a reference to its source document, creating a complete and checkable record. Second, it analyses the receipts, separating them by the account that must be credited, so that the business can see how much came from services, how much from the owner and how much from loans. Third, it saves work in posting: at the end of the month the columns are totalled, and the totals, rather than every individual receipt, are posted to the general ledger.

The CRJ belongs to the business that receives the money. Its partner is the cash payments journal, CPJ, which records all money paid out and which you will study in Term 3.

A CRJ is numbered, for example CRJ 3 for the third month, and each page is headed with the name of the business and the month, for example cash receipts journal of the repair business for March 2026. The journal number is used as a folio reference when amounts are posted to the ledger.

The questions for this section are with you now: the purpose of the CRJ, the types of receipt it records and its heading.

## Subtopic: The Columns of the CRJ

A CRJ for a service business usually has the following columns, from left to right.

The document column, Doc, shows the number of the source document, such as receipt 001, CRR for the cash register roll, or B/S for the bank statement. This creates the link back to the evidence.

The day column shows the day of the month on which the money was received. The month and year are in the heading, so only the day is written.

The details column shows who the money was received from, such as the name of the customer, the owner, the bank, or cash sales for the cash register roll.

The folio column, Fol, is completed only when the journal is posted, showing where amounts were posted. In a CRJ, the folio column next to the details is often left blank, because individual receipts are not posted from it, while the sundry accounts section has its own folio column.

The analysis of receipts column lists each individual amount received when more than one receipt is deposited on the same day. It allows the bookkeeper to show the separate amounts while the bank column shows only the total deposited.

The bank column shows the total amount deposited into the bank on each day. It must agree with the deposit slip. The total of this column is debited to the bank account in the ledger.

The current income column shows the amount of each receipt that is current income, the fee earned for the business's services. Its total is credited to the current income account. Some businesses name this column after their type of income, such as fees income.

The sundry accounts section has three sub-columns: amount, folio and details. It is used for any receipt that does not belong in an analysis column, such as capital, loan, rent income, interest income or debtors control, and the details sub-column names the account to be credited. Each sundry amount is posted individually.

Every receipt appears twice in the journal: once in the bank column, or through the analysis of receipts into the bank column, and once in either the current income column or the sundry accounts column. That double appearance reflects the double entry: debit bank, credit the account named.

The questions for this section are with you now: the name and use of each CRJ column, and why every receipt appears twice.

## Subtopic: Source Documents for the CRJ

Each entry in the CRJ must be supported by a source document. For a service business the documents are the following.

The duplicate receipt is the business's copy of a receipt issued when it receives money from a person, such as a customer paying for a repair, the owner contributing capital, or a tenant paying rent. The receipt number is entered in the document column. Receipts are pre-numbered, so the bookkeeper can check that every number has been entered.

The cash register roll, CRR, is the record kept by the cash register or point-of-sale system of all cash and card sales rung up during the day. The daily total is entered as one line, with CRR in the document column and cash sales or services rendered in the details column.

The deposit slip, or the cash deposit machine receipt, shows the total amount deposited at the bank. It is not usually written in the document column, but it is used to check that the bank column total for each day agrees with the actual deposit.

The bank statement is the source document for money that reaches the bank without the business issuing a receipt: direct deposits and EFTs from customers, loans paid in by the bank, and interest earned on the account. These are entered with B/S in the document column, usually when the statement arrives at month-end.

The duplicate cash invoice is also used where a business issues invoices for services paid immediately. The invoice number is entered in the document column.

Matching documents to entries is an important control. At the end of each day, the bookkeeper checks that every duplicate receipt has been entered, that the CRR total has been entered, and that the bank column agrees with the deposit slip. At month-end, the bank statement is compared with the CRJ to find any receipts not yet recorded.

The questions for this section are with you now: the source documents used for the CRJ, what goes in the document column for each, and how the deposit slip and bank statement are used as checks.

## Subtopic: Analysing a Receipt into Columns

Before entering a receipt, the bookkeeper asks two questions. First: what is the source document? That decides what goes in the document column. Second: which account must be credited? That decides whether the amount goes in the current income column or in the sundry accounts column, and if sundry, which account name goes in its details column.

Consider the repair business's March receipts. On the first, capital of sixty thousand rand from the owner, receipt 001: document 001, details the owner, bank sixty thousand, sundry accounts sixty thousand with details capital. On the third, the cash register roll of four thousand two hundred rand: document CRR, details cash sales, bank four thousand two hundred, current income four thousand two hundred. On the fifth, a loan of twenty-five thousand rand from a bank, shown on the bank statement: document B/S, details the bank, bank twenty-five thousand, sundry twenty-five thousand with details loan.

On the fifteenth, two receipts were deposited together: the cash register roll of three thousand six hundred rand and receipt 003 for nine hundred rand from a customer for a repair. The analysis of receipts column shows three thousand six hundred on the CRR line and nine hundred on the receipt 003 line; the bank column shows the total deposit of four thousand five hundred rand once; and the current income column shows three thousand six hundred and nine hundred on their own lines.

On the twentieth, rent income of two thousand five hundred rand, receipt 004: sundry with details rent income. On the thirty-first, interest of one hundred and fifty rand from the bank statement: document B/S, sundry with details interest income.

The most common errors are entering a loan or capital in the current income column, which overstates income, and forgetting to name the account in the sundry details column, which makes posting impossible.

The questions for this section are with you now: the two questions for every receipt, and entering capital, the CRR, a loan, a combined deposit, rent income and interest income in the correct columns.

## Subtopic: Conventions for Neat and Correct Journals

Accounting records must be clear enough for someone else, such as an auditor, a bank or SARS, to read and check them years later. A set of conventions, followed in exams as well as in practice, makes that possible.

Write in ink, never in pencil, so that entries cannot be rubbed out. If a mistake is made, draw a single neat line through the wrong figure so that it remains readable, and write the correct figure above it; never use correction fluid or tear out pages. A visible correction shows honesty; a hidden one invites suspicion.

Do not write the rand sign or the word rand next to amounts in money columns; the column heading already shows that amounts are in rand. Use spaces, not commas, to separate thousands, as South African practice does: sixty thousand is written six, zero, space, zero, zero, zero. Where cents are shown, they follow a comma in the South African style, or a separate cents column.

Write the month and year in the heading, and only the day in the day column. Write the name of the journal and its number at the top right, such as CRJ 3, because this number becomes the folio reference in the ledger.

Use standard abbreviations: CRR for cash register roll, B/S for bank statement, Doc for document, Fol for folio. Name accounts exactly as they appear in the ledger, such as current income, capital, loan: Big Bank, rent income and interest income, so that posting is unambiguous.

When the journal is closed off at month-end, rule a single line above the totals and a double line below them. The single line means add up; the double line means this is a final total and nothing more will be added. Totals are written on the same line across all columns, so that they can be cross-checked.

Finally, record from source documents in date order, and file the documents in the same order. A journal that is neat, complete and in order is evidence of good internal control.

The questions for this section are with you now: ink and corrections, the rand sign and spaces, the journal number, abbreviations, and the single and double lines.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through one notebook with lanes and one end-of-day pile, plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: A Notebook with Lanes

Picture the cash receipts journal as a notebook ruled into lanes, like a running track. Every time money comes in, it runs down the page in two lanes at once.

On the left are the labels: which paper it came from, which day, and who paid. Receipt 001, day one, the owner. CRR, day three, cash sales.

Then the money lanes. The bank lane catches every rand that reached the bank, every single time. Then there is the current income lane, for money earned from fixing appliances; this lane is busy almost every day. And on the far right is the sundry lane, a lane for the unusual visitors: the owner's capital, the bank's loan, the rent from the storeroom, the interest. Because sundry visitors are all different, they must say their name in the little details box next to the amount.

So when the owner puts in sixty thousand rand: bank lane sixty thousand, sundry lane sixty thousand, name tag capital. When a customer pays for a fixed fridge: bank lane, and current income lane. When the bank lends money: bank lane, and sundry lane with the name tag loan.

Every receipt runs in two lanes. That is not a mistake; it is the double entry. One lane says the money arrived in the bank. The other lane says what kind of money it was.

And there is one extra lane, analysis of receipts. It is for days when two or more amounts go to the bank together in one deposit. Each amount gets its own line in the analysis lane, and the bank lane shows just the total that was deposited.

Keep the lanes in mind and try the questions for this part: which lane does each receipt run in?

## Subtopic: The End-of-Day Pile

At closing time, the repair shop's bookkeeper empties the drawer onto the desk. Here is the pile and what to do with each piece.

The receipt book: every bottom copy is a receipt the shop wrote when someone paid. Each one becomes its own line, with its number in the document column.

The till roll: the cash register printed the day's total. One line, CRR in the document column, cash sales in the details, into current income.

The deposit slip from the bank: this one does not get its own line. It is the check. Add up everything the shop received today; it must equal the amount on the deposit slip. If it does not, something is missing or something was not banked.

At month-end one more paper arrives: the bank statement. It shows money that came straight into the bank without anyone writing a receipt: a customer who paid by EFT, a loan from the bank, a few rand of interest. Each of those becomes a line with B/S in the document column.

Here is the bookkeeper's two-question habit. Question one: which paper is this? That fills the document column. Question two: what kind of money is it? If it was earned by fixing appliances, current income lane. If it is anything else, sundry lane, with its name.

Keep the pile in mind and try the questions for this part: which paper, which lane, and what the deposit slip checks.
