# Part 1 — Expert

This session connects the cash receipts journal with the accounting equation. Every line in a CRJ is a transaction that changes assets, owner's equity or liabilities, and the totals of a closed CRJ summarise the effect of a whole month of receipts on the equation. The anchor is the appliance repair business again, with two closed journals: March, its first month, full of capital and a bank loan, and April, an ordinary trading month made up mostly of repairs. Reading the equation from the journal turns columns of figures into a statement about how the business's financial position changed. The governing idea is that the bank column of the CRJ always measures an increase in assets, that the current income column always measures an increase in owner's equity through income, and that each sundry entry must be analysed by its account name to decide whether it increases owner's equity, increases liabilities or decreases another asset. Analysing individual CRJ entries in the equation, summarising a month's CRJ in the equation and interpreting the result are examinable, and the skill is extended to the CPJ in Term 3.

## Subtopic: Reading a CRJ Line as an Equation Change

Every CRJ line has a debit to bank, so every line increases the asset bank. The column used for the credit tells you the rest.

If the amount is in the current income column, owner's equity increases by the same amount, and the reason is income. For example, the cash register roll of three thousand nine hundred and fifty rand on the first of April: assets plus three thousand nine hundred and fifty, owner's equity plus three thousand nine hundred and fifty, reason current income.

If the amount is in the sundry column, read the account name in the sundry details column and classify it.

Capital: owner's equity increases, reason capital contribution. The additional capital of ten thousand rand on the eighth of April: assets plus ten thousand, owner's equity plus ten thousand.

Rent income, interest income and other income accounts: owner's equity increases, reason income. Rent of two thousand five hundred rand: assets plus two thousand five hundred, owner's equity plus two thousand five hundred. Interest of one hundred and eighty rand: assets plus one hundred and eighty, owner's equity plus one hundred and eighty.

Loan: liabilities increase. The March loan of twenty-five thousand rand: assets plus twenty-five thousand, liabilities plus twenty-five thousand, owner's equity unchanged.

Debtors control or an asset sold at book value: another asset decreases. A debtor paying one thousand rand: assets plus one thousand, bank, and minus one thousand, debtors; owner's equity and liabilities unchanged.

Notice that the method of receipt, cash, card or EFT, never affects the analysis; only the account credited matters.

The questions for this section are with you now: reading the effect of a current income entry, and classifying each sundry account name as capital, income, liability or asset.

## Subtopic: Summarising a Month in the Equation

Because each column total summarises many lines of the same kind, the closed CRJ can be read as a summary of the month's effect on the equation.

The bank column total is the total increase in the asset bank. In April it was thirty-one thousand three hundred and thirty rand.

The current income total is the increase in owner's equity from fee income: eighteen thousand six hundred and fifty rand in April.

The sundry column must be split by account. In April: capital ten thousand rand, an increase in owner's equity through capital contribution; rent income two thousand five hundred rand and interest income one hundred and eighty rand, increases in owner's equity through income. There were no loans and no asset sales.

So April's receipts increased assets by thirty-one thousand three hundred and thirty rand, and increased owner's equity by eighteen thousand six hundred and fifty plus ten thousand plus two thousand five hundred plus one hundred and eighty, which is also thirty-one thousand three hundred and thirty rand. Liabilities did not change. The equation balances.

Now March, the first month. The bank total was one hundred and three thousand five hundred and fifty rand. Current income was fifteen thousand nine hundred rand. The sundry column contained capital sixty thousand rand, loan twenty-five thousand rand, rent income two thousand five hundred rand and interest income one hundred and fifty rand. Owner's equity therefore increased by fifteen thousand nine hundred plus sixty thousand plus two thousand five hundred plus one hundred and fifty, which is seventy-eight thousand five hundred and fifty rand, and liabilities increased by twenty-five thousand rand. Seventy-eight thousand five hundred and fifty plus twenty-five thousand is one hundred and three thousand five hundred and fifty. The equation balances.

The questions for this section are with you now: summarising the April and March CRJs in the equation, and splitting the sundry column by type of account.

## Subtopic: Separating Capital from Income

In both months owner's equity increased, but for different reasons, and the difference matters to anyone judging the business.

In March, of the seventy-eight thousand five hundred and fifty rand increase in owner's equity, sixty thousand rand was the owner's own capital and only eighteen thousand five hundred and fifty rand was income: current income fifteen thousand nine hundred, rent two thousand five hundred and interest one hundred and fifty. In April, of the thirty-one thousand three hundred and thirty rand increase, ten thousand rand was additional capital and twenty-one thousand three hundred and thirty rand was income: eighteen thousand six hundred and fifty plus two thousand five hundred plus one hundred and eighty.

Income is the part that shows the business earning money from others. Capital is the owner putting her own money in. An owner who sees her bank balance grow should always ask how much of the growth was earned and how much she supplied herself.

Two further points. First, a CRJ shows only receipts, so it cannot show profit. Profit needs expenses too, which are recorded in the cash payments journal. April's income of twenty-one thousand three hundred and thirty rand is not April's profit until the expenses paid in April are subtracted. Second, the CRJ shows only the increases in owner's equity caused by receipts. Drawings, which decrease owner's equity, are payments and appear in the CPJ.

This is why accountants keep separate ledger accounts for capital and for each type of income, and why the sundry details column must always name the account precisely. If capital were recorded as current income, owner's equity would still be correct in total, but income and profit would be badly overstated.

The questions for this section are with you now: separating capital from income in each month, why the CRJ cannot show profit, and the effect of recording capital as income.

## Subtopic: Exam-Style Analysis from a CRJ

Exam questions often give a completed CRJ, or a list of its entries, and ask for an analysis table. Work through each line with three questions. Which account is debited? Always bank. Which account is credited? Read the column and, for sundry, the details. What is the effect on assets, owner's equity and liabilities, and the reason for any change in owner's equity?

A sample set: receipt 008, rent from the tenant, two thousand five hundred rand. Debit bank, credit rent income. Assets plus two thousand five hundred; owner's equity plus two thousand five hundred, income; liabilities zero.

Bank statement, loan from a bank, twenty thousand rand. Debit bank, credit loan. Assets plus twenty thousand; owner's equity zero; liabilities plus twenty thousand.

Cash register roll, four thousand rand. Debit bank, credit current income. Assets plus four thousand; owner's equity plus four thousand, income; liabilities zero.

Receipt 009, the owner's additional capital, eight thousand rand. Debit bank, credit capital. Assets plus eight thousand; owner's equity plus eight thousand, capital contribution; liabilities zero.

Receipt 010, a customer settling an account owed, one thousand two hundred rand. Debit bank, credit debtors control. Assets plus one thousand two hundred and minus one thousand two hundred; owner's equity zero; liabilities zero.

Totals: assets increase by two thousand five hundred plus twenty thousand plus four thousand plus eight thousand, which is thirty-four thousand five hundred rand net, because the debtor's payment changes no total. Owner's equity increases by two thousand five hundred plus four thousand plus eight thousand, fourteen thousand five hundred rand, and liabilities by twenty thousand rand. Fourteen thousand five hundred plus twenty thousand is thirty-four thousand five hundred. Balanced.

The questions for this section are with you now: completing an analysis table from CRJ entries, including a debtor's payment, and checking the totals.

## Subtopic: The Equation, the Ledger and Common Errors

The equation analysis and the ledger tell the same story in two forms. When the April CRJ is posted, the bank account is debited with thirty-one thousand three hundred and thirty rand, the increase in assets. Current income, capital, rent income and interest income are credited with eighteen thousand six hundred and fifty, ten thousand, two thousand five hundred and one hundred and eighty rand, the increases in owner's equity. If there had been a loan, the loan account would have been credited, the increase in liabilities. In other words, every debit posted from the CRJ is an increase on the left of the equation, and every credit is an increase on the right, or, for debtors and assets sold, a decrease on the left. Seeing this link makes the debit and credit rules easier to remember: assets increase on the debit side because they sit on the left of the equation, and owner's equity and liabilities increase on the credit side because they sit on the right.

Examiners look for five common errors in CRJ analysis.

First, treating a loan as income: owner's equity is wrongly increased and liabilities are left unchanged. Second, giving no reason, or the wrong reason, when owner's equity changes: capital contribution and income must be distinguished. Third, recording a debtor's payment or the sale of an asset at book value as income, which overstates owner's equity. Fourth, forgetting that the bank increases on every line, so that a receipt is shown only as an increase in owner's equity with no change in assets, which leaves the equation unbalanced. Fifth, adding the whole bank column and calling it income, which confuses receipts with earnings.

A good habit is to finish every analysis by checking each line separately, the change on the left equal to the change on the right, and then the column totals. If both checks pass and every owner's equity change has the correct reason, the analysis is almost certainly right.

The questions for this section are with you now: how CRJ postings match the equation, and the five common errors.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through one CRJ and two questions, plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Two Questions for Every Line

Look at any line in the cash receipts journal and ask two questions.

Question one: did the bank go up? Always yes. Every line in the CRJ is money that came in, so assets go up, every time. Easy.

Question two: what kind of money was it? Look at which column the second amount is in.

If it is in current income, it was earned from the business's services. Earned money belongs to the owner, so owner's equity goes up. Write the reason: income.

If it is in sundry, read the name tag. Capital: the owner's own money, owner's equity up, reason capital. Rent income or interest income: earned, owner's equity up, reason income. Loan: borrowed, so liabilities go up, and owner's equity does not move. Debtors: someone paying what they owed, so one asset goes up and another goes down, and nothing else moves.

That is it. Two questions, and you can turn every CRJ line into a change in the accounting equation.

Try April's lines. The till roll: bank up, owner's equity up, income. The owner's extra ten thousand: bank up, owner's equity up, capital. The storeroom rent: bank up, owner's equity up, income. The customer's EFT for a stove: bank up, owner's equity up, income. The bank's interest: bank up, owner's equity up, income. No loans in April, so liabilities do not change at all.

Here is a trap to dodge. When the money came through the bank statement, some learners think it must be sundry. Not so. The customer who paid for a stove by EFT still paid for a repair, so it is still current income. Where the paper came from tells you what to write in the document column, not what kind of money it is. Only the second question, what kind of money, decides the lane and the effect on the equation.

Keep the two questions in mind and try the questions for this part: what moved, and why?

## Subtopic: What the Month Is Telling You

Now step back and look at a whole month at once.

March, the repair shop's first month: one hundred and three thousand five hundred and fifty rand came into the bank. Sounds rich! But read the name tags. Sixty thousand was the owner's own money. Twenty-five thousand was a loan that must be paid back. Only eighteen thousand five hundred and fifty was actually earned. So the honest story of March is: the owner and the bank set the shop up, and it started earning.

April: thirty-one thousand three hundred and thirty rand came in. Ten thousand was the owner topping up. Twenty-one thousand three hundred and thirty was earned. No borrowing. That is a healthier story: most of the money now comes from the business doing its job.

On the scale: in March the left pan grew by one hundred and three thousand five hundred and fifty, and the right pan grew by the same, seventy-eight thousand five hundred and fifty on the owner's side and twenty-five thousand on the debts side. In April the left pan grew by thirty-one thousand three hundred and thirty, all on the owner's side. Level both times.

But one warning. Money in is only half the story. The CRJ does not show the wages, the electricity or the rent the shop paid. Profit needs both halves, and the other half lives in the cash payments journal, which is coming in Term 3.

So when someone shows you a big bank balance, be the accountant in the room. Ask three questions: how much was the owner's own money, how much was borrowed, and how much was earned? Only the last answer tells you whether the business can stand on its own feet.

Keep the two months in mind and try the questions for this part: how much was earned, how much was the owner's, and how much was borrowed.
