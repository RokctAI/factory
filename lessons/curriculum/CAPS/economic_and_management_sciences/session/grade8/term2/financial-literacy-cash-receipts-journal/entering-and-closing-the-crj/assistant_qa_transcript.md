### subtopic_1 — The Entering Routine
**Assistant:** Why is the bank total for a combined deposit written only on the last line of the group?

**Tutor:** Because the bank column must show the amount that actually reached the bank, and on that day the bank received one deposit, not two. On the fourth of April the repair business banked a customer's one thousand two hundred and fifty rand and the till's two thousand one hundred rand together. The deposit slip and the bank statement will both show a single amount of three thousand three hundred and fifty rand. If the bookkeeper wrote both separate amounts in the bank column, the CRJ would show two lines that do not appear on the statement, making the month-end comparison confusing. If the bookkeeper wrote the separate amounts and the total, the bank column would count the money twice, and the cross-cast would fail by three thousand three hundred and fifty rand. Writing the total once, on the last line of the group, keeps the bank column equal to actual deposits. The analysis of receipts column holds the individual amounts, so each source document still has its own visible line, and the current income column shows each amount against its own document. Some textbooks place the bank total on the first line of the group instead; both are acceptable as long as it appears once and the format is used consistently. What matters is the principle: individual documents in the analysis and income columns, one deposit total in the bank column. This also helps detect fraud: if the deposit slip shows less than the sum of the individual receipts, some money was not banked.

### subtopic_2 — Entering the April Receipts
**Assistant:** If the cross-cast agrees, can the CRJ still contain errors?

**Tutor:** Yes, because the cross-cast only proves that every amount in the bank column was also entered in current income or sundry, and that the additions are correct. It cannot tell whether the right amount or the right column was used. Several errors pass the cross-cast. If additional capital of ten thousand rand were entered in the current income column instead of sundry, the bank total would still equal current income plus sundry, but current income would be overstated by ten thousand rand and capital would be missing from the sundry postings. That error would make profit look ten thousand rand higher. If a receipt for one thousand two hundred and fifty rand were entered as one thousand five hundred and twenty rand in both columns, the cross-cast would agree, but both totals would be wrong. If a receipt were left out completely, the cross-cast would agree but the CRJ would be incomplete. If a sundry amount were labelled with the wrong account name, such as rent income written as interest income, the totals would be correct but the posting would go to the wrong account. That is why the cross-cast is one check among several. The others are comparing every entry with its source document, checking that receipt numbers run in sequence with none missing, checking each day's bank amount against the deposit slip, and comparing the month's receipts with the bank statement. Together these checks catch most errors before they reach the ledger.

### subtopic_3 — Closing Off the CRJ
**Assistant:** Why are the sundry amounts posted on different dates in some textbooks?

**Tutor:** Because there are two accepted conventions for dating sundry postings, and different textbooks follow different ones. In the first convention, every amount from the CRJ, including sundry amounts, is posted with the month-end date, such as the thirtieth of April, because posting happens when the journal is closed off at the end of the month. In the second convention, analysis column totals are posted with the month-end date, because they are totals for the whole month, but each sundry amount is posted with the date of the actual transaction, such as the eighth of April for the additional capital and the eighteenth for the rent, because each one is an individual transaction that can be dated precisely. The second convention gives more information in the ledger: someone looking at the capital account can see exactly when the owner contributed the extra money. Both conventions produce the same balances, because the amounts and sides are identical; only the date column differs. In an exam, follow the convention used in your textbook or shown in the question's example, and apply it consistently. The more important points, which carry most of the marks, are the side, the amount, the details, which name the contra account, bank, and the folio, which shows the journal and month, such as CRJ 4. Never post the sundry total as one amount, because there is no sundry account in the ledger.

### subtopic_4 — Posting the CRJ to the General Ledger
**Assistant:** What should the bookkeeper do if the bank statement shows an amount not in the CRJ?

**Tutor:** First find out what it is, and then, if it is a genuine receipt the business has not yet recorded, enter it in the CRJ from the bank statement before closing the journal. When the April bank statement arrived, the bookkeeper compared it line by line with the CRJ. Most entries matched the deposits made from the cash register rolls and receipts. Two amounts did not appear in the CRJ yet: the EFT of one thousand seven hundred and fifty rand from a customer and the interest of one hundred and eighty rand. Both were genuine receipts that the business could only learn about from the statement, so they were entered with B/S in the document column, the EFT as current income because it was for a repair, and the interest as sundry, interest income. Sometimes the unknown amount needs investigation. A deposit might have no clear reference, and the bookkeeper must contact customers to find out who paid and for what. It might be a mistake by the bank, money meant for another account, in which case the bank must be told and the amount not recorded as income. The comparison also works the other way. If the CRJ shows a deposit that does not appear on the statement, the bookkeeper must check whether it is still being processed, typically a deposit made on the last day of the month, or whether the money was never banked. In Grade 9 and Grade 10 you will formalise this comparison as the bank reconciliation.
