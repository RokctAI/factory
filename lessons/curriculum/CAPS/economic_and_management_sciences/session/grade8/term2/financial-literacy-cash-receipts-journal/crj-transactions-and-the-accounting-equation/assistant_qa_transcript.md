### subtopic_1 — Reading a CRJ Line as an Equation Change
**Assistant:** Why does the method of receipt never change the effect on the equation?

**Tutor:** Because the accounting equation records what the business owns and who has a claim on it, and the method of payment changes neither. Suppose a customer pays one thousand seven hundred and fifty rand for a stove repair. If she pays in notes at the counter, the business deposits the notes and its bank increases by one thousand seven hundred and fifty rand. If she taps her card, the money reaches the bank account electronically, and the bank increases by the same amount. If she pays by EFT from home, the bank increases by the same amount. In every case the business has earned one thousand seven hundred and fifty rand of income for a repair, so owner's equity increases by one thousand seven hundred and fifty rand. The method only affects which source document is used, a receipt, the cash register roll or the bank statement, and therefore what is written in the document column. It does not affect which column the amount goes in, or how assets, owner's equity and liabilities change. Learners sometimes treat an EFT shown on the bank statement as sundry, because it came from the bank statement like interest and loans. That is the error to avoid. Interest and loans are sundry because of what they are, income from the bank and borrowed money, not because of where the record came from. Always classify by what the money was for. This principle carries into the CPJ: whether wages are paid by EFT or in cash, they are still an expense that reduces owner's equity.

### subtopic_2 — Summarising a Month in the Equation
**Assistant:** How can March's bank receipts be huge while the business has earned so little?

**Tutor:** Because most of March's receipts were not earned at all; they were financing. In its first month, the repair business received one hundred and three thousand five hundred and fifty rand. Sixty thousand rand was the owner's capital and twenty-five thousand rand was a bank loan. Together that is eighty-five thousand rand of financing, money supplied by the owner and a lender to set the business up. Only eighteen thousand five hundred and fifty rand was income: fifteen thousand nine hundred from repairs, two thousand five hundred rent and one hundred and fifty interest. This is completely normal for a new business, which needs money to buy equipment and cover its first expenses before customers arrive in large numbers. But it shows why a bank balance is a poor measure of success on its own. An owner who looked only at her bank app might feel very successful at the end of March, when in fact the business's own earning power was still small, and twenty-five thousand rand of the money must be repaid with interest. The equation analysis protects against this mistake by keeping three things separate: capital, which increases owner's equity but is not earned; income, which increases owner's equity because it was earned; and loans, which increase liabilities. In Grade 10 you will see this separation formalised in the cash flow statement, which divides cash movements into operating, investing and financing activities. For now, always ask of a large receipt: was it earned, contributed or borrowed?

### subtopic_3 — Separating Capital from Income
**Assistant:** Can you work out the business's profit from the CRJ?

**Tutor:** No, only the income part of it, because profit is income minus expenses, and expenses are not in the CRJ. The CRJ records money received. Its current income column and the income accounts in the sundry column, such as rent income and interest income, give you the total income received in cash for the month: in April, eighteen thousand six hundred and fifty plus two thousand five hundred plus one hundred and eighty, which is twenty-one thousand three hundred and thirty rand. But during April the business also paid wages, water and electricity, stationery, bank charges and perhaps rent for its own premises. Those payments are recorded in the cash payments journal, which you will study in Term 3. Only when the expenses from the CPJ are subtracted from the income from the CRJ can profit be calculated. Even then, a complete profit figure in later grades will include adjustments for income earned but not yet received and expenses incurred but not yet paid, which cash journals alone do not capture. There is a second reason the CRJ alone cannot show profit: some receipts in it are not income at all. Capital and loans increase the bank but have nothing to do with profit. If you added the whole bank column and called it income, April's figure would be thirty-one thousand three hundred and thirty rand instead of twenty-one thousand three hundred and thirty, overstating income by the ten thousand rand the owner put in. So from the CRJ you can find total receipts and total cash income, but profit needs the CPJ as well.

### subtopic_4 — Exam-Style Analysis from a CRJ
**Assistant:** What happens in the equation when an asset is sold for its book value?

**Tutor:** The total of assets stays the same, because one asset is exchanged for another of equal value, and neither owner's equity nor liabilities change. Suppose the repair business sells an old drill press, recorded in its books at three thousand rand, for three thousand rand, and the buyer pays by EFT. In the CRJ, the bank column shows three thousand rand and the sundry column shows three thousand rand with the details equipment. In the equation, assets increase by three thousand rand, bank, and decrease by three thousand rand, equipment. The net change in assets is zero. Owner's equity is unchanged because nothing was earned: the business did not provide a service, it simply converted a piece of equipment into cash. Liabilities are unchanged because nothing was borrowed or repaid. This is the same pattern as a debtor paying an account, which converts the asset debtors into the asset bank. When you summarise a month's CRJ in the equation, these swaps are the reason the bank total can be larger than the total increase in assets. In the photography studio example earlier in the term, receipts totalled eighty-three thousand six hundred and eighty-five rand, but three thousand nine hundred rand were swaps, so total assets increased by only seventy-nine thousand seven hundred and eighty-five rand. In Grade 10 you will learn that assets are often sold for more or less than their book value, creating a profit or loss on sale that does change owner's equity.
