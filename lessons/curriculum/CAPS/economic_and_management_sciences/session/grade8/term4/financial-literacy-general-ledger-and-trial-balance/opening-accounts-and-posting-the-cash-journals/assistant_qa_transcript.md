### subtopic_1 — Opening Ledger Accounts
**Assistant:** Why is the bank total posted with the details total receipts instead of an account name?

**Tutor:** Because the bank column total is made up of many receipts that were credited to several different accounts, so no single account name would be correct. In most ledger entries, the details column names the contra account, the account that holds the other half of the double entry. When wages of six thousand rand are posted to the wages account, the details say bank, because bank was credited with the same amount. But the CRJ bank total of thirty-one thousand three hundred and thirty rand was matched by credits to four different accounts: current income eighteen thousand six hundred and fifty, capital ten thousand, rent income two thousand five hundred and interest income one hundred and eighty. Writing all four names in the bank account would be clumsy, and writing only one would be wrong. So the convention is to write total receipts for the CRJ total and total payments for the CPJ total. The folio, CRJ 4 or CPJ 4, then tells anyone who wants the detail exactly where to look: in the April journal, which lists every receipt or payment with its account. This convention also keeps the bank account short and readable. Instead of dozens of lines for every deposit and EFT during the month, it shows the opening balance, one line for all the money in, one line for all the money out, and the closing balance. The owner can see the month's cash movement at a glance. If the bank account were posted line by line, it would be much longer and harder to check, and the cash journals would lose part of their purpose, which is to collect and summarise transactions before they reach the ledger.

### subtopic_2 — Posting the Cash Receipts Journal
**Assistant:** Why are sundry amounts posted one by one but analysis columns posted as a total?

**Tutor:** Because every amount in an analysis column belongs to the same ledger account, while each sundry amount belongs to a different account. The wages column in the April CPJ contains two payments of three thousand rand each. Both belong in the wages account, so the column total of six thousand rand can be posted in one entry. That is exactly why analysis columns exist: they collect transactions that occur often and belong to one account, so that posting is quick and the ledger is not cluttered. The repair parts column works the same way. The sundry column is different. In April it contains rent expense, stationery, drawings, water and electricity, equipment, bank charges and insurance, seven different accounts. Its total of fifteen thousand one hundred and twenty-five rand does not belong to any single account; there is no sundry account in the ledger. If someone posted that total to one account, say rent expense, the rent expense balance would be overstated by ten thousand six hundred and twenty-five rand, and six other accounts would be missing their entries. So each sundry amount is posted separately to its own account, and the sundry details column, which names the account, tells the bookkeeper where each one goes. The sundry total is still useful, though: it is used in the cross-cast when closing off the journal, to prove that the bank column equals the sum of all the other columns. If a business finds that the same sundry account appears many times each month, it may decide to give that account its own analysis column, which saves posting time.

### subtopic_3 — Posting the Cash Payments Journal
**Assistant:** What do c/d and b/d mean, and why are they on opposite sides?

**Tutor:** C/d stands for carried down and b/d for brought down. They are two halves of the same step in balancing an account, and they sit on opposite sides because one closes the month and the other opens the next month with the true balance. Take the bank account at the end of April. The debit side totals one hundred and five thousand five hundred and ten rand, the opening balance plus the receipts. The credit side has payments of twenty-five thousand three hundred and twenty-five rand. The difference, eighty thousand one hundred and eighty-five rand, is what remains in the bank. To close the month neatly, the bookkeeper writes this difference on the smaller side, the credit side, as balance c/d. Now both sides add up to the same total, one hundred and five thousand five hundred and ten, and the totals are written and ruled off. This shows that April is finished. But the money has not disappeared; the business still has eighty thousand one hundred and eighty-five rand in the bank. So the same amount is written on the other side, the debit side, below the totals, dated the first of May, as balance b/d. That entry is the opening balance for May, and it is on the debit side, which is correct, because bank is an asset and assets have debit balances. If you ever bring a balance down on the same side as you carried it down, the account will be wrong by twice the balance. A simple check: the balance b/d always ends up on the bigger side, the side the account's balance naturally belongs to.

### subtopic_4 — Balancing Ledger Accounts
**Assistant:** Why do the nominal accounts show more than just April's income and expenses?

**Tutor:** Because nominal accounts accumulate the income and expenses of the whole financial year, and the repair business's financial year began on the first of March. Balance sheet accounts, such as bank, equipment, capital and the loan, carry their balances forward indefinitely: the bank balance at the end of one month is the opening balance of the next, and so on, year after year. Nominal accounts also carry their balances forward from month to month, but only within a financial year. On the first of April, the current income account already contained fifteen thousand nine hundred rand earned in March. When the April total of eighteen thousand six hundred and fifty rand is posted, the account shows thirty-four thousand five hundred and fifty rand: the income for March and April together. Similarly, the wages account shows twelve thousand rand, six thousand for each month. This is useful. At any time, the owner can see how much has been earned and spent so far this year, and the trial balance at the end of April can be used to calculate the profit for the two months. If the owner wants the figures for April alone, they can be found in the April journals or by subtracting the opening balances. At the end of the financial year, the nominal account balances are transferred to a profit and loss account to calculate the year's profit, and the nominal accounts start the new year at zero. That is why they are sometimes called temporary accounts. You will do these closing transfers in later grades. For now, remember that a nominal account balance means the year so far.
