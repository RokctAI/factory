# Part 1 — Expert

This session opens the general ledger properly. In Term 2 you saw the ledger as one step in the accounting cycle, and in Terms 2 and 3 you entered a whole month of receipts and payments in the cash journals of the appliance repair business. Now you learn how the ledger is built: the T-account, the double-entry principle, and the rules that decide whether an amount goes on the debit side or the credit side of an account. The anchor is the repair business in April: its ledger already holds the balances brought forward from March, and every April receipt and payment will be recorded in two accounts, once as a debit and once as a credit. The governing idea is that every transaction affects at least two accounts with equal debit and credit amounts; that assets, expenses and drawings increase on the debit side, while owner's equity, liabilities and income increase on the credit side; and that this double entry is what keeps the accounting equation in balance and allows the trial balance to check the records. The layout of a ledger account, the double-entry principle, the debit and credit rules, and the analysis of transactions into the accounts debited and credited are examinable, and they are the foundation of all Accounting from Grade 9 to Grade 12.

## Subtopic: The General Ledger

The general ledger is the book of final entry. It contains a separate account for every item the business needs to keep track of: each asset, each liability, owner's equity, and each kind of income and expense. The journals record transactions one by one in date order; the ledger sorts them by account, so that the owner can see at a glance how much is in the bank, how much is owed on the loan, or how much has been spent on wages since the start of the year.

The general ledger is divided into two sections. The balance sheet accounts section contains the accounts that appear on the balance sheet: capital, drawings, assets such as equipment and bank, and liabilities such as the loan. The nominal accounts section contains the income and expense accounts, which are used to calculate profit: current income, rent income, interest income, wages, repair parts, rent expense, stationery, water and electricity, bank charges and insurance.

Each account has a folio number. Balance sheet accounts are numbered B1, B2, B3 and so on, and nominal accounts N1, N2, N3. At the repair business, capital is B1, drawings B2, equipment B3, loan B4 and bank B5; current income is N1, rent income N2, interest income N3, and the expense accounts follow from N4 onwards. The folio numbers are written in the journals when amounts are posted, and the journal folio, such as CRJ 4 or CPJ 4, is written in the ledger account, so that every entry can be traced from document to journal to ledger and back. This is called the audit trail.

The repair business's financial year began on the first of March. Its ledger therefore already contains the balances at the end of March, which are carried forward into April. The balance sheet accounts carry their balances from year to year, while the nominal accounts collect the income and expenses of the current financial year only.

The questions for this section are with you now: the purpose of the general ledger, its two sections, folio numbers and the audit trail.

## Subtopic: The T-Account

Each ledger account is drawn in the shape of a capital letter T, which is why it is called a T-account. The name of the account is written across the top, with its folio number on the right. A vertical line divides the account into two sides. The left side is the debit side, abbreviated Dr. The right side is the credit side, abbreviated Cr.

Each side has four columns: the date, the details, the folio and the amount. The date column shows the year and month once at the top, then the day of each entry. The details column names the contra account, which is the other account affected by the same transaction. The folio column shows the journal the amount came from, such as CRJ 4 for the cash receipts journal of April. The amount column shows the rand amount.

For example, when the April wages total of six thousand rand is posted, the wages account is debited: date the thirtieth, details bank, folio CPJ 4, amount six thousand. The bank account is credited as part of the total payments for the month. The details in the wages account say bank, because bank is the contra account, the account where the other half of the transaction was recorded.

Some textbooks draw the account with a single date column at the far left and amounts only, especially for quick working. The full format with all four columns on each side is the one expected in examinations, and it should be drawn neatly with a ruler.

The words debit and credit simply mean left and right in a ledger account. They do not mean good and bad, and they do not mean the same thing as on a bank statement, which is written from the bank's point of view.

The questions for this section are with you now: the layout of a T-account, the debit and credit sides, the four columns on each side, and the meaning of the contra account.

## Subtopic: The Double-Entry Principle

The double-entry principle states that every transaction affects at least two accounts, and that the amount debited must equal the amount credited. For every debit there is an equal credit.

This is not an arbitrary rule. It follows from the accounting equation. Every transaction has at least two effects on assets, owner's equity and liabilities, and those effects keep the equation in balance. Double entry records both effects, one as a debit and one as a credit.

Consider the owner's additional capital of ten thousand rand on the eighth of April. Two things happened: the business's bank balance increased, and the owner's capital increased. So the bank account is debited with ten thousand rand, and the capital account is credited with ten thousand rand. In the equation, assets increase by ten thousand and owner's equity increases by ten thousand.

Consider the wages of three thousand rand paid on the seventh. Bank decreased and wages, an expense, increased. Wages is debited with three thousand rand and bank is credited with three thousand rand. In the equation, assets decrease and owner's equity decreases, because expenses reduce profit.

Because every transaction is recorded with equal debits and credits, the total of all the debit entries in the whole ledger must always equal the total of all the credit entries. This is what the trial balance tests.

In the cash journals, the double entry is built into the columns. Every amount appears in the bank column and again in an analysis or sundry column. When the journals are posted, the bank column total goes to one side of the bank account, and the other columns go to the opposite side of their own accounts.

The questions for this section are with you now: the double-entry principle, its link to the accounting equation, and how the cash journals prepare the double entry.

## Subtopic: The Debit and Credit Rules

Which side an amount goes on depends on the kind of account and on whether the transaction increases or decreases it.

Assets increase on the debit side and decrease on the credit side. Money received increases bank, so it is debited to bank; money paid decreases bank, so it is credited to bank. Buying equipment increases equipment, so equipment is debited.

Owner's equity, the capital account, increases on the credit side and decreases on the debit side. Additional capital from the owner is credited to capital.

Liabilities increase on the credit side and decrease on the debit side. A loan received is credited to the loan account; a loan repayment is debited to the loan account.

Income increases owner's equity, so income accounts follow the owner's equity rule: income is credited. Current income, rent income and interest income are all credited when they are earned.

Expenses decrease owner's equity, so expense accounts work in the opposite direction from owner's equity: expenses are debited. Wages, repair parts, rent expense and bank charges are all debited when they are incurred.

Drawings also decrease owner's equity, so drawings are debited.

A short way to remember the rules is that assets, expenses and drawings are debit accounts, which increase on the debit side, while capital, liabilities and income are credit accounts, which increase on the credit side. Some learners remember the six groups by their first letters.

The rules match the accounting equation. Assets are on the left of the equation and increase on the left, or debit, side. Owner's equity and liabilities are on the right of the equation and increase on the right, or credit, side.

The questions for this section are with you now: the debit and credit rules for assets, capital, liabilities, income, expenses and drawings.

## Subtopic: Analysing April Transactions into Debits and Credits

Before posting, an accountant can analyse any transaction by naming the account debited, the account credited and the amount.

Cash register roll, current income three thousand nine hundred and fifty rand on the first: debit bank, because an asset increased; credit current income, because income was earned.

Additional capital, ten thousand rand on the eighth: debit bank; credit capital.

Rent received from the tenant, two thousand five hundred rand on the eighteenth: debit bank; credit rent income.

Interest earned, one hundred and eighty rand on the thirtieth: debit bank; credit interest income.

Rent of the workshop paid, four thousand five hundred rand on the fifth: debit rent expense, because an expense increased; credit bank, because an asset decreased.

Diagnostic tool bought, five thousand two hundred rand on the twenty-eighth: debit equipment, an asset increased; credit bank, an asset decreased.

Drawings by the owner, two thousand five hundred rand on the twenty-fourth: debit drawings; credit bank.

Bank charges, two hundred and forty-five rand on the thirtieth: debit bank charges; credit bank.

Notice the pattern for a cash business. Every receipt is debited to bank and credited to another account. Every payment is credited to bank and debited to another account. Bank is involved in every cash transaction, which is why the cash journals have a bank column.

A loan works in both directions. When the garden service from Term 3 received a loan of ten thousand rand, it debited bank and credited loan, because an asset and a liability both increased. When it repaid one thousand rand, it debited loan and credited bank, because the liability and the asset both decreased.

Total the April journals and the double entry holds for the whole month. The receipts debited to bank total thirty-one thousand three hundred and thirty rand, and the credits to current income, capital, rent income and interest income total the same. The payments credited to bank total twenty-five thousand three hundred and twenty-five rand, and the debits to wages, repair parts and the sundry accounts total the same. In figures: eighteen thousand six hundred and fifty plus ten thousand plus two thousand five hundred plus one hundred and eighty is thirty-one thousand three hundred and thirty; and six thousand for wages plus four thousand two hundred for repair parts plus fifteen thousand one hundred and twenty-five for the sundry accounts is twenty-five thousand three hundred and twenty-five.

The questions for this section are with you now: naming the account debited and credited for each April transaction, and checking that debits equal credits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Every transaction tells two stories. Double entry writes down both of them, one on the left and one on the right.

## Subtopic: Two Sides of Every Story

Think of the ledger as a big file of folders, one folder per item: bank, capital, loan, equipment, wages, current income, and so on. Each folder is a T: left side debit, right side credit.

Now a transaction. The owner puts ten thousand rand of extra capital into the business. Two stories. Story one: the bank has more money. Story two: the owner has put more into the business. So we write in two folders. Bank, on the left. Capital, on the right. Ten thousand on each side.

Another one. The business pays three thousand rand wages. Story one: the bank has less money. Story two: the business has spent money on wages. So bank on the right, wages on the left. Three thousand on each side.

Every transaction is like this: one left, one right, same amount. If you only write one side, half the story is missing.

And in each folder, next to the amount, you write the name of the other folder, so you can always find the other half of the story. In the wages folder, you write bank. In the bank folder, you write wages, or for a whole month of payments, total payments.

## Subtopic: Which Side?

How do you know which side? Sort the folders into two teams.

Team debit grows on the left: assets, like bank and equipment; expenses, like wages and rent; and drawings.

Team credit grows on the right: capital; liabilities, like the loan; and income, like current income and rent income.

To make something grow, write on its own team's side. To make it shrink, write on the other side.

Money comes into the bank: bank grows, so bank on the left. Money goes out: bank shrinks, so bank on the right.

The repair business earns rent of two thousand five hundred rand: bank grows, left; rent income grows, right.

It buys a diagnostic tool for five thousand two hundred rand: equipment grows, left; bank shrinks, right.

Here is a check you can use every time. Ask two questions. Which two folders changed? Did each one grow or shrink? Then use the teams to choose the sides. If you end up with two lefts or two rights, something is wrong. Go back and check.

One more example, a tricky one. The owner takes two thousand five hundred rand for private use. Which folders? Bank and drawings. Bank shrinks: right. Drawings grow: drawings is on team debit, so left. It is not an expense, but it sits on the same side as one, because it also makes the owner's share smaller.

Now try the practice questions: name the folders, choose the sides, and make sure every left has a matching right.
