### subtopic_1 — Purpose and Format of the Trial Balance
**Assistant:** Why is the trial balance dated on a single day instead of covering a period like the journals?

**Tutor:** Because the trial balance lists balances, and a balance is the amount in an account at a particular moment. The cash receipts journal and the cash payments journal record transactions that happened during April, so they cover a period, the whole month. The ledger accounts collect those transactions, and when an account is balanced on the thirtieth of April, the balance tells us what is in that account on that day. The trial balance copies those balances onto one page, so it is a snapshot, like a photograph, rather than a video. That is why the heading reads trial balance on thirty April, not for April. The same idea returns later in the financial statements: the balance sheet is also prepared on a date, because it shows assets, owner's equity and liabilities at a moment, while the income statement is prepared for a period, because it shows the income earned and expenses incurred over time. Notice something interesting about the nominal accounts in this trial balance. Their balances are on the thirtieth of April, but the amounts include March as well, because the financial year began on the first of March and income and expense accounts keep accumulating until the year ends. So wages shows twelve thousand rand, the total for two months. The trial balance is still dated on one day; it simply shows the amount that has built up in each account by that day. Getting the date right in the heading is a small detail, but in tests it often carries a mark.

### subtopic_2 — Listing the Balance Sheet Accounts
**Assistant:** Drawings is a balance sheet account, so why does it go in the debit column with the assets?

**Tutor:** Because the column is decided by the side of the balance in the ledger, not by the section the account is listed in. Drawings records the money or goods the owner takes out of the business for personal use. Taking out drawings decreases owner's equity. In the double entry principle, owner's equity increases on the credit side and decreases on the debit side, so drawings is debited every time the owner takes something. At the repair business, the drawings account has three thousand rand from March and two thousand five hundred rand from April, both on the debit side, a debit balance of five thousand five hundred rand. So in the trial balance it goes in the debit column. Drawings is listed with the balance sheet accounts because, at the end of the year, it is subtracted from owner's equity on the balance sheet, together with capital and the net profit. It is not an expense, because the money was not spent to earn income; the owner simply took it. The same thinking applies to the bank account. A favourable bank balance is a debit balance and goes in the debit column, but an overdraft is a credit balance and goes in the credit column, even though the account is the same. The safe method is always to look at the side of the balance in the balanced ledger account and copy it into the matching column.

### subtopic_3 — Listing the Nominal Accounts and Totalling
**Assistant:** How can a whole month of payments leave the trial balance totals unchanged on the debit side?

**Tutor:** Because a payment usually moves an amount from one debit balance to another. When the repair business paid wages of three thousand rand, the wages account was debited, so the debit balance of wages went up by three thousand. At the same time the bank account was credited, so the debit balance of bank went down by three thousand. Both accounts are in the debit column of the trial balance, so the debit total does not change at all; the amount has only moved from bank to wages. The same happens with the purchase of equipment for five thousand two hundred rand, and with the owner's drawings. A receipt is different. When the business received current income, bank was debited, increasing a debit balance, and current income was credited, increasing a credit balance. So both totals went up by the same amount. Across April, receipts of thirty-one thousand three hundred and thirty rand were deposited. That is exactly why the trial balance grew from one hundred and three thousand five hundred and fifty rand at the end of March to one hundred and thirty-four thousand eight hundred and eighty rand at the end of April, the same increase on both sides. If a payment reduced a liability, such as repaying part of the loan, both totals would fall by the same amount. Whatever the transaction, the two totals move together, which is the double entry principle at work.

### subtopic_4 — Errors the Trial Balance Reveals and Hides
**Assistant:** Why does a difference that divides exactly by nine suggest a transposition error?

**Tutor:** Because swapping two neighbouring digits always changes a number by a multiple of nine. Take the stationery payment of six hundred and eighty rand written as eight hundred and sixty rand. The difference is one hundred and eighty, and one hundred and eighty divided by nine is twenty. The reason lies in place value. If two neighbouring digits a and b swap places, the number changes by ten a plus b minus ten b minus a, which is nine times a minus b. Whatever the digits are, the result is a multiple of nine. In the stationery example, the eight and the six swap in the tens and hundreds places, so the change is ninety times the difference between eight and six, which is one hundred and eighty. So if the trial balance is out by an amount divisible by nine, a bookkeeper looks first for swapped digits. It is a clue, not a proof, because other errors can also give a multiple of nine by chance. A second clue is the wrong-column error: if a debit balance of five hundred rand is written in the credit column, the debit total is five hundred too small and the credit total five hundred too big, so the difference is one thousand, twice the balance. Halving the difference gives the amount to look for. Remember that these clues help only when the totals disagree. Errors of omission, wrong accounts and reversals leave the totals equal, so they must be found by checking the source documents and journals.
