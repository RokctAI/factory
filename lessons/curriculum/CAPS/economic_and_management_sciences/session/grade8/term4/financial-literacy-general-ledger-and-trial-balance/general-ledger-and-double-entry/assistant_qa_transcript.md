### subtopic_1 — The General Ledger
**Assistant:** Why do accountants use the words debit and credit instead of just plus and minus?

**Tutor:** Because in a ledger account the same side does not mean plus for every kind of account, so plus and minus would be confusing, while debit and credit always mean the same two sides: left and right. Think about what happens when the owner contributes capital. The bank account increases and the capital account increases. Both are pluses. If we only had plus and minus, we would record two pluses, and there would be no way to check that the transaction had been recorded completely and correctly. With debit and credit, the bank account is debited and the capital account is credited, so the transaction has one left-side entry and one right-side entry of equal amounts. That balance between left and right is what makes double entry self-checking: across the whole ledger, total debits must equal total credits. Whether a debit is an increase or a decrease depends on the type of account. For assets, expenses and drawings, a debit is an increase. For capital, liabilities and income, a debit is a decrease. This follows from the accounting equation: assets sit on the left of the equation and increase on the left side of their accounts, while owner's equity and liabilities sit on the right and increase on the right. The words come from Latin: debit from debere, to owe, and credit from credere, to trust or believe. Their original meanings related to people who owed money or were owed money, but in modern accounting they simply mean the left and right sides of an account. Avoid thinking of debit as bad and credit as good, which comes from everyday banking language.

### subtopic_2 — The T-Account
**Assistant:** Why is the bank statement's credit the opposite of the ledger's credit?

**Tutor:** Because the bank statement is written from the bank's point of view, while the business's ledger is written from the business's point of view, and the same money is an asset to one and a liability to the other. When the repair business has eighty thousand rand in its bank account, that money is an asset of the business: it is recorded as a debit balance in the bank account in the business's ledger. But to the bank, the same eighty thousand rand is money it owes to the business, because the business can withdraw it at any time. In the bank's own records, the business's account is a liability, and liabilities have credit balances. So when the business deposits money, the bank credits the business's account, increasing what the bank owes, while the business debits its own bank account, increasing its asset. When the bank deducts bank charges, it debits the business's account on the statement, reducing what it owes, while the business credits its bank account in the ledger, reducing its asset. This is why a bank statement may describe a deposit as a credit, and why people say their account has been credited when money arrives. It is not a contradiction; it is the same transaction seen from opposite sides. For the ledger, always apply the business's rules: money in is a debit to bank, money out is a credit to bank. When reading a bank statement, ignore the words debit and credit and look instead at whether the balance went up or down. You will compare the two records formally in the bank reconciliation in Grade 9 and Grade 10.

### subtopic_3 — The Double-Entry Principle
**Assistant:** Why are expenses debited if they reduce owner's equity?

**Tutor:** Because owner's equity increases on the credit side, so anything that reduces owner's equity must be recorded on the opposite side, the debit side. Owner's equity is the owner's interest in the business. It increases when the owner contributes capital and when the business earns income, and it decreases when the business incurs expenses and when the owner takes drawings. The capital account, which represents owner's equity, is a credit account: increases go on the credit side. Income increases owner's equity, so income accounts behave like capital and are credited when income is earned. Expenses decrease owner's equity, so they behave in the opposite way: they are debited. You can think of an expense account as a separate, detailed record of reductions in owner's equity. Instead of debiting every payment for wages, rent and stationery directly to the capital account, which would mix up capital with day-to-day spending, the business keeps a separate account for each kind of expense. At the end of the financial year, the totals of the income and expense accounts are transferred to a profit and loss account, the difference is the profit or loss, and that profit is added to owner's equity. You will do these closing transfers in later grades. The same reasoning explains why drawings are debited: they reduce owner's equity directly. So the debit rule for expenses and drawings is not a separate rule to memorise. It is the capital rule turned around: capital and income increase owner's equity on the credit side, while expenses and drawings decrease it on the debit side.

### subtopic_4 — The Debit and Credit Rules
**Assistant:** How can I check that I have analysed a transaction correctly?

**Tutor:** Use three checks: does the transaction have an equal debit and credit, does the analysis match the accounting equation, and does the reasoning make sense in plain language. First, the double-entry check. For every transaction you should name one account debited and one account credited, with the same amount. If you find yourself with two debits, two credits or different amounts, something is wrong. For example, if you analysed the purchase of a diagnostic tool as a debit to equipment and a debit to bank, you would immediately see that both entries are on the same side. Second, the accounting equation check. Work out the effect on assets, owner's equity and liabilities. For the tool, equipment increases and bank decreases: both are assets, so total assets do not change, and nothing else changes. That matches debit equipment, an increase in an asset, and credit bank, a decrease in an asset. For rent received, bank increases, an asset, and owner's equity increases through income, which matches debit bank and credit rent income. Third, the plain language check. Say the transaction aloud as the two stories it tells. The business paid wages: the business has less money in the bank, and it has spent more on wages. Less in the bank means credit bank; more spent on wages means debit wages. If your entries tell a different story, for instance that the bank has more money after paying wages, you have reversed the sides. In a cash business there is a fourth, quick check: bank is always one of the two accounts. Debit bank for every receipt, credit bank for every payment, and the other account takes the opposite side.
