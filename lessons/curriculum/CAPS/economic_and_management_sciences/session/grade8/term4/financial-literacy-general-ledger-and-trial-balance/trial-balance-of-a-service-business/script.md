# Part 1 — Expert

This session completes the Grade 8 accounting cycle for the appliance repair business by preparing its trial balance on the thirtieth of April. In the previous session every April journal was posted to the general ledger and every account was balanced. Now those balances are listed on a single page, with debit balances in one column and credit balances in the other, and the two columns are added. For the repair business, both columns total one hundred and thirty-four thousand eight hundred and eighty rand. The governing idea is that the trial balance tests the double entry principle: because every transaction was recorded with an equal debit and credit, the total of the debit balances must equal the total of the credit balances. A trial balance that balances shows the ledger is arithmetically correct, but it does not prove that there are no errors. The format of the trial balance, the side on which each balance appears, the division into balance sheet and nominal accounts, the errors it can and cannot reveal, and the use of the trial balance to work out profit and check the accounting equation are all examinable, and they lead directly to the financial statements studied in Grade 9 and in FET Accounting.

## Subtopic: Purpose and Format of the Trial Balance

A trial balance is a list of all the balances in the general ledger on a particular date, showing debit balances in a debit column and credit balances in a credit column.

It has three purposes. First, it checks the arithmetic accuracy of the ledger: if the two totals are equal, the double entry has been completed for every transaction. Second, it brings all the balances together on one page, so that the owner does not have to page through every ledger account. Third, it is the starting point for preparing the financial statements: the income statement and the balance sheet.

The heading names the business, says what the document is, and gives the date. For example: Trial balance of the appliance repair business on thirty April. The date is a single day, because the balances are the balances on that day.

The trial balance has four columns: the account name, the folio, which is the ledger folio number such as B5 or N4, the debit column and the credit column.

The accounts are listed in folio order and in two sections. The balance sheet accounts section comes first, with folios starting with B. The nominal accounts section follows, with folios starting with N. Some textbooks show the two section headings as separate lines; the totals are the same either way.

The trial balance is a list, not a ledger account. It has no details column and no b/d or c/d. Each account appears once, with its balance in exactly one column.

The questions for this section are with you now: the purpose, heading and columns of the trial balance.

## Subtopic: Listing the Balance Sheet Accounts

The balance sheet accounts are capital, drawings, equipment, loan and bank, folios B1 to B5. Their balances at the thirtieth of April come straight from the balanced ledger.

Capital, B1: sixty thousand rand brought down on the first of April, plus ten thousand rand additional capital in April, a credit balance of seventy thousand rand. It goes in the credit column, because capital is part of owner's equity.

Drawings, B2: three thousand rand plus two thousand five hundred rand, a debit balance of five thousand five hundred rand. Drawings decrease owner's equity, so the account has a debit balance and goes in the debit column, even though it is listed among the balance sheet accounts.

Equipment, B3: eighteen thousand plus five thousand two hundred, a debit balance of twenty-three thousand two hundred rand. It is an asset, so it goes in the debit column.

Loan, B4: no April entries, so the credit balance stays twenty-five thousand rand. It is a liability, so it goes in the credit column.

Bank, B5: the favourable balance of eighty thousand one hundred and eighty-five rand brought down on the first of May, which was the balance on the evening of the thirtieth of April. A favourable bank balance is an asset and goes in the debit column. If the bank account had an overdraft, the balance would go in the credit column instead, because the business would owe the bank.

The rule of thumb is: assets, expenses and drawings appear in the debit column; owner's equity, liabilities and income appear in the credit column.

The questions for this section are with you now: placing capital, drawings, equipment, loan and bank in the correct column.

## Subtopic: Listing the Nominal Accounts and Totalling

The nominal accounts are the income and expense accounts, folios N1 to N10. Because the financial year began on the first of March, their balances are the totals for March and April together.

The income accounts have credit balances. Current income, N1: fifteen thousand nine hundred plus eighteen thousand six hundred and fifty, thirty-four thousand five hundred and fifty rand. Rent income, N2: two thousand five hundred plus two thousand five hundred, five thousand rand. Interest income, N3: one hundred and fifty plus one hundred and eighty, three hundred and thirty rand.

The expense accounts have debit balances. Wages, N4, twelve thousand rand. Stationery, N5: seven hundred and fifty plus six hundred and eighty, one thousand four hundred and thirty rand. Water and electricity, N6: one thousand four hundred plus one thousand three hundred and eighty, two thousand seven hundred and eighty rand. Bank charges, N7: two hundred and twenty plus two hundred and forty-five, four hundred and sixty-five rand. Repair parts, N8, four thousand two hundred rand. Rent expense, N9, four thousand five hundred rand. Insurance, N10, six hundred and twenty rand.

Now total each column. Debit column: bank eighty thousand one hundred and eighty-five, equipment twenty-three thousand two hundred, drawings five thousand five hundred, and the seven expenses, which together come to twenty-five thousand nine hundred and ninety-five. The debit total is one hundred and thirty-four thousand eight hundred and eighty rand.

Credit column: capital seventy thousand, loan twenty-five thousand, and the three incomes, which together come to thirty-nine thousand eight hundred and eighty. The credit total is also one hundred and thirty-four thousand eight hundred and eighty rand.

The totals are written on the same line, with a single line above and a double line below, as in a balanced ledger account. The trial balance balances.

Compare this with the trial balance at the end of March, which totalled one hundred and three thousand five hundred and fifty rand on each side. April's transactions added thirty-one thousand three hundred and thirty rand to each side: every April receipt increased both a debit balance and a credit balance by the same amount, and every April payment moved an amount from one debit balance to another, or reduced a debit balance and a credit balance together.

The questions for this section are with you now: the nominal account balances, the column totals and why they agree.

## Subtopic: Errors the Trial Balance Reveals and Hides

If the two totals are not equal, there is at least one error. Typical errors that make the totals disagree are: posting only one half of a transaction; posting both halves to the same side; posting different amounts on the two sides; adding a ledger account incorrectly; and listing a balance in the wrong column of the trial balance.

A useful check is the difference between the totals. If a balance has been placed in the wrong column, the difference is twice that balance, so halve the difference and look for a balance of that amount. If two digits have been swapped, called a transposition, for example stationery of six hundred and eighty posted as eight hundred and sixty, the difference of one hundred and eighty is divisible by nine.

But a trial balance that balances does not prove the ledger is correct. Some errors leave the totals equal. An error of omission: a transaction is left out completely, so neither side is posted. An error of principle or a wrong account: an amount is posted to the correct side of the wrong account, such as stationery debited to repair parts. A reversal: the debit and the credit are swapped. An original-entry error: the wrong amount is entered in the journal, so the same wrong amount is posted to both sides. Compensating errors: two errors of the same amount on opposite sides cancel out.

For this reason the bookkeeper also checks the source documents, the journals and the bank statement. The trial balance is a test of arithmetic, not a guarantee of accuracy.

The questions for this section are with you now: which errors cause the totals to differ, and which errors the trial balance cannot find.

## Subtopic: Using the Trial Balance

The trial balance gives the owner a quick view of the business and is the basis for the financial statements.

The nominal accounts show the result for the two months. Total income is thirty-four thousand five hundred and fifty plus five thousand plus three hundred and thirty, which is thirty-nine thousand eight hundred and eighty rand. Total expenses are twenty-five thousand nine hundred and ninety-five rand. The net profit for March and April is thirty-nine thousand eight hundred and eighty minus twenty-five thousand nine hundred and ninety-five, which is thirteen thousand eight hundred and eighty-five rand. This calculation is the basis of the income statement.

The balance sheet accounts allow the accounting equation to be checked. Assets: bank eighty thousand one hundred and eighty-five plus equipment twenty-three thousand two hundred, which is one hundred and three thousand three hundred and eighty-five rand. Owner's equity: capital seventy thousand, plus net profit thirteen thousand eight hundred and eighty-five, minus drawings five thousand five hundred, which is seventy-eight thousand three hundred and eighty-five rand. Liabilities: the loan of twenty-five thousand rand. Owner's equity plus liabilities is one hundred and three thousand three hundred and eighty-five rand, the same as the assets. A equals OE plus L.

That is the whole cycle you have studied this year: a transaction produces a source document, the source document is entered in a journal, the journal is posted to the ledger, the ledger balances are listed in the trial balance, and the trial balance leads to the income statement and balance sheet.

The questions for this section are with you now: calculating profit from the trial balance and checking the accounting equation.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The trial balance is a scoreboard for the filing cabinet. Every folder has one number on its front; now we copy all those numbers onto one page.

## Subtopic: One Page, Two Columns

Draw a page with a left column and a right column. Left is debit, right is credit.

Copy each folder's number into the column where it lives. Things the business owns, expenses and drawings go on the left: bank eighty thousand one hundred and eighty-five, equipment twenty-three thousand two hundred, drawings five thousand five hundred, wages twelve thousand, and the other expenses. What the owner put in, what the business owes, and income go on the right: capital seventy thousand, loan twenty-five thousand, current income thirty-four thousand five hundred and fifty, rent income five thousand, interest income three hundred and thirty.

Add the left: one hundred and thirty-four thousand eight hundred and eighty. Add the right: one hundred and thirty-four thousand eight hundred and eighty. They match. Every transaction went in twice, once left and once right, so the two sides must end up equal.

## Subtopic: When It Does Not Balance

If the sides do not match, something went in only once, or on the wrong side, or with a wrong number. Find the difference and play detective. Half the difference might be a number in the wrong column. A difference that divides by nine might be two swapped digits.

But matching sides do not mean everything is perfect. If a whole transaction was forgotten, or filed in the wrong folder on the correct side, the sides still match. That is why the bookkeeper still checks the documents and the bank statement.

And the scoreboard tells a story. Income of thirty-nine thousand eight hundred and eighty, minus expenses of twenty-five thousand nine hundred and ninety-five, is a profit of thirteen thousand eight hundred and eighty-five rand so far this year.

Now try the practice questions: place each balance in the right column, total the trial balance, hunt for errors and work out the profit.
