# Part 1 — Expert

This session sets out what managers actually do. Whatever their level, managers perform four basic tasks, often remembered by the letters POLC: planning, organising, leading and control. The anchor is the Mthatha supermarket's decision to launch a home delivery service. Top management plans it, with a target of four hundred deliveries a month and a delivery fee of thirty-five rand. The managers organise it, buying a van, appointing a driver and dividing the work. They lead the team, motivating packers and the driver and communicating the new routine. And they control it, measuring the first month's three hundred and twenty deliveries against the target and deciding what to change. The governing idea is that the four management tasks form a continuous cycle: planning sets the goals and the way to reach them, organising arranges the people and resources, leading directs and motivates people to do the work, and control checks whether the goals are being reached and corrects the course when they are not. The meaning of each task, the steps in planning and control, delegation, motivation and communication, and how the tasks apply at each level of management are examinable, and they lead into Grade 10 Business Studies.

## Subtopic: The Four Management Tasks

The four management tasks, also called the management functions, are planning, organising, leading and control.

Planning is deciding in advance what the business wants to achieve and how it will achieve it: setting goals and choosing the actions to reach them.

Organising is arranging the people, tasks and resources needed to carry out the plan: dividing the work, assigning it to people, and providing the money, equipment and materials.

Leading, also called directing, is guiding and motivating people to do the work: giving instructions, communicating, motivating and resolving problems.

Control is checking whether the plan is being achieved: measuring results, comparing them with the goals, and taking corrective action where necessary.

The tasks form a cycle. Control shows whether the plan is working; the results feed into the next round of planning; and the cycle starts again. They are not done once and finished: a manager plans, organises, leads and controls continuously, often on the same day.

Every level of management performs all four tasks, but in different proportions. Top managers spend most of their time planning, especially long-term, strategic planning. Middle managers divide their time more evenly, with much organising and coordinating. Lower managers spend most of their time leading and controlling the daily work on the shop floor, with short-term, operational planning.

The questions for this section are with you now: the meaning of each of the four tasks, why they form a cycle, and how the balance between them differs between levels.

## Subtopic: Planning

Planning is the first task, because the others depend on it. Without a plan, there is nothing to organise, no direction for leading and no standard to control against.

Planning follows a series of steps. First, analyse the current situation: what the business does well, what it does badly, and what opportunities and threats exist outside it. The supermarket notices that many customers, especially older people and working parents, struggle to come to the shop, and that competitors in town do not deliver. Second, set goals. Third, consider different ways of reaching the goals, such as using the business's own van, paying a courier, or partnering with a delivery app. Fourth, choose the best option. Fifth, draw up detailed plans: who does what, by when, and with what budget.

Good goals are SMART. Specific: start a home delivery service for groceries within the town. Measurable: four hundred deliveries a month. Achievable: the business has the stock, the staff and the money for a van. Realistic, or relevant: it fits the goal of growing sales and serving customers better. Time-bound: within six months, and at four hundred a month by the third month of operation.

Plans exist at different levels and over different time spans. Strategic plans are long-term plans for the whole business, made by top management. Tactical plans are medium-term plans for departments, made by middle management. Operational plans are short-term plans for daily and weekly work, made by lower management, such as the delivery roster.

A budget is a plan expressed in money. The delivery plan includes the cost of a van, about two hundred and fifty thousand rand, and monthly running costs of a driver's wages of seven thousand five hundred rand, fuel of four thousand two hundred rand and insurance of nine hundred rand, a total of twelve thousand six hundred rand a month. At thirty-five rand a delivery, the service needs three hundred and sixty deliveries a month to cover its running costs, which is one reason the target was set at four hundred.

Good planners also prepare a contingency plan, a backup for when things go wrong. What happens if the van breaks down, or the driver is ill? The supermarket arranges that a courier company can be called at short notice, so that customers still receive their orders. Planning for problems in advance is far cheaper than reacting to them in a panic.

The questions for this section are with you now: the steps in planning, SMART goals, the levels of plans, and budgets.

## Subtopic: Organising

Organising turns the plan into a structure of tasks, people and resources.

First, the work is identified and divided into tasks: taking orders, picking and packing goods, checking orders, loading the van, driving and delivering, collecting payment and handling complaints. Second, similar tasks are grouped into jobs, and jobs into departments or teams. Third, each job is assigned to a person with the right skills, and new people are appointed if needed: the supermarket hires a driver with a valid licence and a clean driving record and moves two experienced packers into the delivery team. Fourth, resources are allocated: the van, crates, a cellphone for orders and the budget. Fifth, the lines of authority are set: the delivery team reports to a supervisor, who reports to the groceries manager.

Delegation is an essential part of organising. It means giving a subordinate the authority to carry out a task. When the groceries manager delegates the daily delivery roster to a supervisor, three things go with it. Authority is the right to make decisions and give instructions about the task. Responsibility is the duty to do the task properly. Accountability means answering to the manager for the result. A manager can delegate authority and responsibility, but remains accountable to his or her own manager for the final result.

Delegation has clear benefits. It frees managers for more important work, it uses the skills of subordinates, it speeds up decisions close to the work, and it develops people for promotion. It fails when managers delegate without giving enough authority, delegate to people without the necessary skills, or refuse to let go.

Organising also means coordinating, making sure that different parts of the business work together rather than against each other. The delivery team needs fresh produce to have stock ready in the morning, the tills to pass on phone orders, and finance to pay the driver and the fuel account. A short daily meeting between supervisors can keep everyone in step.

Organising is shown in the organisational chart and in job descriptions, which list the tasks, responsibilities and reporting lines of each job.

The questions for this section are with you now: the steps in organising, delegation, and the difference between authority, responsibility and accountability.

## Subtopic: Leading

Leading is the task of influencing people to work willingly and well towards the goals of the business. A perfect plan and a neat structure achieve nothing until people actually do the work, and do it with care.

Leading includes giving clear instructions, so that people know what to do, how and by when. The supervisor explains the delivery routine: orders close at nine for the ten o'clock run and at two for the three o'clock run, every bag is checked against the order slip, and frozen goods are packed last.

It includes communication, which must flow both ways. Managers explain decisions and the reasons for them, and they listen to workers' ideas and concerns. The driver might point out that one route wastes fuel; a packer might suggest colour-coded crates. Good communication avoids mistakes and makes people feel valued.

It includes motivation, the reasons people put effort into their work. People are motivated by fair pay, but also by recognition, interesting work, training and chances of promotion, being treated with respect, being part of a team and seeing that their work matters. The supermarket might recognise the delivery team when customers praise the service, or offer a small bonus when the monthly target is reached.

It includes resolving conflict and solving problems, such as disagreements between the packers and the tills staff about who serves walk-in customers during busy times.

Leading also includes setting an example. A supervisor who arrives on time, follows the hygiene rules and treats customers with respect shows the team what is expected far more effectively than any notice on the wall. In the next session you will look more closely at leadership styles and the qualities of a good manager.

The questions for this section are with you now: instructions, communication, motivation, conflict and the example set by managers.

## Subtopic: Control

Control is the task of checking that the business is achieving its plans and correcting it when it is not. It completes the management cycle.

Control has four steps. First, set standards: the targets and measures that define success, taken from the plan. The supermarket's standards are four hundred deliveries a month, every delivery within two hours of the order cut-off, fewer than two complaints per hundred deliveries, and running costs of no more than twelve thousand six hundred rand a month. Second, measure actual performance. In the first month, the team made three hundred and twenty deliveries. Third, compare actual performance with the standards. Three hundred and twenty is eighty percent of four hundred, a shortfall of eighty deliveries. At thirty-five rand each, the fees came to eleven thousand two hundred rand, one thousand four hundred rand less than the running costs. Fourth, take corrective action.

Before acting, managers find the cause. The manager discovers that many customers did not know about the service and that some orders were lost because the phone was not answered during busy periods. The corrective actions are to advertise the service on the till slips, on social media and in the local community, and to give the order phone to a dedicated person during peak times. By the third month, the service makes four hundred and fifty deliveries, bringing in fees of fifteen thousand seven hundred and fifty rand, three thousand one hundred and fifty rand more than the running costs.

Sometimes control shows that the plan itself was wrong. If deliveries had stayed far below target despite good advertising, the right correction might be to change the plan: lower the target, change the fee, or deliver only on certain days.

Control can happen at three moments. Before the work starts, managers check inputs: the van is roadworthy and the driver's licence is valid. During the work, they monitor progress: the supervisor checks each bag before it leaves. After the work, they review results: the monthly count of deliveries and complaints. The earlier a problem is caught, the cheaper it is to fix.

Businesses use many control tools: budgets that compare actual spending with planned spending, stock counts, quality checks, customer feedback, financial statements and cash registers that record every sale.

The questions for this section are with you now: the four steps of control, comparing actual results with standards, and choosing corrective action.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Planning, organising, leading, control. Think of it as P, O, L, C, and think of it as a road trip.

## Subtopic: A Road Trip Called POLC

Imagine you are taking your family on a trip from Mthatha to Durban.

Planning is deciding where you are going and how. Durban, by Saturday afternoon, along the N2, with a budget for fuel and food. Without a plan you would just drive around.

Organising is getting everything ready. Who drives which part of the trip? Who packs the food? Is the car serviced, is there a spare wheel, is there money for tolls? You divide the jobs and gather what you need.

Leading is keeping everyone going. You explain the plan, keep people in a good mood, settle the argument about the music, and encourage the tired driver to stop and rest at the next town.

Control is checking the map as you go. Are we where we should be by lunchtime? If you see you are running late, or you missed a turn-off, you correct the course.

And when you get home, you use what you learned to plan the next trip better. That is why POLC is a cycle, not a straight line.

## Subtopic: The Delivery Van, Step by Step

Now the supermarket's delivery service.

Plan: four hundred deliveries a month at thirty-five rand each. Running costs twelve thousand six hundred rand a month, so they need at least three hundred and sixty deliveries just to cover costs.

Organise: buy a van, hire a driver, move two packers into a delivery team, set up a phone for orders, and put a supervisor in charge.

Lead: explain the routine, cut-off times and checks; listen to the driver's ideas; thank the team when customers say thank you.

Control: count the deliveries. Month one: three hundred and twenty, which is eighty out of every hundred hoped for. Fees: eleven thousand two hundred rand, one thousand four hundred short of the costs. Find out why: people did not know about it, and the phone rang unanswered. Fix it: advertise and staff the phone.

Notice that the managers did not panic or blame the driver. They used the numbers to find the real problem, and the fix was cheap and simple.

Month three: four hundred and fifty deliveries, fifteen thousand seven hundred and fifty rand in fees, three thousand one hundred and fifty more than the costs. The cycle worked. And then it starts again: with the service now paying its way, the owner can plan the next step, perhaps a second van or deliveries on Sundays.

Now try the practice questions: name the task, follow the steps, and do the control calculation yourself.
