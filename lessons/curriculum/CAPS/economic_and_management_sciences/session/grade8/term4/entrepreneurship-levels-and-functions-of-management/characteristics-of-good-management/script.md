# Part 1 — Expert

This session asks what separates good management from poor management. The anchor is two supervisors at the Mthatha supermarket who each lead a team of eight people. In one team, the shelves are full, customers are served quickly, and in the past year only one person resigned. In the other, mistakes are common, workers are often absent, and four people resigned in the same year. The two teams have the same pay, the same equipment and the same rules; the difference is the way they are managed. The governing idea is that good managers combine the right skills for their level, conceptual, human and technical, with personal qualities such as integrity, fairness, clear communication, sound decision-making and the ability to motivate and develop people; that they choose a leadership style that suits the situation; and that good management can be seen in results, such as productivity, quality, staff turnover and customer satisfaction. The characteristics of a good manager, the management skills at each level, leadership styles, the steps in decision-making and ethical management are examinable, and they lead into Grade 10 Business Studies.

## Subtopic: Qualities of a Good Manager

Good managers share a number of personal qualities, and most of them can be learned and improved with practice.

Vision and planning: a good manager knows where the team or business is going and can explain it, so that people understand how their work fits in.

Integrity and honesty: a good manager tells the truth, keeps promises, follows the rules he or she expects others to follow, and does not use the position for personal gain. Workers trust managers with integrity and are more willing to follow them.

Fairness: a good manager treats people equally and consistently, without favouritism or discrimination, and applies rules and rewards in the same way to everyone.

Communication: a good manager gives clear instructions, explains the reasons behind decisions, listens to workers, customers and other managers, and gives feedback, both praise and correction, in a respectful way.

Decision-making: a good manager gathers information, considers options and makes timely decisions, then takes responsibility for them, including admitting mistakes.

Motivation and people development: a good manager encourages people, recognises good work, delegates meaningful tasks and helps people to learn new skills and grow towards promotion.

Organisation and time management: a good manager plans the work, sets priorities, and uses his or her own time and the team's time well.

Flexibility and problem-solving: a good manager adapts when circumstances change, such as a power outage, a late delivery or a sudden rush of customers, and looks for solutions rather than blame.

Self-confidence and calmness under pressure: a good manager stays calm in a crisis, which helps the team stay calm too.

Knowledge of the work: a good manager understands the work of the team well enough to guide, train and judge it.

No manager is perfect in every quality. Good managers know their own weaknesses, ask for feedback, and keep learning, for example through short courses, mentoring by a more experienced manager, or simply by reflecting on what went well and what did not at the end of each week.

The questions for this section are with you now: the qualities of a good manager and why each one matters.

## Subtopic: Management Skills at Each Level

Managers need three broad kinds of skill, and the mix changes with the level of management.

Technical skills are the knowledge and ability to perform the specific work of the business or department: operating the tills, baking bread, cutting meat safely, using the stock system, or preparing a budget. Supervisors need strong technical skills, because they train workers and check their work, and because they often help to do it.

Human skills, also called interpersonal skills, are the ability to work with, understand, communicate with and motivate people. They include listening, resolving conflict, building teams and treating people with respect. Human skills are important at every level, because every manager works through people.

Conceptual skills are the ability to see the business as a whole, understand how its parts fit together and how it is affected by the outside world, think ahead and solve complex problems. Top managers need strong conceptual skills, because they make strategic decisions for the whole business, such as whether a second branch would succeed in a different town.

As a manager moves up the levels, the need for technical skills becomes smaller and the need for conceptual skills becomes larger, while human skills remain important throughout. A brilliant baker promoted to head baker needs to add human skills; if she later becomes a department manager and then general manager, she must develop conceptual skills, and she will bake much less.

Managers also need practical skills such as computer literacy, basic financial knowledge to read budgets and reports, and knowledge of the law, for example labour law and health and safety regulations.

The questions for this section are with you now: technical, human and conceptual skills, and how the mix changes from lower to top management.

## Subtopic: Leadership Styles

A leadership style is the way a manager gives direction, makes decisions and involves people. Three basic styles are usually described.

The autocratic style. The manager makes decisions alone and tells workers what to do, with little discussion. It is fast and clear, and it works well in emergencies, such as evacuating the store when a fire alarm sounds, or with new workers who do not yet know the job. But if used all the time, it can make workers feel unimportant, kill their initiative and lead to resentment.

The democratic, or participative, style. The manager involves workers in decisions, asks for their ideas and explains the reasons for decisions, although the manager still makes the final decision. Workers feel valued, they are often more motivated and committed, and the decisions benefit from their knowledge. But it takes more time, and it is not suitable when a quick decision is needed.

The laissez-faire, or free-rein, style. The manager gives workers broad goals and leaves them to decide how to reach them, with little supervision. It works well with highly skilled, experienced and motivated people, such as a team of specialists. With inexperienced or unmotivated workers, it can lead to confusion, poor quality and missed deadlines.

No single style is best in all situations. A good manager adapts the style to the situation and the people: autocratic when there is a crisis or when workers are new, democratic when there is time and workers have useful knowledge, and laissez-faire when workers are experts who need freedom. This is sometimes called situational leadership.

Leadership and management overlap but are not identical. Management focuses on plans, structures and control; leadership focuses on influencing and inspiring people. The best managers are also good leaders.

The questions for this section are with you now: the autocratic, democratic and laissez-faire styles, their advantages and disadvantages, and when each is appropriate.

## Subtopic: Decision-Making and Problem-Solving

Managers make decisions all day, and good managers follow a sensible process, especially for important decisions.

Step one: identify the problem clearly. The fresh produce supervisor notices that a lot of fruit is being thrown away. The real problem must be defined: is it that too much is being ordered, that it is being stored badly, or that it is not being sold quickly enough?

Step two: gather information. She checks the order records, the temperature of the cold room and which items spoil most.

Step three: identify possible solutions. Order smaller quantities more often; repair the cold room; reduce the price of ripe fruit in the afternoon; turn overripe fruit into fruit salads for the deli.

Step four: evaluate the options, weighing the costs, benefits and risks of each and whether it is practical.

Step five: choose the best solution, or a combination. She decides to order smaller quantities three times a week and to mark down ripe fruit after three o'clock.

Step six: implement the decision, explaining it to the team and giving each person a task.

Step seven: evaluate the result. After a month, she measures the waste again. If it has fallen, the decision worked; if not, she goes back through the steps.

Good decisions also involve the right people. Workers closest to the problem often have the best information and ideas, and they are more likely to support a solution they helped to design. Creative techniques such as brainstorming, where the team suggests as many ideas as possible before any are judged, help to find new solutions.

The questions for this section are with you now: the steps in decision-making, and why involving workers improves decisions.

## Subtopic: Ethics, Fairness and Responsibility

Good management is not only about results; it is also about how results are achieved.

An ethical manager does what is right, not only what is legal. That means being honest with customers, workers, suppliers and the owners; never accepting bribes or gifts that might influence decisions, for example from a supplier who wants a bigger order; protecting confidential information; and reporting dishonesty rather than ignoring it. Many businesses have a code of ethics or code of conduct that sets out these standards.

A fair manager follows labour law and the business's own policies. That includes paying people correctly and on time, respecting working hours and leave under the Basic Conditions of Employment Act, following fair procedures before disciplining anyone, and not discriminating on grounds such as race, gender, religion, disability or pregnancy, as the Employment Equity Act requires. A fair manager values the diversity of the team and gives everyone the same chances of training and promotion.

A responsible manager cares for people's health and safety, for example by making sure the bakery equipment is guarded and that workers are trained to use it, as required by the Occupational Health and Safety Act. A responsible manager also considers the community and the environment, reducing waste and treating the neighbourhood with respect.

Ethical management pays off. Customers and suppliers trust the business, workers are loyal, and the business avoids fines, legal action and damage to its reputation. Unethical management might bring a short-term gain, but it usually ends in losses and lost trust.

The questions for this section are with you now: ethical, fair and responsible management, the relevant laws, and why ethical management benefits the business.

## Subtopic: Recognising Good Management in Results

Good management can be seen in the results of a team or business, and managers can measure those results.

Productivity: how much is produced with the resources used, such as the number of customers served per cashier per hour, or the number of loaves per baker per shift.

Quality: how few mistakes are made, such as incorrect orders, wrongly priced items or spoiled stock.

Staff turnover: the percentage of workers who leave in a year. At the supermarket, the first supervisor's team of eight lost one person in the year, a turnover of one divided by eight, twelve and a half percent. The second supervisor's team of eight lost four people, a turnover of four divided by eight, fifty percent. High turnover is expensive, because new workers must be recruited and trained, and it is often a sign of poor management.

Absenteeism: how often workers are absent without good reason. High absenteeism can signal low morale.

Customer satisfaction: complaints, compliments and repeat customers.

Morale and teamwork: whether people help each other, take initiative and speak up with ideas.

These measures must be read carefully. A team might have high turnover because many of its members were promoted, which is a good sign, or because of something outside the manager's control. But when several measures point the same way, as with the second supervisor's mistakes, absences and resignations, they usually reveal a management problem.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Two supervisors, two teams of eight, the same shop, the same pay. Let us see why one team works and the other does not.

## Subtopic: Two Supervisors

Supervisor A arrives early, greets everyone by name and explains the day's plan in two minutes. When a new cashier makes a mistake, she shows her the right way quietly, not in front of customers. When a worker suggests a better way to stack the cool drinks, she tries it and says thank you. She treats everyone the same. In a fire drill, she gives short, firm orders and everyone moves.

Supervisor B arrives late, shouts instructions, and changes his mind halfway through the day. He has favourites who get the easy shifts. He blames workers in front of customers. He never asks for ideas, so nobody offers any. When there is a problem, he looks for someone to blame instead of a solution.

Now the results. Team A: one person left all year. One out of eight is twelve and a half percent. Team B: four people left. Four out of eight is fifty percent: half the team. Team B also has more mistakes, more absences and more customer complaints.

Same shop, same pay, same rules. The difference is the manager.

## Subtopic: A Checklist for Good Management

Here is a checklist you can use for any manager, even a class captain or a sports coach.

Do they know where the team is going, and explain it clearly? That is vision and communication.

Are they honest and fair, with no favourites? That is integrity and fairness.

Do they listen, and give praise as well as correction? That is human skill.

Do they know the work well enough to teach it? That is technical skill. Can they see the big picture? That is conceptual skill.

Do they choose the right style for the moment? Firm orders in an emergency, asking for ideas when there is time, freedom for experts.

Do they solve problems step by step, instead of blaming people?

Do they follow the law and keep people safe?

And do the results show it: good work, few mistakes, low turnover, happy customers?

Notice that none of the items asks whether the manager is loud, popular or the best at the job. Quiet, thoughtful people often make excellent managers, because they listen well and think before they act.

A manager who ticks most of these boxes is a good manager. And every one of these things can be learned, starting with how you lead a group project at school.
