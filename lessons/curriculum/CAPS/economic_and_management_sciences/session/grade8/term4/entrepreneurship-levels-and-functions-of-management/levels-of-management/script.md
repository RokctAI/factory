# Part 1 — Expert

This session introduces management and the three levels at which it happens in a business. The anchor is a family-owned supermarket in Mthatha with sixty employees. The owner, who is also the managing director, and a general manager decide where the business is going. Six department managers run the fresh produce, bakery and butchery, groceries, tills, finance and human resources departments. Ten supervisors lead the shifts on the shop floor, and forty-two workers pack shelves, bake, cut meat, serve at the tills and clean. Every one of the eighteen people in management has a different kind of job, and the supermarket works well only when the three levels work together. The governing idea is that management is the process of getting work done through other people by using the business's resources efficiently to reach its goals; that larger businesses divide management into top, middle and lower levels; and that each level has its own responsibilities, time horizon and kind of decisions. The meaning of management, the three levels of management, the responsibilities and decisions of each level, and the organisational chart are examinable, and they lead into Grade 10 Business Studies.

## Subtopic: What Management Is

Management is the process of planning, organising, leading and controlling the resources of a business, its people, money, equipment, materials and information, so that it reaches its goals efficiently and effectively. A manager is a person who is responsible for that process and for the work of other people.

Two words in that definition are important. Effectively means achieving the goal: the supermarket keeps its shelves full and its customers satisfied. Efficiently means achieving it without wasting resources: the shelves are full without stockpiling food that spoils, and the tills are staffed without paying people to stand idle. A good manager achieves both.

Every business needs management, from a one-person car wash to a mining company with thousands of employees. In a small business, the owner is usually the only manager and does everything: decides what to sell, orders stock, hires and trains staff, serves customers and checks the money. As a business grows, there is too much work for one person, so management is shared among several people and divided into levels.

The levels of management are usually shown as a pyramid. At the top is a small number of senior managers; in the middle, a larger number of middle managers; at the bottom of the management pyramid, the largest group of managers, the supervisors; and below them, the workers, who do the operational work but do not manage other people. In the Mthatha supermarket there are two top managers, six middle managers and ten supervisors: eighteen managers in all, leading forty-two workers, sixty people altogether.

The questions for this section are with you now: the meaning of management, the difference between efficiently and effectively, and why larger businesses need levels of management.

## Subtopic: Top Management

Top management consists of the most senior managers in a business: the owner or owners, the managing director or chief executive officer, the general manager, and, in a company, the board of directors. In the supermarket, top management is the owner, who is the managing director, and the general manager.

Top management is responsible for the business as a whole. Its main tasks are these. It sets the vision, mission and long-term goals of the business, for example to become the leading supermarket in the district within five years. It makes strategic decisions, which are major, long-term decisions that affect the whole business: whether to open a second branch in Butterworth, whether to start a home delivery service, whether to borrow money to rebuild the store. It decides on the overall budget and how resources are divided between departments. It appoints and supervises middle managers. And it represents the business to the outside world: banks, suppliers, government and the community.

Top managers plan for the long term, usually three to five years or more. Their decisions carry high risk, because a wrong choice, such as opening a branch where there are too few customers, can cost the business a great deal. They spend most of their time thinking, planning and meeting people, and very little time doing operational work themselves.

The questions for this section are with you now: who belongs to top management, its responsibilities, and the meaning of strategic decisions.

## Subtopic: Middle Management

Middle management consists of the heads of departments or functions: in the supermarket, the managers of fresh produce, bakery and butchery, groceries, tills and customer service, finance, and human resources.

Middle managers are the link between top management and lower management. They take the broad goals and plans set by top management and turn them into specific plans for their own departments. If top management decides that the supermarket will increase sales by ten percent next year, the fresh produce manager must decide how her department will contribute: perhaps by sourcing more local vegetables, improving displays and reducing waste.

Their main tasks are to make tactical decisions, which are medium-term decisions, usually for one to three years or for the coming year, about how to implement the strategy in a department; to draw up departmental plans and budgets; to coordinate the work of the supervisors under them; to monitor the department's performance and report to top management; and to pass information both ways, down from top management to the supervisors and up from the shop floor to top management.

Middle managers need a good understanding of the work of their departments and strong skills in working with people, because they must both carry out instructions from above and motivate the people below. When communication between top and lower management breaks down, the problem is often found at the middle level.

The questions for this section are with you now: who belongs to middle management, its responsibilities, tactical decisions, and its role as a link.

## Subtopic: Lower Management

Lower management, also called first-line or supervisory management, consists of supervisors, foremen and team leaders who directly lead the workers. In the supermarket, the ten supervisors include the shift supervisors at the tills, the head baker, the head butcher and the receiving supervisor who checks deliveries.

Lower managers are responsible for the day-to-day running of the business. They make operational decisions, which are short-term, routine decisions, often for the next day or week: who works which shift, which shelves must be restocked first, how to deal with a long queue at the tills, what to do when a delivery arrives short. They give instructions to workers and make sure the work is done correctly, safely and on time; they train new workers; they deal with workers' problems and complaints and report them to middle managers; and they check quality, for example that the bread is properly baked and the meat correctly labelled.

Supervisors spend most of their time on the shop floor with the workers, and many of them do some of the operational work themselves. They need strong technical skills, because they must know how the work is done, and good people skills, because they deal with workers directly every day. Many supervisors are promoted from among the workers because they know the work well.

The number of workers a manager leads directly is called the span of control. A supervisor at the tills might lead six cashiers, while the general manager has a span of control of six middle managers. A span that is too wide makes it hard to supervise properly; one that is too narrow wastes managers.

The questions for this section are with you now: who belongs to lower management, operational decisions, its responsibilities, and the span of control.

## Subtopic: How the Levels Work Together

The relationships between the levels are shown in an organisational chart, a diagram with top management at the top, middle managers in the next row, supervisors below them, and lines showing who reports to whom. The chart shows the chain of command, the line of authority from the top to the bottom, so that every person knows who his or her manager is.

Authority, the right to give instructions, flows downwards. Responsibility, the duty to do the work and account for it, flows upwards: workers account to supervisors, supervisors to middle managers, and middle managers to top management.

Information flows in both directions. Top management passes goals and decisions down; the shop floor passes up information about customers, problems and results. The supermarket's tills supervisor might notice that customers are asking for a product the store does not stock; if that information reaches top management, it could lead to a new product line.

Consider one decision that touches all three levels: introducing home delivery. Top management makes the strategic decision to offer it, sets a goal of four hundred deliveries a month, and approves the budget for a delivery van. The groceries manager, in middle management, plans how orders will be taken, picked and packed, and how many staff are needed. A supervisor, in lower management, draws up the daily delivery roster, checks each order before it leaves and deals with customers' complaints.

In a small business, one person may do the work of all three levels. In a very large business, there may be more than three levels, with additional layers such as regional managers. But the principle is the same: different people manage at different levels, with different time horizons and decisions.

The questions for this section are with you now: the organisational chart, the chain of command, the flow of authority, responsibility and information, and how one decision passes through all three levels.

## Subtopic: Levels of Management in Other Organisations

The three levels are found in every kind of organisation that is large enough, not only in businesses that sell goods.

A public company, such as a large retail chain listed on the JSE, has a board of directors elected by the shareholders at the very top, with a chief executive officer and other executive directors who run the company from day to day. Middle management includes regional and divisional managers, and lower management includes the store supervisors in hundreds of branches. Because there are so many people, there may be more than three layers, for example national, regional and branch managers.

A school also has levels. The principal, together with the school governing body, which sets policies and approves the budget, forms top management. Deputy principals and heads of department form middle management: a head of department for Economic and Management Sciences plans the subject for the year and supports the teachers. Grade heads and teachers who coordinate activities perform lower-management tasks, and every teacher manages the learning in a classroom.

A government department has a minister and a director-general at the top, chief directors and directors in the middle, and deputy directors and supervisors at lower levels. A municipality has the municipal manager at the top, with heads of services such as water, roads and finance in the middle.

Non-profit organisations, such as a community feeding scheme, often have a board of trustees at the top, a programme manager in the middle and site coordinators who manage the volunteers.

In each case the same pattern appears: a few people set direction for the long term, a middle group turns that direction into plans for parts of the organisation, and a larger group leads the daily work.

The questions for this section are with you now: the levels of management in a public company, a school, government and a non-profit organisation.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Think of the supermarket as a pyramid of people. Few at the top, more in the middle, and most at the bottom.

## Subtopic: The Supermarket's Pyramid

At the very top sit two people: the owner and the general manager. They look far ahead, five years or more. Should we open a second shop? Should we start home deliveries? These are big, risky decisions about the whole supermarket. That is top management.

In the middle are six department managers: fresh produce, bakery and butchery, groceries, tills, finance and people. Each one takes the big plan and works out what it means for their department, usually for the next year. That is middle management, the link between the top and the floor.

Lower down are ten supervisors: the head baker, the head butcher, the shift supervisors at the tills. They are on the shop floor every day, deciding who works which shift and which shelf to fill first. That is lower management.

Below them are the forty-two workers who do the actual packing, baking, cutting and serving.

Your own school works the same way. The principal and the governing body are at the top, deciding the school's direction for years ahead. The heads of department are in the middle, planning each subject for the year. Teachers and grade heads lead the daily work in classrooms and corridors. Next time a new rule is announced at assembly, ask yourself which level made the decision, and which level will have to make it work every day.

Count them: two plus six plus ten is eighteen managers. Add forty-two workers and you get all sixty people.

## Subtopic: One Idea, Three Levels

Let us follow one idea through the pyramid: home deliveries.

At the top, the owner says yes, we will deliver, and we aim for four hundred deliveries a month. She approves money for a delivery van. That is a strategic decision.

In the middle, the groceries manager plans the details: customers order on WhatsApp, two packers pick the orders each morning, and the van leaves at ten and at three. That is a tactical decision.

On the floor, a supervisor makes the daily roster, checks every bag before it leaves, and calls a customer whose eggs arrived broken. Those are operational decisions.

Now notice the arrows. Instructions go down the pyramid. Reports and problems come up. If the supervisor hears that customers want deliveries on Sundays too, that message must travel up to the top, or the chance is lost.

Here is a quick test for any decision. Ask how long it lasts and how much of the business it touches. Years and the whole business: top management. A year and one department: middle management. Today or this week, on the floor: lower management.

Now try the practice questions: who sits at each level, what kind of decisions they make, and how the levels connect.
