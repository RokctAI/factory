### subtopic_1 — What Management Is
**Assistant:** Why does a small business not need three levels of management?

**Tutor:** Because in a small business there are too few people and too little work for separate layers of managers to make sense, so the owner usually does the work of all three levels personally. Think of a spaza shop or a small car wash with two or three workers. The owner decides whether to expand or to stock a new product, which is a strategic decision. She plans next month's stock and budget, which is tactical. And she decides each morning who washes which car and handles a complaint from a customer, which is operational. She does all of this herself, often while also serving customers. Employing a separate general manager, department managers and supervisors would cost far more in salaries than the business earns, and there would be nobody for them to manage. As the business grows, the owner's time becomes the bottleneck. If the car wash opens three branches and employs thirty people, the owner cannot be at every branch at once, so she appoints a supervisor at each branch to run the day-to-day work. Later she might appoint a manager to look after finance and another to look after operations, so that she can concentrate on the future of the business. Levels of management appear gradually as a business grows, because they allow work to be divided so that each manager can concentrate on one kind of decision. The idea of the three levels is still useful in a small business, though. It reminds the owner to set aside time for long-term thinking, and not to spend every hour on daily problems.

### subtopic_2 — Top Management
**Assistant:** What is the difference between strategic, tactical and operational decisions?

**Tutor:** The difference lies in how far into the future the decision reaches, how much of the business it affects, how risky it is, and therefore which level of management makes it. Strategic decisions are made by top management. They are long-term, usually three to five years or more, they affect the whole business, and they carry high risk because they are hard to reverse. In the supermarket, deciding to open a second branch in Butterworth, to start a home delivery service, or to borrow money to rebuild the store are strategic decisions. Tactical decisions are made by middle management. They are medium-term, often for the next year, they affect one department or function, and they are about how to carry out the strategy. Once home delivery has been approved, the groceries manager decides how orders will be taken, how many packers are needed and when the van will leave. The finance manager decides how to budget for the van's fuel and insurance. Operational decisions are made by lower management. They are short-term, often for today or this week, they affect a small part of the business, and they are often routine. A supervisor decides who packs orders this morning, which customer gets the first delivery, and how to respond when an order arrives damaged. The three kinds are connected: operational decisions carry out tactical plans, and tactical plans carry out the strategy. A useful test is to ask how long the effect will last and how much of the business it will touch. The longer and wider the effect, the higher the level of management that should make it.

### subtopic_3 — Middle Management
**Assistant:** Why is middle management called the link between the levels?

**Tutor:** Because middle managers stand between those who set the direction of the business and those who carry out the daily work, and information, instructions and results must pass through them in both directions. Top management thinks about the whole business and the long term. It sets goals such as increasing sales by ten percent next year or launching home delivery. But those goals are broad. They do not tell the head baker how many loaves to bake or the tills supervisor how many cashiers to roster on a Saturday. Middle managers translate the broad goals into specific departmental plans, budgets and targets that supervisors can act on. That is the downward link. The upward link is just as important. Supervisors see problems and opportunities first: a fridge that keeps breaking, customers asking for a product the store does not sell, a worker who is struggling, a supplier whose deliveries are always late. Middle managers collect this information, deal with what they can, and report the important issues and results to top management, so that strategic decisions are based on what is really happening. Middle managers also coordinate between departments. If home delivery needs fresh produce, groceries and finance to work together, it is the middle managers who must agree on who does what. Because of this position, middle managers need strong communication and people skills as well as knowledge of their departments. When businesses run into trouble because top management is out of touch, or because workers do not understand what the business is trying to achieve, the link at the middle is often where it has broken.

### subtopic_4 — Lower Management
**Assistant:** What does span of control mean, and is a wide or narrow span better?

**Tutor:** Span of control is the number of people who report directly to one manager, and neither wide nor narrow is always better: the right span depends on the kind of work, the skills of the workers and the abilities of the manager. In the supermarket, a tills supervisor who leads six cashiers has a span of control of six. The general manager, who has six department managers reporting to her, also has a span of six, even though many more people work in the business, because only those six report to her directly. A narrow span, with few people per manager, allows close supervision, quick help and good communication. It suits complex or risky work, or new and inexperienced workers. But it needs more managers, which costs more in salaries, and it creates more levels in the pyramid, which can slow down communication and decisions. A wide span, with many people per manager, needs fewer managers and fewer levels, so it is cheaper and information travels faster. It suits simple, routine work done by experienced, well-trained workers who need little supervision. But if the span is too wide, the manager cannot give each person enough attention, problems are noticed late, and quality may suffer. For example, a supervisor with twenty cashiers on a busy month-end Saturday would struggle to deal with every voided sale, cash shortage and customer complaint. Good managers adjust the span as conditions change: they might narrow it when many new workers start, and widen it as those workers gain experience.
