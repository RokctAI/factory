### subtopic_1 — The Sole Trader and the Business Entity
**Assistant:** Why must the owner's private spending be kept out of the business books?

**Tutor:** Because the whole purpose of the books is to show how the business itself is performing, and mixing in private spending makes that impossible. Imagine the car wash owner pays her children's school fees, her family's groceries and her personal cellphone account from the business bank account and records them as business expenses. Expenses would be overstated, so profit would look much smaller than it really is. She might decide the car wash is failing when it is actually doing well, or a bank deciding whether to lend to her would get a false picture. SARS would also be misled: business expenses reduce taxable profit, so claiming private spending as business expenses is a form of tax evasion. The business entity concept solves this by treating the business as a separate unit. Only transactions between the business and the outside world, or between the business and the owner, are recorded. When the owner takes money from the business for private use, it is recorded as drawings, which reduces owner's equity but not profit. That way profit shows the true result of the car wash's activities, and owner's equity shows how much of that profit the owner has left in the business. Legally a sole trader and the business are one person, which is why unlimited liability applies, but for accounting purposes they are kept apart. Companies, which you will study in Term 3, are separate in law as well.

### subtopic_2 — Capital, Assets, Liabilities and Owner's Equity
**Assistant:** If the owner takes money out, why is that not an expense?

**Tutor:** Because an expense is a cost incurred to earn income, and money the owner takes for private use does not help the business earn anything. When the car wash pays its workers' wages, that money buys labour that washes cars and brings in fees: it is part of the cost of earning income, so it is an expense and it reduces profit. When the owner takes six thousand rand to buy groceries for her family, the car wash gets nothing in return that helps it earn income. Instead, the owner is withdrawing part of her interest in the business. So drawings reduce owner's equity directly, without passing through profit. The difference shows up clearly in the calculation. Profit equals income minus expenses: forty-eight thousand minus twenty-six thousand, twenty-two thousand rand. Drawings are not in that calculation. Owner's equity equals capital plus profit minus drawings: fifty thousand plus twenty-two thousand minus six thousand, sixty-six thousand rand. If drawings were wrongly treated as an expense, profit would be shown as sixteen thousand rand, which would understate how well the business performed. Owner's equity would end at the same sixty-six thousand rand either way, but the information about performance would be wrong. In the books, drawings have their own account, and because they decrease owner's equity, drawings increase on the debit side, which is the D at the end of DEAD.

### subtopic_3 — Income, Expenses, Profit and Loss
**Assistant:** Why do assets increase on the debit side but liabilities increase on the credit side?

**Tutor:** Because of the accounting equation and the need for every transaction to balance. The equation is assets equal owner's equity plus liabilities. Assets sit on the left-hand side of the equation, and owner's equity and liabilities on the right. Accounting makes the sides of each account match their side of the equation: accounts on the left of the equation increase on the left of the account, which is debit; accounts on the right of the equation increase on the right of the account, which is credit. Now look at what that achieves. When the owner contributes fifty thousand rand, bank increases by fifty thousand on the debit side and capital increases by fifty thousand on the credit side. Total debits equal total credits, and the equation still balances: assets fifty thousand, owner's equity fifty thousand. When the business borrows twenty thousand rand, bank is debited twenty thousand and loan, a liability, is credited twenty thousand: assets seventy thousand equals owner's equity fifty thousand plus liabilities twenty thousand. Income and expenses fit the same logic because they are temporary parts of owner's equity. Income adds to owner's equity, so it follows owner's equity and increases on the credit side. Expenses and drawings reduce owner's equity, so they increase on the opposite side, debit. This is why the rule is not arbitrary. Once you understand the equation, you can work out the side for any account instead of memorising a list.

### subtopic_4 — Debit and Credit
**Assistant:** Is a debtor an asset even though the business has not received the money?

**Tutor:** Yes, because the business has a legal right to receive that money, and a right to receive future cash is something of value the business controls. Suppose a taxi company brings ten taxis to the car wash every week and pays at the end of the month. After the first week the car wash has done the work and earned the fees, but the money has not arrived. The taxi company is now a debtor: a person or business that owes money to the business. The amount owed is a current asset, because the car wash expects to receive it within a year, in fact within a month. If the car wash did not record it, its assets and income would be understated. In Grade 8 we focus on cash transactions, where money changes hands immediately, and debtors appear mainly as an item to classify. In Grade 9 you will record credit sales in the debtors journal and follow debtors more closely. The opposite of a debtor is a creditor: a person or business to whom the business owes money, for example a soap supplier who delivers now and is paid in thirty days. A creditor is a current liability. A useful memory aid is that debtors are in debt to the business, and creditors gave the business credit. Debtors carry a risk: some may never pay. A business that sells on credit must check customers carefully and follow up late payments, because profit on paper does not pay the wages until the cash arrives.
