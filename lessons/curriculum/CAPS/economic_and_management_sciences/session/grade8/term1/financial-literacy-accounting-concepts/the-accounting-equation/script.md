# Part 1 — Expert

This session introduces the accounting equation, assets equal owner's equity plus liabilities, written A equals OE plus L, the single rule on which the whole of double-entry accounting rests. The anchor is a new driving school: on day one its owner deposits eighty thousand rand of her own money, borrows forty thousand rand from a bank and buys a dual-control car for one hundred thousand rand. After each of those steps, and after every lesson fee, wage and fuel bill that follows, the two sides of the equation stay equal. The governing idea is that everything a business owns was supplied either by its owner or by outsiders, so the total of its assets must always equal the total of the owner's claim and the outsiders' claims, and that every transaction changes the equation in a way that keeps it in balance. The equation, its rearrangements, the effect of each type of transaction on assets, owner's equity and liabilities, and the analysis table are examinable, and the equation is used in every year of Accounting to Grade 12.

## Subtopic: Why Assets Equal Owner's Equity Plus Liabilities

Every asset a business holds had to be paid for by someone. There are only two possible sources. Either the owner supplied it, through capital contributed or profits left in the business, or outsiders supplied it, through loans and credit. The owner's claim on the assets is owner's equity; the outsiders' claim is liabilities. Because every rand of assets is claimed by one or the other, the totals must be equal:

Assets equal owner's equity plus liabilities, A equals OE plus L.

This is called the accounting equation, and it is true at every moment for every business. The left side shows what the business owns. The right side shows where the money came from, or who has a claim on the assets.

Look at the driving school on day one. The owner deposits eighty thousand rand. Assets, the bank, are eighty thousand rand; owner's equity, capital, is eighty thousand rand; liabilities are zero. Eighty thousand equals eighty thousand plus zero. Then she borrows forty thousand rand from a bank. Assets rise to one hundred and twenty thousand rand; owner's equity stays at eighty thousand; liabilities rise to forty thousand. One hundred and twenty thousand equals eighty thousand plus forty thousand.

The equation can be rearranged like any equation in mathematics. To find owner's equity: OE equals A minus L. To find liabilities: L equals A minus OE. The first rearrangement shows clearly what owner's equity means: it is what would be left for the owner if the business sold all its assets and paid all its debts. This is sometimes called the net worth of the business.

Consider a calculation. A business has assets of two hundred and fifty thousand rand and liabilities of seventy thousand rand. Owner's equity is two hundred and fifty thousand minus seventy thousand, one hundred and eighty thousand rand.

The questions for this section are with you now: the meaning of each side of the equation, why it always balances, and calculations using its rearrangements.

## Subtopic: How Transactions Affect the Equation

Every transaction affects at least two items in the equation, and the equation always stays in balance. There are only a few possible patterns.

Pattern one: an asset increases and owner's equity increases by the same amount. This happens when the owner contributes capital, and when the business earns income in cash. Both sides rise equally.

Pattern two: an asset increases and a liability increases. This happens when the business borrows money. Both sides rise equally.

Pattern three: an asset decreases and owner's equity decreases. This happens when the business pays an expense in cash, because expenses reduce profit and so reduce owner's equity, and when the owner takes drawings. Both sides fall equally.

Pattern four: an asset decreases and a liability decreases. This happens when the business repays part of a loan. Both sides fall equally.

Pattern five: one asset increases and another asset decreases by the same amount. This happens when the business buys an asset for cash, such as a vehicle or equipment. Only the left side changes, and its total stays the same, so the equation still balances.

The link between income, expenses and owner's equity is the most important idea in this session. Income increases owner's equity because profit belongs to the owner. Expenses decrease owner's equity because they reduce profit. That is why, in the equation, income and expenses are shown as changes in owner's equity, with the reason written next to them, such as current income or wages.

A useful check after every transaction is to add the changes on the left and on the right. The net change on the left must equal the net change on the right.

The questions for this section are with you now: the five patterns, why income and expenses affect owner's equity, and how to check that a transaction keeps the equation balanced.

## Subtopic: The Analysis Table

Exam questions present transactions and ask you to show their effect in a table with columns for the account debited, the account credited, and the effect on assets, owner's equity and liabilities, with plus, minus or zero and the amount, and a column for the reason when owner's equity changes.

Work through the driving school's first month.

Transaction one: the owner deposits eighty thousand rand as capital. Debit bank, credit capital. Assets plus eighty thousand; owner's equity plus eighty thousand, reason capital contribution; liabilities zero.

Transaction two: a loan of forty thousand rand is received from a bank. Debit bank, credit loan. Assets plus forty thousand; owner's equity zero; liabilities plus forty thousand.

Transaction three: a vehicle is bought for one hundred thousand rand by EFT. Debit vehicles, credit bank. Assets plus one hundred thousand and minus one hundred thousand; owner's equity zero; liabilities zero.

Transaction four: lesson fees of twelve thousand rand are received. Debit bank, credit current income. Assets plus twelve thousand; owner's equity plus twelve thousand, reason income; liabilities zero.

Transaction five: wages of four thousand rand are paid. Debit wages, credit bank. Assets minus four thousand; owner's equity minus four thousand, reason expense; liabilities zero.

Transaction six: fuel of one thousand five hundred rand is paid. Debit fuel, credit bank. Assets minus one thousand five hundred; owner's equity minus one thousand five hundred, reason expense.

Transaction seven: the owner withdraws two thousand rand for private use. Debit drawings, credit bank. Assets minus two thousand; owner's equity minus two thousand, reason drawings.

Transaction eight: five thousand rand of the loan is repaid. Debit loan, credit bank. Assets minus five thousand; liabilities minus five thousand.

At the end of the month the bank holds nineteen thousand five hundred rand and the vehicle is worth one hundred thousand rand, so assets total one hundred and nineteen thousand five hundred rand. Owner's equity is eighty thousand plus twelve thousand minus four thousand minus one thousand five hundred minus two thousand, which is eighty-four thousand five hundred rand. Liabilities are forty thousand minus five thousand, thirty-five thousand rand. Eighty-four thousand five hundred plus thirty-five thousand is one hundred and nineteen thousand five hundred. The equation balances.

The questions for this section are with you now: completing the analysis table for each type of transaction, and checking the final totals.

## Subtopic: Profit, Drawings and the Equation

The equation also shows how a business's results change the owner's position. Owner's equity at any date equals capital contributed, plus profit, minus drawings. Profit itself equals income minus expenses.

In the driving school's first month, income was twelve thousand rand, and expenses were wages four thousand rand plus fuel one thousand five hundred rand, five thousand five hundred rand. Profit was six thousand five hundred rand. Owner's equity is capital eighty thousand plus profit six thousand five hundred minus drawings two thousand, which is eighty-four thousand five hundred rand, exactly the figure from the analysis table.

This gives you a powerful way to find missing figures. If you know the owner's equity at the start and end of a period, and the drawings and any new capital, you can calculate the profit. Suppose owner's equity at the start of a year was one hundred thousand rand and at the end one hundred and thirty thousand rand; the owner contributed additional capital of ten thousand rand and took drawings of eighteen thousand rand. The increase in owner's equity is thirty thousand rand. That increase was caused by capital plus ten thousand, drawings minus eighteen thousand, and profit. So thirty thousand equals ten thousand minus eighteen thousand plus profit, and profit is thirty-eight thousand rand.

Three common errors are worth naming. Treating a loan received as income: wrong, it increases liabilities, not owner's equity. Treating the purchase of an asset as an expense: wrong, it exchanges one asset for another and owner's equity is unchanged. Treating drawings as an expense: owner's equity still falls, but drawings must be shown with the reason drawings, and they do not reduce profit.

The questions for this section are with you now: linking profit and drawings to owner's equity, calculating a missing profit, and avoiding the three common errors.

## Subtopic: Worked Practice with a Second Business

A second business shows that the method works for any transaction. A garden service owned by a sole trader has these transactions in its first two weeks.

First, the owner deposits thirty thousand rand into the business bank account as capital. Assets plus thirty thousand, bank; owner's equity plus thirty thousand, capital.

Second, the business buys a brush cutter, a trailer and tools for twelve thousand rand by EFT. Assets plus twelve thousand, equipment, and minus twelve thousand, bank. Owner's equity and liabilities are unchanged.

Third, the owner brings her own lawnmower, worth three thousand rand, into the business as additional capital. This is a capital contribution that is not money. Assets plus three thousand, equipment; owner's equity plus three thousand, capital. No bank account is involved, so this transaction would be recorded in the general journal rather than a cash journal, but its effect on the equation is the same as any capital contribution.

Fourth, fees of seven thousand five hundred rand are received for garden services. Assets plus seven thousand five hundred, bank; owner's equity plus seven thousand five hundred, income.

Fifth, advertising of nine hundred rand is paid by EFT for flyers and an online advert. Assets minus nine hundred; owner's equity minus nine hundred, expense.

Sixth, the telephone account of four hundred and fifty rand is paid by debit order. Assets minus four hundred and fifty; owner's equity minus four hundred and fifty, expense.

Now total the equation. The bank is thirty thousand minus twelve thousand plus seven thousand five hundred minus nine hundred minus four hundred and fifty, which is twenty-four thousand one hundred and fifty rand. Equipment is twelve thousand plus three thousand, fifteen thousand rand. Total assets are thirty-nine thousand one hundred and fifty rand. Owner's equity is thirty thousand plus three thousand plus seven thousand five hundred minus nine hundred minus four hundred and fifty, which is thirty-nine thousand one hundred and fifty rand. Liabilities are zero. The equation balances.

Profit for the two weeks is income seven thousand five hundred minus expenses one thousand three hundred and fifty, six thousand one hundred and fifty rand. Notice that neither the equipment purchase nor the lawnmower affected profit: both are assets, not expenses.

The questions for this section are with you now: a non-cash capital contribution, and totalling assets, owner's equity and liabilities after a series of transactions.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through one driving school and one kitchen scale, plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: The Scale That Never Tips

Picture an old kitchen scale with two pans. On the left pan you put everything the driving school owns: the money in the bank and the car. On the right pan you put where that stuff came from: the owner's money, and the money borrowed from the bank. The scale is always level. Always. Because every single thing on the left came from somewhere on the right.

Day one: the owner puts in eighty thousand rand. Left pan: eighty thousand in the bank. Right pan: eighty thousand from the owner. Level.

She borrows forty thousand from the bank. Left pan: one hundred and twenty thousand. Right pan: eighty thousand from the owner plus forty thousand owed to the bank. Level.

She buys the car for one hundred thousand. Watch carefully: the bank goes down by one hundred thousand and the car comes in at one hundred thousand. Both changes are on the left pan, so the left pan still weighs one hundred and twenty thousand. Nothing changes on the right. Level.

That is the accounting equation: what you own equals the owner's share plus what you owe to others. Assets equal owner's equity plus liabilities.

Now the trick for finding a missing number. If the business owns two hundred and fifty thousand rand of stuff and owes seventy thousand, the owner's share is what is left after paying everyone else: one hundred and eighty thousand. Take away what you owe from what you own.

Keep the scale in mind and try the questions for this part: left pan, right pan, and what happens when you buy something.

## Subtopic: Lessons, Wages and the Owner's Share

Now the driving school starts working. A learner pays for lessons: twelve thousand rand comes into the bank. The left pan gets heavier by twelve thousand. Who does that extra money belong to? The owner: she earned it. So her share on the right goes up by twelve thousand. Level.

On Friday she pays the instructor four thousand rand in wages and fills up the car for one thousand five hundred rand. The bank goes down by five thousand five hundred. Whose share gets smaller? The owner's: those costs eat into her profit. Level.

She takes two thousand rand home for groceries. Bank down two thousand, her share down two thousand. Level. But remember, that is drawings, not an expense.

She pays back five thousand of the loan. Bank down five thousand, the amount owed to the bank down five thousand. Level.

So here is the simple rule. Money that is earned, the owner's share goes up. Money that is spent on running costs, the owner's share goes down. Money borrowed or repaid, the amount owed changes. Money spent on a thing you keep, like a car, only swaps one asset for another.

At the end of the month: bank nineteen thousand five hundred, car one hundred thousand, together one hundred and nineteen thousand five hundred. Owner's share eighty-four thousand five hundred, loan thirty-five thousand, together one hundred and nineteen thousand five hundred. Level, every time.

Keep the driving school in mind and try the questions for this part: which side moves, and does the owner's share go up or down?
