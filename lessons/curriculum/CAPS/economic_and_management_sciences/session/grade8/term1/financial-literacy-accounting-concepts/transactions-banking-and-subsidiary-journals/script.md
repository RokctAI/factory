# Part 1 — Expert

This session follows money into and out of a business and shows where accounting first writes it down. It covers transactions, the role of the bank, cash receipts and cash payments, and the subsidiary journals, the books of first entry. The anchor is one week at a small hair salon run by a sole trader: customers pay cash or tap their cards, the takings are deposited at the bank, the rent is paid by electronic funds transfer and the stylists' wages go out on Friday. Every one of those events is a transaction, every transaction is supported by a document, and every document is recorded first in a journal. The governing idea is that accounting begins with a transaction, an event that changes the financial position of the business and can be measured in money, and that transactions are sorted by type into separate subsidiary journals, so that similar transactions can be recorded together and totalled before they are posted to the ledger. Transactions, cash and credit, the services of the bank, receipts and payments, and the purpose of each subsidiary journal are examinable, and the journals are developed fully in Terms 2 and 3 and in Grade 10 Accounting.

## Subtopic: Transactions

A transaction is any event that changes the financial position of a business and can be measured in money. When the salon receives two hundred and fifty rand for a haircut, buys shampoo, pays rent, receives a loan or when the owner contributes capital, those are transactions. When the salon owner hires a new stylist who starts next month, that is not yet a transaction, because no money has changed hands and nothing financial has changed; it will become one when wages are paid.

Every transaction has at least two effects, which is why double entry works. A haircut paid in cash increases the bank, an asset, and increases current income, which increases owner's equity. Paying rent decreases the bank and increases rent expense, which decreases owner's equity.

Transactions can be classified in several ways. By timing of payment, a cash transaction is one where payment is made immediately, in notes and coins, by card, or by electronic transfer; a credit transaction is one where goods or services are supplied now and paid for later. In accounting, cash means payment at the time of the transaction, whatever the method; it does not only mean notes and coins. By direction, a cash receipt is money coming into the business and a cash payment is money going out.

Each transaction must be supported by a source document, a written or electronic record that proves the transaction took place and provides the details needed to record it: the date, the amount, the parties and what it was for. Receipts, till slips, deposit slips, EFT notifications, bank statements and invoices are source documents, and they are the topic of the next session. The rule is simple: no document, no entry. Source documents protect the business against errors and fraud, and SARS requires businesses to keep them for five years.

The questions for this section are with you now: the definition of a transaction, cash and credit transactions, cash receipts and payments, and why every entry needs a source document.

## Subtopic: The Bank and Banking

A business keeps its money in a bank, not in a drawer, and it is important to understand what the bank does for it.

A business opens a current account, also called a cheque account or business account, which allows frequent deposits and payments. A bank offers several services. It keeps money safe, which reduces the risk of theft. It allows the business to receive money: customers can pay in cash that the business deposits, pay by card through a card machine, or transfer money directly by electronic funds transfer, EFT. It allows the business to pay: by EFT from online or app banking, by debit order, where a supplier is authorised to take a fixed or variable amount on a set date, and by card. It provides an overdraft facility, allowing the business to pay out more than it has in the account up to an agreed limit, and loans for larger purchases. And it issues a bank statement, usually monthly, listing every amount paid into and out of the account, the bank charges and the balance.

Banks charge fees for their services: monthly account fees, transaction fees, cash deposit fees and card machine fees. These bank charges are an expense to the business.

Payment methods have changed quickly. South African banks stopped issuing and accepting cheques by 2021, and electronic payments replaced them. Older textbooks and past papers still show cheques, cheque counterfoils and cheque numbers, so you should recognise them, but a business today pays by EFT, debit order or card. Card payments and EFTs reduce the risks of handling cash, but businesses must protect passwords and watch for fraud, such as fake proof-of-payment messages.

A business should deposit its cash takings daily, both for safety and so that the bank records match the business's own records. A deposit is recorded on a deposit slip, or by the cash deposit machine's printed receipt, which the business keeps as proof.

The questions for this section are with you now: the services of a bank, EFTs and debit orders, bank charges, and why cash should be deposited every day.

## Subtopic: Cash Receipts and Cash Payments

Cash receipts are all the money a business receives. For a service business the main cash receipts are current income from services, capital contributed by the owner, loans received from a bank, and rent income if the business lets out space. Each receipt is supported by a document: a duplicate copy of a receipt issued to the payer, the cash register roll for many small cash sales, or the bank statement or EFT notification for money paid directly into the account.

Cash payments are all the money a business pays out. For a service business the main cash payments are expenses such as wages, rent, water and electricity, telephone, stationery, advertising and bank charges; purchases of assets such as equipment and vehicles; repayments of loans; and drawings by the owner. Each payment is supported by a document: an EFT payment confirmation, a cash slip or invoice from the supplier, a debit order shown on the bank statement, or, in older records, a cheque counterfoil.

The difference between total cash receipts and total cash payments for a period changes the bank balance. Consider the salon for one week, starting with a bank balance of eight thousand rand. Cash receipts were current income of fourteen thousand six hundred rand. Cash payments were rent of six thousand rand, wages of five thousand four hundred rand, and stationery and products of one thousand two hundred rand, a total of twelve thousand six hundred rand. The bank balance at the end of the week is eight thousand plus fourteen thousand six hundred minus twelve thousand six hundred, which is ten thousand rand.

Notice again that cash receipts are not all income, and cash payments are not all expenses. A loan received is a cash receipt, but it is a liability, not income. Buying equipment is a cash payment, but it is an asset, not an expense. Drawings are a cash payment, but they reduce owner's equity, not profit. This distinction becomes important when you analyse transactions in the cash journals.

The questions for this section are with you now: the main cash receipts and payments of a service business, their source documents, and the bank balance calculation.

## Subtopic: Subsidiary Journals

A journal is a book of first entry, where transactions are first recorded in date order from the source documents. Instead of one long list of every transaction, businesses use several subsidiary journals, each for one type of transaction. The word subsidiary means supporting: the journals support the general ledger, where the information ends up.

The cash receipts journal, CRJ, records all money received by the business, from receipts, the cash register roll and the bank statement. The cash payments journal, CPJ, records all money paid out by the business, from EFT confirmations, cash slips and the bank statement. In Grade 8 these two cash journals are the main focus: the CRJ in Term 2 and the CPJ in Term 3.

In Grade 9 you will add the debtors journal, DJ, which records services or goods sold on credit, from duplicate invoices; the creditors journal, CJ, which records goods or services bought on credit, from original invoices; the petty cash journal, for small cash payments from a petty cash box; and the general journal, GJ, for transactions that do not fit any other journal, such as corrections.

Why use separate journals? First, efficiency: similar transactions recorded together can be totalled, so instead of posting each haircut to the ledger separately, the business posts one monthly total of current income. Second, division of work: in a larger business, different clerks can work on different journals at the same time. Third, control: each journal is linked to particular source documents, so it is easy to check that every document has been recorded and to find errors.

Each cash journal has analysis columns, one for each account that appears often, such as current income in the CRJ, and wages in the CPJ, and a sundry accounts column for accounts that appear only occasionally, such as capital or loan. At the end of the month the journals are closed off by totalling each column, and the totals are posted to the general ledger. From the ledger, a trial balance is drawn up, and from the trial balance the financial statements. That chain from transaction to financial statement is the accounting cycle, the subject of Term 2.

The questions for this section are with you now: the meaning of a journal, the CRJ and CPJ and the documents they use, the Grade 9 journals, and why businesses use subsidiary journals.

## Subtopic: Internal Control over Cash

Cash is the asset most easily lost or stolen, so every business needs internal control: the procedures that protect its assets and make sure its records are accurate.

The most important controls over cash receipts are these. Issue a pre-numbered receipt, or ring up the till, for every amount received, and keep the duplicate copy or the till roll, so that every rand received can be traced. Deposit all takings intact at the bank every day, meaning the whole amount, without paying expenses out of the takings first, so that the deposit matches the day's receipts exactly. Separate duties where possible, so that the person who receives cash is not the same person who records it in the journal. Keep only a small cash float on the premises, locked away.

The most important controls over cash payments are these. Make payments by EFT rather than in cash, so that every payment leaves a record at the bank. Require approval from the owner or manager before a payment is made, and two people to authorise large payments. Pay only against a valid source document, such as an invoice. Protect banking passwords and card PINs, and confirm a supplier's bank details by phone before paying, because fraudsters send fake invoices with changed account numbers.

The final control is the bank reconciliation, which you will learn in Grade 9 and Grade 10. Each month the business compares its own records, the cash journals, with the bank statement. Any difference must be explained, whether it is bank charges the business has not yet recorded, a deposit not yet shown by the bank, an error, or a fraud.

Good internal control does not mean distrust of workers. It protects honest workers from suspicion, because every amount can be accounted for, and it protects the owner from losses that could close a small business.

The questions for this section are with you now: why cash needs internal control, three controls over receipts and three over payments, and the purpose of comparing records with the bank statement.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through one week at a hair salon, plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: A Week at the Salon

Monday: a customer gets braids and taps her card for six hundred rand. Tuesday: three customers pay cash for cuts. Wednesday: the owner pays the rent of six thousand rand from the banking app. Thursday: she buys shampoo and pays cash at the wholesaler. Friday: she pays the stylists' wages by EFT, and a corporate client transfers money for a staff grooming day straight into the bank.

Every one of those is a transaction: something that changed the salon's money and can be counted in rand. Some bring money in; those are cash receipts. Some send money out; those are cash payments. And in accounting, cash does not mean only notes and coins. If the money moves now, by card, EFT or notes, it is a cash transaction. If the salon does the hair now and the client pays next month, that is a credit transaction.

For every transaction there must be a piece of paper or a digital record: the till slip, the receipt book copy, the EFT confirmation, the wholesaler's slip, the bank statement. No paper, no entry. That is how the owner can prove what happened, to herself, to the bank and to SARS.

Now count the money. The salon started the week with eight thousand rand in the bank. Money in: fourteen thousand six hundred rand. Money out: rent six thousand, wages five thousand four hundred, products one thousand two hundred, which is twelve thousand six hundred. So the bank ends at eight thousand plus fourteen thousand six hundred minus twelve thousand six hundred: ten thousand rand.

And why keep the money in the bank? Because a drawer full of cash can be stolen, and because the bank gives a statement that checks your records.

## Subtopic: Two Boxes on the Counter

Imagine the salon owner has two boxes on her counter. Into the first box she drops a copy of every document for money that came in: receipts, the till roll, proof of transfers in. Into the second box she drops every document for money that went out: EFT confirmations, slips from suppliers, the debit orders on the bank statement.

At the end of each day she empties the boxes and writes each document into a book. The money-in box goes into the cash receipts journal, the CRJ. The money-out box goes into the cash payments journal, the CPJ. These are called journals, or books of first entry, because this is the very first place the transaction is written in the accounts.

Why two books and not one? Think of a taxi rank. If every taxi to every destination parked in one long line, nobody could find their taxi. So the rank has separate lines for each route. Journals work the same way: money-in in one book, money-out in another, and later, in Grade 9, credit sales and credit purchases in their own books too.

Inside each journal there are columns, like lanes. In the CRJ there is a lane for current income, because that happens every day, and a sundry lane for the rare ones, like a loan or capital. In the CPJ there are lanes for wages and the other common payments. At month-end she adds up each lane, and instead of copying every single haircut into the main books, she copies one total. That saves time and makes mistakes easier to find.

So the path is: transaction, document, journal, and then, as you will see next term, ledger and financial statements.

Keep the two boxes in mind and try the questions for this part: CRJ or CPJ, and why journals have columns.
