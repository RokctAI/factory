# Part 1 — Expert

This session builds the vocabulary of accounting that every later financial literacy topic in Grade 8 and 9 depends on: sole trader, capital, owner's equity, assets, liabilities, income, expenses, profit and loss, drawings, and the two most misunderstood words in the subject, debit and credit. The anchor is one small business, a car wash on a busy corner, started by a sole trader with fifty thousand rand of her own savings, a high-pressure washer, a borrowed loan and three workers. Every concept in this session can be pointed to somewhere on that corner. The governing idea is that accounting records the financial activities of a business separately from the owner's private life, so that the owner can see what the business owns, what it owes, what it has earned and spent, and whether it is making a profit. The definitions, the classification of items as assets, liabilities, owner's equity, income or expenses, the debit and credit rules, and profit and owner's equity calculations are examinable, and they are the foundation of Grade 10 Accounting.

## Subtopic: The Sole Trader and the Business Entity

Accounting is the process of recording, classifying, summarising and interpreting the financial information of a business, so that its owner and other interested people can make informed decisions. The person who records the transactions is called a bookkeeper, and the work of recording is bookkeeping; the accountant prepares and interprets the financial statements.

A sole trader is a business owned by one person, who supplies the capital, makes the decisions, takes all the profit and carries all the risk. Most small businesses in South Africa, such as hair salons, spaza shops, plumbers, tutors and car washes, are sole traders. A sole trader business is easy and cheap to start, because it does not have to be registered as a company, but the owner has unlimited liability: if the business cannot pay its debts, the owner's personal possessions, such as a house or car, can be used to pay them. You will compare the sole trader with other forms of ownership in Term 3.

Even though the law treats the sole trader and the business as one person, accounting keeps them apart. This is called the business entity concept, sometimes the separate entity rule: the business is treated as a separate unit from its owner, and only the business's transactions are recorded in its books. When the owner of the car wash buys groceries for her family with her own money, nothing is recorded. When she takes money from the business for her family, it is recorded, because money is leaving the business.

Businesses can be classified by what they do. A service business sells services rather than goods: the car wash, a hairdresser, a doctor, a driving school. A trading business buys goods and resells them at a profit: a spaza shop or a clothing store. A manufacturing business makes products from raw materials: a bakery or a furniture factory. In Grade 8 we work with service businesses, so there is no stock of goods to account for.

The questions for this section are with you now: the definition of accounting, the sole trader and unlimited liability, the business entity concept and the three types of business.

## Subtopic: Capital, Assets, Liabilities and Owner's Equity

A business needs resources to operate, and those resources must come from somewhere. Accounting answers two questions: what does the business own, and who supplied the money for it?

Capital is the money or other assets that the owner contributes to the business to start or expand it. When the owner of the car wash deposits fifty thousand rand of her savings into the business bank account, that is her capital contribution. From the business's point of view, capital is an amount the business owes to its owner, because the owner can claim it back if the business closes.

Assets are the possessions of the business, the resources it owns or controls that will bring future benefits. Non-current assets are held for use over a long period, more than a year, and are not meant to be sold: land and buildings, vehicles, equipment such as the high-pressure washer and vacuum cleaners, and furniture. Current assets are expected to be turned into cash or used within a year: money in the bank, cash on hand, called the cash float, and amounts owed to the business by customers, called debtors.

Liabilities are the debts of the business, amounts it owes to people or institutions other than the owner. Non-current liabilities are payable over more than a year, such as a long-term loan from a bank. Current liabilities are payable within a year, such as amounts owed to suppliers, called creditors, or a bank overdraft.

Owner's equity is the owner's total interest in the business: the capital contributed, plus the profits the business has earned, minus any losses, minus the amounts the owner has taken out for private use. This means owner's equity grows when the business makes a profit and shrinks when it makes a loss or when the owner takes money out.

The owner's private withdrawals are called drawings. Drawings are not an expense of the business, because they are not spent to earn income; they reduce owner's equity directly.

The questions for this section are with you now: capital, current and non-current assets and liabilities, owner's equity and drawings, with examples from the car wash.

## Subtopic: Income, Expenses, Profit and Loss

Income is money earned by the business from its normal activities. For a service business, the main income is the fee for its services: current income, also called services rendered or fee income. The car wash earns current income every time it washes a car. Other income items include rent income, if the business lets out part of its property, and interest income on its bank balance.

Expenses are the costs the business pays in order to earn its income: wages paid to workers, water and electricity, rent paid for premises, telephone, stationery, cleaning materials, insurance, advertising, bank charges and interest on loans. Expenses are used up in the process of earning income.

Profit is the amount by which income exceeds expenses over a period. A loss is the amount by which expenses exceed income.

Consider a worked example for the car wash for one month. Current income is forty-eight thousand rand. Expenses are wages eighteen thousand rand, water and electricity four thousand five hundred rand, and cleaning materials three thousand five hundred rand, a total of twenty-six thousand rand. Profit is forty-eight thousand minus twenty-six thousand, which is twenty-two thousand rand.

Now connect profit to owner's equity. The owner started with capital of fifty thousand rand. The business made a profit of twenty-two thousand rand. During the month she took drawings of six thousand rand for household needs. Owner's equity at the end of the month is fifty thousand plus twenty-two thousand minus six thousand, which is sixty-six thousand rand.

Notice a distinction that exams test often. Buying an asset is not an expense. When the car wash buys a new vacuum cleaner for five thousand rand, the business has exchanged money in the bank for equipment it will use for years; one asset has decreased and another increased, and profit is not affected. Paying the electricity account is an expense, because the electricity is used up.

The questions for this section are with you now: income and expense items, calculating profit or loss, calculating owner's equity, and why buying equipment is not an expense.

## Subtopic: Debit and Credit

Debit and credit are the two sides of every account in the double-entry system. In accounting, debit simply means the left side of an account and credit means the right side. They do not mean good or bad, and they do not mean money in or money out of your pocket. The abbreviations are Dr for debit and Cr for credit.

Every transaction affects at least two accounts, and the amount entered on the debit side of one account must equal the amount entered on the credit side of another. That is the double-entry principle, which you will apply fully in Term 4.

Which side records an increase depends on the type of account, and the rule follows the accounting equation. Assets are on the left of the equation, so assets increase on the debit side and decrease on the credit side. Owner's equity and liabilities are on the right of the equation, so they increase on the credit side and decrease on the debit side.

Income and expenses follow from owner's equity. Income increases owner's equity, so income accounts increase on the credit side. Expenses decrease owner's equity, so expense accounts increase on the debit side. Drawings also decrease owner's equity, so drawings increase on the debit side.

A simple way to remember the rule is the word DEAD: debits increase expenses, assets and drawings. Everything else, capital, liabilities and income, increases on the credit side.

Apply the rule to the car wash. When the owner contributes fifty thousand rand capital, the bank, an asset, increases, so bank is debited; capital, owner's equity, increases, so capital is credited. When the business receives forty-eight thousand rand for car washes, bank is debited and current income is credited. When it pays wages of eighteen thousand rand, wages, an expense, increases on the debit side, and bank, an asset, decreases on the credit side.

One common confusion comes from bank statements. When your bank credits your account, your money has increased. That is because the bank's statement is written from the bank's point of view: to the bank, your balance is a liability, money it owes you, and liabilities increase on the credit side. In the business's own books, the same money is an asset, increasing on the debit side.

The questions for this section are with you now: the meaning of debit and credit, the DEAD rule, and which side records an increase for each type of account.

## Subtopic: Classifying Items

The classification skill combines everything in this session and appears in almost every Grade 8 test. For each item, ask three questions in order.

First: does the business own it, and will it bring future benefits? If yes, it is an asset. Then ask whether it will be used for more than a year, a non-current asset, such as vehicles and equipment, or turned into cash within a year, a current asset, such as bank, cash float and debtors.

Second: does the business owe it to someone other than the owner? If yes, it is a liability. A loan repayable over five years is non-current; an amount owed to a supplier payable next month is current.

Third: does it relate to the owner? Capital contributed and drawings are owner's equity items.

If none of these, it is an income or expense item. Ask whether the business earned it, income, or used it up to earn income, expense.

Classify the following for the car wash. The high-pressure washer: non-current asset. Money in the bank: current asset. A loan from a bank repayable over four years: non-current liability. The owner's savings put into the business: capital, owner's equity. The owner taking cash for her family: drawings, owner's equity. Fees for washing cars: income. Wages paid to workers: expense. Water and electricity: expense. A customer who will pay at month-end: debtor, current asset. An amount owed to the soap supplier: creditor, current liability.

The questions for this section are with you now: classifying each item as an asset, liability, owner's equity, income or expense, and as current or non-current where it applies.

## Subtopic: The Car Wash on the Corner

On a busy corner there is a car wash. It belongs to one woman, so it is a sole trader. She takes all the profit, and if things go wrong, her own house could be at risk, because a sole trader has unlimited liability.

She started it with fifty thousand rand of her own savings, which she put into a business bank account. That is capital. It is still her money in a way, because if she ever closes the car wash, she can take it back. So the business owes it to her.

With the money, she bought a high-pressure washer and two vacuum cleaners. These are assets: things the business owns that help it earn money for years. She also kept money in the bank and a small cash float for change. Those are assets too, the kind that move quickly.

She needed more, so she borrowed twenty thousand rand from a bank, to be paid back over four years. That is a liability: money the business owes to someone who is not the owner.

Now the business is running. Every car washed brings in a fee: that is income. Every month she pays three workers, the water and electricity and the soap: those are expenses. If the fees are more than the expenses, she made a profit. If the expenses are more, a loss.

One month the fees were forty-eight thousand rand and the expenses twenty-six thousand rand. Profit: twenty-two thousand rand. She took six thousand rand home for her family. That is drawings, not an expense, because it was not spent to wash cars.

So what is her share of the business now? Start with fifty thousand, add the profit of twenty-two thousand, take away drawings of six thousand: sixty-six thousand rand. That is owner's equity.

Keep the car wash in mind and try the questions for this part: capital, assets, liabilities, income, expenses and drawings.

## Subtopic: Left Side, Right Side

Here is the secret about debit and credit: they just mean left and right. Debit is the left side of an account, credit is the right side. That is all.

Picture a T. Draw a big letter T on the page. Write the name of the account on top. Left of the line is debit, right of the line is credit.

Now the rule. For the things the business owns, assets, an increase goes on the left. For the things the business owes, to the owner or to the bank, an increase goes on the right. Simple: own on the left, owe on the right.

Income makes the owner richer, so it goes with the owner, on the right. Expenses and drawings make the owner poorer, so they go on the opposite side, the left.

Remember the word DEAD: Debit for Expenses, Assets and Drawings. Everything else goes up on the credit side.

Try it. The owner puts fifty thousand rand into the bank. Bank is an asset that went up: left, debit. Capital went up: right, credit. A customer pays for a car wash: bank up, debit; income up, credit. She pays the workers: wages up, debit; bank down, credit.

Every time, one left and one right, same amount. Left equals right. That balance is the heart of all accounting.

And one trap to avoid: when your bank says it credited your account, it is talking from its own side of the counter. In the car wash's own books, money arriving in the bank is a debit.

Keep the T in mind and try the questions for this part: left or right, DEAD, and which account goes up.
