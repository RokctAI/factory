# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines the source documents of a business: receipts, deposit slips, cash register or till slips, electronic funds transfer confirmations, bank statements and cash invoices. The anchor is the paper trail of one day at a small plumbing business: the plumber issues a cash invoice to a homeowner for fixing a geyser, gives a receipt to a customer who pays in notes, rings up washers sold at the counter on the till, deposits the day's notes at the bank, pays a supplier by EFT, and at month-end receives a bank statement that shows every one of those amounts. Every entry the business makes in its journals starts from one of these documents. The governing idea is that a source document is the first written evidence of a transaction, containing the details needed to record it, and that each business keeps either the original or a copy, depending on whether it issued or received the document, as proof for its records, its bank and SARS. The purpose and contents of each document, who issues it and who keeps which copy, and which journal it is recorded in are examinable, and these documents are used in every cash journal in Terms 2 and 3 and throughout Grade 9 and 10 Accounting.

## Subtopic: Why Businesses Need Source Documents

A source document is the original record of a transaction. It is the evidence that the transaction took place, and it supplies the details needed to record it: the date, the names of the parties, a description of what was bought, sold, paid or received, the amount and a document number.

Source documents serve several purposes. They are the basis for recording, because the bookkeeper writes up the journals from them rather than from memory. They provide proof, so that if a customer or supplier disputes an amount, the business can show what was agreed and paid. They support internal control, because pre-numbered documents make it possible to check that every transaction has been recorded and that none is missing. They are required by law: the Tax Administration Act requires businesses to keep their records, including source documents, for five years, so that SARS can audit them, and a VAT-registered business can only claim input VAT if it holds a valid tax invoice. And they are needed by auditors, banks and investors who want to check the accuracy of the financial statements.

A key idea is the original and the duplicate. When a business issues a document, such as a receipt or an invoice, the original goes to the other party and the business keeps a copy, called the duplicate, in its receipt or invoice book or in its computer system. When a business receives a document from someone else, it keeps the original. The bookkeeper records from the copy the business holds: the duplicate for documents it issued, the original for documents it received.

Today many documents are electronic, such as emailed invoices, SMS or app payment notifications and downloadable bank statements. They serve exactly the same purpose and must be kept just as carefully, in a safe digital filing system with backups.

The questions for this section are with you now: the definition of a source document, five purposes of source documents, the five-year rule, and the difference between the original and the duplicate.

## Subtopic: Receipts and Cash Register Slips

A receipt is a document issued by a business when it receives money, as proof that payment was received. A typical receipt shows the name and address of the business, a pre-printed receipt number, the date, the name of the person who paid, the amount in figures and in words, what the payment was for, the method of payment, and the signature of the person who received the money. The original is given to the payer; the duplicate stays in the receipt book.

The business that issues the receipt records the money received in its cash receipts journal from the duplicate receipt. The person who pays keeps the original as proof that they paid.

Receipts are used when a business receives an individual payment, for example a customer paying an account, the owner contributing capital, or rent received from a tenant.

A cash register slip, or till slip, is printed by a cash register or point-of-sale system each time a sale is rung up. It shows the business name, the date and time, the items sold and their prices, the VAT, the total, the amount tendered and the change, and the slip number. The customer receives the slip. The cash register also keeps a record of every sale on its internal roll or in its memory, called the cash register roll, and at the end of the day prints a total, often called the Z-reading.

Businesses with many small cash sales, such as shops, salons and the plumber's counter, do not write a receipt for each sale. Instead, the bookkeeper records the daily total from the cash register roll in the cash receipts journal as one entry. The individual till slips are the customer's proof of purchase, important if they want to return faulty goods under the Consumer Protection Act.

The questions for this section are with you now: the contents of a receipt, who keeps the original and the duplicate, the till slip and the cash register roll, and how each is recorded.

## Subtopic: Deposit Slips, EFTs and Bank Statements

A deposit slip is a form completed when cash is deposited into a bank account. It shows the account holder's name and account number, the date, the notes and coins deposited by denomination, and the total. The bank stamps or prints confirmation; the business keeps its copy or the printed receipt from a cash deposit machine. The deposit slip is used to check that the total deposited each day agrees with the receipts recorded in the cash receipts journal for that day. In the CRJ, individual receipts are listed in the analysis of receipts column and the day's deposit total appears in the bank column.

An electronic funds transfer, EFT, is a payment made electronically from one bank account to another, through internet banking or a banking app. When a business pays by EFT, the banking system produces a payment confirmation, often called a proof of payment, showing the date, amount, beneficiary, reference and a transaction number. The business that pays records the payment in its cash payments journal from this confirmation. The business that receives the money learns of it from an EFT notification or from its bank statement, and records it in its cash receipts journal. A business must never treat a forwarded proof of payment as proof that money has arrived; it must check its own bank account, because fake proofs of payment are a common fraud.

A bank statement is a document issued by the bank, usually monthly, listing all transactions in the account: deposits, EFTs received, EFTs and debit orders paid, card transactions, bank charges and interest, with the running balance. Remember that the statement is written from the bank's point of view, so deposits appear as credits and payments as debits. The bank statement is a source document for transactions the business learns about only from the bank: bank charges and interest are recorded in the cash journals from the statement, as are debit orders and direct deposits made by customers. The bank statement is also compared with the cash journals each month to detect errors and omissions.

The questions for this section are with you now: the deposit slip and how it links to the CRJ, recording EFTs from both sides, and the transactions recorded from the bank statement.

## Subtopic: Cash Invoices and Tax Invoices

An invoice is a document issued by a seller to a buyer listing the goods or services supplied and the amount due. A cash invoice is issued when the buyer pays immediately at the time of the transaction, as opposed to a credit invoice, which you will meet in Grade 9, where payment is made later.

A cash invoice typically shows the seller's name, address and contact details; the invoice number; the date; the buyer's name; a description of the goods or services, the quantity and unit price; the total, with VAT shown if the seller is registered for VAT; and a note that it has been paid. The original is given to the buyer; the seller keeps the duplicate.

The plumber who fixes a homeowner's geyser and is paid on the spot gives the homeowner the original cash invoice and keeps the duplicate; the plumber records the money received in the cash receipts journal. When the plumber buys pipes from a hardware supplier and pays immediately, the supplier issues a cash invoice; the plumber keeps the original and records the payment in the cash payments journal.

For VAT purposes, a VAT-registered seller must issue a tax invoice. A full tax invoice must include the words tax invoice, the seller's name, address and VAT registration number, the buyer's details and VAT number for larger amounts, a serial number, the date, a description of the goods or services, the value, and the VAT charged, or a statement that the price includes VAT at fifteen percent. Without a valid tax invoice, the buyer cannot claim the VAT back as input tax.

A worked example: the plumber charges eight hundred rand for labour and two hundred rand for parts, excluding VAT, a total of one thousand rand. VAT at fifteen percent is one hundred and fifty rand, and the invoice total is one thousand one hundred and fifty rand. If the total had been shown as one thousand one hundred and fifty rand including VAT, the VAT inside it would be one thousand one hundred and fifty multiplied by fifteen over one hundred and fifteen, which is one hundred and fifty rand.

The questions for this section are with you now: the contents of a cash invoice, cash versus credit invoices, the requirements of a tax invoice and the VAT calculation.

## Subtopic: Matching Documents to Journals

The final skill is to match each document to the journal in which it is recorded, from the point of view of the business doing the recording.

For the cash receipts journal, money received, the documents are the duplicate receipt, for individual amounts received; the cash register roll, for the daily total of till sales; the duplicate cash invoice, for services or goods sold for cash; and the bank statement or EFT notification, for EFTs and direct deposits received and for interest earned. The deposit slip supports the daily bank total.

For the cash payments journal, money paid out, the documents are the EFT payment confirmation, for payments made by internet banking; the original cash invoice or cash slip received from a supplier, for purchases paid immediately; and the bank statement, for debit orders, card payments and bank charges. In older records, the cheque counterfoil was the source document for payments by cheque.

A memory aid: if money comes in, look for the copy we kept of what we gave away, the duplicate receipt or invoice, or the bank's word that it arrived. If money goes out, look for the original we were given, the supplier's invoice or slip, or our own proof of payment.

Consider a mixed set from the plumber's day, and ask for each: in or out, and which document? A homeowner pays one thousand one hundred and fifty rand for a geyser repair: in, duplicate cash invoice, CRJ. Counter sales of washers and tape totalling three hundred and forty rand: in, cash register roll, CRJ. A supplier is paid two thousand four hundred rand for pipes by EFT: out, EFT confirmation, CPJ. The bank deducts bank charges of one hundred and twenty-five rand: out, bank statement, CPJ. A customer transfers six hundred rand directly into the account: in, bank statement, CRJ. The owner contributes five thousand rand of capital in cash: in, duplicate receipt, CRJ.

The questions for this section are with you now: matching each source document to the CRJ or CPJ, and choosing the document for a described transaction.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through one plumber's bakkie and one shoebox of paper, plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: The Plumber's Shoebox

A plumber drives his bakkie around town all day. On the passenger seat there is a shoebox, and every piece of paper from the day goes into it. At night his bookkeeper turns that shoebox into neat books. Here is what is inside.

First, the bill for the homeowner on Oak Street who paid for her new geyser element on the spot. He wrote out a cash invoice, gave her the original, and kept the carbon copy. That copy is his proof that money came in.

A receipt book with carbon copies. A customer paid for last week's job in notes, so the plumber wrote out a receipt, gave the top copy to the customer and kept the copy underneath. Money in again.

A long paper roll from the little till at his workshop counter, where he sells washers and tape. Instead of writing a receipt for every washer, the till keeps a roll of all the day's sales and prints a total at night. Money in.

A deposit slip from the bank, showing the notes he paid into his account on the way home. That proves the money really reached the bank.

A proof of payment from his banking app, for the pipes he paid for by EFT. Money out.

A cash slip from the hardware shop, for the tape he paid for on the spot. Money out.

And at the end of the month, the bank sends a statement listing everything, including things nobody gave him paper for: bank charges, a debit order and a customer who paid straight into his account.

That is the whole set of source documents CAPS asks for: receipts, till slips, deposit slips, EFTs, bank statements and cash invoices. Paper first, books second. No paper, no entry.

Keep the shoebox in mind and try the questions for this part: name each document and say whether it shows money in or money out.

## Subtopic: Top Copy, Bottom Copy

Every receipt book and invoice book has two copies of each page: a top copy and a bottom copy. Here is the simple rule. When you give a document away, the top copy goes to the other person, and you keep the bottom copy. When someone gives you a document, you keep their top copy.

So the plumber keeps the bottom copy of every receipt and cash invoice he writes, and records those in the money-in book, the cash receipts journal. He keeps the top copy of every slip or invoice suppliers give him, and records those in the money-out book, the cash payments journal.

Now try it the other way round. The homeowner who paid for the geyser has the top copy of the plumber's invoice. For her, it shows money out. Same piece of paper, opposite direction, because she is on the other side of the counter.

A quick sum the plumber does every time: labour eight hundred rand, parts two hundred rand, that is one thousand rand. Add VAT at fifteen percent, one hundred and fifty rand, and the bill is one thousand one hundred and fifty rand. If the bill only shows the total including VAT, the hidden VAT is the total times fifteen over one hundred and fifteen: one hundred and fifty rand again.

And why keep all this paper for years? Because SARS can ask to see five years of records, because a customer might say she never paid, and because a missing receipt number is the first sign that someone is stealing. Paper protects the honest plumber.

Keep the top copy and bottom copy in mind and try the questions for this part: who keeps which copy, and which book it goes in.
