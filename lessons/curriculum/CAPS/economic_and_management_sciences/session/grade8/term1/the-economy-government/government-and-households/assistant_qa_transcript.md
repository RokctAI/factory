### subtopic_1 — Households and Government in the Circular Flow
**Assistant:** Why do real flows and money flows always run in opposite directions in the circular flow?

**Tutor:** Because every exchange has two sides, and the payment always moves against the thing being paid for. Think of the most basic exchange between a household and government: a nurse works at a provincial hospital. Her labour, which is a real factor of production, moves from her household to the government. In return her salary, which is money, moves from the government to her household. The two arrows describe the same transaction seen from both ends. The same is true for services. Water, schooling and policing move from government to households, and the taxes, rates and service charges that pay for them move from households to government. If you drew only one arrow you would be showing a gift, not an exchange. This is also why the grant is special. When the older person's grant moves from government to a household, there is no real flow running the other way, because the grandmother gives nothing in exchange for it. That is exactly what makes it a transfer payment. In exam diagrams, label every arrow with what flows, and use solid lines for one kind and dotted lines for the other, or a key, so the marker can see you know which are real flows and which are money flows. In Grade 10 Economics the same diagram grows to include the financial sector and the foreign sector, and the rule of opposite directions still holds for every pair.

### subtopic_2 — Government as a Consumer in Relation to Households
**Assistant:** Is a government salary really a cost to the economy, or does the money come back?

**Tutor:** Much of it comes back, which is why economists look at the whole circular flow rather than at one payment alone. When a province pays a teacher, the salary is an expense in the provincial budget, paid from taxes. But the teacher does not keep the money in a drawer. She pays income tax through PAYE, which goes straight back to SARS. She buys groceries and pays fifteen percent VAT on the taxed items, which also returns to government. She pays her municipal account. And she spends the rest in local businesses: the spaza, the supermarket, the taxi, the hairdresser. Those businesses earn income, employ their own workers and pay their own taxes. Economists call this the multiplier effect: one rand of spending becomes income for someone else, who spends part of it again, and so on, so the total effect on the economy is larger than the first payment. That does not mean government salaries are free. The money must first be raised in taxes or borrowed, and if the wage bill grows faster than the economy, government has less left for schools, clinics and roads, or must borrow more and pay interest. That trade-off is a real debate in South Africa, because the public-sector wage bill is the biggest single item in the budget. For your answer, say that salaries are a cost to the budget, a source of income for households, and part of the circular flow that supports local businesses.

### subtopic_3 — Government as a Producer for Households
**Assistant:** Why does a no-fee school still cost money if learners pay nothing?

**Tutor:** Because someone always pays for a service, even when the user pays nothing at the counter. A no-fee school still needs teachers' salaries, textbooks, stationery, electricity, water, cleaning, repairs and food for the nutrition programme. The provincial education department pays for all of this. Teachers are paid directly by the province, and the school receives an allocation for each learner, called the norms and standards funding, to buy learning materials and pay running costs. Schools in the poorest communities receive the highest amount per learner, and that is why they can be declared no-fee schools. Where does the province's money come from? Mostly from the equitable share of national revenue that it receives from the national budget, and that revenue comes from taxes paid by households and businesses all over the country: income tax, VAT, company tax and others. So the honest answer is that every household helps pay for every no-fee school, through taxes, whether or not it has a child there. This is how government redistributes: a household with a high income pays more tax and, if its children attend a fee-paying school, may never use the free service, while a poorer household pays less tax and receives more in services. The point to carry into the national budget session is simple: free at the point of use is never free to produce.

### subtopic_4 — What Households Owe Government
**Assistant:** Why are basic foods like maize meal zero-rated for VAT?

**Tutor:** Because VAT is charged at the same rate whoever buys the goods, and that makes it heavier, as a share of income, on poor households than on rich ones. A household earning three thousand rand a month may spend half its income on food. A household earning sixty thousand rand a month may spend a small fraction on food. If both paid fifteen percent VAT on maize meal, the poor household would lose a much bigger share of its income to tax. Economists call a tax that takes a larger share of income from the poor than from the rich a regressive tax. Zero-rating reduces this effect. A zero-rated item has VAT charged at zero percent, so the shop does not add VAT to the price. The list in South Africa includes brown bread, maize meal, samp, mealie rice, dried beans, lentils, rice, fresh fruit and vegetables, milk, eggs, vegetable oil and pilchards, and paraffin. The government has added items over the years, such as bread flour, cake flour, and sanitary pads, after reviews of what poorer households buy most. Zero-rating costs the government revenue, because it gives up the tax it could have collected, but it protects the standard of living of the poorest households. This connects directly to the next topic, the national budget, where you will compare direct taxes like income tax with indirect taxes like VAT.
