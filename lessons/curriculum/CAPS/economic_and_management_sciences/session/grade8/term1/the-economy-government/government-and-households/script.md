# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines the relationship between government and households: what government takes from households, what it gives back, and how each of the three spheres acts as both a consumer and a producer in that relationship. The anchor is one household of five in a South African township: two parents who work, a grandmother who receives an older person's grant, a child in a no-fee school and a toddler who is taken to the clinic for vaccinations. Following the money and the services between that household and government shows the whole relationship in one picture. The governing idea is that households supply government with labour and with taxes, and government returns income, public goods, services and transfers, so that government acts as a consumer of the factors of production households own and as a producer of the services households use. The roles of each sphere, the flows in the circular flow diagram, and the difference between a service and a transfer are examinable, and the analysis returns in Grade 10 Economics as the public sector in the circular flow.

## Subtopic: Households and Government in the Circular Flow

A household is a person, or a group of people living together, who make joint decisions about earning and spending income. In the economy households have two roles: they own the factors of production, mainly labour, and they consume goods and services.

Between households and government there are four flows. Two run from households to government. The first is a real flow: households supply labour. Teachers, nurses, police officers, clerks, cleaners and engineers in the public service are members of households who sell their labour to government. The second is a money flow: households pay taxes, such as personal income tax deducted from salaries and value-added tax on the things they buy, and they pay municipal rates and service charges.

Two flows run from government to households. The first is a money flow: government pays salaries and wages to its employees and pays transfers, such as social grants, to households that qualify. The second is a real flow: government supplies public goods and services, such as schooling, health care, policing, water, electricity, roads and refuse removal.

The direction of each arrow matters in exam answers. A real flow is a flow of goods, services or factors of production; a money flow is a flow of payments. In a circular flow diagram, real flows and money flows always run in opposite directions between the same two sectors. Labour flows from households to government, and wages flow from government back to households. Services flow from government to households, and taxes flow from households back to government.

One important distinction is between payment for services and a transfer. A salary is paid in exchange for work; a grant is paid with nothing given in return. Economists call a payment with no good or service in exchange a transfer payment, and social grants are the main example.

The questions for this section are with you now: the four flows between households and government, which flows are real and which are money, and the meaning of a transfer payment.

## Subtopic: Government as a Consumer in Relation to Households

When government acts as a consumer, it buys something that households own. What households own and sell is mainly their labour, so government consumes the labour of households by employing them.

Each sphere is an employer. The national government employs soldiers in the South African National Defence Force, police officers in the South African Police Service, officials at Home Affairs, SARS and SASSA, and lecturers in public colleges. The provincial governments employ the largest groups in the public service: teachers in public schools and doctors, nurses and support staff in public hospitals and clinics. The municipalities employ water and electricity technicians, refuse collectors, traffic officers, librarians, firefighters and administrative staff. Together the three spheres employ well over a million people, which makes government the single largest employer in South Africa, and its total wage bill is the biggest single item of spending in the national budget.

Government also consumes property belonging to households. A municipality may rent office space from a private landlord; a provincial department may lease houses for staff; and in rare cases government may buy land from a household for a road or a school, paying compensation.

The importance of this role is that public-sector salaries are the main source of income for a very large number of households, especially in smaller towns where there are few private employers. When government increases salaries, those households have more to spend in local shops, and local businesses benefit. When government cuts posts, the effect spreads through the community.

The questions for this section are with you now: how each sphere acts as a consumer of household labour, and why public-sector salaries matter to a local economy.

## Subtopic: Government as a Producer for Households

When government acts as a producer, it supplies goods and services that households use. Some it supplies free at the point of use, some at a subsidised price, and some at a full price.

The national government produces national security and policing, the justice system, identity documents, passports and birth registration through Home Affairs, and funds universities and colleges through the National Student Financial Aid Scheme. It also produces income support: through SASSA it pays social grants, the largest being the child support grant, the older person's grant and the disability grant. Strictly, a grant is a transfer rather than a produced service, but it is the most important support households receive from the national sphere.

The provincial governments produce public schooling and public health care. Most public schools in poorer communities are no-fee schools, many provide meals through the National School Nutrition Programme, and public clinics provide primary health care, including immunisation, free of charge. Provinces also deliver housing subsidies for qualifying households and run provincial roads and some public transport.

The municipalities produce the services that reach the house itself: clean water and sewerage, electricity in many areas, refuse removal, street lighting, municipal roads, parks, libraries, sports fields, fire and emergency services and cemeteries. Households pay for most of these through monthly service accounts, but poor households registered as indigent receive a free basic allocation, commonly six thousand litres of water and fifty kilowatt hours of electricity per month.

Why does government produce these rather than leave them to the market? Some are public goods that no business could charge for. Others, such as education and health, create benefits for the whole society beyond the person using them, and some households could not afford them at market prices. Government production therefore improves the standard of living and reduces inequality.

The questions for this section are with you now: matching services to the sphere that produces them, the difference between free, subsidised and charged services, and why government produces them.

## Subtopic: What Households Owe Government

The relationship runs both ways, and households also have responsibilities towards government.

The first is to pay taxes. An employee's personal income tax is deducted every month by the employer through pay-as-you-earn, known as PAYE, and paid over to SARS. Households also pay value-added tax of fifteen percent on most goods and services they buy, and excise duties on items such as fuel, alcohol and tobacco. Basic foods such as brown bread, maize meal, rice, dried beans, fresh fruit, vegetables, milk and eggs are zero-rated for VAT, which protects poorer households.

The second is to pay municipal accounts and rates. Water, electricity and refuse services cost money to produce, and when residents do not pay, the municipality cannot maintain its pipes, substations and trucks.

The third is to use public resources responsibly. Saving water, reporting leaks, not dumping rubbish illegally, protecting public property from vandalism and avoiding illegal electricity connections all lower the cost of services for everyone.

The fourth is to participate. Households elect the people who run each sphere, may attend ward meetings and the public participation process for a municipality's integrated development plan and budget, and can hold officials accountable.

A clear exam answer on government and households therefore has two columns: what government provides, as employer, producer and source of transfers; and what households provide, as workers, taxpayers, ratepayers and responsible citizens.

The questions for this section are with you now: PAYE, VAT and zero-rating, and the responsibilities of households towards government.

## Subtopic: The Social Wage and the Standard of Living

When economists measure what government does for households, they do not count only cash. They add together the value of free and subsidised services and the grants, and call the total the social wage. In South Africa the social wage includes no-fee schooling and school meals, free primary health care, housing subsidies, free basic water and electricity, subsidised public transport and social grants. A household earning very little in wages may receive a social wage worth more than its cash income.

The social wage is government's main tool for raising the standard of living of poorer households and for reducing inequality between households. South Africa has one of the most unequal distributions of income in the world, measured by the Gini coefficient, a number between zero, meaning perfect equality, and one, meaning one person has everything. Before taxes and transfers South Africa's figure is extremely high; after the social wage is counted, it is lower, because the money raised in taxes from higher-income households is spent mostly on services and grants that benefit lower-income households.

Social grants deserve special attention because they reach so many people. Millions of households, a large share of all the households in the country, receive at least one grant. The child support grant is paid to the caregiver of a child in a low-income household until the child turns eighteen; the older person's grant from the age of sixty; the disability grant for adults who cannot work because of a disability; and the foster child grant and care dependency grant for children in particular circumstances. All of these are means-tested, which means applicants must show that their income and assets are below set limits.

The social wage also has limits. It is paid for by a relatively small number of taxpayers, so it depends on a growing economy and on taxes being collected efficiently. Grants relieve poverty but do not on their own create jobs, which is why government also spends on education, skills and infrastructure that help households earn their own income. The same tension, between relieving poverty now and building growth for later, returns in the session on the national budget.

The questions for this section are with you now: the meaning of the social wage, the main grants and who receives them, and what means-testing means.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through one household of five and one month of its money, plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: One Household, One Month

Picture a house of five people in a township. Mama is a nurse at the provincial hospital. Papa works at a hardware shop in town. Gogo receives the older person's grant. Thabo is in Grade 8 at the no-fee school down the road, and the baby goes to the clinic for her injections.

Now follow the month. On payday Mama's salary comes from the province. That is government buying her labour, so for that moment government is a customer, a consumer, and Mama is the seller. Papa's salary comes from a business, but both pay tax: before the money even reaches their bank accounts, some has gone to SARS through PAYE.

At the start of the month Gogo collects her grant. Nobody gave government anything for that money; it is a transfer, paid from the taxes of people who earn more. On the way home the family buys groceries. The maize meal and the milk carry no VAT, but the soap, the cooldrink and the airtime carry fifteen percent, and that VAT goes to government too.

Then look at what the family gets back without paying at the counter. Thabo walks to a school where the teachers are paid by the province and lunch is served from the nutrition programme. The baby's vaccinations are free at the clinic. At night a police van passes, paid for by the national government. In the morning the municipal truck takes the rubbish, and water comes out of the tap. The water account still arrives, but because the family's income is low they qualify for a free basic amount each month.

So in one month the family sold labour to government, paid taxes to government, received a grant from government, and used about a dozen government services. That two-way traffic is what this topic means.

Keep that household in mind and try the questions for this part: who is buying, who is selling and what is a transfer.

## Subtopic: Two Hats: Customer and Shop

Here is the simplest way to remember the whole topic. Government wears two hats with households.

Hat one is the customer hat. When government employs a teacher, a nurse, a police officer or the man who drives the refuse truck, it is buying their labour, exactly the way a customer buys bread. The household is the seller and the salary is the price.

Hat two is the shop hat. When government runs a school, a clinic, a library, a water network or a police station, it is producing a service for households, like a shop selling to customers. The difference is that this shop often charges nothing, or very little, at the counter. Households pay for it in a different way: through taxes and rates paid all year round.

Then there is one thing that is neither hat: the grant. The grant is not buying anything and not selling anything. It is money moving from people who have more to people who have less, through government. That is why it has its own name, a transfer.

Now try it fast. Government pays a firefighter: customer hat, buying labour. A municipal library lends books for free: shop hat, producing a service. Gogo's grant: transfer. A province pays a school cleaner: customer hat. A clinic gives free vaccinations: shop hat.

And what does the household owe in return? Pay tax, pay the municipal account, save water, report leaks, look after the park and vote. A shop with no paying customers closes; a municipality with no paying residents cannot fix the pipes.

Keep the two hats in mind and try the questions for this part: customer hat, shop hat or transfer.
