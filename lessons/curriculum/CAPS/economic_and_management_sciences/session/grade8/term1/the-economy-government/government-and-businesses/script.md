# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines the relationship between government and businesses: how each sphere of government buys from businesses as a consumer, how it supplies businesses with infrastructure and services as a producer, and what businesses give back in taxes and in obeying the rules. The anchor is a small construction company in a South African town that wins a tender to build classrooms for the provincial education department, pays company tax to SARS, buys electricity and water from the municipality, and moves its bricks along a national road. The governing idea is that government is both the largest single customer of businesses and the supplier of the roads, ports, power, water and legal order without which no business could operate, and that businesses in turn finance government through taxes and must obey the rules government sets. The flows between the two sectors, the consumer and producer roles of each sphere, procurement and tenders, and regulation are examinable, and they lead into Grade 10 Business Studies and Economics.

## Subtopic: Businesses and Government in the Circular Flow

A business is an organisation that combines the factors of production to produce goods or services, usually to make a profit. Between businesses and government there are four flows, matching the four you saw between households and government.

From businesses to government run two flows. The real flow is goods and services: businesses supply government with everything from pens and paper to medicines, police vehicles, school desks, computers, uniforms, construction work, security services and catering. The money flow is taxes: businesses pay company income tax on their profits, collect VAT from their customers and pay it over to SARS, deduct PAYE from their employees' salaries and pay it over, and pay customs and excise duties, municipal rates and service charges.

From government to businesses also run two flows. The money flow is payment: government pays businesses for the goods and services it buys. In some cases it also pays subsidies or incentives, money given to support particular businesses or industries, such as grants for small black-owned businesses, incentives for manufacturing or support for farmers after a drought. The real flow is public goods and services: roads, railways, ports and airports, electricity and water, policing and the courts, a stable currency, registration of companies, and the education and health of the workers businesses employ.

As with households, real flows and money flows between the same two sectors run in opposite directions. Goods flow from businesses to government while payment flows the other way; infrastructure and services flow from government to businesses while taxes flow back.

The questions for this section are with you now: the four flows between businesses and government, and the difference between a payment for goods and a subsidy.

## Subtopic: Government as a Consumer of Business Goods and Services

Government buys an enormous range of goods and services from businesses, and each sphere has its own shopping list.

The national government buys equipment for the defence force and police, vehicles, fuel and uniforms; computers and software for Home Affairs and SARS; and contracts with businesses to build and maintain national roads through the national roads agency. The provincial governments buy textbooks, stationery and furniture for schools; medicines, medical equipment, linen and food for hospitals; school buildings, clinics and provincial roads from construction firms; and scholar transport services from bus and taxi operators. The municipalities buy pipes, cables, meters, refuse trucks, streetlights and asphalt; contract businesses to build housing and roads; and hire security, cleaning and consulting firms.

All this buying is called public procurement. Because it uses taxpayers' money, section 217 of the Constitution requires that when government contracts for goods or services it must do so in a way that is fair, equitable, transparent, competitive and cost-effective. In practice this means a tender process. For larger contracts, the department advertises what it needs; businesses submit sealed bids stating their price and how they will deliver; a bid committee evaluates the bids against published criteria, including price, quality and preference points for empowerment goals; and the contract is awarded and published. Smaller purchases use quotations from several suppliers.

The tender process matters for two reasons. For businesses, a government contract can be the foundation of a whole company, especially a small business in a rural town where government is the biggest buyer. For citizens, a fair process means the state gets value for money. When tenders are corrupt, overpriced or awarded to businesses that cannot deliver, the cost is paid in classrooms that are never finished and roads that crack within a year.

The questions for this section are with you now: examples of what each sphere buys, the meaning of procurement and the steps in a tender process.

## Subtopic: Government as a Producer for Businesses

Government also produces the conditions that make business possible. Economists call the physical part of this infrastructure, the structures and networks a whole economy depends on.

The national government and the state-owned enterprises it controls produce the national road network, railways for freight, the major ports through which South Africa's imports and exports pass, the airports, and the national electricity generation and transmission grid. The national government also produces services that businesses rely on every day: the Companies and Intellectual Property Commission registers companies and trademarks; SARS administers tax and customs at the borders; the courts enforce contracts; the police protect property; the Reserve Bank keeps the value of the rand stable; and Statistics South Africa publishes the data businesses use to plan.

The provincial governments produce provincial roads, which carry farm produce to markets, and business support through provincial development agencies. They also produce the most important input of all over the long run: educated and healthy workers, through the schools and hospitals they run.

The municipalities produce the services every business premises needs: water and sewerage, the distribution of electricity in their areas, refuse removal, local roads and storm-water drains, street lighting, and land-use zoning and building approvals. A factory cannot open without the correct zoning and a connection to water and power, and both come from the municipality.

When these services fail, businesses pay. Load-shedding forced businesses to buy generators and diesel, damaged equipment and cut production; congestion at ports delayed exports; and potholed roads damage delivery vehicles. That is why infrastructure appears in every discussion of economic growth: it is a public good that lowers the cost of doing business for every firm at once.

The questions for this section are with you now: infrastructure and the services each sphere produces for businesses, and why failures of infrastructure raise business costs.

## Subtopic: Government as Regulator and Tax Collector

Government also sets the rules within which businesses operate. This role is called regulation, and it protects consumers, workers, the environment and fair competition.

Examples of laws that regulate businesses in South Africa include the Companies Act, which governs how companies are formed and run; the Consumer Protection Act, which protects buyers against unfair contracts, unsafe goods and misleading advertising; the Basic Conditions of Employment Act and the Labour Relations Act, which protect workers; the Competition Act, enforced by the Competition Commission, which stops businesses from fixing prices or abusing a dominant position; and environmental laws that control pollution and waste. Municipal by-laws add local rules on trading hours, signage, noise and health standards for food businesses.

In return for the services they receive, businesses pay taxes. A company pays company income tax at twenty-seven percent of its taxable income, that is, its profit after allowable expenses. A business registered for VAT charges fifteen percent VAT on its sales, deducts the VAT it paid on its own purchases, and pays the difference to SARS. Registration for VAT is compulsory once a business's taxable sales exceed one million rand in any twelve-month period. Businesses also act as collectors for government: they deduct PAYE from employees' salaries and pay it to SARS, and they pay contributions to the Unemployment Insurance Fund.

A simple VAT example shows how the system works. A furniture business buys wood for ten thousand rand plus fifteen hundred rand VAT. It sells the finished desks to a school for twenty thousand rand plus three thousand rand VAT. It pays SARS three thousand rand minus fifteen hundred rand, which is fifteen hundred rand. VAT is therefore paid in stages by each business along the chain, but the full burden falls on the final buyer.

The questions for this section are with you now: why government regulates businesses, the main laws, company tax and VAT, and the VAT calculation.

## Subtopic: Government Support for Small Businesses and Empowerment

Government does not treat all businesses the same. Because small businesses create a large share of new jobs and because the economy inherited deep inequalities in who owns businesses, government uses its buying power and its programmes to support small, medium and micro enterprises, known as SMMEs, and to broaden ownership.

The first tool is preferential procurement. The Preferential Procurement Policy Framework Act allows government to award points in a tender for goals other than price, such as ownership by black people, women, young people or people with disabilities, or for businesses located in a particular area. Most of the points still go to price, so a business cannot win with an unreasonable price, but a well-priced bid from a small local business can beat a slightly cheaper bid from a large firm elsewhere.

The second tool is broad-based black economic empowerment, B-BBEE. Businesses receive a B-BBEE level based on ownership, management, skills development, enterprise and supplier development and socio-economic development. A better level helps a business win government work and work from large companies, who must themselves report on whom they buy from. The purpose is to bring people who were excluded under apartheid into ownership and management.

The third tool is direct support. National and provincial agencies offer small business finance, mentorship and training, and the Department of Small Business Development runs programmes for informal traders, spaza owners and co-operatives. Some municipalities build trading stalls and markets for informal traders.

The fourth tool is reducing red tape. Every registration, permit and form costs a small business time and money. Government has simplified company registration, introduced a simpler turnover tax for very small businesses below a set turnover limit, so that the smallest firms can pay one simple tax on their sales, and allows small businesses to register for VAT voluntarily below the compulsory line if they wish.

There is also a responsibility on government to pay on time. Section 38 of the Public Finance Management Act and Treasury regulations require government to pay suppliers within thirty days of receiving a valid invoice. When departments pay late, a small business may be unable to pay its own workers and suppliers, and some close. Paying on time is one of the cheapest ways government can support small businesses.

The questions for this section are with you now: why government supports SMMEs, preference points, B-BBEE and the thirty-day payment rule.

## Subtopic: The Builder Who Won the Tender

Meet a small building company in a country town: four bakkies, twenty workers and an owner who knows every hardware shop in the district. One day the provincial education department advertises a tender: six new classrooms at the primary school. The owner prepares her bid, with her price, her plan and her company documents, and seals it in an envelope with the date. Five other builders do the same. A committee opens the bids, scores them on price, quality and empowerment points, and she wins.

Now watch who is doing what. The province is the customer: it is buying her building service with taxpayers' money. That is government as a consumer. The company is the seller.

But she cannot build a thing without government acting as a producer for her. Her bricks arrive on a truck along the national road. Her site needs water and a power connection from the municipality. Her company was registered with the national companies office. If a supplier refuses to deliver what she paid for, the courts will enforce her contract. And the twenty workers she employs went to public schools and are treated at public clinics.

And at the end of the year she pays company tax on her profit. Every month she pays SARS the VAT she charged the province minus the VAT she paid at the hardware shop, and she pays over the PAYE from her workers' wages.

So the builder sells to government, uses government services, obeys government rules and pays government taxes, all in one job. That is the whole topic in one construction site.

Keep that builder in mind and try the questions for this part: who is the customer, who is the producer and what is a tender.

## Subtopic: The Spaza and the Rules

Now go small. A spaza shop in your street sells bread, airtime, cooldrinks and paraffin. Does government touch this tiny business? All the time.

The municipality sells it electricity for the fridge and water for cleaning, collects its rubbish and may require a trading permit. Health rules say the cooldrinks must be within their sell-by date and the meat must be kept cold. The Consumer Protection Act says the shop cannot sell you something faulty and refuse to take it back. If the owner employs a helper, the labour laws say the helper must be paid at least the national minimum wage and may not be made to work unlimited hours.

Why all these rules? Think of a taxi rank with no marshal. Every driver pushes in, fares change every hour, and passengers get cheated. A good marshal does not drive the taxis; he just makes the rules fair so that the whole rank works. Government regulation is the marshal of the economy. It does not run the spaza, but it makes sure customers are protected, workers are paid fairly and no big business can push all the small ones out by cheating.

And the money? If the spaza is small, it may not need to register for VAT, because the line is one million rand of sales in a year. But the owner still pays income tax on the profit if it is high enough, and every bread wholesaler, every cooldrink supplier and every airtime company the spaza buys from pays its own taxes.

So remember three words for government and business: customer, producer and marshal. Government buys from businesses, produces the roads, power, water and rules businesses need, and keeps the game fair.

Keep the marshal in mind and try the questions for this part: regulation, consumer protection and the VAT line.
