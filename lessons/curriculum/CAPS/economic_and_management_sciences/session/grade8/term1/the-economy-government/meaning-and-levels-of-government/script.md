# Part 1 — Expert

This session defines government and sets out the three levels, or spheres, of government in South Africa: national, provincial and local. The anchor is one ordinary street in a South African town, where the national government pays the police officer on the corner, the provincial government runs the clinic and the school, and the municipality supplies the water in the taps and collects the rubbish on Tuesday morning. The governing idea is that government is a group of people given the authority to make and enforce rules for a country and to provide the public goods and services that the market on its own would not supply, and that the Constitution divides this work between three spheres so that each decision is taken as close to the people as is practical. The meaning of government, the three spheres with their leaders and functions, and the three arms of government are all examinable, and they lead directly into the study of the public sector in Grade 10 Economics.

## Subtopic: What Government Is

Government is the body of people and institutions that has the authority to govern a country: to make laws, to carry out those laws and to settle disputes about them. In economic terms, government is one of the main participants in the economy, alongside households, businesses and the foreign sector. It is a participant with a special position, because it can do two things no business can do: it can make rules that everyone must obey, and it can collect compulsory payments called taxes.

South Africa is a constitutional democracy. The Constitution of the Republic of South Africa, adopted in 1996, is the highest law in the country. Every law, every government action and every official must act within it. Citizens choose their representatives in free elections, normally held every five years, and the government is accountable to the people who elected it.

The Constitution divides the power of the state into three arms so that no single group holds all the power. This is called the separation of powers. The legislature makes the laws; at national level this is Parliament, made up of the National Assembly and the National Council of Provinces. The executive carries out the laws and runs the government departments; at national level it is the President and the Cabinet of ministers. The judiciary interprets the laws and settles disputes; it is the system of courts, with the Constitutional Court at the top on constitutional matters. A useful exam structure is to name the arm, state its function and give the body that performs it at national level.

Why does an economy need a government at all? Because some goods and services would not be provided properly if they were left to businesses that must make a profit. Street lights, national defence, a police service, a court system and public roads are used by everyone at once, and it is very hard to charge each user. These are called public goods. Other services, such as basic education, primary health care and clean water, are so important to every citizen that government makes sure they are available even to people who cannot pay the full price. Government also sets the rules within which businesses compete, protects consumers and workers, and redistributes income through taxes and social grants.

The questions for this section are with you now: the definition of government, the three arms with their functions, and why some goods are provided by government rather than by private businesses.

## Subtopic: The Three Spheres of Government

Section 40 of the Constitution states that government in South Africa is constituted as national, provincial and local spheres of government, which are distinctive, interdependent and interrelated. The Constitution deliberately uses the word spheres rather than levels to show that one is not simply the servant of another; each has its own powers, but they must cooperate. Most textbooks and exam papers still call them the three levels of government, and both words are accepted.

The national government governs the whole country. Its legislature is Parliament in Cape Town; its executive is the President, who is the head of state and head of the national executive, together with the Deputy President and the ministers who form the Cabinet. The administrative capital, where the Union Buildings and most national departments are found, is Pretoria in the City of Tshwane, and the judicial capital, home of the Supreme Court of Appeal, is Bloemfontein. The Constitutional Court sits in Johannesburg.

The provincial government governs one of the nine provinces: Eastern Cape, Free State, Gauteng, KwaZulu-Natal, Limpopo, Mpumalanga, the Northern Cape, North West and the Western Cape. Each province has a provincial legislature elected by its voters, and a Premier who heads the provincial executive with the members of the executive council, the MECs, each responsible for a department such as education or health.

The local government is made up of municipalities. Every part of South Africa falls inside a municipality. There are three categories: metropolitan municipalities, which govern the large cities on their own, such as the City of Johannesburg, the City of Cape Town, eThekwini and Nelson Mandela Bay; district municipalities, which cover large areas; and local municipalities, which share the work in those areas with their district. At present there are eight metropolitan, forty-four district and two hundred and five local municipalities, two hundred and fifty-seven in total. Each municipality has a council elected by its residents, led by a mayor or executive mayor, with ward councillors representing each ward.

The questions for this section are with you now: naming the three spheres, the leader of each, the nine provinces and the categories of municipality.

## Subtopic: Who Does What

The Constitution gives each sphere its own functions, and an exam question usually asks you to match a service to the sphere responsible for it. The principle is that matters affecting the whole country are handled nationally, matters that differ from province to province are handled provincially, and the services delivered to a particular house and street are handled locally.

The national government is responsible for defence through the South African National Defence Force; policing through the South African Police Service; foreign affairs; home affairs, which issues identity documents, birth certificates and passports; the national budget and tax collection through the South African Revenue Service, SARS; social grants paid through the South African Social Security Agency, SASSA; universities and colleges; national roads; and the rules for trade, banking and the currency, which the South African Reserve Bank manages independently.

The provincial governments are responsible mainly for public schools, through the provincial education departments; public hospitals and clinics, through the provincial health departments; provincial roads and public transport within the province; housing programmes, shared with national government; agriculture and nature conservation in the province.

The municipalities are responsible for the services you can see from your gate: water and sanitation, the distribution of electricity in many areas, refuse removal, municipal roads and storm-water drains, street lights, parks, libraries, cemeteries, local fire services, traffic control and building plans. They charge for these services through monthly accounts and property rates.

Some functions are concurrent, which means they are shared. Education, health, housing and transport are examples: national government sets the policy, the norms and the budget split, and provincial or local government delivers the service. When a function fails, the first question a citizen should ask is which sphere is responsible, because that decides whose office to approach.

The questions for this section are with you now: matching services such as passports, school textbooks, refuse removal and hospitals to the correct sphere, and explaining what a concurrent function is.

## Subtopic: Government as a Participant in the Economy

In the circular flow of the economy, which you met in Grade 7, households and businesses trade with each other: households supply the factors of production and receive income, and businesses supply goods and services and receive payment. Government sits inside this flow and connects to both, in four distinct ways that the next two sessions will examine one sphere at a time.

First, government is a consumer. It buys goods and services from businesses: textbooks and desks for schools, medicines for clinics, vehicles for the police, cement and steel for roads, and the services of construction companies, caterers and cleaning firms. Because the state is so large, its purchases, called government procurement, are a major source of business for many South African firms, and the rules for tendering must be fair, open and competitive.

Second, government is an employer and therefore a buyer of labour. Teachers, nurses, police officers, soldiers, clerks and municipal workers are paid salaries by one of the three spheres. The public service is one of the largest employers in the country, and the wage bill is one of the largest items in the national budget.

Third, government is a producer. It produces services that it supplies to households and businesses, such as education, health care, policing, water, electricity and roads, and through state-owned enterprises it also runs businesses such as the national electricity supplier, the railways, the ports and the postal service.

Fourth, government is a collector and redistributor of income. It collects taxes from households and businesses, and it pays transfers, such as social grants, to households that need support. These payments do not buy anything in return; they move income from people who are better off to people who are poorer, which is called redistribution.

To summarise the flows for an exam answer: households pay taxes to government and receive services and grants; businesses pay taxes to government and receive payment for the goods and services government buys; and government uses the money to produce public goods and services. The roles towards households and towards businesses are the topics of the next two sessions, and the taxes and spending are the topic of the national budget.

The questions for this section are with you now: the four roles of government in the economy, procurement, and the meaning of redistribution.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again, told from one street, one taxi ride and one block of flats, plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: One Street, Three Governments

Stand at the taxi rank in the morning and look around you. The police van parked at the corner belongs to the South African Police Service, and the officers are paid by the national government. The clinic across the road, with its queue already out the door, is run by the provincial department of health. The school next to it, with learners in their uniforms walking in, is run by the provincial department of education. The street light that was still on when you left home, the tap you drank from, the refuse truck coming down the road and the potholes everyone complains about belong to the municipality.

So one street, three governments. Why not one big government for everything? Think of a big family business, like a busy shisanyama. The owner decides the prices and the menu for every branch. The branch manager decides the shifts and the stock for his branch. The person at the grill decides when each piece of meat is ready. If the owner tried to watch every grill himself, nothing would get cooked. Government works the same way. The national government decides things for the whole country, the province runs the bigger services for its people, and the municipality looks after the streets and taps right where you live.

Each of the three has a boss. The country has the President and the ministers. Each of the nine provinces has a Premier and MECs. Each municipality has a mayor and councillors, and your ward councillor is the person who should know your street by name.

And here is the important part: you choose all of them. In national and provincial elections you vote for the parties that form Parliament and the provincial legislatures. In local elections you vote for your ward councillor and your council. If the service on your street is poor, you know who to ask.

Keep that street in mind and try the questions for this part: which government owns the police van, the clinic, the school and the refuse truck.

## Subtopic: Make, Do and Judge

Every government, at every level, has three jobs, and the easiest way to remember them is three words: make, do and judge.

Make means making the rules. At national level that is Parliament, where members argue and vote on new laws. Do means carrying out the rules and running the services: the President, the ministers and all the government departments, from Home Affairs that prints your ID to SASSA that pays the grants. Judge means deciding when someone has broken a rule or when a rule is unfair: that is the courts, all the way up to the Constitutional Court in Johannesburg.

Why keep the three jobs apart? Think of a soccer match at the local ground. One group writes the rules of the game, the players play, and the referee decides what is a foul. If one of the players were also the referee, his team would never lose. Splitting the jobs keeps everyone honest. That is the separation of powers.

Now the last test. Your grandmother needs a new ID book because hers was stolen: that is Home Affairs, so national. Your cousin needs a place at the public school: provincial. Water has been off for two days: municipality. A pension grant needs to be paid: SASSA, national. Building a big new national highway: national. Fixing the potholes outside your house: municipality.

One more picture before the questions. Think of government as the biggest customer and the biggest worker in town at the same time. When the school needs new desks, government buys them from a furniture business down the road, so it is a customer. When the clinic needs a nurse, government pays her salary, so it is an employer. When water comes out of your tap, government made that service, so it is a producer. And when your gogo collects her grant at month-end, government is passing on money it collected in taxes from people who earn more, so it is a redistributor. Customer, employer, producer and redistributor: four hats, one government. The money for all four hats comes from the same place, the taxes that households and businesses pay, and that is why the national budget, which is coming soon, matters to every family on the street.

When you can place each problem with the right government and say whether it is about making, doing or judging, you understand how the country is run.

Keep the soccer match in mind and try the questions for this part: make, do and judge, and which government fixes which problem.
