# Part 1 — Expert

This session asks what the national budget is for. Revenue and expenditure are the mechanics; the purpose is to make the economy grow and to share the results more fairly. The anchor is two learners born on the same day in 2012, one in a suburb with tarred roads, a fee-paying school and a family income in the top tenth, and one in a rural village with a long walk to a no-fee school and a household that depends on grants. The budget cannot make their lives equal, but through what it taxes and what it spends, it can narrow the gap between them and enlarge the economy in which both will look for work. The governing idea is that the budget influences economic growth through spending on infrastructure, education and health and through the incentives taxes create, and that it redresses inequality by taxing higher incomes progressively and spending more on lower-income households. Economic growth, inequality and the Gini coefficient, the ways the budget promotes growth and redistribution, and the tension between the two goals are examinable, and they return in Grade 12 Economics as fiscal policy and growth and development.

## Subtopic: Economic Growth and Why It Matters

Economic growth is an increase in the total production of goods and services in an economy over time. It is measured by the growth rate of the gross domestic product, GDP, which is the total market value of all final goods and services produced within a country's borders in a year. Statistics South Africa publishes GDP figures every quarter. To compare years fairly, economists use real GDP, which removes the effect of price increases, so that growth means more goods and services, not just higher prices.

Growth matters because it is the main way an economy creates jobs and raises incomes. When businesses produce more, they employ more workers; when more people work, more households earn income and pay tax, and government has more revenue to spend on services, without raising tax rates. Growth must also be compared with population growth: if GDP grows by one percent while the population grows by one and a half percent, the average income per person actually falls. GDP per person, called GDP per capita, is GDP divided by the population, and it is a rough measure of the average standard of living.

South Africa's growth has been slow for more than a decade, often below one or two percent a year, held back by electricity shortages, problems at ports and on freight rail, low investment, skills shortages and weak business confidence. Slow growth with a growing population has meant that GDP per capita has barely risen, unemployment has stayed above thirty percent, and government revenue has not grown fast enough to meet the demands on the budget.

A simple calculation shows the effect of growth over time. If an economy produces goods and services worth one hundred units and grows at three percent a year, after one year it produces one hundred and three units; after two years about one hundred and six point one; after twenty-four years it has roughly doubled. At one percent a year, doubling takes about seventy years. That difference is the difference between a generation that sees living standards double and one that does not.

The questions for this section are with you now: the definition of economic growth, GDP and GDP per capita, and why the rate of growth matters so much.

## Subtopic: How the Budget Promotes Growth

The budget influences growth in several ways, and an exam answer should name the mechanism, not just say spending helps.

First, infrastructure. Spending on roads, rail, ports, electricity, water and broadband lowers the cost of doing business for every firm at once, makes it possible to move goods and workers efficiently, and attracts investment. This is capital expenditure, and it supports growth for decades.

Second, human capital. Spending on education, skills and health produces workers who are more productive, which raises output and wages. The effect is slow, but it is one of the most powerful long-term drivers of growth.

Third, total spending in the economy. Government spending is part of total demand. When government pays salaries, buys from businesses and builds infrastructure, those payments become income for households and businesses, who spend part of it again. This chain is called the multiplier effect. In a slowdown, government can increase spending or reduce taxes to support demand; in a boom it can do the opposite. Using the budget in this way is called fiscal policy.

Fourth, incentives through the tax system. Lower taxes on businesses that invest, special economic zones with tax relief, tax incentives for research and development or for employing young people, such as the employment tax incentive, all encourage activities that create growth and jobs.

Fifth, stability and confidence. A budget that controls its deficit and debt keeps interest rates lower and gives investors confidence that taxes will not suddenly jump. Uncontrolled borrowing can do the opposite, raising interest costs for government and businesses alike.

The budget can also harm growth: taxes that are too high discourage work and investment, and wasteful or corrupt spending uses scarce money without producing results.

The questions for this section are with you now: the five ways the budget promotes growth, fiscal policy and the multiplier effect.

## Subtopic: Inequality in South Africa

Economic inequality means an uneven distribution of income or wealth among the people of a country. Income is the flow of money earned over a period, such as wages, profits, rent and interest. Wealth is the stock of assets owned at a point in time, such as savings, shares, houses and land. In South Africa wealth is even more unequally distributed than income.

Inequality is measured with the Gini coefficient, a number between zero and one. Zero means perfect equality, where every person has the same income; one means perfect inequality, where one person has all the income. South Africa's Gini coefficient for income is about zero point six three, among the highest in the world. Another simple measure is the share of income received by the richest and poorest groups: the richest tenth of South Africans earns more than half of all income, while the poorest half together earns a small fraction of it.

The causes are historical and economic. Colonialism and apartheid deliberately excluded the majority from land ownership, quality education, skilled jobs, business ownership and the cities' economic centres, and those patterns persist across generations. Today, inequality is driven mainly by the labour market: the gap between people with jobs and those without, and between highly skilled workers with high salaries and unskilled workers with low wages. Unemployment is the single largest driver, because a household with no wage earner has almost no income except grants.

Inequality differs from poverty. Poverty means not having enough to meet basic needs; it can be measured with a poverty line, such as Statistics South Africa's food poverty line, the amount needed to buy enough food for a healthy diet. A country can reduce poverty without reducing inequality, if everyone's income rises by the same proportion; and inequality can fall while poverty stays high, if the rich become poorer.

The questions for this section are with you now: the difference between income and wealth, the Gini coefficient, the causes of inequality and the difference between inequality and poverty.

## Subtopic: How the Budget Redresses Inequality

To redress means to set right. The budget redresses economic inequalities in two directions at once: through what it takes and through what it gives.

On the revenue side, progressive personal income tax takes a larger share of income from higher earners. Taxes on wealth and its transfer, such as capital gains tax, dividends tax, estate duty and donations tax, fall mainly on people who own significant assets. Zero-rating basic foods reduces the regressive effect of VAT on the poor.

On the spending side, the budget spends a much larger amount per person on lower-income households than they pay in tax. Social grants are the most direct form of redistribution, raising the incomes of the poorest households. The social wage, made up of no-fee schools, school meals, free primary health care, subsidised housing, free basic water and electricity, and subsidised transport, adds services that poor households could not otherwise afford. Studies by the World Bank and the National Treasury have found that South Africa's combination of taxes and spending reduces measured inequality more than in most comparable countries; the Gini coefficient after taxes, grants and free services is considerably lower than before them.

Some budget spending aims to remove the causes of inequality, not just ease its effects. Spending on early childhood development and education gives children from poor households the skills to earn higher incomes. NSFAS funds university and college studies for students from poor and working-class households. Land reform and restitution return land or compensation to people dispossessed under apartheid. Support for small businesses and preferential procurement help broaden business ownership. Public employment programmes, such as the Expanded Public Works Programme and the Presidential Employment Stimulus, give unemployed people work and income.

The National Development Plan, adopted in 2012, set the goals of eliminating poverty and reducing inequality by 2030, mainly through faster growth and more jobs. Progress has been slow, which is the subject of the final section.

The questions for this section are with you now: the revenue and spending tools of redistribution, the social wage, and spending that tackles the causes of inequality.

## Subtopic: The Tension Between Growth and Redistribution

The two goals of the budget sometimes support each other and sometimes pull against each other, and a good answer shows both.

They support each other when redistribution builds human capital and opportunity. Educated, healthy children grow into productive workers; grants that keep children in school and fed raise the future workforce's skills; and very high inequality can itself slow growth by creating social instability, crime and protest, and by wasting the talent of people who never get a chance. Growth, in turn, provides the tax revenue that redistribution depends on, and the jobs that are the most lasting route out of poverty.

They pull against each other when the money is limited. Every rand spent on current transfers today is a rand not spent on infrastructure that would raise growth tomorrow, and the reverse. Taxes that are too high on the people and businesses who invest can discourage that investment. Borrowing to pay for both goals adds to debt, and interest then takes money from both.

South Africa faces this tension sharply. Slow growth has limited revenue, while high unemployment has increased the need for grants and services. Government's stated approach is to protect the social wage, keep debt under control, and raise growth through reforms that cost the budget little, such as opening electricity generation to private producers, fixing freight rail and ports, and cutting red tape, so that the economy creates the jobs that grants cannot.

The most effective long-term redistribution is a job. A household with one stable wage earner is far less likely to be poor than a household relying on grants. That is why growth and redistribution are best seen as partners: the budget redistributes to protect people now and to equip them for the jobs that growth must create.

The questions for this section are with you now: how growth and redistribution support each other, how they compete for the same money, and why jobs are the most lasting form of redistribution.

## Subtopic: Two Learners, One Birthday

Two babies are born on the same day in 2012. One goes home to a suburb: tarred roads, a fee-paying school, a family that owns its house and has savings. The other goes home to a village: a dirt road, a long walk to a no-fee school, a family that depends on a child support grant and on one uncle's part-time job.

Neither baby did anything to deserve where they started. That uneven start is what inequality means: some people have far more income and wealth than others. In South Africa the gap is one of the widest in the world. Economists measure it with the Gini number, between zero for everyone equal and one for one person having everything. South Africa's is about zero point six three, which is very high.

What can the budget do? It works from two ends. From the top, it takes more tax from people who earn more: the suburb family pays a bigger share of its income in income tax. From the bottom, it gives more to people who have less: the village child gets a grant, a free school, a meal at break, free clinic visits, and maybe one day a NSFAS bursary to study. Money moves, through taxes and spending, from the first family's side to the second family's side.

That does not make them equal. But it narrows the gap, and the part that matters most for the future is the part that gives the village child skills: early learning, a good school, a chance at college. A grant helps today; an education helps for a lifetime.

Keep the two learners in mind and try the questions for this part: what inequality is, what the Gini number means and how the budget narrows the gap.

## Subtopic: Grow the Pie, Share the Pie

Think of the whole economy as a big pie, everything the country produces in a year. Economists call it GDP.

There are two ways to give more people a bigger slice. You can share the same pie differently, giving some of the big slices to the people with small slices. That is redistribution. Or you can bake a bigger pie, so that there is more for everyone. That is economic growth.

The budget does both. It bakes a bigger pie by building roads, power stations, ports and schools, by keeping people healthy and skilled, and by giving businesses reasons to invest and hire. It shares the pie more fairly through progressive taxes, grants and free services.

Here is the catch. The government's money is also limited. If it spends everything on sharing today, there is nothing left to build the oven for tomorrow. If it spends everything on the oven, people go hungry today. And if it borrows to do both, the interest bill eats into next year's pie.

Watch the growth numbers too. If the pie grows by three percent a year, it doubles in about twenty-four years. At one percent, it takes about seventy years. South Africa's pie has been growing very slowly, while the number of people sharing it keeps rising, so the average slice has hardly grown.

So what is the best way to share the pie? A job. A household with one steady wage is far less likely to be poor than a household living on grants alone. That is why the budget's two jobs, growth and sharing, are partners: share enough to protect people today, and grow the pie so there are jobs for them tomorrow.

Keep the pie in mind and try the questions for this part: growing it, sharing it and why a job beats a grant.
