### subtopic_1 — Economic Growth and Why It Matters
**Assistant:** Why is GDP per capita a better measure of living standards than GDP?

**Tutor:** Because GDP tells you the size of the whole pie, but not how many people must share it. A country's GDP could rise by two percent in a year, which sounds like good news. But if its population rose by three percent in the same year, there would be less output for each person than before, and the average standard of living would have fallen. GDP per capita divides GDP by the population, so it shows the average amount produced per person, a rough guide to the average income. Even GDP per capita has limits. First, it is an average, and an average hides inequality. In South Africa a high average is pulled up by a small number of very high incomes, while most people earn far less. Two countries with the same GDP per capita can have very different lives for ordinary people if one is much more unequal. Second, GDP counts only goods and services sold in markets, so it leaves out unpaid work such as caring for children and elderly relatives, which is mostly done by women. Third, it does not measure health, education, safety or the quality of the environment. That is why the United Nations also uses the Human Development Index, which combines income per person with life expectancy and years of schooling. You will meet these measures again in Grade 12 Economics under growth and development. For now, remember the rule: always compare growth with population growth before saying people are better off.

### subtopic_2 — How the Budget Promotes Growth
**Assistant:** What does the multiplier effect mean, with a real example?

**Tutor:** The multiplier effect is the idea that one rand of new spending creates more than one rand of total income in the economy, because the person who receives it spends part of it again. Take a real kind of example. A municipality spends ten million rand to build a new community hall and pays a local construction company. The company pays its workers' wages and buys bricks, cement and steel from suppliers. The workers spend most of their wages on food, transport, clothes and airtime in local shops, and save a little. The shop owners now have more income, so they restock, pay their own staff and spend part of their profit. Each round is smaller than the one before, because some money is saved, some is paid in taxes and some is spent on imported goods, which leaks out of the local economy. But add up all the rounds and total income created can be well above the original ten million rand. The size of the multiplier depends on how much of each extra rand is spent locally. Where households spend most of their income on locally produced goods, the multiplier is larger; where much is saved or spent on imports, it is smaller. This is why governments often increase spending, especially on infrastructure, during a slowdown: it supports jobs and incomes beyond the project itself. The same effect works in reverse when spending is cut. In Grade 12 Economics you will calculate the multiplier using a formula.

### subtopic_3 — Inequality in South Africa
**Assistant:** If grants reduce inequality, why is South Africa still so unequal?

**Tutor:** Because grants change incomes at the bottom only modestly, while the main driver of inequality, the labour market, keeps producing very unequal incomes. Grants are small compared with wages. The child support grant is a few hundred rand a month, while a skilled professional may earn many tens of thousands of rand. Grants lift households out of the deepest poverty and reduce hunger, and research shows they lower measured inequality noticeably, but they cannot close a gap of that size. The bigger issue is unemployment. Over thirty percent of the labour force is unemployed in the official count, and many more have given up looking. A household with no one earning a wage sits near the bottom, however good the grant system. Among people with jobs, wages are very unequal because skills are unequally distributed, and skills depend on schooling, which itself is unequal in quality. Wealth inequality, in land, property, shares and savings, is even larger and passes from one generation to the next. The budget does reduce inequality: measured after taxes, grants and free services, South Africa's Gini coefficient is considerably lower than before them. But to reduce inequality permanently, the economy needs more jobs, better education and broader ownership of assets. That is why the budget spends on education, NSFAS, land reform and small business support, and why growth is part of the answer to inequality.

### subtopic_4 — How the Budget Redresses Inequality
**Assistant:** Can a government have both fast growth and less inequality at the same time?

**Tutor:** Yes, several countries have managed it, but it requires the right choices over a long time. Some East Asian economies, such as South Korea and Taiwan, grew very fast from the 1960s to the 1990s while keeping inequality relatively low. Common features were land reform early in their development, which spread ownership of land; huge investment in good basic education for everyone, which spread skills; export industries that employed large numbers of workers; and high levels of saving and investment. In those cases, broad access to education and assets meant that when the economy grew, the gains reached most households through jobs. Other countries have grown fast while inequality widened, because the gains went mainly to people who already owned capital or had scarce skills. Others again have tried to redistribute heavily without growth and have run out of money. For South Africa, the lessons point in a familiar direction: improve the quality of education, especially early reading and mathematics; create conditions for labour-intensive industries, small businesses and the informal sector to grow and hire; fix infrastructure such as electricity, rail and ports; and keep public finances stable enough that interest does not crowd out services. The budget can support each of these, but it cannot do them alone. In exam answers about growth and redistribution, show that you understand the trade-off and the possible partnership: growth funds redistribution, and redistribution that builds skills supports growth.
