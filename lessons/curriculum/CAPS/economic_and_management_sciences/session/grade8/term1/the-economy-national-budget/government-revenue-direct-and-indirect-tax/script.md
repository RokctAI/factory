# Part 1 — Expert

This session opens the national budget by examining where government's money comes from. The anchor is a single payslip and a single till slip: a young teacher's monthly payslip, with income tax deducted before she ever sees her salary, and the till slip for her weekly groceries, with fifteen percent VAT added to some items and not to others. One is a direct tax and the other an indirect tax, and between them they raise most of the revenue the South African government spends. The governing idea is that government revenue comes mainly from taxes, that taxes are classified as direct, paid by the person or business on whom the tax is levied, or indirect, levied on goods and services and passed on to the buyer in the price, and that the design of each tax decides who carries its burden. The meaning of the budget, the sources of revenue, direct and indirect taxes with examples, and progressive, proportional and regressive taxes are examinable, and they return in Grade 10 Economics and Grade 12 Accounting.

## Subtopic: The National Budget and Government Revenue

The national budget is government's plan of how much money it expects to receive and how it intends to spend that money over a financial year. South Africa's government financial year runs from the first of April to the thirty-first of March. Every February, the Minister of Finance presents the budget speech in Parliament, setting out the estimates of revenue and expenditure, the tax changes for the coming year and the amounts each department and sphere will receive. Parliament must then debate and pass the budget as law in the Appropriation Act before money can be spent.

The budget has two sides. The revenue side is the money coming in. The expenditure side is the money going out. If revenue equals expenditure, the budget is balanced. If expenditure is greater than revenue, the budget has a deficit, and government must borrow to cover the difference. If revenue is greater, it has a surplus. South Africa has run deficits for most years since 2009, so part of every year's spending is financed by borrowing, and government pays interest on the debt it has built up.

Government revenue comes from four main sources. The largest by far is tax revenue, collected by the South African Revenue Service, SARS, which is responsible for administering the tax laws and collecting taxes and customs duties. Gross tax revenue is now close to two trillion rand a year. The second source is non-tax revenue: fees for services such as passports and licences, fines, interest and rent earned by government, and dividends from state-owned enterprises. The third is borrowing, by selling government bonds to investors in South Africa and abroad and by loans. The fourth, small for South Africa, is grants and donations from other countries or international bodies.

A tax is a compulsory payment to government for which the taxpayer receives nothing directly in return. That distinguishes it from a fee, which buys a specific service.

The questions for this section are with you now: the meaning of the national budget, the financial year, deficit and surplus, the four sources of revenue and the definition of a tax.

## Subtopic: Direct Taxes

A direct tax is a tax levied on the income or wealth of a person or business and paid directly to SARS by that person or business. The taxpayer cannot pass it on to someone else.

The most important direct tax is personal income tax, levied on the taxable income of individuals: salaries, wages, bonuses, profits of sole traders and partners, rental income and interest above an exempt amount. For employees it is collected through pay-as-you-earn, PAYE: the employer deducts it from the salary every month and pays it to SARS. Personal income tax is the largest single source of tax revenue in South Africa, raising well over a third of all tax collected.

South Africa's personal income tax is progressive. The rates rise in steps, called tax brackets, from eighteen percent on the lowest band of taxable income to forty-five percent on the highest. Each rate applies only to the part of income inside its bracket. Every taxpayer also receives a rebate, an amount subtracted from the calculated tax, which means that people earning below a threshold pay no income tax at all. For the 2024/25 and 2025/26 tax years, the primary rebate for a person under sixty-five is seventeen thousand two hundred and thirty-five rand, and the tax threshold is ninety-five thousand seven hundred and fifty rand a year.

Consider a worked example. A person earns a taxable income of two hundred thousand rand a year, which falls in the first bracket, taxed at eighteen percent. Eighteen percent of two hundred thousand rand is thirty-six thousand rand. Subtract the primary rebate of seventeen thousand two hundred and thirty-five rand, and the tax payable is eighteen thousand seven hundred and sixty-five rand for the year. As a share of income, that is about nine point four percent, well below the bracket rate, because of the rebate.

The second important direct tax is company income tax, levied on the taxable income of companies at a flat rate of twenty-seven percent. Other direct taxes include dividends tax at twenty percent on dividends paid to shareholders, capital gains tax on the profit from selling assets such as property or shares, donations tax and estate duty on the estates of people who have died.

The questions for this section are with you now: the definition of a direct tax, PAYE, tax brackets and rebates, and the worked income tax example.

## Subtopic: Indirect Taxes

An indirect tax is a tax levied on goods and services rather than on income. It is paid to SARS by the business that sells or imports the goods, but the business passes it on to the buyer by including it in the price. The buyer therefore pays the tax indirectly, often without noticing.

The most important indirect tax is value-added tax, VAT, levied at fifteen percent on the supply of most goods and services by businesses registered for VAT. It is the second-largest source of tax revenue, raising roughly a quarter of the total. Some goods are zero-rated, which means VAT is charged at zero percent: basic foods such as brown bread, maize meal, rice, dried beans, milk, eggs, fresh fruit and vegetables, and paraffin, along with exports. Some services are exempt, such as most public transport by taxi, bus and train, residential rent and some financial services.

To calculate VAT, multiply the price excluding VAT by fifteen percent. A pair of school shoes priced at four hundred rand excluding VAT carries sixty rand of VAT, so the customer pays four hundred and sixty rand. To find the VAT inside a price that already includes VAT, multiply by fifteen over one hundred and fifteen: on a till slip total of two hundred and thirty rand, the VAT is thirty rand.

Excise duties are indirect taxes on particular goods. Specific excise duties are fixed amounts per unit on alcohol and tobacco products, often called sin taxes because they also aim to reduce harmful consumption. The health promotion levy taxes the sugar in sweetened drinks. Ad valorem excise duties are a percentage of the value of luxury items such as cosmetics and electronics. The fuel levy, a fixed number of cents on every litre of petrol and diesel, and the Road Accident Fund levy on fuel, are also indirect taxes. Customs duties are taxes on imported goods, collected at the border.

The questions for this section are with you now: the definition of an indirect tax, VAT calculations, zero-rating and exemption, and examples of excise and customs duties.

## Subtopic: Progressive, Proportional and Regressive Taxes

Taxes are also classified by how the burden changes as income rises. The test is the share of income paid in tax, not the rand amount.

A progressive tax takes a larger percentage of income from people with higher incomes. Personal income tax is progressive: someone earning very little pays nothing, someone in the first bracket pays an average of a few percent, and someone earning several million rand pays an average approaching forty-five percent. Progressive taxes reduce inequality, because they take more from those most able to pay.

A proportional tax, or flat tax, takes the same percentage of income from everyone. Company income tax at twenty-seven percent of taxable income is proportional among companies.

A regressive tax takes a larger percentage of income from people with lower incomes. VAT is the standard example, even though it has a single rate. Suppose a poor household earns four thousand rand a month and spends all of it on goods carrying VAT; a rich household earns forty thousand rand a month and spends twenty thousand rand of it on such goods, saving the rest. At fifteen percent, VAT on four thousand rand of spending including VAT is about five hundred and twenty-two rand, roughly thirteen percent of the poor household's income. VAT on twenty thousand rand of spending including VAT is about two thousand six hundred and nine rand, roughly six and a half percent of the rich household's income. The rich household pays more rand but a smaller share of its income.

This is why zero-rating basic foods matters. It removes VAT from the goods that make up the largest part of poor households' spending, and so makes VAT less regressive.

A good tax system balances several principles. It should be fair, with taxes related to ability to pay; certain, so taxpayers know what they owe; convenient to pay; and economical to collect. South Africa combines a progressive income tax with a broad VAT, so that the system as a whole raises a lot of revenue while still taking a larger share from people with higher incomes.

The questions for this section are with you now: classifying taxes as progressive, proportional or regressive, and explaining why VAT is regressive even with one rate.

## Subtopic: Why Taxes Matter and Tax Compliance

Taxes pay for almost everything government does, so the amount collected limits what government can spend. Government has three choices when it wants to spend more: raise taxes, borrow more, or cut spending elsewhere. Each choice has costs. Higher taxes reduce the income households and businesses have left to spend and invest. More borrowing raises the interest bill that future taxpayers must pay. Cuts reduce services.

South Africa has a narrow tax base. Although there are tens of millions of adults, only a few million individuals pay most of the personal income tax, and a relatively small number of large companies pay most of the company tax. High unemployment means many people earn no taxable income. This makes revenue vulnerable: when the economy slows, tax collections fall at the same time as the need for grants and services rises.

Tax compliance means paying the correct tax on time. Tax avoidance is using legal methods to reduce tax, such as claiming allowable deductions for retirement contributions. Tax evasion is illegally not paying tax that is owed, for example by hiding income, not declaring sales or keeping VAT collected from customers instead of paying it to SARS. Evasion is a crime that can lead to penalties, interest and imprisonment, and it shifts the burden onto honest taxpayers.

Every taxpayer has rights too. SARS must treat taxpayers fairly, keep their information confidential and explain its decisions, and there is a Tax Ombud to whom taxpayers can complain.

The questions for this section are with you now: the three choices government faces when it wants to spend more, the narrow tax base, and the difference between tax avoidance and tax evasion.

## Subtopic: The Payslip and the Till Slip

Meet a young teacher on her first payday. Her salary is written on the payslip, but before it reaches her bank account a line called PAYE has already gone to SARS. She did not hand it over; her employer took it off and sent it. That is a direct tax: it is a tax on her own income, it has her name on it, and nobody else can pay it for her.

On Saturday she goes shopping. Her till slip shows a loaf of brown bread, maize meal and milk with no VAT, then washing powder, a cooldrink and a phone charger with VAT. Nobody at SARS sent her a bill for that VAT; the shop added it to the price, took it from her at the till, and will pay it over to SARS later. That is an indirect tax: it is a tax on things, not on her name, and it reaches SARS through the shop.

So here is the simple test. Ask: who hands the money to SARS, and whose money is it really? If you hand it over yourself, or your employer does it for you from your own income, it is direct: income tax, company tax. If a business collects it from you inside the price of a thing, it is indirect: VAT, fuel levy, sin taxes on alcohol and cigarettes, customs duty on imported goods.

Now the counting. If the phone charger costs two hundred rand before VAT, fifteen percent of two hundred is thirty rand, so she pays two hundred and thirty rand. If the till slip just says two hundred and thirty rand including VAT, the hidden VAT is two hundred and thirty times fifteen over one hundred and fifteen: thirty rand again.

Keep the payslip and the till slip in mind and try the questions for this part: direct or indirect, and how much VAT is hiding in the price.

## Subtopic: Who Feels the Tax More

Now meet two shoppers in the same supermarket queue. One earns four thousand rand a month and spends all of it. The other earns forty thousand rand a month and spends twenty thousand of it.

Both pay the same fifteen percent VAT on the same tin of polish. But at the end of the month, the first shopper has paid about five hundred rand of VAT out of four thousand rand of income: that is about thirteen rand out of every hundred she earns. The second has paid about two thousand six hundred rand out of forty thousand rand: about six and a half rand out of every hundred. The rich shopper pays more rand, but the poorer shopper feels it more. That is what economists mean by a regressive tax.

Income tax works the other way round. The more you earn, the bigger the share that goes to tax, starting at nothing for people below the threshold and climbing towards forty-five percent on the very top slice. That is a progressive tax, and it is the opposite of regressive. A flat tax, like company tax at twenty-seven percent, takes the same share from everyone: proportional.

That is why government does not charge VAT on maize meal, brown bread, milk and eggs. They are the things the first shopper buys most of, so taking VAT off them protects her.

One last thing. Paying less tax by using the rules, like saving for retirement, is avoidance and it is legal. Hiding income or keeping the VAT you collected is evasion, and it is a crime. When some people evade, everyone else pays more.

Keep the two shoppers in mind and try the questions for this part: progressive, proportional or regressive.
