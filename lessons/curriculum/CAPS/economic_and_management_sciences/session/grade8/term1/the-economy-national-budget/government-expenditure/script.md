# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines the expenditure side of the national budget: what government spends its revenue on, and why. The anchor is one rand of tax, followed out of SARS and into a classroom, a clinic, a new house, a grandmother's grant, a commuter train and a police station. The CAPS topic names six areas of spending, education, health, housing, social grants, transport and security, and each is a choice about how to raise the standard of living with limited money. The governing idea is that government expenditure is the use of public money to provide goods, services and transfers that the market would not supply fairly or at all, that every rand spent in one area is a rand not available for another, and that the budget is therefore a statement of national priorities. The six areas, the difference between current and capital expenditure, the division of revenue between spheres, and simple budget calculations are examinable, and they return in Grade 10 Economics as the public sector and in Grade 12 as fiscal policy.

## Subtopic: How Government Divides Its Spending

Government expenditure can be classified in two useful ways.

The first is by type. Current expenditure is spending on items used up within the year: salaries of teachers, nurses and police officers, medicines, textbooks, fuel, electricity and the interest on government debt. Capital expenditure is spending on assets that last for many years: building schools, clinics, hospitals, houses, roads, railways, dams and police stations, and buying major equipment. Transfer payments, the third type, are payments with nothing received in return, mainly social grants and subsidies. A healthy budget includes enough capital expenditure to build the infrastructure future growth depends on, but in South Africa current spending, especially salaries and interest, takes most of the budget.

The second is by function, meaning what the money is for. The National Treasury groups spending into functions such as learning and culture, health, social development, community development including housing, economic development including transport, and peace and security. Debt-service costs, the interest on government's borrowing, are shown separately.

Before departments can spend, the revenue raised nationally is divided between the three spheres. This is called the division of revenue, and it is set by the Division of Revenue Act passed with every budget. After allowing for debt costs and a contingency reserve, the national government keeps a share for its own functions, such as defence, police, higher education and social grants; the provinces receive an equitable share and conditional grants to run schools, hospitals and provincial roads; and local government receives its equitable share for free basic services and infrastructure grants. The provincial equitable share is divided between the nine provinces using a formula based mainly on the number of school learners and the population and its need for health care, so provinces with more people and more poverty receive more.

The questions for this section are with you now: current, capital and transfer expenditure, the division of revenue and why provinces receive different amounts.

## Subtopic: Education and Health

Education is the largest single area of government spending, under the function called learning and culture. It includes the salaries of more than four hundred thousand public school teachers paid by the provinces, no-fee schools for the majority of learners, the National School Nutrition Programme which provides a daily meal to millions of learners, school infrastructure, early childhood development, and post-school education through universities, TVET colleges and the National Student Financial Aid Scheme, NSFAS, which pays fees and allowances for students from poor and working-class households.

Spending on education is an investment in human capital, the knowledge and skills of the workforce. Educated workers are more productive, earn higher incomes, pay more tax and are less likely to be unemployed, so education spending pays for itself over time and reduces inequality between generations. The challenge in South Africa is not only how much is spent but how well: learner outcomes in reading and mathematics remain weak in many schools, so quality of teaching, textbooks and school management matter as much as the size of the budget.

Health spending, run mainly by the provinces, pays for public hospitals and clinics, doctors, nurses and community health workers, medicines and vaccines, and the world's largest HIV treatment programme, providing antiretroviral medicines to several million people free of charge. Primary health care at clinics is free for everyone. About four out of five South Africans depend on the public health system because they have no medical aid. The National Health Insurance Act of 2024 sets out a plan to move towards a single national fund for health care, to be phased in over many years.

Healthy people can work, learn and care for their families, so health spending also raises productivity and the standard of living.

The questions for this section are with you now: what education and health spending pays for, human capital, NSFAS and the role of the public health system.

## Subtopic: Housing and Social Grants

Housing spending addresses one of the most visible legacies of apartheid: millions of households living in informal settlements or overcrowded conditions. Government provides fully subsidised houses, often called RDP or BNG houses, to qualifying households with low incomes; since 1994 several million subsidised houses have been delivered. It also funds the upgrading of informal settlements with water, sanitation and roads, provides serviced sites on which households can build, supports social rental housing, and offers the First Home Finance subsidy to help households that earn too much for a free house but too little to get a full home loan. Housing is a concurrent function: national government sets policy and funds it, and provinces and metropolitan municipalities deliver it.

Social grants are the largest transfer payments in the budget, administered by SASSA. The main permanent grants are the child support grant, paid for a child in a low-income household until age eighteen; the older person's grant, from age sixty; the disability grant; the foster child grant; the care dependency grant for children with severe disabilities; and the grant-in-aid for people who need full-time care. After the 2025 budget the child support grant is five hundred and sixty rand a month per child, and the older person's grant is a little over two thousand three hundred rand a month. In addition, the Social Relief of Distress grant of three hundred and seventy rand a month, introduced in 2020 during the COVID-19 pandemic, has been extended for unemployed adults with no other income. Together, more than twenty-five million people receive a grant each month, which is about four in ten South Africans.

Grants reduce poverty and hunger directly, help children stay in school and support local economies, because recipients spend the money in local shops. Their limitation is that they are paid for by a small tax base and do not by themselves create jobs.

The questions for this section are with you now: the forms of housing support, the main grants and who receives them, and the strengths and limitations of grants.

## Subtopic: Transport and Security

Transport spending, part of economic development, pays for national roads through the national roads agency, provincial and municipal roads, the commuter rail system run through the passenger rail agency, bus rapid transit systems in some metros, subsidies for commuter bus services, and road safety. It also supports freight rail and the ports through state-owned enterprises. Good transport lowers the cost of getting workers to jobs and goods to markets, so it is one of the most important forms of capital expenditure for economic growth. In South Africa, minibus taxis carry the majority of commuters but receive little direct subsidy, and the decline of commuter rail in many areas has raised transport costs for poor households living far from work, a legacy of apartheid spatial planning.

Security, grouped under peace and security, pays for the South African Police Service, the courts and the justice system, correctional services, which runs the prisons, and the South African National Defence Force. Policing and justice protect people and property, and a business will not invest where its stock and workers are not safe, so security spending supports both the standard of living and economic growth. High levels of crime in South Africa mean that households and businesses also spend heavily on private security, which is an extra cost of doing business.

Across all six areas, the same questions apply to every rand: Is this the most important use? Is it delivering results? Could it be done at lower cost? Those questions are why Parliament's committees examine each department's spending, and why the Auditor-General audits every department and municipality every year and reports on irregular and wasteful expenditure.

The questions for this section are with you now: what transport and security spending pays for, why they matter for growth, and the role of the Auditor-General.

## Subtopic: Choices, Trade-offs and Calculations

Because revenue is limited, every spending decision has an opportunity cost: the next best use of the money that is given up. If government adds billions to the public-sector wage bill, that money is not available for new clinics or roads. If debt-service costs rise, less is left for services. In recent budgets, interest on government debt has grown faster than almost any other item, and it is now one of the largest single items, comparable to the entire health budget. That is why reducing the deficit is a priority: every rand paid in interest is a rand not spent on the six areas in this topic.

Budget questions in exams often include simple calculations. Consider a simplified illustrative example. A government spends one thousand rand on the six areas: education three hundred rand, health two hundred rand, social grants two hundred rand, housing one hundred rand, transport one hundred rand and security one hundred rand. Education's share is three hundred out of one thousand, which is thirty percent. If total spending rises by ten percent to one thousand one hundred rand, and education keeps the same share, it receives three hundred and thirty rand. If instead the extra one hundred rand all went to health, health's share would rise from twenty percent to three hundred out of one thousand one hundred, about twenty-seven point three percent.

A second type of calculation uses per-person figures. If a province receives an education budget of fifty billion rand and has two million learners, the spending per learner is fifty billion divided by two million, which is twenty-five thousand rand per learner per year.

The questions for this section are with you now: opportunity cost in the budget, debt-service costs, and share and per-person calculations.

## Subtopic: Follow One Rand

Take one rand that you paid in VAT when you bought a cooldrink. It goes to SARS, and from there into the big national pot. Now follow it.

A piece of it goes to your school. Not to the building only: most of it pays your teacher's salary, and some buys your textbooks and the lunch the nutrition programme serves at break. A piece goes to the clinic, for the nurse, the vaccines for your baby cousin and the pills an uncle collects every month. A piece goes to SASSA and lands in your gogo's hand as her grant, or in your aunt's hand as the child support grant for your little cousin. A piece goes towards a new RDP house in the next ward or to put water pipes and toilets into an informal settlement. A piece fixes a road or keeps the trains and buses running. And a piece pays the police officer who drives past at night and the court that hears a case.

So that is six stops for one rand: school, clinic, grant, house, road and police. Those are exactly the six areas CAPS asks you to know: education, health, social grants, housing, transport and security.

But there is a seventh stop that nobody likes: interest. Government has borrowed a lot of money over the years, and the lenders must be paid every year. A growing piece of every rand now goes to interest instead of to schools and clinics.

And here is the hard part of being government. The pot is only so big. Every time one stop gets more, another stop gets less. Give teachers a bigger raise, and there may be less to build clinics. That give-and-take is called opportunity cost.

Keep that rand in mind and try the questions for this part: name the six stops and the seventh.

## Subtopic: Sharing Out the Pot

Imagine government's pot is one thousand rand, to keep the numbers easy. Three hundred rand goes to education, two hundred to health, two hundred to grants, and one hundred each to housing, transport and police. Add them up: three hundred plus two hundred plus two hundred plus one hundred plus one hundred plus one hundred is one thousand. Good, nothing left over and nothing missing.

What share is education? Three hundred out of one thousand. Divide and multiply by a hundred: thirty percent. Health is twenty percent.

Now a new year. The pot grows by one hundred rand, to eleven hundred, and the Minister puts the whole hundred into health. Health now has three hundred rand out of eleven hundred: about twenty-seven percent. Education still has three hundred rand, but now that is out of a bigger pot, so its share drops to about twenty-seven percent too. The rands did not change, but the share did.

There is one more way to share: by person. If a province has fifty billion rand for schools and two million learners, each learner gets twenty-five thousand rand a year. That is what pays for the teacher, the books, the lights and the meal.

Why does this matter to you? Because the shares tell you what a government thinks is most important. When the share for education rises, the country is betting on the future skills of its learners. When the share for grants rises, it is protecting people who are struggling right now. When the share for interest rises, it means the country is paying for yesterday instead of building tomorrow. Read any budget headline in February with that question: whose share went up, and whose went down?

And who decides? The Minister presents the plan in February, Parliament debates and votes, the money is divided between national, the nine provinces and the municipalities, and at the end the Auditor-General checks whether it was spent properly.

Keep the pot in mind and try the questions for this part: shares, per-person amounts and opportunity cost.
