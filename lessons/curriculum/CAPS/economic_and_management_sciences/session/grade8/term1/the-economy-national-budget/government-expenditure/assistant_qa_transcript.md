### subtopic_1 — How Government Divides Its Spending
**Assistant:** Why does a province with more learners get a bigger share of the money?

**Tutor:** Because the services the provinces deliver, mainly schools and health care, cost more where there are more people to serve, and the Constitution requires the money to be divided equitably. Every year, the Division of Revenue Act divides the provincial equitable share among the nine provinces using a formula. The biggest part of the formula, close to half, is the education component, based on the number of school-age children and the number of learners enrolled. The next biggest is the health component, based on the population and especially the number of people without medical aid who depend on public hospitals and clinics. Smaller components cover basic services, poverty, the size of each province's economy and the cost of running the provincial government. So Gauteng and KwaZulu-Natal, with the largest populations, receive the largest equitable shares, while the Northern Cape, with the smallest population, receives the smallest, even though it is the largest province by area. The formula is reviewed by the Financial and Fiscal Commission and updated each year with new population and enrolment data from Statistics South Africa. Provinces cannot raise much revenue of their own, apart from things like vehicle licence fees and hospital fees, so they depend on this share for most of their budgets. The result is that a learner in a poor rural province receives roughly the same basic funding as a learner in a rich urban one, which is one way the budget redistributes.

### subtopic_2 — Education and Health
**Assistant:** If education is the biggest item, why are so many schools still struggling?

**Tutor:** Because the amount of money is only one ingredient in a good education, and the way the money is used matters as much. South Africa spends a larger share of its budget and of its total income on education than many countries at a similar level of development. Yet learner results in reading and mathematics remain weak in many schools. Several reasons are usually given. First, most of the education budget pays salaries, so if teachers are not well trained in how to teach reading or mathematics, extra money does not automatically improve learning. Second, there are large differences between schools: historically advantaged schools often have better buildings, more experienced teachers and parents who can pay extra fees, while many township and rural schools still have overcrowded classrooms, shortages of textbooks and, in some cases, unsafe toilets. Third, management matters: schools where principals and governing bodies run things well get better results with the same funding. Fourth, problems outside school, such as hunger, long travel distances and poverty at home, affect learning, which is one reason the nutrition programme exists. Finally, early childhood development, the years before Grade 1, shapes later learning, and government has only recently moved that function into the education department to expand it. For an exam answer, say that spending is necessary but not sufficient, and that the quality and efficiency of spending decide the outcome.

### subtopic_3 — Housing and Social Grants
**Assistant:** Why does government spend money on interest instead of on schools?

**Tutor:** Because it has to. Interest is the price of money government borrowed in the past, and if it failed to pay, no one would lend to it again and the economy would be thrown into crisis. When government runs a budget deficit, it borrows the difference by selling bonds, promising to pay interest every year and to repay the amount borrowed later. South Africa has run deficits for most years since 2009, so government debt has grown from under a third of the size of the economy to around three quarters of it. The interest payable on that debt, called debt-service costs, has risen fast, and is now one of the largest single items in the budget, comparable to the whole health budget. Debt-service costs crowd out other spending: every rand paid to lenders is a rand not available for teachers, nurses, grants or roads. That is the opportunity cost of past borrowing. To slow the growth of debt, government has tried to keep spending growth below revenue growth and aims for a primary surplus, which means revenue is larger than all spending except interest. Borrowing is not always bad: borrowing to build a dam or a railway that will serve the economy for fifty years can be sensible, because future users benefit. The problem is borrowing to pay this year's salaries, which leaves the debt behind without creating an asset.

### subtopic_4 — Transport and Security
**Assistant:** Do social grants stop people from looking for work?

**Tutor:** The evidence from South African research mostly says no, and in some cases grants help people find work. Most grants go to people who are not expected to work: children, through the child support grant; people over sixty, through the older person's grant; and people with disabilities who cannot work. Those grants cannot discourage the recipient from working. Researchers have studied what happens to other adults in households that receive a grant, especially the older person's grant, and have found that some working-age adults in those households are more likely to look for work, because the grant pays for transport to search for jobs, for a phone and data, or for someone to care for young children while they look. The Social Relief of Distress grant of three hundred and seventy rand a month is paid to unemployed adults, and studies found that many recipients used it partly to pay for job searching. The amount is far below any wage, so it does not replace the need to earn. The real challenge is the shortage of jobs, not unwillingness to work: South Africa's official unemployment rate is above thirty percent. That is why the budget also spends on public employment programmes, such as the Expanded Public Works Programme, and on education and infrastructure that help the economy create jobs. For an exam answer, give both sides: grants reduce poverty and support job searching, but they are costly, depend on a small tax base, and cannot replace economic growth.
