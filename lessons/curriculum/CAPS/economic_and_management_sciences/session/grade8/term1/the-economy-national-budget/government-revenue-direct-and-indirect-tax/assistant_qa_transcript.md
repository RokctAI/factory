### subtopic_1 — The National Budget and Government Revenue
**Assistant:** Why can government not simply print more money instead of collecting taxes?

**Tutor:** Because printing money does not create more goods and services; it only creates more rands chasing the same goods, and prices rise. Imagine the economy as a market where a fixed number of loaves, taxi seats and school shoes are for sale. If government printed billions of new rands and spent them, buyers would have more money, but the bakeries, taxis and shoe factories could not instantly produce more. Sellers would raise prices, and everyone's existing rands would buy less. That general rise in prices is called inflation. In extreme cases it becomes hyperinflation: Zimbabwe experienced it in 2007 and 2008, when prices rose so fast that the central bank printed notes of one hundred trillion Zimbabwe dollars and the currency became worthless, wiping out people's savings and wages. To prevent this, South Africa's Constitution makes the South African Reserve Bank independent, with the primary aim of protecting the value of the currency, and government does not instruct it to print money for spending. Government's real choices are therefore to raise revenue through taxes and other income, to borrow, or to spend less. Borrowing is not free either: it must be repaid with interest, and in South Africa interest on government debt has become one of the fastest-growing items of spending. For an exam answer, say that taxes transfer existing purchasing power from taxpayers to government without increasing the money supply, while printing money would cause inflation.

### subtopic_2 — Direct Taxes
**Assistant:** Why does a rebate mean someone in the eighteen percent bracket pays much less than eighteen percent?

**Tutor:** Because the rebate is subtracted from the tax after the bracket rate is applied, and it is a fixed amount that matters most to people with lower incomes. Take the worked example from the lesson: taxable income of two hundred thousand rand, all inside the first bracket. Eighteen percent gives thirty-six thousand rand. The primary rebate of seventeen thousand two hundred and thirty-five rand is subtracted, leaving eighteen thousand seven hundred and sixty-five rand. Divide that by two hundred thousand and the average tax rate is about nine point four percent, roughly half the bracket rate. Now take someone earning ninety-five thousand seven hundred and fifty rand. Eighteen percent of that is seventeen thousand two hundred and thirty-five rand, exactly equal to the rebate, so the tax is zero. That is why that figure is called the tax threshold: below it, no income tax is payable. For a very high earner, say two million rand, the rebate is still seventeen thousand two hundred and thirty-five rand, a tiny fraction of the tax, so the average rate stays close to the top bracket rate. This shows the difference between the marginal rate, the rate charged on the last rand earned, which is the bracket rate, and the average rate, total tax divided by total income. Progressive tax systems use both rising brackets and rebates to make the average rate rise smoothly with income. Older taxpayers receive extra rebates from age sixty-five and seventy-five, which raises their thresholds.

### subtopic_3 — Indirect Taxes
**Assistant:** Why are taxi fares exempt from VAT but petrol is taxed with a fuel levy?

**Tutor:** Because the two decisions protect different things. Most passengers who use minibus taxis, buses and trains are working people with modest incomes, and transport to work is a necessity, not a luxury. Making public passenger transport exempt from VAT keeps fares lower for exactly the households that would feel a regressive tax most. Exempt means the taxi operator does not charge VAT on fares; the operator also cannot claim back VAT on its own costs, so it is not quite the same as zero-rated, but the effect on fares is to keep them lower than they would otherwise be. The fuel levy, on the other hand, is a fixed number of cents on every litre of petrol and diesel. It raises a large amount of revenue that is easy to collect, because it is paid by a small number of fuel companies rather than millions of motorists. It also links the cost of using roads to the amount of driving done, and discourages some fuel use, which reduces pollution. The Road Accident Fund levy, also charged per litre, pays compensation to people injured in road accidents. The catch is that taxi operators also buy diesel and petrol, so the fuel levy does reach taxi fares indirectly. When fuel taxes rise, fares tend to follow, and this is one reason the Minister of Finance considers the fuel levy carefully every February, sometimes leaving it unchanged to protect commuters.

### subtopic_4 — Progressive, Proportional and Regressive Taxes
**Assistant:** If VAT is regressive, why does South Africa rely on it so much?

**Tutor:** Because VAT has strengths that make it very hard to replace, and because government reduces its regressive effect in other ways. First, it raises a huge amount: roughly a quarter of all tax revenue. Second, it is broad: everyone who buys goods pays it, including people who earn income in cash and never file an income tax return, so it reaches parts of the economy that income tax misses. Third, it is efficient to collect, because businesses collect it in stages and each one wants a valid tax invoice to claim back its input tax, which makes evasion harder. Fourth, it taxes spending rather than saving and investment, which economists argue is better for growth. Against that, the regressive effect is real, as the two shoppers showed. Government's response is to judge the whole system rather than one tax. Zero-rating basic foods removes VAT from a large share of poor households' spending; exempting public transport keeps fares lower; and the money VAT raises is spent heavily on grants, no-fee schools and free clinics, which benefit poorer households most. Meanwhile income tax remains strongly progressive. When the National Treasury proposed raising VAT in 2025, there was intense public debate about the effect on the poor, and the increase was withdrawn. For an exam answer, list advantages and disadvantages of VAT and mention zero-rating as the main way to reduce its regressive effect.
