# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines two costs that can come with economic development, damage to the environment and unemployment, and then the productive use of resources that allows an economy to develop while keeping its environment healthy. It covers two adjacent CAPS subtopics of the standard of living: the impact of development on the environment and unemployment, and the productive use of resources to promote a healthy environment. The anchor is a coal-mining town on the Mpumalanga Highveld, where the mines and power stations created jobs and electricity for the whole country, but also some of the worst air pollution in the world, acid water seeping from old mines, and, as mines close, workers with skills the new economy does not need. The governing idea is that development raises the standard of living only when it is sustainable, meeting today's needs without destroying the resources future generations need, and that using resources productively, so that more is produced with less waste, protects both jobs and the environment. Sustainable development, environmental impacts, the measurement, types and effects of unemployment, and practical ways to use resources productively are examinable, and they lead into Grade 12 Economics environmental sustainability and Grade 10 Geography.

## Subtopic: Development and Its Impact on the Environment

Economic development is a broader idea than economic growth. Growth is an increase in production; development is an improvement in the standard of living and well-being of the people, including better health, education, housing, services and opportunities, and a fairer distribution of the benefits. A country can grow without developing, if the gains go to a few people or if growth destroys the environment people depend on.

Production always uses natural resources, and much of it creates waste. When development is badly managed, the environment suffers in several ways.

Air pollution comes mainly from burning fossil fuels. South Africa generates most of its electricity from coal, and the coal-fired power stations concentrated on the Mpumalanga Highveld release sulphur dioxide, nitrogen oxides and fine particles that cause asthma, bronchitis and heart disease. Vehicle exhaust, industry and household burning of coal and wood add to it, especially in winter.

Water pollution comes from untreated sewage when municipal treatment works fail, from fertilisers and pesticides washing off farms, from factory waste, and from acid mine drainage, which is acidic water carrying heavy metals that seeps out of old gold and coal mines into rivers and groundwater, especially around Johannesburg and in Mpumalanga.

Land degradation includes soil erosion from overgrazing and poor farming, the loss of farmland to unplanned urban sprawl, illegal dumping, and abandoned mines that leave scarred land and dust from mine dumps.

Loss of biodiversity happens when natural habitats are cleared, wetlands drained and species over-harvested, as with poaching of rhino and abalone.

Climate change, caused by greenhouse gases such as carbon dioxide from burning coal, oil and gas, raises temperatures and makes droughts, floods and heatwaves more frequent and severe. South Africa is a significant emitter because of its coal-based energy, and is also vulnerable: Cape Town's near day-zero water crisis in 2018 and the KwaZulu-Natal floods of 2022 show the cost of extreme weather.

Environmental damage lowers the standard of living directly through illness, lost farmland and water shortages, and it is often worst in poor communities living nearest to power stations, mines and dumps.

The questions for this section are with you now: the difference between growth and development, the main types of environmental damage, and why poor communities suffer most.

## Subtopic: Unemployment: Meaning and Measurement

Unemployment is the situation in which people who are able and willing to work, and who are actively looking for work, cannot find a job. To measure it, Statistics South Africa runs the Quarterly Labour Force Survey, interviewing a sample of households every three months.

The working-age population is everyone aged fifteen to sixty-four. Within it, the labour force is the employed plus the unemployed, that is, everyone working or actively looking for work. People who are not working and not looking, such as full-time students, homemakers and retired people, are not economically active and are not in the labour force.

The unemployment rate is the number of unemployed people divided by the labour force, multiplied by one hundred. Consider an illustrative example: if the labour force is twenty-four million people and eight million of them are unemployed, the unemployment rate is eight million divided by twenty-four million, times one hundred, which is about thirty-three point three percent.

South Africa uses two definitions. The official, or narrow, definition counts only people who actively looked for work in the past four weeks. The expanded, or broad, definition also counts discouraged work-seekers, people who want to work but have given up looking because they believe there are no jobs. In recent years the official rate has been above thirty percent and the expanded rate above forty percent, among the highest in the world. Youth unemployment is even higher: among people aged fifteen to twenty-four, more than half of those in the labour force are unemployed.

Economists distinguish types of unemployment by cause. Structural unemployment occurs when the skills of workers do not match the jobs available, often because the economy has changed, as when mines and factories close or automate. Cyclical unemployment rises during a recession, when demand falls and businesses cut jobs. Frictional unemployment is the short time people spend between jobs or looking for their first job. Seasonal unemployment affects workers in industries that need them only at certain times, such as fruit picking or tourism. South Africa's unemployment is mostly structural, linked to low skills and the legacy of unequal education and spatial planning.

The questions for this section are with you now: the definitions of unemployment and the labour force, the unemployment rate calculation, narrow and expanded definitions, and the four types.

## Subtopic: The Effects of Unemployment

Unemployment is one of the most important influences on the standard of living, because for most households a job is the main source of income.

For individuals and households, unemployment means lost income, deeper poverty, dependence on grants and on relatives, debt, and often hunger. It also has non-financial costs: loss of self-esteem and skills, stress and depression, and, for young people who never find a first job, a lack of work experience that makes the next job even harder to find.

For communities, high unemployment is linked to higher crime, substance abuse, social unrest and service-delivery protests, and to less spending in local businesses, which can cause further job losses.

For the economy, unemployment is a waste of the most important resource, people's labour. Output is lower than it could be. Government collects less income tax, because fewer people earn taxable income, and less VAT, because people spend less, while it must spend more on grants. Unemployment therefore puts pressure on the budget from both sides.

Development can cause unemployment as well as cure it. New technology and automation can replace workers; the closure of coal mines and power stations as the economy shifts to cleaner energy will cost jobs in particular towns; and a growing economy may create jobs that require skills the unemployed do not have. This is why South Africa's climate policy speaks of a just transition: the move to a low-carbon economy should protect the workers and communities who depend on coal, through retraining, new industries in the same regions and social support.

Government responses to unemployment include education and skills training, including TVET colleges and learnerships; public employment programmes such as the Expanded Public Works Programme and the Presidential Youth Employment Initiative; the employment tax incentive, which lowers the cost for businesses of hiring young workers; support for small businesses and the informal sector; and reforms that remove obstacles to growth, such as fixing electricity supply.

The questions for this section are with you now: the effects of unemployment on households, communities and the economy, the just transition, and government responses.

## Subtopic: Sustainable Development and Productive Use of Resources

Sustainable development is development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs. This definition comes from the Brundtland Report of the United Nations in 1987, and it rests on three pillars: economic growth, social fairness and environmental protection, all at the same time.

South Africa's Constitution supports it. Section 24 gives everyone the right to an environment that is not harmful to their health or well-being, and the right to have the environment protected for present and future generations, through measures that prevent pollution, promote conservation and secure ecologically sustainable development while promoting justifiable economic and social development.

Productive use of resources means producing more goods and services from the same resources, or the same output with fewer resources, while wasting less. It applies to all the factors of production, but especially to natural resources such as water, energy, land and raw materials.

For water, it means fixing leaks in municipal networks, which lose a large share of treated water before it reaches consumers; using drip irrigation on farms; recycling water in industry; harvesting rainwater; and charging prices that encourage households to save. For energy, it means efficient appliances, LED lighting, solar water heaters, insulation and, on a larger scale, renewable electricity. South Africa's renewable energy programme has added wind and solar power stations, mainly in the Northern Cape, Eastern Cape and Western Cape, and households and businesses have installed rooftop solar. For materials, it means the waste hierarchy: reduce, reuse, recycle, and only then dispose. Recycling paper, cans, plastic and glass saves raw materials and energy and creates income: informal waste pickers collect a large share of the recyclable packaging in South Africa. For land, it means protecting farmland and wetlands, rehabilitating mined land, and planning cities that are dense and connected rather than spread out.

Productive use of resources also creates green jobs: in installing solar panels, building wind farms, recycling, clearing invasive alien plants that waste water through programmes such as Working for Water, and in eco-tourism. This links the two parts of this session: a healthier environment and more employment can be achieved together.

The questions for this section are with you now: the definition and three pillars of sustainable development, section 24 of the Constitution, the waste hierarchy and examples of green jobs.

## Subtopic: Roles of Government, Businesses and Households

A healthy environment is everyone's responsibility, and each participant in the economy has a different role.

Government sets and enforces the rules. The National Environmental Management Act requires an environmental impact assessment before major projects such as mines, factories, dams and roads can go ahead, so that their effects on air, water, land and communities are studied and reduced before approval. Air quality and waste laws set limits on emissions and control how waste is handled. Government also uses the budget and taxes: the carbon tax, introduced in 2019, puts a price on greenhouse gas emissions so that polluters pay part of the cost they impose on others; the plastic bag levy discourages throw-away bags; and the tyre and packaging schemes fund recycling. Municipalities must provide refuse removal, treat sewage properly and manage landfills.

Businesses must comply with these laws, but the best businesses go further, because using resources productively also lowers their costs. A factory that saves water and electricity pays smaller bills; a mine that rehabilitates its land avoids fines and keeps its licence; a retailer that reduces packaging saves money and attracts customers who care about the environment. Many large companies listed on the Johannesburg Stock Exchange now report on their environmental and social impact as well as their profits.

Households also make choices every day: how much water and electricity they use, what they buy, what they throw away and where. Illegal dumping, burning rubbish and leaving taps running all add up across millions of households. Communities can organise clean-ups, food gardens and recycling collection points, and can report polluters to the authorities.

A useful way to remember the three roles is: government sets the rules and the prices, businesses produce more with less, and households consume responsibly.

The questions for this section are with you now: environmental impact assessments, the carbon tax and plastic bag levy, and the roles of businesses and households.

## Subtopic: The Coal Town

Picture a town on the Highveld in Mpumalanga. For fifty years the coal mine and the power station were everything. Fathers and sons worked underground; the power station sent electricity to the whole country; the shops were busy on payday. That is development: jobs, income, electricity, schools built with the money.

But look up. On a winter morning the sky is grey and the air tastes of smoke. Children cough. Look down: an old mine leaks orange, acid water into the stream, and the cattle that drink there get sick. That is the hidden bill of the coal town: air pollution, water pollution and scarred land, paid by the people who live closest.

Now the mine is closing. The coal is running out, and the country is moving to cleaner energy. A man who drilled coal for twenty years is suddenly unemployed. He is able to work, willing to work and looking for work, but there is no job that fits his skills. That is structural unemployment: the economy changed, and the skills did not match.

So what should happen? Not keep burning coal forever, because the pollution and the climate damage hurt everyone. But not leave the town to die either. The answer the country has chosen is called a just transition: build solar and wind farms in the same area, retrain the workers to install and maintain them, clean up the old mine land, and support families while the change happens.

Here is how to count the unemployment. If a town has twenty-four thousand people in the labour force and eight thousand cannot find work, the rate is eight divided by twenty-four, times a hundred: about thirty-three percent. One in every three.

## Subtopic: Reduce, Reuse, Recycle and Earn

Now go to your own kitchen bin. In it: a cooldrink can, a plastic bottle, a newspaper, a glass jar and potato peels. All of it was made from natural resources, aluminium, oil, trees, sand and soil, using water and electricity. If it goes to the dump, those resources are gone.

So use the order of the waste ladder. First, reduce: buy less packaging, carry your own shopping bag. Second, reuse: fill the jar with something else, wash the bottle and use it again. Third, recycle: the can, the bottle, the paper and the glass can be melted or pulped and made into new things, using far less energy than starting from scratch. The peels go on the compost heap and become soil for the garden. Only what is left goes to the dump.

And here is the part people forget: recycling is a job. Every morning, waste pickers push huge bags through the suburbs, collecting cans and plastic to sell to buy-back centres. Small recycling businesses sort, bale and sell. People are paid to clear thirsty alien trees from riverbanks so that more water flows. Workers install solar panels on roofs and build wind farms in the Karoo. These are called green jobs.

Use water and electricity like they cost money, because they do: close the tap while brushing, report a leaking pipe, switch off lights. Every litre and every unit saved is less coal burned and less water lost.

So the healthy environment and the jobs are not enemies. Using resources productively means producing more with less, wasting less, and paying people to clean up and build clean energy. That is sustainable development: look after today without stealing from tomorrow.

Keep your kitchen bin in mind and try the questions for this part: the waste ladder, green jobs and sustainable development.
