# Part 1 — Expert

This session defines the standard of living and compares lifestyles in modern, mainly urban, societies with lifestyles in rural societies in South Africa. The anchor is two cousins of the same age: one lives in a high-rise flat in a metro, takes a bus to school, buys every meal ingredient from a supermarket and shares the internet on a fibre line; the other lives in a village in the former homeland areas, helps herd goats after school, eats maize from the family field and walks to a hilltop for a cellphone signal. Each lifestyle has costs and benefits, and comparing them shows what the standard of living actually measures. The governing idea is that the standard of living is the level of material well-being of a household or a society, determined by the income and the goods and services available to it, that it differs from the broader quality of life, and that in South Africa the gap between urban and rural standards of living is shaped by history, access to services and employment. The definitions, the indicators, the comparison of modern and rural lifestyles and the reasons for urbanisation are examinable, and they lead into Grade 12 Economics growth and development.

## Subtopic: Standard of Living and Quality of Life

The standard of living is the level of material well-being of a person, household or society, measured by the quantity and quality of goods and services they can obtain. It depends mainly on income, because income decides what a household can buy, and on access to public services such as water, electricity, sanitation, education, health care and transport.

Quality of life is a broader idea. It includes the standard of living but also things that cannot be bought: health and life expectancy, safety, freedom, clean air and water, leisure time, family and community relationships, culture and a sense of belonging. A household can have a high standard of living but a poorer quality of life if it lives with long working hours, pollution, crime or loneliness, and a household with modest income can enjoy a good quality of life in a safe, close community with clean surroundings.

Economists use several indicators to measure the standard of living. Income per person, measured nationally as GDP per capita, is the most common. Others include the share of households with access to piped water, electricity and flush toilets; the type of dwelling, such as formal house, informal dwelling or traditional dwelling; school enrolment and literacy; life expectancy and infant mortality; and the share of income spent on food, since poorer households spend a larger share of their income on food. The Human Development Index of the United Nations combines income per person, life expectancy and years of schooling into one number between zero and one.

Standards of living also depend on the cost of living. A household earning ten thousand rand a month in a city where rent, transport and food are expensive may be able to buy less than a household earning eight thousand rand a month in a town where its housing is owned and food is partly grown at home. Comparisons must therefore consider what income can actually buy.

South Africa collects much of this information through the national census, last held in 2022, and the annual General Household Survey of Statistics South Africa. These surveys show large improvements since 1994 in access to electricity, piped water and formal housing, with the great majority of households now connected to electricity. They also show persistent gaps: rural households are far less likely to have piped water inside the dwelling or a flush toilet, and households headed by women, and households in which no one is employed, are much more likely to be poor. When you read a statistic about the standard of living, always ask three questions: which households does it describe, where do they live, and is it an average that might hide large differences?

The questions for this section are with you now: the definitions of standard of living and quality of life, the indicators, and why the cost of living matters in comparisons.

## Subtopic: Modern Urban Lifestyles

A modern society, in the sense used in CAPS, is one organised mainly around a market economy, specialised employment, technology and urban living. Most South Africans now live in urban areas, and the share keeps rising.

The urban lifestyle is shaped by specialisation and money. Most people work for wages or salaries in specialised jobs: in shops, offices, factories, transport, government and services. Almost everything a household consumes is bought: food from supermarkets and spaza shops, water and electricity from the municipality, transport from taxis, buses or trains, entertainment from streaming services and shopping centres. A cash income is therefore essential; without one, an urban household has very few ways to survive.

Urban households generally have better access to services. Piped water inside the dwelling, electricity, flush toilets, refuse removal, clinics, hospitals, schools with more subject choices, libraries, banks and fast internet are more common in cities. Jobs are more varied and pay, on average, higher wages, and there are more opportunities for further study and business.

Urban life also has costs. Rent and transport are expensive, and many workers spend a large share of their income and several hours a day commuting, a legacy of apartheid planning that placed townships far from economic centres. Overcrowding, informal settlements without services, crime, traffic congestion, air and noise pollution, and the loss of extended family support networks lower the quality of life for many urban residents. Unemployment in cities is high too, and an unemployed urban household cannot fall back on growing its own food.

Technology defines the modern lifestyle: cellphones, digital banking, e-commerce, online learning and remote work have changed how people earn, spend and communicate, and have created new kinds of jobs. They also create a digital divide between those who can afford data and devices and those who cannot.

The questions for this section are with you now: the features of a modern urban lifestyle, its advantages and its disadvantages.

## Subtopic: Rural Lifestyles

A rural society is one in which people live in villages, farms and small settlements outside towns and cities, with lower population density and a closer link to the land. In South Africa, a large part of the rural population lives in the former homeland areas, where land is often held communally under traditional authorities, and on commercial farms, where farm workers and their families live.

Rural livelihoods combine several sources of income. Some households farm for their own use, called subsistence farming, growing maize, vegetables and keeping chickens, goats or cattle. Others are small-scale farmers who sell part of their produce. Farm workers earn wages on commercial farms. Many rural households depend heavily on social grants and on money sent home by family members working in cities, called remittances. Local trade, such as spaza shops, hawking, crafts and taxi services, adds income.

Access to services is generally poorer. Distances to clinics, high schools, banks and shops are greater; roads may be gravel; many households still collect water from a communal tap, borehole or river; and internet connections are weaker. Fewer jobs are available, so unemployment and poverty rates are higher in rural areas, especially in provinces such as the Eastern Cape, Limpopo and KwaZulu-Natal.

Rural life also has strengths that affect quality of life. Households often own their homes and land rights, so they pay little or no rent. Food can be grown, which protects against price increases. Community and extended family networks are strong, providing care for children and elderly people. The air is cleaner and there is less traffic and noise. Traditional culture and customs are often more closely kept.

Rural areas are changing too. Electricity and cellphone coverage now reach most villages, grant payments have brought cash into local economies, and rural towns have shopping centres. But the gap in services and jobs remains a key reason for movement to the cities.

The questions for this section are with you now: rural livelihoods and remittances, access to services, and the strengths of rural life.

## Subtopic: Comparing Lifestyles and Urbanisation

An exam question will often ask you to compare the two lifestyles under headings. Useful headings are income and employment, access to services, cost of living, housing, food, community and environment.

Under income and employment, urban areas offer more and better-paid jobs, while rural areas depend more on farming, grants and remittances. Under services, urban areas have better access to water, electricity, sanitation, health, education and internet. Under cost of living, urban households pay more for rent, transport and food, while rural households can reduce costs by owning land and growing food. Under housing, urban areas have both well-built formal housing and crowded informal settlements, while rural areas have more owner-built and traditional homes with more space. Under food, urban households buy almost everything, while rural households may produce part of their own. Under community and environment, rural areas usually have stronger family networks and cleaner surroundings, while urban areas have more pollution and crime but also more diversity and choice.

The movement of people from rural to urban areas is called urbanisation. People are pushed out of rural areas by a lack of jobs, poor services, drought and land shortages, and pulled to cities by the hope of employment, better schools and health care, and modern amenities. These are called push and pull factors. Under apartheid, influx control laws restricted black South Africans' movement to cities; after these laws were abolished in 1986, urbanisation accelerated.

Urbanisation brings opportunities and problems. Cities gain workers and consumers, but when people arrive faster than housing and services can be built, informal settlements grow, unemployment rises and services are stretched. Rural areas lose many young working-age adults, leaving behind children and older people, and lose the skills and energy needed for local development. Government responds with rural development programmes, investment in rural infrastructure and support for small-scale farmers, and with housing and informal settlement upgrading in cities.

The questions for this section are with you now: comparing lifestyles under headings, push and pull factors, and the effects of urbanisation on cities and rural areas.

## Subtopic: Two Cousins, Two Lifestyles

Two cousins, both fourteen. Palesa lives in a flat in the city. Nomvula lives in a village four hundred kilometres away.

Palesa's morning: she switches on the light, takes a hot shower, eats cereal bought at the supermarket and catches a bus to school. Her mother works in an office; her father drives a delivery truck. Everything the family eats, drinks and uses is bought with money. At night the family streams a series on the home internet.

Nomvula's morning: she fetches water from the communal tap, helps her grandmother feed the chickens and walks forty minutes to school. Her mother works on a farm during the harvest; her uncle in Johannesburg sends money home every month; her grandmother collects the older person's grant. The family grows maize and pumpkins, and the goats are the family savings account. To send a message she walks up the hill where the signal is better.

Who has the higher standard of living? Count the goods and services: Palesa's family has more income, more electricity, more choice of shops and schools, faster internet and a clinic ten minutes away. That is a higher standard of living.

But who has the better quality of life? That is harder. Palesa's family pays big rent every month, spends hours in traffic and worries about crime. Nomvula's family owns its home, grows part of its own food, has neighbours who watch out for the children and breathes clean air. Standard of living counts what you can buy; quality of life counts how good life feels, including the things money cannot buy.

Now notice that both families depend on each other. Part of the money in Nomvula's village comes from her uncle's city wages, and part of the food in Palesa's supermarket comes from farms in rural areas. Rural and urban lifestyles are two parts of one economy, linked by people, money and goods moving between them every day. When the city economy grows, the village receives more remittances; when a drought hits the farms, the city pays more for food.

Keep the two cousins in mind and try the questions for this part: standard of living, quality of life, and which cousin has more of which.

## Subtopic: The Long Taxi Ride to the City

Every January, the long-distance taxis leave the villages full of young people with bags. Most are heading to the cities to look for work. This movement from the countryside to the city is called urbanisation.

Why do they go? Some things push them out of the village: no jobs, no money, a drought that killed the crops, a clinic too far away. Other things pull them to the city: the hope of a job, better schools and colleges, shops, lights and excitement. Push and pull.

What happens when they arrive? If they find work, they send money home, and that money pays for school shoes and food in the village. Economists call that money remittances, and for many rural families it is as important as the grant. But if they arrive faster than the city can build houses and pipes, they may end up in a shack in an informal settlement, with no tap inside and a long queue at the toilet. And if no job comes, life in the city is hard, because in the city you cannot grow your own maize.

What happens to the village? It loses many of its young adults. The grandmothers and the children stay. Fields may lie unplanted because there are not enough hands.

So neither lifestyle is simply better. The city offers more jobs and services but costs more and can be harsh. The village offers community, space and food from the land, but fewer jobs and services. The government's job is to make both places better: houses, water and jobs in the city, and roads, clinics, schools and farming support in the village, so that young people can choose where to live instead of being pushed.

Keep the taxi ride in mind and try the questions for this part: push factors, pull factors and remittances.
