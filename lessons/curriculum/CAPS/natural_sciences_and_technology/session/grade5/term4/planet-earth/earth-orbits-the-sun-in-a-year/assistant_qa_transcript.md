### subtopic_1 — The Sun and Its Planets
**Assistant:** If the Sun is a star, why does it look so much bigger and brighter than the other stars?

**Tutor:** Because it is so much closer to us. The Sun is about 150 million kilometres away, which sounds very far, and it is. But the next nearest star is about a quarter of a million times further away than the Sun. Think of a car's headlights. When the car is right in front of you, the lights are so bright you cannot look at them. When the same car is far away on a long, straight road in the Karoo, its headlights look like two tiny dots. The lights have not changed; only the distance has. Stars work the same way. Many of the stars you see at night are actually bigger and brighter than our Sun, but they are so far away that they look like tiny points of light. During the day, the Sun's light is so strong that it lights up the whole sky and hides the other stars, even though they are still there. At night, when our side of the Earth faces away from the Sun, we can see them.

### subtopic_2 — Earth's Orbit Around the Sun
**Assistant:** Why do we not feel the Earth moving around the Sun if it is moving so fast?

**Tutor:** Because we are moving along with it, smoothly and steadily. Think about being in a taxi on a smooth, straight highway. If the taxi travels at a steady speed, you can close your eyes and almost forget that you are moving at a hundred kilometres an hour. You only really feel movement when the taxi speeds up, slows down suddenly or goes round a sharp bend. The Earth moves around the Sun at an almost steady speed, along a very gentle curve, so there are no sudden jolts to feel. And everything around us, the ground, the air, the oceans, the buildings and the clouds, is moving along with us at exactly the same speed, so nothing seems to rush past. That is why people long ago believed that the Earth stood still. It took careful observation of the planets and stars, and later telescopes, to show that the Earth really is moving around the Sun.

### subtopic_3 — A Year, Leap Years and Seasons
**Assistant:** How do I remember which year is a leap year?

**Tutor:** A simple trick is this: in most cases, a leap year is a year that can be divided exactly by four. For example, 2024 divided by four is 506, with nothing left over, so 2024 was a leap year. Add four, and you get the next ones: 2028, 2032, 2036 and so on. A year like 2026 is not a leap year, because it does not divide exactly by four. You can also remember that leap years often fall in the same years as the summer Olympic Games, which happen every four years. Another way to remember why we need leap years at all is to think of saving a little bit of change. Every year, the Earth's trip takes a quarter of a day longer than 365 days. It is like putting a quarter of something aside each year. After four years, you have saved up a whole one, and you spend it as an extra day, 29 February. Without leap years, the calendar would slowly drift, and after many years, December would no longer fall in summer in South Africa.
