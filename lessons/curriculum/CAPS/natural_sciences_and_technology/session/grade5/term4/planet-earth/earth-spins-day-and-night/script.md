# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers how the Earth spins on its axis, making one complete turn in about 24 hours, which we call a day, and how the spinning gives us day and night. The anchor is an early morning outing by a Grade 5 class from Durban to the beachfront, where they watch the Sun rise out of the Indian Ocean in the east. Later that morning, at school, they video-call a class in São Paulo in Brazil. The Brazilian learners are yawning in their pyjamas, because there it is still the middle of the night. How can it be morning in Durban and night in Brazil at the same moment? To find out, the class darkens the classroom, shines a torch at a globe and slowly turns it. The big idea is that the Earth spins on its axis once in about 24 hours, so that each place turns into the sunlight and out of it again, giving day and night. Explaining that the Earth spins once in about 24 hours to give day and night is examinable.

## Subtopic: Spinning on an Axis

The teacher holds up the globe. A globe is a model of the Earth. It has a rod through it from top to bottom, and it can spin around that rod.

The real Earth also spins, but it has no rod. Instead, it spins around an imaginary line called its axis. The axis runs through the middle of the Earth from the North Pole to the South Pole. The Earth spins around its axis like a top spinning on a table.

One complete spin of the Earth takes about 24 hours. We call this a day. A day includes both the daytime, when it is light, and the night-time, when it is dark. So a day is one spin of the Earth, while a year, as we learnt in the last lesson, is one orbit around the Sun. In one year, the Earth spins about 365 times.

The Earth spins from west to east. Places in the east turn towards the Sun first. That is why the Sun rises in Durban up to about an hour earlier than it rises in Cape Town, which is further west.

The Earth spins quite fast. At the equator, the middle of the Earth, the ground moves at about 1,670 kilometres an hour. But we do not feel it, because the ground, the air, the oceans and everything else spin with us, smoothly and steadily.

The questions for this section are with you now: the Earth's axis, one spin in about 24 hours, and the direction of spin.

## Subtopic: Day and Night

The teacher switches off the lights and shines a torch at the globe. The torch is the Sun. One half of the globe is lit up, and the other half is dark.

The same is true of the real Earth. The Sun can light up only the half of the Earth that faces it. On that half, it is daytime. The other half faces away from the Sun, into dark space, so it is night-time there.

A learner sticks a small paper star on South Africa and another on Brazil. The teacher turns the globe slowly from west to east. South Africa turns into the light first: sunrise. It moves across the lit side: daytime. Then it turns into the dark: sunset, and then night. At the moment when South Africa is in the morning light, Brazil, further west, is still on the dark side, in the middle of the night. São Paulo is about five hours behind South Africa.

So day and night are caused by the Earth spinning. The Sun does not switch off at night, and it does not go anywhere. It is always shining. It is our part of the Earth that turns away from it. At this very moment, while it is day for us, it is night for people on the other side of the Earth.

That is also why countries have different times. When it is noon in Johannesburg, people in Brazil are having breakfast, and people in Japan are having supper.

The questions for this section are with you now: why we have day and night, and why different places have different times.

## Subtopic: Sunrise, Sunset and Shadows

From the Durban beach, the Sun seemed to rise up out of the sea in the east, travel across the sky during the day, and set in the west in the evening. It looks as if the Sun is moving around the Earth. But the Sun is not moving across our sky. We are. As the Earth spins from west to east, the Sun only appears to move from east to west, the same way trees seem to rush backwards when you look out of the window of a moving taxi.

We can track the Sun's apparent movement with shadows. A shadow forms on the side of an object away from the Sun. The class pushes a stick into the ground and marks the end of its shadow every hour. In the early morning, the shadow is long and points west. At midday, when the Sun is highest, the shadow is shortest. In the afternoon, the shadow grows long again and points east. Long ago, people used shadow sticks, called sundials, as clocks.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking the Sun moves across the sky; the Earth is spinning. Two: thinking night comes because the Sun switches off; our side of the Earth turns away from it. Three: mixing up a day and a year; a day is one spin, and a year is one orbit. Four: thinking it is the same time all over the world; it is day on one half and night on the other.

The questions for this section are with you now: sunrise and sunset, and how shadows change during the day.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the Durban sunrise — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: The Spinning Earth

The Earth spins around an imaginary line called its axis.

One spin takes about 24 hours. That is one day.

Keep the spinning globe in mind and try the questions for this part: the Earth's spin.

## Subtopic: Light Side, Dark Side

The half of the Earth facing the Sun has day.

The half facing away has night.

As the Earth spins, places move from day into night.

Hold the torch and the globe in mind and try this part's questions: day and night.

## Subtopic: Shadows Tell the Time

The Sun seems to rise in the east and set in the west.

Shadows are long in the morning and evening, and short at midday.

Pocket summary of the lesson. The Earth spins on its axis, an imaginary line from the North Pole to the South Pole. One spin takes about 24 hours, one day. The half of the Earth that faces the Sun has day, and the half that faces away has night. The Earth spins from west to east, so the Sun seems to rise in the east and set in the west. Shadows change length and direction during the day.
