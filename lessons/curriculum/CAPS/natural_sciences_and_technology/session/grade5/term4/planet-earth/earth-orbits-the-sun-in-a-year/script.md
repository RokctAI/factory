# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers how the Earth moves around the Sun in a path called an orbit, and how one complete trip takes about 365 days, which we call a year. The anchor is a visit by a Grade 5 class to Sutherland in the Northern Cape, a small town famous for its dark skies and for the Southern African Large Telescope, one of the largest telescopes in the world. During the day, an astronomer uses a special safe telescope to show the class the Sun. At night, the class lies on blankets outside and looks up at thousands of stars, and the astronomer points out the planets Venus and Jupiter, shining like bright stars. Then she uses a ball for the Sun and a marble for the Earth to show how the Earth travels around the Sun. The big idea is that the Earth moves around the Sun in an orbit, and one orbit takes about 365 days, one year. Explaining that the Earth orbits the Sun in about 365 days is examinable.

## Subtopic: The Sun and Its Planets

The astronomer starts with the Sun. The Sun is a star. It is a huge, extremely hot ball of glowing gas that gives out its own heat and light. It looks small in the sky only because it is very far away, about 150 million kilometres from the Earth. The Sun is so big that more than a million Earths could fit inside it. Light from the Sun is so fast that it reaches us in about eight minutes.

All the other stars that the class sees at night are suns too, but they are much, much further away, so they look like tiny points of light.

Eight planets travel around the Sun. In order from the Sun, they are Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune. The Earth is the third planet from the Sun. Together, the Sun, the planets and their moons make up the solar system. The word solar means of the Sun.

Planets do not make their own light. We can see Venus and Jupiter at night because sunlight bounces off them, the same way sunlight bounces off the Moon. That is why Venus, which is close to us, can look like the brightest star in the evening sky, even though it is not a star at all.

Never look straight at the Sun, not even with sunglasses, and never through binoculars or a telescope. The astronomer's telescope has a special filter that makes it safe. Looking at the Sun without one can badly damage your eyes in seconds.

The questions for this section are with you now: the Sun as a star, the planets and the solar system.

## Subtopic: Earth's Orbit Around the Sun

Next, the astronomer puts a big yellow ball on a table to be the Sun, and walks a blue marble, the Earth, in a big loop around it. She says that the path the Earth follows around the Sun is called its orbit. The Earth's orbit is almost a circle, but not quite; it is very slightly stretched, like a slightly flattened circle.

The Earth never stops moving along its orbit. It travels at about 30 kilometres every second, much faster than any aeroplane, yet we do not feel it, because everything around us, the air, the oceans and the ground, is moving along with us.

What keeps the Earth in its orbit? The Sun pulls on the Earth with a force called gravity. Without that pull, the Earth would fly off in a straight line into space. With it, the Earth keeps curving around the Sun, again and again, as it has for billions of years.

Long ago, many people believed that the Sun moved around the Earth, because every day the Sun seems to travel across our sky. Today we know that it is the Earth that moves around the Sun. The Sun only seems to move across the sky because the Earth is spinning, which we will learn about in the next lesson.

The Moon also has an orbit. It travels around the Earth about once a month, while the Earth carries it along on its journey around the Sun.

The questions for this section are with you now: what an orbit is, and how the Earth moves around the Sun.

## Subtopic: A Year, Leap Years and Seasons

One complete trip of the Earth around the Sun is called a year. It takes about 365 days. That is why our calendar has 365 days, from 1 January to 31 December.

But the trip actually takes about 365 and a quarter days. Each year, that extra quarter of a day is left over. After four years, the four quarters add up to one whole extra day. So every fourth year, we add one day to the calendar, 29 February. That year has 366 days and is called a leap year. The year 2024 was a leap year, and the next one is 2028. Learners born on 29 February have a real birthday only once every four years.

As the Earth travels around the Sun, we have seasons. The Earth is tilted, a little like a spinning top that leans to one side. In December, the southern half of the Earth, where South Africa is, leans towards the Sun, so we get long, hot days: summer. In June, the southern half leans away from the Sun, so we get short, cold days: winter. That is why Sutherland can get snow in winter, and why Christmas in South Africa falls in summer.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking the Sun moves around the Earth; the Earth moves around the Sun. Two: thinking the Sun is a planet; it is a star. Three: thinking every year has exactly 365 days; a leap year has 366. Four: thinking summer happens because the Earth is closer to the Sun; it is because of the Earth's tilt.

The questions for this section are with you now: a year, leap years and why we have seasons.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the Sutherland star night — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Our Star and Our Planet

The Sun is a star. It is huge, hot and gives light.

Earth is the third of eight planets that go around the Sun.

Never look straight at the Sun.

Keep the big yellow ball in mind and try the questions for this part: the Sun and the planets.

## Subtopic: Round and Round the Sun

The Earth moves around the Sun. Its path is called an orbit.

The Sun's gravity keeps the Earth in its orbit.

Hold the marble going around the ball in mind and try this part's questions: the orbit.

## Subtopic: One Trip, One Year

One trip around the Sun takes about 365 days. That is one year.

Every four years, a leap year has 366 days.

Pocket summary of the lesson. The Sun is a star, and the Earth is the third of eight planets in the solar system. The Earth moves around the Sun in a path called an orbit, held by the Sun's gravity. One orbit takes about 365 and a quarter days, which is one year. The extra quarters add up to an extra day, 29 February, every four years, in a leap year. The tilt of the Earth as it orbits gives us seasons.

Hold the starry Sutherland sky in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: a year and a leap year.
