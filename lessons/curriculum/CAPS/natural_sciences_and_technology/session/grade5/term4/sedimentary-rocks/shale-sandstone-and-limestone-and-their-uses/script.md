# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers three common sedimentary rocks, sandstone, shale and limestone: how to recognise them by their layers, grains and other properties, and how we use them. The anchor is a rock museum set up by a Grade 5 class in Lichtenburg in North West. Lichtenburg has a large limestone quarry and a cement factory just outside the town. A parent who works at the factory brings in lumps of pale grey limestone and a bag of cement. Another learner's uncle, who lays paving, brings a slab of golden-brown sandstone from the Free State. The teacher adds a piece of dark grey shale from the Karoo. Each rock gets a table and a label, and the learners act as museum guides, explaining what each rock is made of and what it is used for. The big idea is that sandstone, shale and limestone are sedimentary rocks with visible layers, made from different sediments, and that people use them for building and for making cement. Naming and describing these rocks and their uses is examinable.

## Subtopic: Sandstone and Shale

The first table holds the sandstone. Sandstone is made of grains of sand that were laid down in layers and glued together by natural mineral cement. Up close, you can see the separate grains, and it feels rough and gritty, like sandpaper. If you rub two pieces together, a little sand rubs off. It is often cream, yellow, brown or red, coloured by minerals such as iron. Thin stripes show its layers. The golden cliffs at Golden Gate and the high cliffs of Table Mountain are both made of sandstone.

The second table holds the shale. Shale forms from mud, a mixture of very fine silt and clay, that settled in calm water, such as at the bottom of a lake or a quiet sea. The grains are so tiny that you cannot see them, so shale feels smooth. It is usually grey or black, sometimes red or green. Its layers are very thin, and shale splits easily along them into flat, thin sheets, a bit like the pages of a book. It is quite soft and breaks easily. Huge areas of the Karoo are made of shale, and it often contains fossils, because mud gently buries plants and animals.

The guides add a simple test. They drip water onto each rock and watch. The water soaks slowly into the sandstone, because there are tiny spaces between its sand grains. It mostly runs off the shale, because its tiny grains are packed so tightly. A rock that lets water soak in is called porous. Sandstone is porous, which is why some deep sandstone layers hold underground water that people pump up from boreholes.

The questions for this section are with you now: the properties of sandstone and shale.

## Subtopic: Limestone

The third table holds the limestone. Most limestone formed in warm, shallow seas. Many sea creatures, such as shellfish and corals, build shells and skeletons from a mineral taken from the seawater. When they died, their shells and skeletons piled up on the sea floor, were broken into tiny pieces, and over millions of years were squashed and hardened into limestone. So limestone is often a rock made of shells. Some pieces even contain whole fossil shells.

Limestone is usually white, cream or grey. Its grains are often very fine, so it can feel smooth, and it is softer than many rocks, so a steel nail can scratch it.

Limestone has a special test. The guides drip a few drops of vinegar onto each rock. On the limestone, the vinegar fizzes, making tiny bubbles of a gas called carbon dioxide. Sandstone and shale usually do not fizz. Vinegar is a weak acid, and the acid reacts with the mineral in limestone.

Rainwater is also very slightly acidic, so over many thousands of years, it slowly dissolves limestone underground, making caves. The Cango Caves near Oudtshoorn are limestone caves.

The questions for this section are with you now: how limestone forms and how to test for it.

## Subtopic: Using Sedimentary Rocks

The last part of the museum is about uses.

Limestone is used to make cement. At the Lichtenburg factory, limestone from the quarry is crushed and mixed with clay, then heated in a giant kiln to about 1,450 degrees Celsius, far hotter than any oven. The hot lumps are cooled and ground into a fine grey powder: cement. Mixed with sand, stone and water, cement makes concrete for buildings, bridges and roads. Crushed limestone, called agricultural lime, is also spread on farmland to improve acid soils.

Sandstone is used in buildings. It is fairly easy to cut into blocks, yet strong and long-lasting. The Union Buildings in Pretoria are built of sandstone, and so are many old churches and houses in the eastern Free State. Sandstone is also used for paving, garden walls and steps.

Shale is used in buildings too. It is dug from quarries, crushed, mixed with water, shaped and fired in kilns to make bricks and roof tiles. Flat slabs of shale are also used for paving and walls.

At the end of the day, the guides walk visitors around the school and point out the rocks in the buildings: a sandstone step at the office, a wall of red shale bricks, and the concrete floor of the hall, made with cement from limestone. Rocks that formed millions of years ago, on sea floors and in deserts, now hold up the school.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking shale is made of sand; it is made of mud. Two: thinking all rocks fizz in vinegar; limestone does. Three: thinking cement comes straight out of the ground; it is made by heating limestone and clay. Four: thinking sedimentary rocks have no layers; they usually show clear layers.

The questions for this section are with you now: how we use limestone, sandstone and shale.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the Lichtenburg rock museum — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Sand Rock and Mud Rock

Sandstone is made of sand grains. It feels gritty.

Shale is made of mud. It is smooth and splits into thin sheets.

Keep the sandstone slab in mind and try the questions for this part: sandstone and shale.

## Subtopic: Shell Rock That Fizzes

Limestone is made mostly of old sea shells.

Vinegar makes it fizz.

Hold the fizzing limestone in mind and try this part's questions: limestone.

## Subtopic: Rocks We Build With

Limestone makes cement.

Sandstone is cut into building blocks.

Shale is fired into bricks and tiles.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Sandstone, shale and limestone are sedimentary rocks with layers. Sandstone is made of sand grains and feels gritty. Shale is made of mud, feels smooth and splits into thin sheets. Limestone is made mostly from the shells and skeletons of sea creatures, and it fizzes with vinegar. Limestone is heated with clay to make cement, sandstone is used as building stone, and shale is used to make bricks and tiles and for paving.

Hold the rock museum in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: uses of sedimentary rocks.
