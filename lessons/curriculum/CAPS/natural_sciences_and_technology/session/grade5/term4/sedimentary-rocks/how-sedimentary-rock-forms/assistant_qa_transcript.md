### subtopic_1 — Breaking Down and Carrying Away
**Assistant:** Why does the heaviest sediment settle first?

**Tutor:** Moving water and wind can carry sediment only while they have enough strength. A fast-flowing river after a storm is strong enough to roll big pebbles along its bottom and carry sand and mud in the water. When the river slows down, for example when it flows out into a wide dam, a lake or the sea, it loses its strength. The heaviest pieces, the pebbles and coarse sand, are the hardest to carry, so they are dropped first, close to where the river slowed down. Lighter sand is carried a little further before it drops. The tiniest, lightest pieces, the silt and clay, can stay floating in the water for a long time, so they are carried furthest, out into the calm, deep water, before they slowly settle. You can see this in the jar test: when you shake soil and water and leave it to stand, the stones sink in seconds, the sand settles within a minute, but the clay can keep the water cloudy for a day or more.

### subtopic_2 — Settling in Layers
**Assistant:** How do we know that the Golden Gate sandstone was once a desert?

**Tutor:** Scientists who study rocks, called geologists, look for clues in the rock, a bit like detectives. Sand grains blown by the wind rub against each other in the air, so they become very round and smooth, with a frosted surface, like frosted glass. Grains carried by water are usually less rounded. Under a magnifying glass, the grains in the Golden Gate sandstone are well rounded, like wind-blown sand. Another clue is the shape of the layers. Sand dunes have sloping sides, and as the wind piles sand onto a dune, it makes thin layers that slope at an angle. Geologists can see these sloping layers inside the sandstone, just like the layers inside a modern dune in the Kalahari. Fossils also help. Rocks in this area hold the remains and footprints of early dinosaurs and other animals that lived in a dry land. Putting the clues together, geologists concluded that the rock formed from desert dunes about 200 million years ago.

### subtopic_3 — Squashed and Glued into Rock
**Assistant:** If sedimentary rock takes millions of years to form, is any forming today?

**Tutor:** Yes. Sedimentary rock is forming right now, all over the world, but it is far too slow for us to see it happen. Every day, rivers like the Orange River and the Thukela carry millions of tonnes of sediment to the sea, and it settles on the sea floor in layers. Mud and sand settle in dams and lakes. Shells and the tiny skeletons of sea creatures pile up on the ocean floor. In thousands and millions of years, as more layers press down on them and minerals glue the grains together, these layers will become new sedimentary rock: sand will become sandstone, mud will become shale, and shells will become limestone. So the beach sand you walk on today could one day, far in the future, become part of a sandstone cliff, perhaps with a fossil of a shell inside it.
