# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers how sedimentary rock forms, in five steps: rocks break down into small pieces, the pieces are carried away by wind and water, they settle in layers, the layers are squashed together, and they harden into rock. The anchor is a trip by a Grade 5 class from Bethlehem in the Free State to the Golden Gate Highlands National Park, near Clarens. The park is famous for its tall sandstone cliffs, which glow gold, orange and red in the late afternoon sun. From the road, the learners can see clear stripes across the cliffs, some pale, some darker, stacked like the pages of a closed book. The park ranger tells them that this rock was once loose sand, blown across a huge, dry desert about 200 million years ago, at the time of the early dinosaurs. The big idea is that sedimentary rock forms when small pieces of broken rock settle in layers and are slowly squashed and hardened into new rock. Explaining how sedimentary rock forms is examinable.

## Subtopic: Breaking Down and Carrying Away

The ranger begins with the first two steps.

Step one is breakdown. As we learnt in the soil lessons, rocks on the Earth's surface are slowly broken down by weathering: heating and cooling, ice in cracks, plant roots, and moving water and wind. The broken pieces can be big pebbles, smaller grains of sand, tiny specks of silt and clay, or even broken shells. All these loose pieces are called sediment.

Step two is transport. Sediment does not stay where it was made. It is carried away. Rivers and streams carry it downhill, rolling pebbles along the bottom and carrying sand and mud in the water. That is why rivers often look brown after rain. Wind blows sand and dust across dry land and piles it up into dunes. Waves move sand along beaches. In very cold places, slow rivers of ice called glaciers carry rocks too.

As sediment is carried, the pieces bump and rub against each other, so they become smaller, smoother and rounder. That is why the pebbles in a river bed are round, not sharp.

The sand that became the Golden Gate cliffs was mostly carried by wind, across a desert of dunes, a bit like the Kalahari or the Namib today.

The ranger points down at the stream running through the park. Even today, it carries tiny grains of sand worn off the cliffs, the first steps of the same story starting again.

The questions for this section are with you now: breakdown and transport of sediment.

## Subtopic: Settling in Layers

Step three is deposition. Sediment is deposited, or put down, when the wind or water carrying it slows down and loses its strength.

When a river slows down as it flows into a lake, a dam or the sea, it drops its sediment. The heaviest pieces, pebbles and coarse sand, drop first. The lightest pieces, fine silt and clay, are carried further and settle last, in the calmest water. When wind dies down, it drops its sand.

Sediment settles in flat layers, one on top of another. Each flood, each season or each change in what the river carries adds a new layer. A layer of sand might be covered by a layer of mud, then a layer of shells, then more sand. Over thousands and millions of years, the layers pile up, many metres thick.

The bottom layer was laid down first, so in undisturbed layers, the bottom layer is the oldest and the top layer is the youngest, just like a pile of washing where the first thing you put down is at the bottom.

In class, the learners make a model. They pour sand, soil and small stones into a jar of water, one at a time, waiting each time for it to settle. They see a clear layer form each time, stacked one on top of the other.

The questions for this section are with you now: deposition and how layers build up.

## Subtopic: Squashed and Glued into Rock

Step four is compaction. As more and more layers pile up, they become very heavy. The weight of the layers above presses down on the layers below, squeezing the grains closer together and pushing out much of the water.

Step five is hardening, also called cementation. Water seeping through the layers carries dissolved minerals, like salt dissolved in water. These minerals come out of the water and form a natural cement between the grains, gluing them together. Slowly, the loose sediment hardens into solid sedimentary rock.

Different sediments make different sedimentary rocks. Sand grains become sandstone. Mud, made of silt and clay, becomes shale. Shells and the skeletons of sea creatures become limestone. We will look at these three rocks in the next lesson.

You can usually recognise a sedimentary rock by its clues. It often has visible layers. You can often see or feel the grains it is made of, and some sedimentary rocks crumble when rubbed. And it may contain fossils, the remains of plants and animals that were buried in the sediment.

This takes millions of years. Much later, movements of the Earth's crust can lift the rock up, and weathering and erosion wear away the land around it, so that the layers are uncovered, as at Golden Gate.

The learners make a sedimentary sandwich: slices of white bread, brown bread and bread spread with jam, stacked and pressed under a pile of heavy books. After a while, the slices are flatter and stuck together, like rock layers.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking sedimentary rock forms quickly; it takes millions of years. Two: thinking the top layer is the oldest; the bottom layer is usually the oldest. Three: thinking sediment is only sand; it includes pebbles, silt, clay and shells. Four: thinking sedimentary rock forms from melted rock; it forms from layers of sediment.

The questions for this section are with you now: compaction, hardening and the time it takes.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the Golden Gate cliffs — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Bits of Rock on the Move

Rock breaks into small pieces called sediment.

Wind and water carry the sediment away.

Keep the brown river in mind and try the questions for this part: breakdown and transport.

## Subtopic: Layer on Layer

When the wind or water slows down, the sediment settles.

It settles in flat layers. The bottom layer is the oldest.

Hold the striped jar in mind and try this part's questions: layers.

## Subtopic: Pressed into Rock

The heavy layers press down and squash the grains.

Minerals glue them into rock, over millions of years.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Sedimentary rock forms in five steps. Breakdown: weathering breaks rock into sediment. Transport: wind and water carry the sediment away. Deposition: it settles in layers when the wind or water slows down. Compaction: the weight of the layers above squeezes the grains together. Hardening: minerals glue the grains into solid rock. It takes millions of years, and the bottom layers are the oldest.

Hold the golden cliffs in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: how sediment becomes rock.
