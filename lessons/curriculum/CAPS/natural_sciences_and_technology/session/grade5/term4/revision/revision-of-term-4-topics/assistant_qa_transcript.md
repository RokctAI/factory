### subtopic_1 — Planet Earth
**Assistant:** How do I stop mixing up a day and a year?

**Tutor:** Link each word to a different movement of the Earth. A day goes with spinning: think of a learner spinning round once on the spot. One spin of the Earth on its axis takes about 24 hours, and that is one day, with a daytime and a night-time. A year goes with travelling: think of a learner walking all the way around the school field. One trip of the Earth around the Sun, its orbit, takes about 365 days, and that is one year. Both movements happen at the same time. You can show this by walking slowly in a big circle around a chair, which stands for the Sun, while turning round and round as you walk. Each turn is a day, and the whole circle is a year. If you counted the turns carefully, you would need about 365 of them in one circle. So remember: spin for a day, orbit for a year.

### subtopic_2 — The Surface of the Earth
**Assistant:** How are soil, sedimentary rocks and fossils connected?

**Tutor:** They are all part of one long story that keeps repeating. It starts with weathering. Rock on the Earth's surface is broken down by heat, cold, ice, roots, water and wind into small pieces. Some of these pieces stay where they are and mix with humus to become soil. Others are carried away by wind and water as sediment, for example when rain washes soil off a bare hillside into a river. The river carries the sediment to a dam, a lake or the sea, where it settles in layers. Sometimes, a dead plant or animal is buried in those layers. Over millions of years, the layers are squashed and hardened into sedimentary rock, and the buried hard parts may turn into fossils. Much later, the rock is lifted up and weathered again, and some of its grains become new soil, starting the story once more. So the soil in your garden, the sandstone in a building and the fossil in a museum are all connected by the slow work of weathering, erosion and time.

### subtopic_3 — Sedimentary Rocks and Fossils
**Assistant:** What is the best way to revise all the names in the fossil lessons?

**Tutor:** Group them, so that each name has a home. Make three boxes. The first box is the fish: the coelacanth, known from fossils, caught alive near East London in 1938, noticed by Marjorie Courtenay-Latimer and identified by Professor Smith, and called a living fossil. The second box is the dinosaurs: Massospondylus, the long-necked plant-eater with nests at Golden Gate, and Heterodontosaurus, the small dinosaur with different kinds of teeth. The third box is the Cradle of Humankind in Gauteng: Mrs Ples, the skull found at Sterkfontein in 1947; Little Foot, a very complete skeleton; and Homo naledi from the Rising Star Cave. Then add the two kinds of fossils: body fossils, which are parts of the body, like teeth, bones, shells, leaves and seeds, and trace fossils, which are signs left behind, like footprints, nests, eggs, burrows and droppings. Say each box out loud, draw a quick picture for each, and test yourself with a friend by asking which box each name belongs in.
