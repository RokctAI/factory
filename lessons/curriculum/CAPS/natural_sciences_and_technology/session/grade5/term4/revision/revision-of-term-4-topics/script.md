# Part 1 — Expert

This session revises the Term 4 topics of Planet Earth and Beyond: planet Earth; the surface of the Earth; sedimentary rocks; and fossils. The anchor is a revision lesson in a Grade 5 classroom in Kimberley in the Northern Cape, where the class builds an Earth Story wall. The space corner has a yellow paper Sun, a globe on a stand and a torch. The soil corner has three jars of soil, a soil profile drawn on cardboard, and a photograph of a donga. The rock corner has pieces of sandstone, shale and limestone, a bottle of vinegar and a bag of cement. The fossil corner has a fossil shell, a plaster cast of a dinosaur footprint, and pictures of a coelacanth and of Mrs Ples. Learners take turns as guides, explaining each corner to visitors from other classes, who ask them hard questions. The big idea is that the Earth moves in space, and that its surface of rock and soil holds the story of the Earth and of life. The whole of Term 4 is examinable.

## Subtopic: Planet Earth

The first visitor asks: why do we have years and days?

The guide at the space corner explains. The Sun is a star, a huge ball of hot glowing gas, and the Earth is the third of eight planets that travel around it. The Earth moves around the Sun in a path called an orbit, held by the pull of the Sun's gravity. One orbit takes about 365 days, which is one year. In fact, it takes about 365 and a quarter days, so every four years the extra quarters add up to one day, 29 February, and that year, a leap year, has 366 days.

At the same time, the Earth spins on its axis, an imaginary line through the North and South Poles. One spin takes about 24 hours, which is one day. The guide shines the torch at the globe. The half facing the Sun has day, and the half facing away has night. As the Earth spins from west to east, each place turns into the light and out of it again. That is why the Sun seems to rise in the east and set in the west, even though it is the Earth that is moving. Shadows are long in the morning and evening and shortest at midday.

The guide adds that the tilt of the Earth as it orbits gives us seasons, with summer in South Africa in December.

The questions for this section are with you now: the Earth's orbit and spin, years, leap years, and day and night.

## Subtopic: The Surface of the Earth

The second visitor asks: where does soil come from?

The guide at the soil corner explains that the Earth's crust, its thin, rocky outer layer, is made of rocks and soil. Soil, air, water and sunlight together support life.

Soil comes from rock. Weathering, by heating and cooling, freezing water in cracks, plant roots, and moving water and wind, breaks rock into smaller and smaller grains. A soil profile shows topsoil at the top, dark with humus and full of roots and living things; subsoil below it, paler and stony; and rock at the bottom. Topsoil forms when rock grains mix with humus from dead plants and animals. It takes hundreds of years to form just a few centimetres.

Soil contains rock grains, air, water, dead remains and living things. There are three main types. Sandy soil has big grains, feels gritty and lets water drain through fast. Clayey soil has tiny grains, feels sticky and holds water, cracking when dry. Loamy soil is a mixture of sand, silt and clay with humus, and it is best for plants.

Because soil forms so slowly, it must be conserved. Water and wind erode bare soil, forming dongas. Plants protect soil, so we keep it covered, avoid overgrazing, build stone walls to slow water, plough across slopes and use mulch and compost.

The questions for this section are with you now: rocks and soil, soil types and conserving soil.

## Subtopic: Sedimentary Rocks and Fossils

The last visitors ask: how can a rock tell a story?

The guides at the rock and fossil corners explain. Sedimentary rock forms in five steps: breakdown of rock into sediment; transport by wind and water; deposition in flat layers; compaction by the weight of the layers above; and hardening, as minerals glue the grains together. It takes millions of years, and in undisturbed layers the bottom layer is the oldest. Sandstone is made of sand and is used as building stone. Shale is made of mud, splits into thin sheets and is used for bricks, tiles and paving. Limestone is made mostly of shells, fizzes in vinegar, and is heated with clay to make cement.

Fossils are the remains or traces of ancient plants and animals, preserved in sedimentary rock. They form when a living thing is buried quickly, and minerals slowly turn its hard parts to stone. Body fossils include teeth, bones, shells, leaves and seeds. Trace fossils include footprints, nests, eggs, burrows and droppings. South Africa's rich fossil record includes the coelacanth, caught alive near East London in 1938; dinosaurs like Massospondylus; and the Cradle of Humankind, with Mrs Ples, Little Foot and Homo naledi.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking the Sun moves around the Earth; the Earth orbits the Sun and spins on its axis. Two: thinking a day is one orbit; a day is one spin, and a year is one orbit. Three: thinking sandy soil holds water best; clayey soil does. Four: thinking a footprint cannot be a fossil; it is a trace fossil.

At the end of the day, one visitor asks the hardest question of all: how do all four corners fit together? The guides point to the wall: the spinning, orbiting Earth gives us sunlight, days and seasons; rain and sun weather rock into soil and sediment; sediment becomes rock in layers; and the layers hold fossils, the story of life.

The questions for this section are with you now: sedimentary rocks, fossils and South Africa's fossil record.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the Earth Story wall — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Spin and Orbit

One orbit around the Sun: one year.

One spin on the axis: one day.

Keep the torch and the globe in mind and try the questions for this part: planet Earth.

## Subtopic: Rock to Soil

Weathering turns rock into soil, very slowly.

Sandy, clayey and loamy soils are different.

Keep the soil covered to conserve it.

Hold the three jars of soil in mind and try this part's questions: the surface of the Earth.

## Subtopic: Layers and Fossils

Sediment settles in layers and hardens into rock.

Fossils are kept in those layers.

Pocket summary of the term. The Earth orbits the Sun in about 365 days, one year, and spins on its axis in about 24 hours, one day, giving day and night. The Earth's crust is rock and soil; soil forms slowly from weathered rock and must be conserved. Sandy, clayey and loamy soils have different properties. Sedimentary rock forms from layers of sediment that are squashed and hardened; sandstone, shale and limestone are used for building and cement. Fossils are preserved in sedimentary rock, and South Africa has a rich fossil record.

Hold the whole Earth Story wall in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: sedimentary rocks and fossils.
