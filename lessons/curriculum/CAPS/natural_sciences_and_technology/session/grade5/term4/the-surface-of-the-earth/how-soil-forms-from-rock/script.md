# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers where soil comes from: rocks slowly break down into smaller and smaller pieces, and over a very long time these grains, mixed with the remains of living things, become soil. It also covers the layers we see in the ground: topsoil, subsoil and rock. The anchor is a field trip by a Grade 5 class from Harrismith in the Free State to a road cutting near the town, where builders cut through a hillside to make a road. The cut is like a giant slice through a cake. At the top, there is a dark brown layer full of grass roots. Below that is a paler, orange-brown layer with small stones. At the bottom is solid grey rock, with cracks running through it, and broken pieces of rock lying at the foot of the cut. The big idea is that soil comes from rock, and that topsoil forms very slowly as rock breaks into grains and mixes with the remains of plants and animals. Explaining how soil forms from rock, and the layers of rock, subsoil and topsoil, is examinable.

## Subtopic: Rocks Break Down

The teacher picks up a piece of broken rock from the foot of the cut. Rock seems strong and permanent, but over many years it cracks and crumbles. The breaking down of rock into smaller pieces is called weathering. Several things weather rock.

Heating and cooling. In the Free State, rocks are baked by the hot sun during the day and cooled at night. Rock expands a tiny bit when it is heated and shrinks when it cools. After thousands of days and nights, the outside of the rock cracks, and thin flakes peel off.

Freezing water. In winter, rain water seeps into cracks in the rock and freezes at night. Water expands when it turns to ice, so it pushes the crack wider, like a wedge. When the ice melts, more water gets in, and it happens again. In the Drakensberg, not far from Harrismith, this splits big boulders apart.

Plant roots. Seeds land in cracks, and as the plant grows, its roots push into the crack and widen it. You can see this where a tree root has cracked a pavement.

Moving water and wind. Rivers roll stones against each other, knocking off tiny grains and making the stones round and smooth. Wind blows sand against rocks, slowly scraping them, like sandpaper.

Rain water can also slowly dissolve or soften some kinds of rock. And animals help too: burrowing animals, like moles, meerkats and ants, dig through broken rock and soil, mixing them and moving grains to the surface.

In class, the learners test this. They shake rough stones with water in a closed jar for five minutes, and the water turns cloudy with tiny grains that have been knocked off.

The questions for this section are with you now: weathering and the things that break rock down.

## Subtopic: Layers: Topsoil, Subsoil and Rock

Back at the road cutting, the class draws the layers. A drawing of the layers of soil from the surface down to the rock is called a soil profile.

Topsoil is the top layer. It is usually dark brown or black, loose and crumbly. It is full of roots and living things, such as earthworms, ants and beetles. Its dark colour comes from humus, the rotted remains of dead plants and animals. Topsoil is the richest layer for growing plants, and most roots grow here. It is often only a few centimetres to a few tens of centimetres thick.

Subsoil is the layer under the topsoil. It is usually paler, often yellow, orange or red-brown, because it has little humus. It has bigger pieces of broken rock and fewer living things. Only the deep roots of trees and big plants reach it.

At the bottom is rock, the solid layer that the soil came from. It is sometimes called bedrock or parent rock. Near its top, it is cracked and broken by weathering.

In class, the learners compare samples they collected from each layer in labelled bags. They rub each one between their fingers and look at it with a magnifying glass. The topsoil is dark, soft and crumbly, with bits of root and a tiny beetle. The subsoil is lighter in colour, harder, and has more small stones. The piece of rock is hard, heavy and does not crumble at all.

The layers show the story of the soil: rock at the bottom, breaking up into subsoil, which becomes topsoil at the top.

The questions for this section are with you now: the soil profile, and the layers of topsoil, subsoil and rock.

## Subtopic: From Grains to Topsoil

How do broken pieces of rock become rich topsoil? First, weathering breaks rock into small pieces, then into tinier and tinier grains: gravel, sand, silt and, smallest of all, clay. Next, tough little plants, like lichens and mosses, grow on the grains. When they die, their remains rot. Earthworms, insects, fungi and bacteria, which are living things too small to see, break down dead leaves, roots and animals into humus. The humus mixes with the rock grains. Bigger plants can now grow, and when they die, they add more humus. Slowly, a layer of dark, rich topsoil builds up.

This takes a very long time. It can take hundreds of years, sometimes more than a thousand, to form just a few centimetres of topsoil. That is why soil is so precious.

Once topsoil has formed, it is more than just grains and humus. Between the grains are tiny spaces filled with air and water, which roots and soil animals need. A single handful of good topsoil can contain millions of living things, most of them far too small to see.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking soil has always been there; it formed from rock. Two: thinking rocks never change; weathering breaks them down. Three: thinking topsoil and subsoil are the same; topsoil is darker and has more humus and living things. Four: thinking soil forms in a few years; it takes hundreds of years.

The questions for this section are with you now: how rock grains and humus form topsoil, and how long it takes.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the Harrismith road cutting — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Cracking Rock

Heat, cold, ice, roots, water and wind break rock.

## Subtopic: Three Layers

Topsoil on top: dark, with roots and humus.

Subsoil in the middle: paler, with stones.

Rock at the bottom.

Hold the road cutting in mind and try this part's questions: the soil layers.

## Subtopic: Slow, Slow Soil

Rock grains mix with humus from dead plants and animals.

This makes topsoil, very slowly.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Soil comes from rock. Weathering by heating and cooling, freezing water, plant roots, moving water and wind breaks rock into smaller and smaller grains. A soil profile shows topsoil at the top, dark with humus and full of life, subsoil in the middle, paler with stones, and rock at the bottom. Topsoil forms when rock grains mix with humus from dead plants and animals. It takes hundreds of years to form a few centimetres.
