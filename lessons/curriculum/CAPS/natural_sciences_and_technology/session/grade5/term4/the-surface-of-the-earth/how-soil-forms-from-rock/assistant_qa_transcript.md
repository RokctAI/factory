### subtopic_1 — Rocks Break Down
**Assistant:** Why does water freezing in a crack break the rock?

**Tutor:** Most things shrink a little when they get colder and turn solid, but water is unusual. When water freezes into ice, it expands, taking up about a tenth more space. You may have seen this if someone put a full bottle of water in the freezer: the bottle bulges or even cracks. Now picture a crack in a rock high in the Drakensberg. During the day, rain or melting snow trickles into the crack and fills it. At night, the temperature drops below freezing, and the water turns to ice. The ice needs more space, so it pushes hard against the sides of the crack, forcing it a little wider. During the day, the ice melts, and the water seeps a little deeper into the wider crack. At night, it freezes again. After many winters of this, the crack becomes so wide that a piece of rock splits right off. That is why you often find piles of broken, sharp-edged rocks at the bottom of mountain cliffs.

### subtopic_2 — Layers: Topsoil, Subsoil and Rock
**Assistant:** Is the soil in my garden the same as the rock underneath it?

**Tutor:** Partly. The tiny grains in your garden soil usually came from the rock underneath, or from rock that was carried there by rivers or wind long ago. So if the rock below is red, the soil is often red too, and if the rock is sandy, the soil is often sandy. But soil is not just crushed rock. It also contains humus, which comes from dead plants and animals, and that gives topsoil its dark colour and much of its goodness for plants. It contains air in the small spaces between the grains, water that clings to the grains, and countless living things, from earthworms to bacteria. So you can think of soil as a mixture: broken rock, plus humus, plus air, plus water, plus life. The rock gives the soil its grains and many of its minerals, and living things turn it into something that plants can grow in.

### subtopic_3 — From Grains to Topsoil
**Assistant:** If soil takes so long to form, why is there so much of it?

**Tutor:** Because the Earth is very, very old, and soil has been forming in many places for a very long time. The Earth is about four and a half billion years old. Even if it takes hundreds of years to form a few centimetres, over tens of thousands of years, many metres of soil can build up in places where it is not washed or blown away. In flat valleys and plains, like parts of the Free State, soil has been collecting for a very long time, which is why the area is good for farming maize and wheat. But soil is not spread evenly. On steep slopes and mountain tops, it is thin or missing, because rain and wind carry it away. And people can lose soil much faster than it forms, when they clear plants, overgraze land or plough badly. In a single heavy storm, a bare field can lose soil that took hundreds of years to form. That is why the next lessons are about different types of soil, and about how to conserve it.
