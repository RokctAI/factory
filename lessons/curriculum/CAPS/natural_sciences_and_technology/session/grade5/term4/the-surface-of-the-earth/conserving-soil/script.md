# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers why soil must be conserved: it forms very slowly, but it can be washed or blown away quickly, especially when people damage the land, and there are many ways to protect it. The anchor is a visit by a Grade 5 class from Matatiele in the Eastern Cape, near the Drakensberg, to a hillside above a village. The grassy slope is cut by dongas, deep ditches with steep, bare sides, some taller than a grown-up. At the bottom, a stream is brown with mud, and further down, the small dam is filling up with silt. A local community team shows the class how they are fixing the damage: planting grass, building low walls of stones, and moving their cattle so that the grass can grow back. The big idea is that soil forms very slowly and must be conserved, by keeping it covered and protected from water and wind. Explaining why and how soil should be conserved is examinable.

## Subtopic: Soil on the Move

The team leader reminds the class that it can take hundreds of years to form just a few centimetres of topsoil. But that same topsoil can be lost in a single storm.

The carrying away of soil by water or wind is called soil erosion. It is different from weathering. Weathering breaks rock into small pieces. Erosion carries the pieces away.

Water erosion happens when heavy rain falls on bare ground. The raindrops splash soil loose, and the water running down the slope carries it away. At first, the water makes small grooves. If nothing stops it, the grooves join up and grow into gullies, and then into dongas, which keep getting deeper and wider with every storm. The soil ends up in streams, making them brown, and then in dams, where it settles as silt and makes the dams shallower, so they hold less water.

Wind erosion happens when strong wind blows across dry, bare soil, picking up the fine grains and carrying them away as dust. In dry years, huge dust storms have swept across farms in the Free State and North West, carrying away the precious topsoil.

The soil that is lost first is the topsoil, the richest layer. What is left behind is the poorer subsoil, where crops and grass grow badly.

An old man from the village tells the class that when he was a boy, this slope was covered in thick grass, and there was only a small ditch where the dongas are now. In his lifetime, the ditch has grown into a donga deep enough to hide a cow.

The questions for this section are with you now: soil erosion by water and wind, and how dongas form.

## Subtopic: What Causes Soil Erosion

Next, the class asks why this hillside was damaged. The team leader explains that plants protect soil. Leaves break the force of the raindrops, and roots hold the soil together like a net. Soil that is covered with plants hardly erodes at all. Bare soil erodes quickly. So most soil erosion happens when people remove the plant cover.

Overgrazing. When too many cattle, sheep or goats feed in one place for too long, they eat the grass right down to the ground, and their hooves trample the bare soil. That is what happened on this hillside.

Clearing land. Cutting down trees and bushes for firewood or building leaves the soil bare.

Poor ploughing. When farmers plough straight up and down a slope, the furrows become channels for rainwater to rush down, carrying soil with it.

Paths and roads. Feet, cattle and car tyres wear paths into slopes, and rain then rushes down them.

Fires. Veld fires that burn too often or too hot leave bare ground until the grass regrows.

Back at school, the class tests this with two trays of soil, propped up at the same slope. One tray has bare soil, and the other has a piece of grass growing in the soil. They pour the same amount of water, from the same watering can, onto each. The water that runs off the bare tray is brown and thick with soil. The water from the grassy tray is almost clear, because the plants held the soil in place.

Soil can also be damaged by pollution, such as litter, spilt oil and poisonous chemicals, which harm the living things in the soil.

The questions for this section are with you now: how plants protect soil, and what people do that causes erosion.

## Subtopic: Ways to Conserve Soil

Then the team shows how they are healing the land. To conserve soil means to protect it and use it wisely, so that it is not lost or damaged.

Keep the soil covered. They plant grass on the bare patches and along the edges of the dongas, so that roots hold the soil.

Slow the water down. They build low walls of stones across the slope and across the dongas. The walls slow the water, so it drops the soil it carries and soaks into the ground. Wire baskets packed with stones, called gabions, are placed in the worst dongas.

Graze carefully. The community moves its cattle from one area to another, giving the grass time to grow back.

Plough across the slope. On farms, ploughing along the slope, not up and down it, makes each furrow a little dam that holds rain. This is called contour ploughing. On steep hills, farmers cut flat steps called terraces.

Plant trees as windbreaks, and cover garden soil with mulch, a layer of dry grass or leaves, and add compost.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking soil grows back quickly; topsoil takes hundreds of years to form. Two: thinking erosion and weathering are the same; weathering breaks rock, erosion carries soil away. Three: thinking more cattle on the land is always better; overgrazing leaves the soil bare. Four: ploughing up and down a slope; plough across it.

The questions for this section are with you now: ways to conserve soil.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the Matatiele dongas — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Soil on the Move

Water and wind can carry soil away. This is called erosion.

Dongas are deep ditches made by water.

Keep the brown, muddy stream in mind and try the questions for this part: soil erosion.

## Subtopic: Bare Soil Washes Away

Plant roots hold soil. Leaves shield it from rain.

Overgrazing, cutting trees and poor ploughing leave soil bare.

Hold the bare hillside in mind and try this part's questions: causes of erosion.

## Subtopic: Save Our Soil

Plant grass and trees. Build stone walls. Plough across slopes.

Move the cattle. Cover garden soil with mulch.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Soil forms very slowly but can be lost quickly. Soil erosion is the carrying away of soil by water and wind, and it can form deep dongas and fill dams with silt. Plants protect soil, so erosion happens mostly when people remove plant cover by overgrazing, clearing land, poor ploughing or making paths. We conserve soil by keeping it covered with plants, slowing water with stone walls, grazing carefully, contour ploughing, planting windbreaks and using mulch and compost.

Hold the healing hillside in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: conserving soil.
