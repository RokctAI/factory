### subtopic_1 — The Earth's Crust
**Assistant:** How do scientists know what is inside the Earth if nobody has been there?

**Tutor:** They use clues, a bit like a doctor who listens to your chest without opening you up. The most important clues come from earthquakes. When an earthquake happens, it sends vibrations, called waves, through the whole Earth. Instruments all around the world, including in South Africa, record when these waves arrive. The waves travel at different speeds through different materials, and some kinds of waves cannot pass through liquid at all. By comparing the recordings from many places, scientists can work out where the layers are, how thick they are, and which parts are solid and which are not. Other clues come from volcanoes, which bring up hot, melted rock from deep below, and from meteorites, rocks from space that are made of the same materials as the inside of the Earth. Putting all these clues together, scientists have built up a picture of the crust, the mantle and the core, even though no drill or mine has ever gone deeper than the crust.

### subtopic_2 — Rocks and Soil on the Surface
**Assistant:** Are there places on Earth with no soil?

**Tutor:** Yes, there are. On steep mountain tops, like parts of the Magaliesberg and the Drakensberg, the wind and rain wash away any loose material, so there is bare rock. In very dry deserts, like parts of the Namib Desert along the west coast of southern Africa, there is mostly sand and rock, with very little real soil, because there are few plants to add dead remains. Under thick ice, like in Antarctica, there is rock but almost no soil. And of course, under the deep oceans, the crust is covered with mud and sand, not with garden soil. Where there is no soil, very few plants can grow, and so very few animals can live. That shows how important soil is. It also shows why we must look after it, because, as we will learn, soil forms extremely slowly and can be washed or blown away much faster than it forms.

### subtopic_3 — What Life Needs: Soil, Air, Water and Sunlight
**Assistant:** If plants make their own food, why do they need soil at all?

**Tutor:** Plants make the main part of their food, a kind of sugar, from water and carbon dioxide, using the energy of sunlight. But that is not everything they need. First, they need water, and the soil holds water for their roots to take up. Second, they need small amounts of minerals, such as nitrogen and potassium, to build healthy leaves, stems, flowers and fruit. These minerals come from the broken-down rock and the rotting remains in the soil. A plant without them grows weak and yellow, the same way a person who eats only pap and nothing else would not be as healthy as one who has a mix of foods. Third, the soil gives plants something to hold on to. The roots anchor the plant so that it does not fall over in the wind or wash away in the rain. That is why farmers and gardeners add compost and fertiliser to soil: they are adding minerals, so that the plants have everything they need.
