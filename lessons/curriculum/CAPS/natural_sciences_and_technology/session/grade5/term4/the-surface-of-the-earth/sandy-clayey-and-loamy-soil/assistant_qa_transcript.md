### subtopic_1 — What Is in Soil?
**Assistant:** Why does water run through sandy soil faster than through clayey soil?

**Tutor:** It is all about the size of the spaces between the grains. Imagine a jar full of marbles and a jar full of flour. Between the marbles there are big gaps, so if you pour water into the jar, it rushes straight down to the bottom. Between the specks of flour, the gaps are tiny, so the water creeps through very slowly, and a lot of it gets stuck, clinging to the flour. Sandy soil is like the marbles: its grains are big, so the spaces between them are big, and water drains through quickly. Clayey soil is like the flour: its grains are very tiny and pack closely together, so the spaces are tiny, and water moves through very slowly and stays in the soil. That is why a sandy garden needs watering often, and why a clayey field turns into a muddy puddle after heavy rain. Loamy soil has a mixture of big and small grains, with humus that acts like a sponge, so it is in between: it drains well but still holds enough water for plants.

### subtopic_2 — Sandy, Clayey and Loamy Soil
**Assistant:** How can I find out what kind of soil is in my garden at home?

**Tutor:** Try the feel test. Take a small handful of soil, add a few drops of water so that it is damp but not runny, and work it between your fingers. If it feels gritty and rough and falls apart when you try to make a ball, it is sandy soil. If it feels smooth and sticky, and you can roll it into a long, thin sausage and even bend it into a ring without it breaking, it is clayey soil. If it forms a ball that holds its shape but crumbles when you press it, and it feels soft and a little gritty, it is probably loamy soil. Look at its colour too: dark brown or black soil usually has lots of humus. You can also do the settling test at home with a clear jar: half fill it with soil, add water, shake it hard and leave it for a day. If most of the jar is a thick layer of sand at the bottom, the soil is sandy. Remember to wash your hands after handling soil.

### subtopic_3 — Testing Soils: Water and Settling
**Assistant:** Can you change a sandy or clayey soil into a good soil?

**Tutor:** Yes, gardeners and farmers do this all the time, and the main secret is humus. Compost is made by letting kitchen scraps, like vegetable peels and eggshells, and garden waste, like grass cuttings and dead leaves, rot in a heap. It turns into dark, crumbly material that is full of humus. When you mix compost into sandy soil, the humus acts like a sponge between the big grains, holding water and minerals so they do not drain away so fast. When you mix compost into clayey soil, the humus opens up the tight clay, making spaces for air and water to move, so it drains better and does not set so hard. Earthworms help too, because they pull compost down into the soil and dig tunnels that let air in. It takes time, often a few seasons, but adding compost every year slowly turns poor soil into rich, loamy soil. Many school food gardens in South Africa keep a compost heap for exactly this reason.
