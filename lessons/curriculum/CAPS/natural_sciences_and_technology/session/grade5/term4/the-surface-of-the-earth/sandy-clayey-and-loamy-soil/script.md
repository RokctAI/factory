# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers what soil is made of, the three main types of soil, sandy, clayey and loamy, and how to test them. The anchor is a school food garden project in Giyani in Limpopo. The Grade 5 class wants to grow spinach, tomatoes and beans to help feed learners at school. But first they must choose the best soil. They collect three samples in buckets. The first is pale, gritty sand from the bank of a dry riverbed. The second is reddish, sticky clay from near the edge of the dam. The third is dark, crumbly soil from a granny's vegetable patch, where the mealies grow tall. Over two weeks, they look at the soils, feel them and test them. The big idea is that soil is a mixture of rock grains, air, water, dead remains and living things, and that the size of its grains makes it sandy, clayey or loamy, each with different properties. Describing and comparing sandy, clayey and loamy soils, and what soil contains, is examinable.

## Subtopic: What Is in Soil?

The class starts by finding out what soil contains.

Rock grains. Soil is mostly made of grains of weathered rock. The biggest are sand grains, which you can see and feel. Smaller are silt grains, like fine flour. The smallest are clay grains, far too small to see one at a time.

Air. The learners drop a dry lump of soil into a glass of water. Tiny bubbles rise out of it. The bubbles are air that was trapped in the spaces between the grains. Roots and soil animals need this air.

Water. They put some damp soil in a sealed plastic bag in the sun. After a while, drops of water form on the inside of the bag. The water came out of the soil.

Dead remains. With a magnifying glass, they spot bits of dead leaves, twigs and roots. These rot to form humus, which makes soil dark and gives plants minerals.

Living things. They find an earthworm, ants, a millipede and white threads of fungus. There are also billions of bacteria, too small to see.

So soil is not dead dirt. It is a mixture of rock grains, air, water, dead remains and living things.

The questions for this section are with you now: what soil contains, and how we can show it.

## Subtopic: Sandy, Clayey and Loamy Soil

Next, the class compares the three soils by looking at them and rubbing them between their fingers.

Sandy soil has big grains. It feels gritty and rough. The grains do not stick together, so when it is damp, it falls apart if you try to roll it into a ball. There are big spaces between the grains, so water runs through quickly, and the soil dries out fast. Sandy soil holds very little water and few minerals, so many plants struggle in it. It warms up quickly and is easy to dig.

Clayey soil has very tiny grains. When it is wet, it feels smooth and sticky, and you can roll it into a long sausage or bend it into a ring, like play dough. The spaces between the grains are tiny, so water drains through very slowly. Clayey soil holds a lot of water and can become waterlogged, with no air for roots. When it dries, it shrinks, cracks and goes rock hard. Potters use clay to make pots.

Loamy soil is a mixture of sand, silt and clay, with plenty of humus. It is dark and crumbly. When it is damp, it holds together in a ball but crumbles when pressed. It holds enough water for plants, but extra water drains away, so there is still air for roots. It is rich in minerals. Loamy soil is the best soil for growing most plants.

All three soils are found in South Africa. Sandy soils cover much of the Kalahari in the Northern Cape and the coast near the sea. Clayey soils are common in some valleys and near rivers and dams, where fine grains settle out of slow water. Loamy soils are found in many good farming areas, and gardeners make more of it by adding compost.

## Subtopic: Testing Soils: Water and Settling

The class does two tests.

The first test is a drainage test. They place three funnels, each lined with cotton wool, over three measuring jugs. They put the same amount of each soil in a funnel and pour the same amount of water, 100 millilitres, onto each. To make it a fair test, they change only the type of soil. After five minutes, they measure how much water has drained through. The water rushes through the sandy soil, so the most water collects under it. The least drains through the clayey soil, which holds most of the water. The loamy soil is in between.

The second test is a settling test. They put soil in a jar, add water, close the lid, shake it hard and leave it to stand. The heaviest pieces sink first. After a day, they see layers: small stones and gravel at the bottom, then sand, then silt, then a thin layer of clay on top, while bits of humus float on the water. The thicker the sand layer, the sandier the soil. The sandy soil from the riverbed settles quickly into a thick layer of sand with clear water above it. The clayey soil leaves the water cloudy for days, because the tiny clay grains sink very slowly.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking soil is just dead dirt; it is full of air, water and living things. Two: thinking sandy soil holds water well; water runs straight through it. Three: thinking clayey soil is best for gardens; it gets waterlogged and goes hard. Four: thinking soil has no air in it; bubbles rise when soil is put in water.

At the end of the project, the class plants the vegetables in the loamy soil, mixing in compost to keep it rich.

The questions for this section are with you now: the drainage test and the settling test.
