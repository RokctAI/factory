### subtopic_1 — Soil on the Move
**Assistant:** Why does rain wash away bare soil but not soil covered with grass?

**Tutor:** Picture two patches of ground in a heavy storm, one bare and one covered with thick grass. On the bare patch, each big raindrop hits the soil hard, like a tiny bomb, and splashes grains of soil into the air. The water cannot soak in fast enough, so it collects and runs down the slope, carrying the loose grains with it. The faster it runs, the more soil it carries. On the grassy patch, the raindrops land on the leaves first, which soften their fall, so the soil is not splashed loose. The water drips gently down the stems and soaks into the ground, helped by the tunnels made by roots and earthworms. Any water that does run off has to wind its way between thousands of grass stems, which slows it down. And underneath, the roots grip the soil like a net, so even running water cannot easily pull it away. That is why the first rule of soil conservation is simple: keep the soil covered.

### subtopic_2 — What Causes Soil Erosion
**Assistant:** What can learners do to help conserve soil at school or at home?

**Tutor:** Quite a lot. At school, you can help plant grass or trees on bare patches, especially on slopes where rain runs off. Stick to paths instead of making new shortcuts across the grass, because feet wear away the plants and make channels for the rain. If your school has a food garden, cover the soil between the plants with mulch, such as dry grass or leaves, to protect it from the sun, wind and rain and to keep it moist. Start a compost heap with vegetable peels and garden waste, and mix the compost into the soil to keep it rich. Do not throw litter, and never pour oil, paint or chemicals onto the ground. At home, you can do the same things in the yard. If you see a small gully starting to form after rain, you and an adult can place a line of stones across it to slow the water. Small actions by many people add up to a big difference.

### subtopic_3 — Ways to Conserve Soil
**Assistant:** Can a donga ever be fixed?

**Tutor:** Yes, but it takes patience and many years of work. A donga keeps growing because water rushes into it from the bare slopes above and digs it deeper with every storm. So the first step is to stop the water rushing in, by getting grass to grow again on the slopes above, and by not letting animals overgraze them. The next step is to slow the water inside the donga. People build low walls of stones, or put in gabions, which are wire baskets packed with stones, across the donga. When the water hits these walls, it slows down and drops the soil it is carrying, which slowly builds up behind the walls, filling the donga a little at a time. Then grass and other plants can take root in the new soil, holding it in place. Some communities in the Eastern Cape and KwaZulu-Natal have healed dongas this way. It is much easier and cheaper to stop a donga from forming in the first place than to fix it later, which is why protecting plant cover is so important.
