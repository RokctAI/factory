### subtopic_1 — What Is a Fossil?
**Assistant:** How do scientists know how old a fossil is?

**Tutor:** There are two main ways. The first is the order of the rock layers. Sedimentary rock forms in layers, with the oldest at the bottom and the youngest at the top, as long as the layers have not been turned over. So a fossil in a deep layer is older than a fossil in a layer above it. This tells scientists which fossil is older, but not exactly how old. The second way gives actual numbers of years. Some rocks, especially layers of volcanic ash that sometimes lie between sedimentary layers, contain tiny amounts of special substances that slowly change into other substances at a steady, known rate, like a very slow clock. By measuring how much has changed, scientists can work out how long ago the rock formed. If a fossil lies between two ash layers, one dated at 256 million years and one at 254 million years, the fossil must be somewhere in between. Using both methods together, palaeontologists have worked out the ages of fossils from all over the world, including the Karoo.

### subtopic_2 — How a Fossil Forms
**Assistant:** Why don't the soft parts of animals become fossils?

**Tutor:** Because they usually rot away long before they can be preserved. Skin, muscles, organs and eyes are soft and moist, and they are a feast for bacteria, fungi, insects and scavengers. Within days or weeks after an animal dies, they are eaten or decay. Bones, teeth and shells are much harder and tougher, and they can last for many years, long enough to be buried and slowly turned to stone by minerals. The same is true for plants: hard wood, seeds and tough leaves are more likely to become fossils than soft petals. However, very rarely, soft parts are preserved too, when an animal is buried extremely fast in very fine mud with little oxygen, so that rotting slows down. Scientists have found fossil feathers, skin impressions and even the outlines of jellyfish this way. These rare fossils are extremely valuable, because they show us things that bones alone cannot, such as what an animal's skin looked like.

### subtopic_3 — Fossils Tell the Story of Life
**Assistant:** Can I go and dig for fossils myself?

**Tutor:** In South Africa, fossils are part of our national heritage, which means they belong to everyone, and they are protected by law. You are not allowed to dig up, remove or sell fossils without a special permit. That is because each fossil is a precious clue to the history of life, and when it is dug out carelessly, scientists lose important information, such as which rock layer it came from and what was lying next to it. But you can still be a fossil hunter. Visit a museum or a fossil centre, like the ones in Nieu-Bethesda, Johannesburg, Cape Town or the West Coast Fossil Park, where you can see real fossils and sometimes watch scientists at work. When you are in the Karoo or near cliffs and quarries, look closely at the rocks. If you think you have found a fossil, do not remove it. Take a photo with something next to it to show its size, note exactly where it is, and report it to a museum. Many important fossils have been found this way by farmers, hikers and children.
