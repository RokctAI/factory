# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers what fossils are, how they form in sedimentary rock, and why they are a record of the history of life on Earth. The anchor is a visit by a Grade 5 class from Graaff-Reinet to the village of Nieu-Bethesda in the Karoo, in the Eastern Cape. Near the village is a fossil centre, and behind it, a dry riverbed with layers of grey and greenish rock. In the centre, the learners see the stone skull of an animal that looks a bit like a reptile and a bit like a mammal, with long fangs. A palaeontologist, a scientist who studies fossils, tells them that this animal lived right here more than 250 million years ago, before the first dinosaurs, when the Karoo was a wide, wet land of rivers and swamps. The big idea is that fossils are the remains or traces of ancient plants and animals preserved in sedimentary rock, and that they are a record of the history of life. Explaining what fossils are and how they form is examinable.

## Subtopic: What Is a Fossil?

The palaeontologist explains that a fossil is the remains, or a trace, of a plant or animal that lived a very long time ago, preserved in rock. The word fossil comes from an old Latin word meaning dug up.

Fossils can be the hard parts of a living thing, such as bones, teeth, shells, wood, leaves and seeds, that have slowly turned to stone. They can also be signs that a living thing left behind, such as a footprint, a burrow or a nest. We will look at these two kinds in the next lesson.

Most fossils are found in sedimentary rocks, such as sandstone, shale and limestone. That is because fossils form when dead plants and animals are buried in sediment, like mud and sand, which later becomes rock. Fossils are almost never found in rocks that formed from hot, melted rock, because the heat would destroy them.

Fossils are very old. Most are thousands or millions of years old. A bone from an animal that died last year is not a fossil; it is just a bone.

The Karoo is one of the best places in the world to find fossils, because its rocks are layers of sediment that were laid down by ancient rivers and lakes, burying many animals and plants.

In the dry riverbed behind the centre, the palaeontologist shows the class a real fossil still lying in the rock: a row of ancient bones, turned to stone. It is left exactly where it was found, so that visitors can see how fossils look in nature.

The questions for this section are with you now: what fossils are, and where they are found.

## Subtopic: How a Fossil Forms

The palaeontologist tells the story of the fossil skull in steps.

First, the animal died near a river, about 255 million years ago.

Second, it was buried quickly. A flood covered the body with mud and sand before scavengers could eat it and before it could rot away completely. This is very important. Most dead plants and animals are eaten, or rot, or are scattered, and leave nothing behind. Only a tiny few are buried fast enough to become fossils. That is why fossils are rare and precious.

Third, the soft parts rotted away. Skin, muscles and organs rot quickly, but hard parts, such as bones and teeth, last much longer.

Fourth, the layers piled up. Over millions of years, more layers of sediment covered the bones. The weight pressed down, and the sediment slowly hardened into rock.

Fifth, the bones turned to stone. Water seeping through the rock carried dissolved minerals. Very slowly, the minerals filled the tiny spaces in the bones and replaced the bone material, until the bones became stone in the same shape. Sometimes, the bone or shell dissolved away completely, leaving a hollow space in its shape, called a mould. If the mould filled with minerals, it made a stone copy, called a cast.

Sixth, the fossil was uncovered. Millions of years later, weathering and erosion wore away the rock above, and the skull was exposed, where a fossil hunter spotted it.

## Subtopic: Fossils Tell the Story of Life

Fossils are a record of the history of life on Earth. They are like the pages of a very old book, and the rock layers are the pages, with the oldest at the bottom.

By studying fossils, scientists have learnt that many kinds of plants and animals that once lived on Earth no longer exist. They have become extinct, like the dinosaurs and the animal whose skull the class saw. Fossils show what these living things looked like, what they ate and where they lived.

Fossils in deeper, older layers are different from fossils in higher, younger layers. This shows that life on Earth has changed over a very long time. The oldest rocks with fossils hold only tiny, simple living things. Fish, then plants on land, then reptiles, dinosaurs, mammals and, much later, people appear in younger and younger layers.

Fossils also show how places have changed. Fossil sea shells found high on dry land show that the land was once under the sea.

In South Africa, fossils are protected by law. If you find one, leave it where it is, take a photo and tell a museum.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking a fossil is the original bone, unchanged; minerals have turned it to stone. Two: thinking every dead animal becomes a fossil; only a tiny few do. Three: thinking fossils form in a few years; it takes thousands to millions of years. Four: thinking people lived at the same time as the dinosaurs; dinosaurs died out millions of years before the first people.

The questions for this section are with you now: fossils as a record of the history of life.
