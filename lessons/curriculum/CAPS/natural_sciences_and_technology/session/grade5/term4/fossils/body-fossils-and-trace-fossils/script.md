# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the two kinds of fossils: body fossils, which are parts of a plant or animal, such as teeth, bones, shells, leaves and seeds, and trace fossils, which are signs that a living thing left behind, such as footprints, nests, eggs and droppings. The anchor is a visit by a Grade 5 class from Vredenburg to the West Coast Fossil Park near Langebaanweg in the Western Cape. Years ago, miners digging for a mineral used in fertiliser found huge numbers of fossil bones and teeth here, about five million years old. In the park, the learners see a dig site where thousands of bones lie jumbled together in the sand, from ancient bears, short-necked giraffes, sabre-toothed cats, seals and many more. Then their guide shows them a photograph of footprints found in rock on the shore of Langebaan Lagoon, nearby. They were made by a person who walked down a wet sand dune about 117,000 years ago. The big idea is that fossils can be parts of the body of a living thing or traces of what it did. Describing and giving examples of body fossils and trace fossils is examinable.

## Subtopic: Body Fossils

A body fossil is a fossil of an actual part of the body of a plant or animal. Usually it is a hard part that has turned to stone.

Teeth are the most common body fossils of all, because they are the hardest part of an animal. At the West Coast Fossil Park, there are teeth of many sizes, from tiny mouse teeth to huge teeth of elephant-like animals.

Bones are body fossils too: skulls, jaws, ribs, leg bones and backbones. Sometimes a whole skeleton is found, but usually only some bones.

Shells of sea creatures, such as snails, clams and ancient animals called ammonites, are common body fossils, often found in limestone.

Plants can become body fossils too. Fossil leaves often appear as dark, flat prints on sheets of shale. Seeds and pieces of wood can be preserved, and petrified wood is wood that has turned completely to stone, but still shows its rings and bark.

Body fossils show scientists what living things looked like: their size and shape, and the parts of their bodies. By fitting bones together, they can build the skeleton of an extinct animal. By comparing them with living animals, they learn how the extinct animal might have moved.

Sometimes the body part itself is gone, but its exact shape remains. When a shell dissolves away in rock, it leaves a hollow mould, and if minerals fill that hollow, they make a stone cast of the shell. Moulds and casts still show the shape of the body, so they are counted with body fossils.

## Subtopic: Trace Fossils

A trace fossil is not part of the body. It is a sign, or trace, that a living thing left behind, showing what it did while it was alive.

Footprints and tracks are the best-known trace fossils. The Langebaan footprints formed when a person walked across wet sand on a dune. The sand dried and was soon covered by more blowing sand, and over time it hardened into sandstone, keeping the prints. Today, the original footprints are kept safe in a museum in Cape Town. In other parts of South Africa and Lesotho, there are fossil footprints of dinosaurs, some showing three large toes.

Nests and eggs are trace fossils. They show where and how ancient animals bred. In the Golden Gate Highlands National Park, scientists found nests of dinosaur eggs about 190 million years old.

Burrows are trace fossils. Ancient worms, crabs and even some mammal-like animals dug tunnels in mud or sand that were later filled in and turned to rock.

Droppings, the poo of animals, can become fossils too. Fossil droppings can contain bits of bone or plants, showing what the animal ate.

To tell the two kinds apart, ask one simple question: is this a part of the living thing's body, or is it something the living thing made or did? A tooth is part of the body, so it is a body fossil. A footprint was made by the foot, so it is a trace fossil.

## Subtopic: Reading the Clues

Palaeontologists are like detectives, and fossils are their clues. Body fossils and trace fossils tell different parts of the story.

Teeth tell us about diet. Sharp, pointed teeth, like those of a sabre-toothed cat, show a meat-eater. Flat, ridged teeth for grinding, like those of a giraffe, show a plant-eater.

Footprints tell us about movement. The size of a footprint shows how big the animal was. The distance between prints shows whether it walked or ran. Prints of two feet or four feet show how many legs it walked on. Many tracks side by side show that animals moved in groups.

Back at school, the class tests this. They spread damp sand in a long tray outside. One learner walks across it, and another runs across it. The running footprints are further apart and deeper at the toes. A learner who hops leaves single prints, and two learners walking side by side leave two tracks. Then the class swaps roles: one group makes tracks while the others look away, and the second group must work out what happened just from the prints, like real palaeontologists.

Nests and eggs show that some dinosaurs looked after their eggs, a bit like birds today.

Where fossils are found tells us about the place. The seals and fish at Langebaanweg show that the sea and a river mouth were close by five million years ago.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking only bones are fossils; teeth, shells, leaves and seeds are too. Two: thinking footprints cannot be fossils; they are trace fossils. Three: thinking plants never become fossils; leaves, seeds and wood do. Four: thinking sharp teeth show a plant-eater; they show a meat-eater.
