# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers South Africa's rich fossil record, with three famous examples: the coelacanth, a fish known from fossils that was found alive; African dinosaurs, such as Massospondylus; and the Cradle of Humankind, where fossils of early human relatives were found. The anchor is a visit by a Grade 5 class from Krugersdorp to Maropeng, the visitor centre of the Cradle of Humankind, about an hour's drive north-west of Johannesburg. The name Maropeng means returning to the place of origin in Setswana. Inside, the learners pass through a timeline of the history of life, from tiny early living things, to fish, to dinosaurs, to mammals and to people. They see a model of a coelacanth, a dinosaur skeleton, and copies of famous skulls found in caves only a few kilometres away. The big idea is that South Africa's rocks hold one of the richest fossil records in the world, from ancient fish to dinosaurs to early human relatives. Naming and describing these examples of South Africa's fossil record is examinable.

## Subtopic: The Coelacanth

The first exhibit is a big, steel-blue fish with white spots and thick, fleshy fins that look almost like stubby legs. It is a coelacanth, pronounced SEE-luh-kanth.

Scientists knew coelacanths only from fossils. Fossil coelacanths are found in rocks up to about 400 million years old, but none were found in rocks younger than about 66 million years, the same time the last dinosaurs died out. So scientists believed that coelacanths were extinct.

Then, in December 1938, a fishing boat caught a strange fish near the mouth of the Chalumna River, near East London in the Eastern Cape. Marjorie Courtenay-Latimer, the curator of the East London Museum, saw that it was unusual and asked for help. Professor James Smith, a fish expert from Rhodes University in Makhanda, recognised it as a coelacanth. It was one of the most amazing discoveries of the century: a fish that everyone thought had died out was still alive. The fish was named Latimeria after Marjorie Courtenay-Latimer.

Since then, more coelacanths have been found, including off the Comoros Islands and, in 2000, by divers in deep underwater canyons off Sodwana Bay in KwaZulu-Natal. Because it looks so much like its fossil relatives, it is called a living fossil. The first coelacanth can still be seen at the East London Museum.

Coelacanths are large fish, up to about two metres long. They live deep in the sea, in rocky caves, and they move their fleshy fins in pairs, a bit like an animal walking. Scientists study them to learn how fish with fleshy fins are related to the first animals that crawled onto land.

The questions for this section are with you now: the coelacanth and why it is called a living fossil.

## Subtopic: African Dinosaurs

The next hall is about dinosaurs. South Africa's rocks, especially in the Karoo, the Free State and the Eastern Cape, hold some of the oldest dinosaur fossils in the world, from about 220 to 180 million years ago.

The most famous is Massospondylus. It was a plant-eater with a long neck, a long tail and a small head, up to about five metres long. It could walk on its back legs and use its front legs to grab plants. In 1854, it became one of the first dinosaurs ever named from Africa. As we learnt, its nests, with eggs and the tiny footprints of hatchlings, were found in the Golden Gate Highlands National Park.

Heterodontosaurus was a small dinosaur, about the size of a turkey, found in the Eastern Cape. Its name means different-toothed lizard, because it had three kinds of teeth, including tusk-like fangs.

Melanorosaurus was one of the bigger early dinosaurs, a plant-eater found in the Eastern Cape and Free State.

Even older Karoo rocks hold fossils of reptiles and mammal-like reptiles from before the dinosaurs, like the skull at Nieu-Bethesda. And near Barberton in Mpumalanga, rocks more than three billion years old hold some of the oldest signs of life on Earth, left by tiny living things.

The questions for this section are with you now: South Africa's dinosaurs and other ancient fossils.

## Subtopic: The Cradle of Humankind

The last part of Maropeng is about the caves nearby. The Cradle of Humankind is a World Heritage Site, an area of hills and limestone caves where many fossils of early human relatives and ancient animals have been found. Long ago, bones were washed or fell into the caves, were buried by sediment, and were glued into rock by mineral-rich water dripping in the caves.

In 1947, at Sterkfontein Caves, Robert Broom and John Robinson found a nearly complete skull more than two million years old. It became known as Mrs Ples. Later, also at Sterkfontein, scientists found Little Foot, one of the most complete skeletons of an early human relative ever found. And in 2013, in the Rising Star Cave, cavers found more than 1,500 pieces of bone from a newly discovered human relative, now called Homo naledi. Naledi means star in Sesotho.

Earlier still, in 1924, a small skull was found at Taung in North West: the Taung Child, studied by Raymond Dart.

Work in the caves is slow and careful. Scientists use small tools, brushes and even dental drills to free bones from the hard rock, a little at a time, and they record exactly where each piece was found. Little Foot took about twenty years to dig out of the rock.

These fossils help scientists understand how human beings came to be. They suggest that Africa is where the story of humankind began.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking the coelacanth is extinct; it is alive today. Two: thinking dinosaurs never lived in Africa; South Africa has some of the oldest dinosaur fossils. Three: thinking all dinosaurs were huge meat-eaters; Massospondylus ate plants and Heterodontosaurus was small. Four: thinking Mrs Ples was a modern person; she was an early human relative who lived more than two million years ago.

The questions for this section are with you now: the Cradle of Humankind and its fossils.
