### subtopic_1 — The Coelacanth
**Assistant:** How could the coelacanth survive for millions of years without anyone finding it?

**Tutor:** Coelacanths live in the deep, dark sea, usually between about 100 and 300 metres down, in rocky caves and canyons on steep underwater slopes. During the day, they rest in these caves, and at night they drift slowly, hunting for fish and squid. Very few people ever go that deep, and fishing nets usually do not reach those rocky places, so coelacanths were almost never caught. When one was caught, fishermen in places like the Comoros may have seen it as just another strange fish, without knowing it was special to scientists. As for the missing fossils, coelacanths living in deep, rocky places are much less likely to be buried in sediment and turned into fossils than animals living in shallow seas. So the fossil record seemed to stop, but the fish carried on quietly in the deep. The coelacanth teaches scientists an important lesson: when we stop finding fossils of a living thing, it does not always mean it has died out.

### subtopic_2 — African Dinosaurs
**Assistant:** Were the early human relatives at the Cradle of Humankind the same as people today?

**Tutor:** No. They were relatives of modern people, but different from us in important ways. Mrs Ples, for example, belonged to a group called Australopithecus. She walked upright on two legs, like us, but her brain was much smaller, about a third the size of a modern human brain, and her face and jaw stuck out more. She was shorter than most people today. Homo naledi had a mixture of features: hands and feet in some ways like ours, but a small brain. Scientists study the shapes of skulls, teeth, hip bones and foot bones to work out how these relatives walked, what they ate and how they lived. These fossils are like pieces of a puzzle, and each new find adds to the picture of how human beings came to be. It is important to remember that they are not our grandparents in a simple straight line; they are more like distant cousins on a large family tree, with Africa at its roots.

### subtopic_3 — The Cradle of Humankind
**Assistant:** Why is South Africa so rich in fossils?

**Tutor:** There are a few reasons. First, South Africa has huge areas of sedimentary rock, especially in the Karoo, which covers a large part of the country. These rocks were laid down over a very long time, by rivers, lakes, swamps and deserts, burying plants and animals again and again. Second, these rock layers cover many different periods of time, from before the dinosaurs, through the time of the early dinosaurs, so scientists can follow how life changed. Third, the land is dry in many places, with few trees and little soil covering the rocks, so the rocks and fossils are easy to see. Fourth, the limestone caves of the Cradle of Humankind trapped bones and protected them for millions of years. Finally, South Africa has a long history of scientists, museums and fossil hunters, from farmers in the Karoo to cavers at Rising Star, who have found and studied these fossils. Together, this makes South Africa one of the most important places in the world for understanding the history of life.
