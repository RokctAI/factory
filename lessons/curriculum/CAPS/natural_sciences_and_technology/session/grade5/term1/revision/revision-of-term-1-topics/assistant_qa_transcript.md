### subtopic_1 — Living Things and Skeletons
**Assistant:** How can I remember which bones protect which organs?

**Tutor:** Think of three boxes, from the top of your body down. The first box is the skull, a hard helmet around the most important organ of all, the brain, which controls everything you do. The skull also has two deep cups for your eyes. The second box is the rib cage, a cage of curved bars around your chest. Inside it are the two organs that keep you breathing and pumping: the lungs and the heart. Tap your chest gently and imagine the ribs as the bars of a birdcage keeping them safe. The third is a long tunnel rather than a box: the backbone. Running down inside the tunnel is the spinal cord, the cable that carries messages between the brain and the body. So it is skull for brain, ribs for heart and lungs, and backbone for spinal cord. A quick way to say it is head, chest, back. If you point to each place as you say it, you will remember it in the test.

### subtopic_2 — Structures and Food Chains
**Assistant:** What is the difference between a producer and a consumer?

**Tutor:** A producer is a living thing that makes its own food. In almost every food chain, the producer is a green plant or algae. It uses energy from sunlight, water and carbon dioxide to make sugar in its leaves, so it produces food. A consumer is a living thing that cannot make its own food and must eat, or consume, other living things. All animals are consumers. A herbivore, like a grasshopper, consumes plants. A carnivore, like a snake, consumes animals. An omnivore, like a person, consumes both. So in the food chain grass, arrow, grasshopper, arrow, frog, arrow, snake, the grass is the producer, and the grasshopper, the frog and the snake are all consumers. A good trick is to remember that producers produce, so they are always at the start of the chain, and consumers come after them. The Sun is not a producer. It is not alive, and it does not make food. It provides the energy that producers use.

### subtopic_3 — Life Cycles
**Assistant:** How do I draw a life cycle so I get full marks?

**Tutor:** First, choose the right stages and put them in the right order. For a plant, use seed, germination or seedling, adult plant, flowers, and fruit or new seeds. For a frog, use egg or frogspawn, tadpole, tadpole with legs or froglet, and adult frog. For a moth or butterfly, use egg, caterpillar or larva, pupa and adult. Second, draw it as a circle, not a straight line. Place the stages around the circle, like the numbers on a clock. Third, join them with arrows that all go round in the same direction, usually clockwise. Fourth, and this is the step learners often forget, draw an arrow from the adult back to the first stage, because the adult reproduces and starts the cycle again. Fifth, label every stage with its name. Simple, clear drawings with labels are better than beautiful drawings without labels. If you have time, add a short note, like the tadpole breathes with gills, to show you understand each stage.
