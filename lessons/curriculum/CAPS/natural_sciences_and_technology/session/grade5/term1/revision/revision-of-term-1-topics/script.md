# Part 1 — Expert

This session revises the Term 1 topics of Life and Living: plants and animals on Earth; animal skeletons; skeletons as structures; food chains; and life cycles. The anchor is a revision morning in a Grade 5 classroom in Pietermaritzburg. The class has turned the room into a nature trail with five stations. Station 1 has a map of South Africa's habitats and pictures of a crab and a fish. Station 2 has the class's model skeleton and a chicken wing bone. Station 3 has an egg, a straw model of a dog and a crab shell. Station 4 has picture cards to build food chains. Station 5 has a jar of tadpoles and a bean plant in flower. Groups move from station to station, answering the questions on each card. The big idea is that plants and animals live in many habitats and depend on each other, that vertebrates have skeletons of bones and joints, that every food chain begins with a green plant, and that every living thing has a life cycle. The whole of Term 1 is examinable.

## Subtopic: Living Things and Skeletons

At Station 1, the map shows that the Earth has many habitats, and each has plants and animals suited to it. South Africa's habitats include fynbos, Karoo, bushveld, grassland, forest, wetlands and the sea. Indigenous plants and animals have always lived here naturally, like the springbok and the king protea. Alien plants, like wattle and pine, were brought by people, and some are invasive.

Plants and animals depend on each other. This is called interdependence. Animals need plants for food, shelter and oxygen. Plants need animals to pollinate flowers, spread seeds and make the soil rich. All of them need water, soil, air and sunlight.

Animals are sorted into two groups. Invertebrates have no backbone and no bones inside; some are soft, like worms, and some have a hard outer covering, like crabs and beetles. Vertebrates have a backbone and a skeleton of bones inside. They are fish, amphibians, reptiles, birds and mammals. Most kinds of animals are invertebrates.

At Station 2, the model skeleton reminds the class of three jobs. The skeleton supports the body and gives it shape. It protects soft organs: the skull protects the brain, the backbone protects the spinal cord, and the ribs protect the heart and lungs. And it allows movement. Bones are hard and alive, and they meet at joints. Hinge joints, like the knee and elbow, bend one way. Ball and socket joints, like the hip and shoulder, swing in many directions. The joints of the skull are fixed. Muscles are joined to bones by tendons. Muscles can only pull, so they work in pairs, like the biceps and triceps.

The questions for this section are with you now: habitats, interdependence, vertebrates and invertebrates, and skeletons.

## Subtopic: Structures and Food Chains

At Station 3, the class sorts structures. A structure is anything with a shape that can carry a load. Frame structures are strong parts joined together with spaces between them, like pylons, ladders and roof trusses. Triangles make them stiff. A vertebrate skeleton is a frame structure: bones are the members, joined at joints, and covered by muscles and skin. Shell structures are hollow with a strong, usually curved covering, like an egg or a helmet. A crab's outer skeleton is a shell structure. It cannot grow, so the crab must moult. The straw dog shows how to model a frame skeleton.

At Station 4, the cards build food chains. Green plants make their own food, using water from the soil, carbon dioxide from the air and energy from sunlight. Chlorophyll in the leaves traps the light. The plant gives off oxygen. This is photosynthesis.

Animals cannot make their own food. They need food for energy, growth and repair, and plants are their primary food source. Herbivores, like rhinos, eat only plants. Carnivores, like lions, eat other animals. Omnivores, like baboons and people, eat both.

A food chain shows what eats what. It always starts with a green plant, the producer. The arrows point from the food to the eater: grass, arrow, grasshopper, arrow, frog, arrow, snake. Energy is passed along the chain, but most of it is used up at each step, so chains are short, and there are many more plants than top consumers.

The questions for this section are with you now: frame and shell structures, photosynthesis, feeding groups and food chains.

## Subtopic: Life Cycles

At Station 5, the tadpoles and the bean plant show life cycles. Living things grow, which means they get bigger, and develop, which means they change into new forms.

A life cycle is a circle of stages. A plant goes from seed to seedling to adult plant, makes flowers, is pollinated and makes new seeds. Some animals change shape completely in metamorphosis. A frog goes from egg to tadpole to froglet to adult. A mopane emperor moth goes from egg to caterpillar, the mopane worm, to pupa in the soil to adult moth. Adults reproduce and start a new generation.

Death can happen at any stage of the life cycle. Some animals, like turtles, fish and frogs, produce many young and give no care, so most die but a few survive. Others, like birds, mammals and crocodiles, produce fewer young and care for them.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: calling a crab a vertebrate because it is hard; its hard covering is outside, and it has no backbone. Two: drawing food chain arrows from the eater to the food; they point from the food to the eater. Three: saying plants get their food from the soil; they make it in their leaves using sunlight. Four: saying muscles push bones; muscles can only pull, so they work in pairs.

The questions for this section are with you now: growth and development, life cycles, and caring for young.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the classroom nature trail — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Homes, Groups and Bones

Each habitat has its own plants and animals. They depend on each other.

Vertebrates have a backbone. Invertebrates do not.

The skeleton holds us up, protects us and helps us move. Muscles pull bones at joints.

Keep Stations 1 and 2 in mind and try the questions for this part: living things and skeletons.

## Subtopic: Frames, Shells and Food

A skeleton is a frame. A crab's shell is a shell structure.

Plants make food from water, carbon dioxide and sunlight.

Food chains start with a plant. Arrows point to the eater.

Hold the food chain cards in mind and try this part's questions: structures and food chains.

## Subtopic: Round and Round

Seed to seed. Egg to adult. A life cycle goes round.

Death can happen at any stage. Some animals care for their young.

Pocket summary of the term. Plants and animals live in many habitats and depend on each other. Vertebrates have a skeleton of bones and joints that supports, protects and moves the body. A skeleton is a frame, and a crab's shell is a shell structure. Green plants make food, and food chains carry energy from plants to animals. Every living thing has a life cycle.

Hold the whole nature trail in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: life cycles.
