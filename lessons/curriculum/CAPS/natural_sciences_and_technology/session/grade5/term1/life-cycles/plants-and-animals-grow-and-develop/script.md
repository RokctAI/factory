# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers how plants and animals grow and develop throughout their lives, and the difference between growth, which is getting bigger, and development, which is changing into a new form. The anchor is a growth project in a Grade 5 classroom in Mthatha in the Eastern Cape. On the windowsill, a row of bean seeds has been planted in yoghurt tubs. A learner named Lwazi brings a diary about the hen at his grandmother's home, which has just hatched eight fluffy chicks. Behind the classroom door is a height chart where every learner marks their height with a pencil line at the start of each term. For the whole term the class measures, photographs and records. The big idea is that all living things grow and develop throughout their lives, changing from one stage to the next. Describing growth and development in plants and animals is examinable.

## Subtopic: Growing Bigger

Growth is one of the seven life processes of all living things. To grow means to get bigger: taller, longer, wider or heavier.

Living things grow by making more of the tiny building blocks that their bodies are made of. These building blocks are called cells. A cell is so small that you need a microscope to see one, and your body is made of many millions of millions of them. When you grow, your body makes new cells, using materials from your food.

The class measures growth in different ways. Each bean seedling is measured with a ruler every three days, from the soil to the tip of the shoot. Lwazi weighs one chick on his grandmother's kitchen scale every week. And the height chart shows that most learners grew a few centimetres taller over the term.

Living things need the right conditions to grow. Plants need light, water, air and minerals from the soil. Animals need food, water, air and warmth. When conditions are poor, growth slows down. One bean tub was forgotten behind a curtain and grew pale and weak. A chick that is not fed well grows more slowly.

Different living things grow at different speeds and to different sizes. A bean plant can grow many centimetres in a few weeks, while a baobab tree grows very slowly and can live for more than a thousand years. Many animals, like people, stop getting taller when they are adults. But many plants, like trees, keep growing a little bit every year for their whole lives.

The questions for this section are with you now: what growth is, how to measure it, and what living things need to grow.

## Subtopic: Changing as We Develop

Living things do not only get bigger. They also change as they get older. This is called development.

Look at the bean. First it is a hard, dry seed. When it gets water and warmth, it germinates: a root pushes out and grows down, then a shoot pushes up through the soil. The first leaves open. More leaves grow, the stem gets stronger, and after some weeks, flowers appear. The flowers then become bean pods with new seeds inside. The seed, the seedling and the flowering plant all look very different, but they are the same plant at different stages.

Lwazi's chicks are covered in soft, yellow fluffy down when they hatch. After a few weeks, real feathers grow in, and the chicks start to look like small hens and roosters. After a few months, they are adults that can lay eggs or crow.

People develop too. A newborn baby cannot hold up its own head. It learns to sit, crawl, stand, walk and talk. A child loses milk teeth and grows adult teeth. In the teenage years, the body changes again as children develop into adults. Our brains develop too, as we learn new skills.

Animals develop in other ways too. A young impala calf can stand and run within an hour of being born, because it must keep up with the herd to escape predators. A newborn kitten's eyes are closed for its first week or two, and it cannot walk properly for a few weeks. Different animals develop at different speeds, but all of them change as they grow.

So growth means getting bigger, and development means changing into new forms and learning new abilities. Usually both happen together.

The questions for this section are with you now: what development means, and the stages of the bean plant, the chicken and people.

## Subtopic: Stages of Life

Every living thing goes through stages from the start of its life to the end of its life.

For a person, the main stages are baby, toddler, child, teenager, adult and old age. Each stage has its own changes. An old person may become less strong, and their hair may turn grey, but they keep learning and can share their knowledge with younger people.

For many animals, the young look like small versions of the adult. A puppy, a calf, a lamb and a baby elephant look like their parents, only smaller. They mainly grow bigger.

But some animals change their body shape completely as they develop. A frog starts as an egg in a pond, hatches into a tadpole with a tail and gills that lives under water, then grows legs, loses its tail and becomes a frog that breathes air. A butterfly starts as an egg, hatches into a caterpillar, turns into a pupa and comes out as a butterfly with wings. This big change in body shape is called metamorphosis.

Plants have stages too: seed, seedling, young plant, adult plant with flowers, fruits and seeds, and finally old age and death.

In the next lesson, we will see how the last stage of one life links to the first stage of the next, in a life cycle.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking growth and development are the same; growth is getting bigger, and development is changing. Two: thinking plants stop growing; many plants, like trees, grow a little every year all their lives. Three: thinking a tadpole is a fish; it is a young frog, an amphibian. Four: thinking adults stop changing; people keep changing and learning all their lives.

The questions for this section are with you now: the stages of life in people, animals and plants, and metamorphosis.
