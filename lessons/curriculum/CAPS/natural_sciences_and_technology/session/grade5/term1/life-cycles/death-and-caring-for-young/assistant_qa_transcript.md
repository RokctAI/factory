### subtopic_1 — Death Can Happen at Any Stage
**Assistant:** Why does the turtle mother not stay to protect her eggs?

**Tutor:** A sea turtle is built for life in the sea, not on land. On the beach she is slow and heavy, and she would quickly overheat and dry out in the sun. She also needs to swim back out to feed and to lay more nests during the season. Turtles do not have a way to feed or protect their young, and the eggs take about two months to hatch, which is far too long for her to wait on the beach. So, instead of caring for a few young, turtles lay many eggs, deep in warm sand where the sun keeps them warm. The temperature of the sand even decides whether the hatchlings will be male or female: warmer nests produce more females. This way of having young has worked for turtles for millions of years. But today, people have made it much harder for turtles, with lights on beaches that confuse hatchlings, plastic in the sea and fishing nets. That is why the beaches of iSimangaliso are protected.

### subtopic_2 — Many Young, No Care
**Assistant:** Is it better to have many young or to care for a few?

**Tutor:** Neither way is better. They are two different plans that both work, and each suits a different kind of animal. Having many young costs little for each one. A fish can release thousands of tiny eggs, each with only a little food stored inside. Most will die, but there are so many that a few survive. This works well in places like the sea, where it is very hard for a parent to protect its young. Caring for a few young costs a lot for each one. An elephant mother spends almost two years carrying her calf and years feeding and protecting it. She can only have a calf every four or five years. But each calf has a much better chance of surviving. This works well for big animals that live a long time and can teach their young. Many animals fall somewhere in between. Birds, for example, lay a few eggs and care for their chicks for a few weeks or months.

### subtopic_3 — Few Young, Lots of Care
**Assistant:** Why do baby elephants stay with their mothers for so many years?

**Tutor:** Elephants have a lot to learn, and a big brain that takes years to fill. A baby elephant cannot even use its trunk properly at first. It has to practise for months to learn to pick up grass, drink water and spray dust. Its mother and the other females in the herd show it which plants are good to eat, where to find water in the dry season, and how to stay safe from lions. Elephant herds are led by an old female called the matriarch, who remembers where water can be found even in bad droughts, many years later. Young elephants learn this knowledge by following her. Young females usually stay with the herd their whole lives and help to look after the new calves. Young males leave when they are teenagers, at around twelve to fifteen years old. This long childhood is like ours. Animals that live long lives and need to learn a lot usually have few young and care for them for a long time.
