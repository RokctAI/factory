### subtopic_1 — What a Food Chain Shows
**Assistant:** Why do the arrows point to the eater, when it is the eater that moves towards its food?

**Tutor:** It does seem strange at first, because when a snake catches a frog, the snake moves towards the frog. But a food chain is not a map of movement. It is a map of energy. The arrows show which way the energy goes. When the snake eats the frog, the energy stored in the frog's body passes into the snake. So the arrow goes from the frog to the snake. A good way to remember it is to read the arrow as the words gives energy to. The grass gives energy to the grasshopper. The grasshopper gives energy to the frog. The frog gives energy to the snake. If you read it backwards, it makes no sense: the snake gives energy to the frog? No! Another trick is to remember that the arrow always points to the mouth that is doing the eating. If you check your arrows this way every time, you will never get them backwards in a test.

### subtopic_2 — Every Chain Starts with a Plant
**Assistant:** What happens to a food chain when the top consumer dies?

**Tutor:** When a fish eagle or a lion dies, its body still contains energy and materials. It does not just disappear. Scavengers such as vultures, jackals and hyenas may eat some of it. Then smaller living things take over. Flies lay eggs on it, and their maggots feed on it. Beetles feed on it too. And tiny living things called bacteria and fungi, too small to see, break down what is left. Living things that break down dead plants and animals are called decomposers. Earthworms, termites and some beetles do the same job with dead leaves and dung. Decomposers turn dead material into simple substances that go back into the soil or water. Plants then take these up through their roots, and they become part of a new food chain. So in nature, nothing is really wasted. Every food chain ends with decomposers, even if we do not usually draw them.

### subtopic_3 — Passing Energy Along
**Assistant:** Can one animal be in more than one food chain?

**Tutor:** Yes, most animals are in many food chains at the same time. A frog at the dam might eat grasshoppers, flies, beetles and spiders, so it is in several chains, one for each of its foods. And a frog is eaten by snakes, herons, otters and even big fish, so it is food in several chains too. If we draw all the chains in a habitat on one big page, the arrows cross and join into a net. Scientists call this a food web. A food web gives a truer picture of a habitat, because it shows that most animals have more than one choice of food. This also helps animals survive. If one kind of food runs out, a frog can switch to another. An animal that eats only one kind of food, like an animal that eats only bamboo, is in much more danger if that food disappears. That is why habitats with many different kinds of living things are usually stronger and recover better after a drought or a fire.
