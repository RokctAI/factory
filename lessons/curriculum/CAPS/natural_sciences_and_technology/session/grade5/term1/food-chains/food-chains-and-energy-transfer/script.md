# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers food chains: what they show, how to draw them with arrows, why every food chain starts with a green plant, and how energy is transferred from one living thing to the next along the chain. The anchor is a day trip by a Grade 5 class from Colesberg to the Gariep Dam on the Orange River, the biggest dam in South Africa. They stand on the dam wall and look out over the water. Suddenly an African fish eagle calls its loud, ringing cry, swoops down and snatches a fish from the surface with its talons. Along the grassy shore, they find grasshoppers, a frog and the shed skin of a snake. The teacher asks: where did the eagle's energy come from? The big idea is that energy from the Sun is captured by plants and then passed along a food chain each time one living thing eats another. Drawing and explaining food chains, and the transfer of energy along them, are examinable.

## Subtopic: What a Food Chain Shows

A food chain shows the feeding relationships between living things: what eats what. It is written as a list of living things joined by arrows.

Here is a food chain from the shore of the dam. Grass, arrow, grasshopper, arrow, frog, arrow, snake.

The arrow means is eaten by, or gives energy to. The grass is eaten by the grasshopper. The grasshopper is eaten by the frog. The frog is eaten by the snake. The arrows always point from the food to the animal that eats it. They show which way the food, and its energy, moves.

Every living thing in a food chain has a role. The first one is always a green plant. It is called the producer, because it produces, or makes, its own food. All the animals in the chain are called consumers, because they consume, or eat, other living things. The grasshopper is a herbivore that eats the producer. The frog eats the grasshopper, and the snake eats the frog, so they are both carnivores.

A food chain is usually short, with three, four or five links. There are rarely more than five or six.

A common mistake is to draw the arrows the wrong way, from the snake to the frog, because the snake goes to the frog. But the arrow shows energy, not movement, and the energy goes from the frog into the snake.

The questions for this section are with you now: what a food chain is, what the arrows mean, producers and consumers.

## Subtopic: Every Chain Starts with a Plant

Every food chain starts with a green plant, because plants are the only living things that can make their own food. They capture energy from sunlight and store it in the food they make.

In the dam, the plants are not only reeds and water weeds. The water is also full of tiny green living things, too small to see one by one, called algae. They float near the surface, where they get sunlight, and they make food just like a plant. Tiny water animals eat the algae. Small fish eat the tiny water animals. Big fish, such as the sharptooth catfish, eat small fish. And the fish eagle eats big fish.

So here is a longer chain from the dam. Algae, arrow, tiny water animals, arrow, small fish, arrow, catfish, arrow, fish eagle.

Sometimes we start a food chain with the Sun, to show where the energy first comes from. Sun, arrow, grass, arrow, grasshopper. The Sun is not a living thing and is not eaten, but it is the source of energy for the whole chain.

On land, the producer might be grass, a tree, a bush, seeds or fruit. A chain could be: marula fruit, arrow, elephant. Or: maize, arrow, mouse, arrow, owl. Or: maize, arrow, chicken, arrow, person.

When we write a food chain, we always begin with the producer on the left and end with the last consumer on the right. The animal at the end of a chain, which is not usually eaten by anything else, is often called the top consumer. The fish eagle and the lion are top consumers.

The questions for this section are with you now: why chains start with a plant, algae as producers, and the Sun as the source of energy.

## Subtopic: Passing Energy Along

Energy is passed along a food chain each time one living thing eats another. But not all of it gets through.

The grasshopper uses most of the energy from the grass it eats to stay alive: to hop, to breathe, to grow and to keep its body working. Some is lost as heat, and some is left in the parts it cannot digest. Only a small part of the energy is stored in its body. When the frog eats the grasshopper, it gets only that small part.

So at each step along the chain, less and less energy is passed on. That is why food chains are short. By the fifth or sixth link, there is not enough energy left to feed another animal.

It also explains why there are always many more plants than herbivores, and more herbivores than carnivores. Think about the dam. There are billions of algae, millions of tiny water animals, thousands of small fish, hundreds of catfish, and just a few fish eagles. A fish eagle must eat many fish to get enough energy.

If one link in a chain is removed, the others are affected. If people overfish the dam and take away too many fish, the fish eagles go hungry. If farmers spray poison that kills the grasshoppers, the frogs have less food, and so do the snakes.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: drawing arrows from the eater to the food; arrows point from the food to the eater. Two: starting a food chain with an animal; every chain starts with a plant, or with the Sun and then a plant. Three: thinking all the energy is passed on; most is used up at each step. Four: thinking the Sun is a producer; the Sun is the source of energy, and the plant is the producer.

The questions for this section are with you now: how energy is passed along a food chain, and what happens if a link is removed.

## Subtopic: Less and Less Energy

Energy passes along the chain, but most is used up at each step.

So chains are short, and there are few top consumers.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A food chain shows feeding relationships, with arrows pointing from the food to the eater. Every chain starts with a green plant, the producer, which captures energy from the Sun. Consumers eat plants or other animals.
