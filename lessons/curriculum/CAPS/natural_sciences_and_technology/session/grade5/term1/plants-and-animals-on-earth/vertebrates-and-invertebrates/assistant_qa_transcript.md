### subtopic_1 — Sorting Animals into Groups
**Assistant:** Why do scientists sort animals using the backbone, and not by size or colour?

**Tutor:** Size and colour can trick you. A baby elephant and a big beetle can be similar sizes, and a green snake and a green grasshopper are the same colour, but they are very different animals inside. Scientists look for features that tell us how an animal's body is built, because that shows which animals are closely related. The backbone is one of the most important of these features. All animals with a backbone also have a skeleton of bone inside, a skull around a brain, and a spinal cord running down inside the backbone. That is a whole body plan that fish, frogs, lizards, birds and mammals share. Invertebrates do not have this plan at all. Some are soft, and some, like crabs and beetles, have a hard covering on the outside instead. So the backbone is like a big signpost. Once you know which side of it an animal is on, you already know a lot about how its body works.

### subtopic_2 — Animals Without Bones
**Assistant:** If a crab has no bones, what holds its body together?

**Tutor:** A crab wears its support on the outside. Its hard shell covers its whole body, legs and claws, like a suit of armour. This hard outer covering is sometimes called an outer skeleton. Its muscles are attached to the inside of this covering, so when the muscles pull, the legs and claws move at the joints. The outer covering also protects the crab's soft body from waves, rocks and hungry birds. But there is a problem. A hard covering cannot grow. So, as the crab gets bigger, it has to crawl right out of its old covering, leaving it behind. This is called moulting. For a few days the new covering is soft, and the crab hides away until it hardens. If you find an empty crab shell on the beach, it may not be a dead crab at all, just an old covering that a growing crab left behind. Insects, spiders and scorpions grow in the same way.

### subtopic_3 — Animals with Backbones
**Assistant:** Is a snake a vertebrate, even though it can bend so much?

**Tutor:** Yes, a snake is a vertebrate, and its backbone is the secret to its bending. You have about thirty-three vertebrae in your backbone. Some snakes have more than two hundred, and some large pythons have even more, with a pair of ribs attached to most of them. Because there are so many small bones, each joined to the next by a joint that can move a little, the whole backbone can curve and twist in smooth loops. It is a bit like a long chain of beads, which bends easily, compared with a stick, which does not. A snake's skeleton protects its organs and gives its muscles something to pull on, so it can slide across the ground, climb trees and even swim. Snakes have dry, scaly skin and most of them lay eggs, so they belong to the reptile group. So bending a lot does not mean having no bones. It means having lots of them.
