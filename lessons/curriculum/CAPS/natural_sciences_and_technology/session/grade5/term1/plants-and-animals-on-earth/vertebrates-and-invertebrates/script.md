# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers how scientists sort animals into two big groups: invertebrates, which have no backbone and no bones inside their bodies, and vertebrates, which have a backbone and a skeleton made of bone inside their bodies. The anchor is a sorting walk by a Grade 5 class from Gqeberha. In the morning they explore the rock pools at low tide and walk along the beach. They see crabs, mussels, sea snails, a starfish, a sea anemone, a stranded bluebottle, gulls overhead and a dead fish washed up on the sand. In the afternoon they visit the museum in town, where they look at skeletons. Back in class, they sort everything they saw into two columns on the board. The big idea is that every animal is either a vertebrate or an invertebrate, and the backbone is the clue that decides it. Identifying and grouping vertebrates and invertebrates, with examples, are examinable.

## Subtopic: Sorting Animals into Groups

Scientists sort living things into groups to make them easier to study. Animals that share important features are put in the same group. One of the most important questions to ask about an animal is this: does it have a backbone?

A backbone is a column of small bones down the middle of the back. You can feel your own backbone if you run your fingers down the middle of your back. Each bump is one small bone called a vertebra. Many vertebrae together make the backbone.

Animals with a backbone are called vertebrates. They also have other bones inside their bodies, joined together into a skeleton.

Animals without a backbone are called invertebrates. The word in means not, so invertebrate means not a vertebrate. Invertebrates have no bones at all inside their bodies.

This one question splits the whole animal kingdom into two groups. Every animal you can think of belongs in one or the other.

How can you tell, if you cannot see inside an animal? Scientists look at skeletons in museums, study X-ray pictures and look at how an animal moves and holds its shape. A dog, a lizard or a pigeon holds its body up off the ground on stiff legs, because bones inside support it. An earthworm or a slug has no bones, so it lies flat and moves by squeezing and stretching its soft body. If you hold a sardine from the fridge, you can feel its firm backbone, but a piece of calamari, which comes from a squid, has no bones in it at all.

Here is a surprise. Most of the animals on Earth are invertebrates. Scientists estimate that about ninety-five out of every hundred kinds of animals are invertebrates. There are more kinds of beetles alone than there are kinds of all the vertebrates put together.

The questions for this section are with you now: what a backbone is and how it is used to sort animals.

## Subtopic: Animals Without Bones

On the beach, most of what the class found were invertebrates.

Some invertebrates have soft bodies with no hard parts. Earthworms, slugs, jellyfish and octopuses are soft all the way through. The bluebottle on the sand is a soft, floating animal with long stinging tentacles. The class does not touch it, because even a stranded bluebottle can still sting.

Some invertebrates protect their soft bodies with a hard outer covering. A mussel and a sea snail have shells. A crab has a hard shell over its whole body, including its legs. This hard outside covering is sometimes called an outer skeleton. Insects like ants and beetles, and spiders and scorpions, also have a hard outer covering.

Invertebrates come in many shapes. Insects have six legs and three body parts. Spiders have eight legs. Crabs and shrimps have ten legs. Worms have no legs. Starfish have arms, usually five, arranged around the middle. Many invertebrates are small, but some are big. The giant squid can grow longer than a minibus taxi.

Invertebrates in South Africa include the mopane worm, which is the caterpillar of a moth, dung beetles, termites, honeybees, the shongololo, which is a millipede, and the baboon spider.

Invertebrates are very important, even though many are small. Bees pollinate flowers and make honey. Earthworms and termites break down dead plants and make the soil rich. Dung beetles bury dung. Many invertebrates are food for birds, lizards, frogs and fish. Some, like mosquitoes and ticks, can carry diseases, so we also need to know about them to stay healthy.

The questions for this section are with you now: soft-bodied invertebrates, invertebrates with hard outer coverings, and their features.

## Subtopic: Animals with Backbones

At the museum, the class looks at skeletons. Every one of them has a backbone. These are vertebrates.

Vertebrates are sorted into five smaller groups. Fish live in water, breathe with gills and have fins and scales, like the galjoen, our national fish. Amphibians, like frogs and toads, begin life in water and have smooth, moist skin. Reptiles, like snakes, lizards, tortoises and crocodiles, have dry, scaly skin. Birds, like the gulls on the beach and the blue crane, have feathers and wings and lay eggs with hard shells. Mammals, like dogs, whales, dolphins, seals and people, have hair or fur and feed their babies on milk.

The skeleton inside a vertebrate holds its body up and gives it shape. This means vertebrates can grow very large. The largest animal that has ever lived, the blue whale, is a vertebrate. The heaviest land animal, the African elephant, is a vertebrate too.

The dead fish on the beach had fins, scales and gills, and when the class looked closely, they saw a thin backbone with many fine bones attached. So the fish is a vertebrate.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking a crab is a vertebrate because it is hard; its hardness is on the outside, and it has no backbone. Two: thinking a whale or a dolphin is a fish; they are mammals that breathe air and feed their young on milk, but they are still vertebrates. Three: thinking most animals are vertebrates; most kinds of animals are invertebrates. Four: thinking a snake has no bones because it bends so easily; it has a very long backbone made of many vertebrae.

The questions for this section are with you now: the five groups of vertebrates and their features.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the rock pool walk — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Backbone or Not

Feel the bumps down your back. That is your backbone.

Animals with a backbone are vertebrates.

Animals with no backbone are invertebrates.

Keep your own backbone in mind and try the questions for this part: sorting animals.

## Subtopic: No Bones Inside

Invertebrates have no bones inside.

Some are soft, like worms and jellyfish.

Some have a hard outside, like crabs, snails and beetles.

Most animals are invertebrates.

Hold the crab in the rock pool in mind and try this part's questions: invertebrates.

## Subtopic: Bones Inside

Vertebrates have a backbone and a skeleton inside.

Five groups: fish, amphibians, reptiles, birds and mammals.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Animals are sorted into two big groups. Invertebrates have no backbone and no bones inside; some are soft and some have a hard outer covering. Vertebrates have a backbone and a skeleton of bones inside, and they are fish, amphibians, reptiles, birds or mammals. Most kinds of animals are invertebrates.

Hold the museum skeletons in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: vertebrates.
