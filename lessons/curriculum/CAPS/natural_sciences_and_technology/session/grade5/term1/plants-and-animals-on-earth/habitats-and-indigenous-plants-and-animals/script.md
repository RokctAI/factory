# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the great variety of plants and animals on Earth, the different habitats they live in, and the indigenous plants, animals and habitats of South Africa. The anchor is a Grade 5 class in Kimberley planning a pretend road trip around the country. They spread a big map of South Africa on the floor and mark six stops: the fynbos near Cape Town, the dry Karoo, the bushveld of Limpopo, the grassland of the highveld, the wetlands of iSimangaliso in KwaZulu-Natal, and the sea along the coast. At each stop they list the plants and animals they expect to find. The big idea is that the Earth has many different habitats, each with its own mix of plants and animals suited to living there, and that South Africa is one of the richest countries in the world for indigenous plants and animals. Naming habitats and describing the plants and animals that live in them are examinable.

## Subtopic: A Planet Full of Life

Scientists have named nearly two million kinds of living things, and they think there are millions more that nobody has described yet. There are tiny ants and huge whales, short mosses and tall trees, fish in the deepest sea and birds on the highest mountains.

Each kind of living thing has a place where it lives. This place is called its habitat. A habitat gives a plant or animal everything it needs: food, water, air, shelter, space and a safe place to have young. A habitat can be very big, like a whole ocean or a grassland, or very small, like a rock pool or a rotting log.

Living things are suited to their habitats. Their body parts and behaviour help them to survive there. A fish has gills to breathe in water. A camel thorn tree in the Kalahari has very long roots that reach water deep underground. A frog has moist skin and lays its eggs in water, so it needs a habitat with ponds or streams.

Because the Earth has so many different habitats, from hot deserts to icy poles and from dark forests to open grasslands, it has a huge variety of living things. Scientists call this variety biodiversity.

Habitats have living parts, such as plants, animals and fungi, and non-living parts, such as soil, rocks, water, air, sunlight and temperature. The non-living parts help decide which plants can grow there, and the plants help decide which animals can live there.

The questions for this section are with you now: what a habitat is and why there are so many kinds of living things.

## Subtopic: Indigenous Plants and Animals

The class learns a new word: indigenous. An indigenous plant or animal is one that has always lived naturally in a place. It was not brought there by people from another country. The springbok, the blue crane and the king protea are indigenous to South Africa.

South Africa has more than 20 000 kinds of indigenous plants. About two out of every three of them grow nowhere else on Earth. That is why scientists from all over the world come here to study our plants.

Our country chooses some of its indigenous plants and animals as national symbols. The national animal is the springbok. The national bird is the blue crane. The national flower is the king protea. The national tree is the real yellowwood. The national fish is the galjoen.

Some plants and animals were brought here from other countries by people. They are called alien, or exotic. Some alien plants, like jacaranda trees in Pretoria, are well known. Others, like black wattle, pine and prickly pear, spread quickly and crowd out indigenous plants. They are called invasive alien plants. Many of them also use a lot of water. Government teams pull them out along rivers and on mountains so that indigenous plants can grow back and more water can flow into our rivers and dams.

We protect indigenous plants and animals in national parks and nature reserves, such as the Kruger National Park, the Addo Elephant National Park and Kirstenbosch National Botanical Garden in Cape Town.

The questions for this section are with you now: what indigenous means, our national symbols, and invasive alien plants.

## Subtopic: South Africa's Habitats

The class now visits each stop on the map.

The fynbos grows in the Western Cape. It has winter rain and hot, dry summers. It is full of shrubs with small, tough leaves, like proteas, ericas and reeds called restios. The Cape sugarbird drinks nectar from proteas.

The Karoo is dry, with very little rain. Plants called succulents, like vygies and the quiver tree, store water in thick leaves or stems. Animals like the suricate, or meerkat, live in burrows to escape the heat.

The bushveld, also called savanna, has grass with scattered trees, like marula, mopane and baobab trees. Elephants, giraffes, lions and many kinds of antelope live here.

The grassland of the highveld is wide and open, with cold, frosty winters. Few trees grow here. Grasses, bulbs and animals like blesbok, black wildebeest and the blue crane live here.

Wetlands, like the lakes and swamps of iSimangaliso Wetland Park, are always wet. Reeds, papyrus and water lilies grow there. Hippos, crocodiles, flamingos and many frogs live there.

The sea along our long coast is home to dolphins, whales, sharks, seals, penguins, sardines, crabs and kelp, a large seaweed.

South Africa also has small patches of forest, like the Knysna forest, with tall yellowwood trees, ferns and the Knysna turaco.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking every plant in South Africa is indigenous; jacaranda, pine and wattle trees were brought from other countries. Two: thinking a desert or the Karoo has no life; many plants and animals are suited to living there. Three: thinking indigenous means rare; some indigenous plants and animals are very common. Four: thinking one habitat suits every animal; each animal is suited to its own habitat.

The questions for this section are with you now: the main habitats of South Africa and their plants and animals.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the road trip map — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: So Many Living Things

There are millions of kinds of plants and animals.

Each lives in its own habitat, a home place that gives food, water, shelter and space.

Living things are suited to their habitats.

Keep the big map in mind and try the questions for this part: habitats and variety.

## Subtopic: Born Here

Indigenous means it has always lived here naturally.

The springbok, the blue crane and the king protea are indigenous.

Alien plants were brought by people. Some, like wattle and pine, spread too much.

Hold the springbok in mind and try this part's questions: indigenous and alien.

## Subtopic: Six Stops

Fynbos: proteas. Karoo: succulents and meerkats. Bushveld: marula trees and elephants.

Grassland: grass and blesbok. Wetland: reeds and hippos. Sea: dolphins and kelp.

Pocket summary of the lesson. The Earth has a huge variety of plants and animals living in many different habitats. Each habitat gives its living things what they need, and they are suited to it. Indigenous plants and animals have always lived in a place naturally. South Africa has fynbos, Karoo, bushveld, grassland, forest, wetland and sea habitats, each with its own indigenous living things.

Hold the six stops in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: South Africa's habitats.
