# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers two jobs of the skeleton: supporting the body and protecting the soft organs inside it. It looks closely at the skull, the backbone and the ribs, and at the limb bones and hip bones that we use for movement. The anchor is the start of a big cycle race around the Cape Peninsula in Cape Town. A Grade 5 class stands at the side of the road early in the morning. Thousands of riders are lining up, clipping on their helmets and pulling on gloves and knee guards. The teacher points out that every rider is also wearing protection that cannot be seen: a skull around the brain, a backbone around the spinal cord and a rib cage around the heart and lungs. The big idea is that the skeleton holds the body up, protects the soft, important organs and gives the limbs a frame to move with. Naming the main parts of the skeleton and describing how they support and protect the body are examinable.

## Subtopic: Holding the Body Up

The first job of the skeleton is support. Bones are hard and stiff, so they hold up the softer parts of the body, such as the muscles, skin and organs.

Look at a rider standing next to a bicycle. The leg bones carry the weight of the whole body down to the feet. The backbone holds the body upright, like the main pole of a tent. The hip bones, also called the pelvis, form a strong bowl at the bottom of the backbone. The pelvis carries the weight of the upper body and passes it on to the legs. The bones of the feet form arches that spread the weight and act a little like springs.

The skeleton also gives the body its shape. Two people may be different heights, but everybody's skeleton follows the same plan, so we all have a similar shape.

Support matters even more for big animals. An elephant can weigh as much as five or six cars. Its leg bones are thick and stand straight down under its body, like pillars, to carry all that weight. A giraffe's very long neck is held up by only seven neck bones, the same number as in your neck, but each of its neck bones is much longer than yours.

Animals that live in water need less support, because the water helps hold them up. That is one reason why whales can grow so huge. If a whale is stranded on a beach, its skeleton cannot hold up its heavy body properly, and its own weight presses on its lungs.

The questions for this section are with you now: how the skeleton supports the body and gives it shape.

## Subtopic: Protecting Soft Organs

The second job of the skeleton is protection. Some organs are soft and very important, so they are wrapped in bone.

The skull is a hard case around the brain. The brain controls everything you do, from thinking to breathing, and it is as soft as firm jelly. The skull also protects the eyes, which sit in two deep hollows called eye sockets. The jaw bone is the only part of the skull that moves, so that we can chew and talk.

The backbone protects the spinal cord. The spinal cord is a thick bundle of nerves that carries messages between the brain and the rest of the body. It runs down through a tunnel in the middle of the backbone, safely inside the vertebrae.

The ribs protect the heart and lungs. Most people have twelve pairs of ribs. They curve around from the backbone to the front of the chest, where most of them join a flat bone called the breastbone. Together the ribs and breastbone form the rib cage. It is strong enough to protect, but it can also move a little, so the chest can get bigger and smaller when you breathe.

The cycle riders still wear helmets. A hard knock in a fall could be strong enough to crack the skull or shake the brain inside it. The helmet adds another layer that absorbs the knock. Seat belts in cars and gloves and knee guards in sport protect our bodies in the same way.

The questions for this section are with you now: the skull, the backbone and the rib cage, and the organs they protect.

## Subtopic: Limbs and Hips for Movement

The third part of the skeleton is built for movement. Limbs are the arms and legs. Each arm has one long bone in the upper arm, two bones in the forearm, and many small bones in the wrist and hand. Each leg has a thigh bone, which is the longest and strongest bone in the body, two bones in the lower leg, and many small bones in the ankle and foot. A small, flat bone called the kneecap protects the front of the knee joint.

The legs are joined to the hip bones, and the arms are joined to the shoulder blades and collarbones. These connections let the limbs swing and turn.

Other vertebrates have limbs suited to how they move. A bird has wings for flying and strong legs for walking and perching. A penguin's wings have become stiff flippers for swimming. A frog has long back legs for jumping, and a seal has flippers. A snake has no limbs at all, but it still moves using its long backbone and ribs.

A cyclist uses the leg bones and hip joints to push the pedals, and the arm bones to hold the handlebars and steer.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking the ribs protect the stomach; they mainly protect the heart and lungs. Two: thinking the skull is one single bone; it is many bones joined at fixed joints, plus the jaw bone that moves. Three: thinking the backbone only holds us up; it also protects the spinal cord. Four: thinking a giraffe has many more neck bones than we do; it has seven, just like us, but they are much longer.

The questions for this section are with you now: the bones of the limbs and hips and how vertebrates move.

Arms and legs are limbs. The legs join the hip bones.
