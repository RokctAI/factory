### subtopic_1 — Muscles Pull on Bones
**Assistant:** Why do my muscles feel sore the day after I play lots of soccer?

**Tutor:** When you use your muscles much harder or for much longer than usual, the tiny fibres inside them get small tears and become a little swollen. This is normal and not dangerous. Over the next day or two, your body repairs the fibres, and while it does, the muscles can feel stiff and sore. The good news is that the repaired muscle is usually a little stronger than before. That is how training works: muscles get stronger by being used and then repaired. To help, you can warm up before you play, cool down afterwards with gentle walking and stretching, drink plenty of water, eat well and get enough sleep. Next time, the same game will probably not make you as sore. But sharp pain, pain in a joint, or swelling that does not go away is different, and you should tell an adult so that it can be checked.

### subtopic_2 — Muscles Work in Pairs
**Assistant:** Is the heart a muscle too, even though it is not attached to a bone?

**Tutor:** Yes, the heart is a muscle, but it is a special kind. There are muscles attached to bones, like the biceps and triceps, which you can control. You decide when to kick a ball or wave your arm. The heart is made of heart muscle, which keeps squeezing all by itself, day and night, without you having to think about it. Each squeeze pushes blood out through tubes called blood vessels to the whole body. In children it beats about seventy to one hundred times a minute when they are resting, and faster when you dance or run. There is a third kind of muscle in the walls of the stomach and intestines, which slowly squeezes food along. So not all muscles move bones. But the muscles that make us walk, jump and dance are the ones attached to the skeleton with tendons, and they work in pairs across joints.

### subtopic_3 — Joints Let Us Move
**Assistant:** How can a cheetah run so fast if it has the same kinds of muscles and joints as other animals?

**Tutor:** A cheetah has the same basic parts, but they are shaped for speed. Its body is light and slim, with long, thin legs, so there is less weight to move. Its leg muscles are very powerful and can contract very quickly. Its backbone is extra bendy. As it runs, the backbone curls up to bring the back legs forward, then stretches out long to reach far ahead. This acts almost like an extra pair of legs and makes each stride very long. Its claws do not pull all the way in like other cats', so they grip the ground like the spikes on running shoes, and its long tail helps it balance when it turns sharply. All of this lets a cheetah reach about one hundred kilometres per hour, faster than cars on many town roads. But its muscles tire quickly, so it can only sprint for a short distance before it must stop and rest. Muscles, joints and bones all work together to make that sprint possible.
