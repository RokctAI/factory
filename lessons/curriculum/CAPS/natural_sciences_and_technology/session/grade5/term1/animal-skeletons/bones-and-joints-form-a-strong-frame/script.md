# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the vertebrate skeleton: what bones are, what joints are, and how bones and joints together form a strong frame inside the body. The anchor is a science lesson in a Grade 5 classroom in Bloemfontein. In the corner stands a life-sized model of a human skeleton, which the class has nicknamed Mr Bones. On the front table is a tray of chicken bones left over from a Sunday roast, which the teacher has boiled clean and dried in the sun. The learners wear gloves, wash their hands afterwards, and use magnifying glasses to compare the chicken bones with Mr Bones. The big idea is that a vertebrate's skeleton is a frame of hard bones, joined at joints, that sits inside its body. Describing the skeleton as bones and joints that form a frame is examinable.

## Subtopic: Bones Inside the Body

All vertebrates have a skeleton inside the body. Mammals, birds, reptiles, amphibians and fish all have one. Because it is on the inside, covered by muscle and skin, it is sometimes called an inside skeleton.

A skeleton is made of many bones. An adult person has 206 bones. A newborn baby has more, about 300, but some of them slowly join together as the child grows, which is why adults have fewer.

Bones are hard and strong. They are made mostly of a material rich in calcium, the same mineral found in milk, maas, cheese and yoghurt. If you look closely at a broken chicken bone, you see a hard outer layer and, in the middle of the long bones, a soft, spongy centre called marrow. Marrow makes new blood cells.

Bones are alive. They grow as you grow, and if a bone breaks, it can heal. A doctor holds the broken parts still with a plaster cast, and over a few weeks new bone grows across the break and joins the pieces.

Bones come in many shapes and sizes. Mr Bones has long bones in his arms and legs, short bones in his wrists and ankles, flat bones in his skull and ribs, and small, knobbly bones in his backbone. The longest bone in the body is the thigh bone. The smallest bone is deep inside the ear and is about the size of a grain of rice.

The questions for this section are with you now: what bones are made of, how many there are, and how they grow and heal.

## Subtopic: Joints Where Bones Meet

A joint is a place where two or more bones meet.

Some joints let the bones move. Your knee and elbow are hinge joints. They work like the hinge of a door, bending and straightening in one direction only. Your shoulder and hip are ball and socket joints. The round end of one bone fits into a cup in another bone, so the arm and leg can swing forwards, backwards, sideways and around in a circle. Between the small bones of your backbone are joints that each move only a little, but together they let you bend and twist.

Some joints do not move at all. The bones of the skull are joined by fixed joints. On Mr Bones's skull you can see thin, wiggly lines where the flat bones fit together like puzzle pieces.

At movable joints, the ends of the bones are covered by a smooth, rubbery layer called cartilage. Cartilage stops the bones from rubbing and grinding against each other. You can see shiny, white cartilage at the ends of the chicken leg bones. Tough bands called ligaments hold the bones together at a joint, so they do not slip apart.

The class finds the same joints in the chicken. The chicken's wing has a joint that bends like an elbow, and the top of its leg bone has a round end that fits into the hip, just like ours.

The questions for this section are with you now: hinge, ball and socket and fixed joints, cartilage and ligaments.

## Subtopic: A Strong Frame

When bones are joined together at joints, they form a frame. A frame is a structure made of strong parts joined together, like the steel frame of a building or the poles of a tent.

The skeleton is a strong frame inside the body. It gives the body its shape. Without a skeleton, a person would flop to the ground like a pile of washing. It holds up the soft parts of the body, such as muscles, skin and organs, the way the poles of a tent hold up the canvas.

The skeleton is strong but not too heavy. Many bones are hollow tubes, which are strong for their mass. Bird bones are extra light, with air spaces inside, which helps birds to fly.

Different vertebrates have skeletons suited to the way they live. A fish's skeleton has a backbone with many thin bones that support its fins. A snake's skeleton has a very long backbone and many ribs but no leg bones. A bird's skeleton has wing bones and a large breastbone where the strong flying muscles attach. A frog has long, strong back leg bones for jumping.

The class compares Mr Bones and the chicken. Both have a skull, a backbone, ribs, hip bones and leg bones. The chicken has wings where we have arms. The pattern of the frame is the same, but the shapes and sizes of the bones are different.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking bones are dead, like stones; bones are alive, grow and can heal. Two: thinking a baby has fewer bones than an adult; a baby has more, and some join together. Three: thinking every joint moves; the joints of the skull are fixed. Four: thinking the skeleton is one solid piece; it is many bones joined at joints.

The questions for this section are with you now: the skeleton as a frame and how skeletons differ between vertebrates.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with Mr Bones and the chicken bones — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Hard, Living Bones

Vertebrates have a skeleton inside the body.

Adults have 206 bones. Bones are hard and contain calcium.

Bones are alive. They grow and can heal.

Keep Mr Bones in mind and try the questions for this part: bones.

## Subtopic: Where Bones Meet

A joint is where bones meet.

Hinge joints bend one way, like a knee. Ball and socket joints swing around, like a shoulder.

Skull joints do not move.

Hold your bending knee in mind and try this part's questions: joints.

## Subtopic: The Body's Frame

Bones joined at joints make a frame.

The frame gives the body its shape and holds it up.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A vertebrate has a skeleton of bones inside its body. Bones are hard, strong and alive. Bones meet at joints; some joints move, like hinge and ball and socket joints, and some are fixed, like those in the skull. Bones joined at joints make a strong frame that gives the body its shape and holds it up.

Hold the chicken and Mr Bones side by side in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: the skeleton as a frame.
