### subtopic_1 — Bones Inside the Body
**Assistant:** Why does a baby have more bones than an adult?

**Tutor:** A baby's skeleton is not finished yet. When a baby is born, many of its bones are still partly made of soft, bendy cartilage, and some bones are in several separate pieces. This helps the baby to be born and lets the skeleton grow quickly. The skull, for example, has soft spots between its flat bones, so it can squeeze a little during birth and grow as the brain grows. As the child grows older, calcium is laid down, the cartilage slowly turns to hard bone, and some of the separate pieces join together into one bone. The bones at the bottom of the backbone, for example, join together to make one strong bone. By the time a person is fully grown, around the age of twenty to twenty-five, there are 206 bones. So no bones go missing. Some of them simply join up with their neighbours.

### subtopic_2 — Joints Where Bones Meet
**Assistant:** What happens inside a joint when I bend my elbow?

**Tutor:** Your elbow is a hinge joint where your upper arm bone meets the two bones of your forearm. The ends of these bones are shaped to fit snugly together, like a hinge on a door, so they can only bend and straighten in one direction. The ends are covered in smooth cartilage, which is slippery and a little squishy. Around the joint is a tough bag filled with a small amount of fluid, a bit like oil in a machine, which helps the bones glide over each other. Strong ligaments wrap around the joint like tape, holding the bones in place so they do not slide apart. When a muscle in your arm pulls, the forearm swings up around the hinge, and your elbow bends. Because of the cartilage and fluid, this happens smoothly and quietly thousands of times a day. If you play sport and twist a joint too far, you can stretch or tear a ligament. That is called a sprain.

### subtopic_3 — A Strong Frame
**Assistant:** Is the skeleton of a chicken really like ours?

**Tutor:** Yes, much more than you might expect. All vertebrates share the same basic plan. A chicken has a skull to protect its brain, a backbone down its back, ribs around its heart and lungs, hip bones, and two legs with a thigh bone, a lower leg and toes. Its wing has the same main bones as our arm: one bone in the upper part and two bones in the lower part, then a hand that has become a small wing tip. But the bones are shaped for a different life. A chicken has a very large breastbone with a ridge down the middle, where its big wing muscles attach. Many of its bones are thin and light, with air spaces inside, as in most birds. Its neck has more bones than ours, which lets it turn its head a long way round to peck and preen. So when you eat a piece of chicken, you are looking at a skeleton that is a cousin of your own.
