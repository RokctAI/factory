### subtopic_1 — Holding the Body Up
**Assistant:** If the rib cage protects the lungs, how do the lungs still get bigger when I breathe in?

**Tutor:** The rib cage is strong, but it is not stiff like a box. Each rib is joined to the backbone by small joints, and at the front most ribs are joined to the breastbone by bendy cartilage. Between the ribs are thin muscles. When you breathe in, these muscles lift the ribs up and out, a bit like lifting the handle of a bucket. At the same time, a big sheet of muscle under the lungs, called the diaphragm, moves down. Together these movements make the space inside the chest bigger, and air rushes into the lungs to fill it. When you breathe out, the ribs move down and in again. Put your hands on the sides of your chest and take a deep breath. You can feel your ribs moving outwards. So the rib cage is like a cage made of flexible bars: it keeps the heart and lungs safe from knocks, but it still lets them do their work.

### subtopic_2 — Protecting Soft Organs
**Assistant:** Why is it so dangerous to hurt your backbone?

**Tutor:** Your backbone has two jobs, and both are important. It holds your body upright, and it protects the spinal cord, which runs through a tunnel inside it. The spinal cord is like a thick telephone cable full of nerves. It carries messages from the brain down to the arms, legs and organs, and messages from the body back up to the brain. If the spinal cord is badly hurt, those messages may not get through. Then a person may not be able to feel or move parts of the body below the injury. That is why a person who may have hurt their neck or back after a fall or an accident should not be moved by bystanders. You should call for help, and trained paramedics will keep the neck and back still while they move the person. It is also why we wear seat belts, why we never dive into shallow water or water where we cannot see the bottom, and why riders wear helmets.

### subtopic_3 — Limbs and Hips for Movement
**Assistant:** Do all vertebrates have the same bones in their limbs?

**Tutor:** It is surprising, but the bones follow the same pattern in many vertebrates, even when the limbs look very different. Think of your arm. It has one bone in the upper arm, two bones in the forearm, then wrist bones, and then finger bones. A bat's wing has these same bones, but its finger bones are extremely long, with skin stretched between them. A bird's wing has the same pattern, but some of the hand bones have joined together. A whale's flipper has the same pattern hidden inside, short and flat, inside a paddle. A cat's front leg has it too, ready for walking and pouncing. So the same set of bones has been shaped in different ways for flying, swimming, walking and holding things. Scientists see this pattern as a clue that all these animals are related. A few vertebrates, like snakes, have lost their limbs completely, and they move with their long backbone and ribs instead.
