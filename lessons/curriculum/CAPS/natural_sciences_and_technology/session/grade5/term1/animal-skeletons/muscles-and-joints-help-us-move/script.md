# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers how vertebrates move: muscles are attached to the bones of the skeleton, muscles pull on the bones, and joints between the bones let them move. The anchor is a dance practice in the hall of a primary school in Soweto. The Grade 5 class is learning a pantsula routine for the Heritage Day concert. They shuffle their feet, kick their legs high, swing their arms, spin around and stamp in time to the music. Every few minutes the teacher stops the music and asks the dancers to freeze, then to feel which muscles are working and which joints are bending. Later, back in the classroom, they build a model arm out of cardboard, a split pin and two elastic bands. The big idea is that bones cannot move by themselves: muscles pull on them, and joints let them bend and turn. Explaining how muscles and joints work together with the skeleton to make movement is examinable.

## Subtopic: Muscles Pull on Bones

A muscle is a part of the body made of many long, stretchy fibres. You have more than six hundred muscles. Some are big, like the muscles in your thighs and bottom, and some are tiny, like the muscles that move your eyes.

Many muscles are attached to bones. They are joined to the bones by strong, tough cords called tendons. You can feel a tendon at the back of your ankle, just above your heel. It is the thick cord that joins your calf muscle to your heel bone.

When a muscle works, it gets shorter and fatter. This is called contracting. As it contracts, it pulls on the bone it is attached to, and the bone moves. When the muscle relaxes, it gets longer and thinner again.

Here is the important part: a muscle can only pull. It can never push. So how do we straighten an arm after we have bent it? We need a second muscle on the other side.

The teacher asks a dancer to hold out her arm with the palm facing up and to put her other hand on the front of her upper arm. When she bends her elbow, she feels the muscle at the front bulge and harden. This muscle is called the biceps. When she straightens her arm, the biceps goes soft, and the muscle at the back of the upper arm, called the triceps, becomes hard.

The questions for this section are with you now: muscles, tendons and how muscles pull on bones.

## Subtopic: Muscles Work in Pairs

Because muscles can only pull, they work in pairs. One muscle pulls a bone one way, and its partner pulls it back the other way.

The biceps and the triceps are a pair. To bend the arm, the biceps contracts and pulls the forearm up, while the triceps relaxes. To straighten the arm, the triceps contracts and pulls the forearm down, while the biceps relaxes. They take turns, like two people pulling a rope in a game of tug of war.

The same thing happens in the legs. When the dancers kick, the muscles at the front of the thigh contract and straighten the knee. When they bend the knee to stamp again, the muscles at the back of the thigh contract and pull the lower leg back.

Back in the classroom, the class builds a model arm. Two strips of cardboard are the upper arm and forearm. A split pin joins them, like the elbow joint. One elastic band is stretched along the front, like the biceps, and one along the back, like the triceps. When they pull the front band tight, the forearm swings up. When they pull the back band, it swings down. The model shows that a pair of pulling muscles can move a bone both ways.

Muscles need energy from food and oxygen from the air we breathe to work. When you dance for a long time, your muscles get tired, and you breathe faster to bring them more oxygen.

The questions for this section are with you now: how muscles work in pairs and the model arm.

## Subtopic: Joints Let Us Move

Muscles pull on bones, but bones only move where there is a joint. If there were no joints, your skeleton would be one stiff piece, and you could not move at all.

Different joints allow different movements. A hinge joint, like the knee or the elbow, bends and straightens in one direction. A ball and socket joint, like the shoulder or the hip, lets a limb swing in many directions and turn in a circle. When a pantsula dancer spins a leg around in a wide circle, the hip's ball and socket joint makes that possible. The many small joints in the backbone let the dancers bend forwards and twist. The joints in the fingers and toes are small hinge joints.

All vertebrates move in this way, with muscles pulling bones at joints. A cheetah's powerful leg muscles and flexible backbone let it sprint very fast. A frog's large thigh muscles straighten its long back legs suddenly to make it jump. A bird's big chest muscles pull its wing bones down to fly.

Sometimes the parts that make movement possible get hurt. If a joint is twisted too far, the ligaments that hold it together can be stretched or torn. This is called a sprain, and the joint becomes swollen and sore. If a bone cracks or snaps, it is called a fracture, and a doctor puts it in a cast so that it can heal. A torn muscle or tendon also needs rest to mend. While a leg is in a cast, its muscles are not used, so they become thinner and weaker. After the cast comes off, gentle exercise makes them strong again.

To keep muscles and joints healthy, we need exercise, healthy food and rest. Dancers warm up before they start, by stretching and moving gently, so that their muscles and joints are ready and less likely to get hurt.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking muscles can push; they can only pull. Two: thinking bones move by themselves; muscles pull them. Three: thinking one muscle can bend and straighten the arm; it takes a pair. Four: thinking muscles are joined straight onto bones without anything in between; they are joined by tendons.

The questions for this section are with you now: how joints let us move, and how other vertebrates move.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the pantsula dancers — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Pull, Never Push

Muscles are joined to bones by tendons.

A muscle gets shorter and pulls the bone.

Muscles can only pull. They cannot push.

Keep the bulging biceps in mind and try the questions for this part: muscles.

## Subtopic: Two Muscles, One Job

Muscles work in pairs.

The biceps bends the arm. The triceps straightens it.

The model arm with two elastic bands shows this.

Hold the cardboard arm in mind and try this part's questions: pairs of muscles.

## Subtopic: Bend and Swing

Bones move at joints.

Knees bend like hinges. Hips and shoulders swing around.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Vertebrates move because muscles are attached to the skeleton. Muscles pull on bones by getting shorter; they can never push, so they work in pairs, like the biceps and triceps. The bones move at joints, such as hinge joints and ball and socket joints.
