### subtopic_1 — What Is a Structure?
**Assistant:** Why does a triangle make a frame stiffer than a square?

**Tutor:** Try this with four ice-cream sticks and four split pins. Join them into a square and push gently on one corner. The square easily leans over and squashes into a diamond shape, even though none of the sticks bends. The joints just turn. Now join three sticks into a triangle and push on a corner. It will not change shape at all, unless a stick bends or breaks. That is because the length of each side of a triangle fixes the angles. There is only one possible shape for three sides of those lengths. A square can have many shapes with the same four sides. So engineers add a diagonal piece across a square, which splits it into two triangles and makes it stiff. That is why you see triangles in roof trusses, electricity pylons, cranes and bridges. In the skeleton, the shape is held firm in another way, by muscles, ligaments and strong joints.

### subtopic_2 — Frame Structures
**Assistant:** If a skeleton is a frame, why can a building not move like a body does?

**Tutor:** Both are frames, but they are built for different jobs. A building must stay perfectly still, so its joints are fixed. The steel beams are bolted or welded together, and the concrete is set hard, so nothing can bend at the joints. If the joints of a building could move, it would wobble or fall in the wind. A body must move about, so most of its joints are movable. Bones meet at hinge joints, ball and socket joints and other joints that let them bend and turn. Muscles attached to the bones pull them into new positions, and ligaments hold them together so the frame does not fall apart. Some of our joints are fixed, though, like the joints between the bones of the skull, because there the job is protection, not movement. So a skeleton is a very clever frame. It carries a load like a building but can also change its shape like a machine.

### subtopic_3 — The Skeleton as a Frame
**Assistant:** Why did the straw model sag when the legs were too long and thin?

**Tutor:** Every member in a frame has to carry part of the load. The legs of the model carry the weight of the body down to the table. A short, thick member is hard to bend, but a long, thin member bends much more easily under the same load. Try it with a raw spaghetti stick: a short piece is quite stiff, but a long piece bends and snaps easily. When the model's legs were too long and thin, the weight of the clay skull and body made them bend, so the model sagged and wobbled. Animals have the same problem. Big, heavy animals like elephants and rhinos have thick, sturdy leg bones that stand straight under the body like pillars. Light, fast animals like springbok have long, slim legs, but their bodies are light, so the legs can carry them. To make the model stronger, you could use shorter legs, double straws for each leg, or add a diagonal brace to make triangles.
