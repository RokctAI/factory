### subtopic_1 — What Is a Shell Structure?
**Assistant:** If a shell is so strong, why does an egg crack so easily when I tap it on a bowl?

**Tutor:** An eggshell is strong against a push that is spread out, but weak against a sharp push on one small spot. When you squeeze an egg evenly in your hand, your fingers press all around it, and the curved shell passes the push around the whole covering. Every part of the shell shares the work, so it holds. When you tap it on the edge of a bowl, all the force lands on one tiny point. The thin shell cannot spread that out fast enough, so it cracks there, and the crack spreads. That is also why a hen can sit on her eggs without breaking them, because her weight is spread over the curved tops, but a chick can peck its way out from the inside, by chipping at one small spot. Engineers use this idea too. Domes and arches are very strong when the load is spread over them, which is why they are used for big roofs and bridges.

### subtopic_2 — Skeletons on the Outside
**Assistant:** Is a tortoise's shell an outer skeleton like a crab's?

**Tutor:** Not quite, and that is why the tortoise is such an interesting animal. A tortoise is a vertebrate. It has a backbone, ribs, a skull and leg bones, like other reptiles. But its ribs and backbone have grown wide and flat and joined to bony plates in the skin, making the domed shell. The shell is covered with hard scales made of the same material as your fingernails. So a tortoise's shell is a shell structure, but it is made of bone and is joined to its inside skeleton. A tortoise cannot climb out of its shell the way a crab can. The shell grows with it, adding new layers at the edges of each scale. A crab's outer skeleton is not made of bone at all, and it must be shed when the crab grows. South Africa has more kinds of tortoises than almost any other country, including the leopard tortoise, the angulate tortoise and the tiny speckled padloper.

### subtopic_3 — Growing Inside a Shell
**Assistant:** Why are there no giant crabs or insects as big as cars?

**Tutor:** There are a few reasons, and the outer skeleton is one of them. As an animal with an outer skeleton gets bigger, its shell must get much thicker and heavier to hold up its body and protect it. On land, a crab as big as a car would need such a heavy shell that it could hardly move. Moulting is another problem. Every time it moults, the animal is soft and floppy until the new shell hardens. A small crab can hide under a rock, but a huge one would have nowhere to hide and might even collapse under its own weight without support. In water, things are easier, because the water helps to hold the animal up. That is why the biggest animals with outer skeletons, like the Japanese spider crab, live in the sea. Vertebrates, with their frame of bones inside, can grow much bigger, because the frame grows with them and they never need to moult.
