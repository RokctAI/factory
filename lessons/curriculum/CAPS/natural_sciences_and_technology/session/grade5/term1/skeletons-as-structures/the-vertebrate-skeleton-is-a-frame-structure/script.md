# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers structures, and especially frame structures. It explains why a vertebrate skeleton is a frame structure and how we can build a model of one. The anchor is a building site right next to a primary school in Durban. For weeks the Grade 5 class has watched it from their classroom window. First the builders put up tall columns and long beams, joined together into a skeleton of steel and concrete. Only later do they add the walls, windows and roof. One day the teacher says, that building is growing the same way you did, from the frame outwards. Then the class builds model skeletons of their own. The big idea is that a frame structure is made of strong parts joined together, that it carries a load and gives shape, and that a vertebrate skeleton is a frame structure. Describing a skeleton as a frame structure and making a model of one are examinable.

## Subtopic: What Is a Structure?

In Technology, a structure is anything with a shape that can support a load or hold something without falling apart. A load is the weight or force that a structure must carry.

Structures are all around us. Some are made by people, like bridges, buildings, chairs, water towers, electricity pylons, tents and cooldrink cans. Some are natural, like trees, birds' nests, spider webs, honeycombs, eggs and the skeletons of animals.

Engineers sort structures into three main kinds. A solid structure is made of solid material all the way through, like a brick wall, a dam wall or a mountain. A shell structure is hollow, with a strong outer covering that carries the load, like an egg, a bucket or a helmet. A frame structure is made of strong parts, called members, joined together, with open spaces between them, like a ladder, an electricity pylon or the steel frame of the building next to the school.

Every structure needs to be strong enough for its load, stable so that it does not fall over, and made of suitable materials. Good structures also use material carefully, so that they are not heavier or more expensive than they need to be.

Many structures are a mix of kinds. A house has a frame of columns and beams, solid brick walls, and sometimes a curved shell roof. But usually one kind does most of the work.

The questions for this section are with you now: what a structure is, what a load is, and the three kinds of structures.

## Subtopic: Frame Structures

A frame structure is a set of strong parts joined together to make a shape that carries a load. The parts are often long and thin, like poles, beams, rods or tubes. The places where they are joined are called joints.

Frame structures are useful because they are strong but light. They use much less material than a solid structure of the same size, because most of the space inside is empty. That is why tall towers, bridges, roofs and bicycles are often frames.

At the building site, the class sees steel columns standing upright, carrying the weight of each floor down to the ground. Long beams run sideways between the columns. Some of the steel parts cross from corner to corner, making triangles. The foreman explains that triangles make a frame stiff, because a triangle cannot be pushed out of shape the way a square can.

Later, the frame of the building is covered with walls, windows and a roof. These coverings keep out the rain and wind, but the frame inside still carries most of the load.

Other frame structures the class can name are the roof trusses in the school hall, the metal frame of a bicycle, a kite, the poles of a tent and the steel towers that carry electricity lines across the country.

The questions for this section are with you now: what makes a frame structure, and why frames are strong and light.

## Subtopic: The Skeleton as a Frame

A vertebrate skeleton is a frame structure. The bones are the strong members. The places where bones meet are the joints. Together, they make a frame that carries the load of the body and gives it shape.

Like the building, the skeleton is covered. Muscles, fat and skin cover the bony frame, the way walls and windows cover the steel frame. But the bones inside still carry the load.

The backbone is like the main beam, running the length of the body. The leg bones are like columns, carrying the weight down to the ground. The ribs curve around from the backbone to the breastbone, making a strong cage. The hip bones and shoulder bones connect the limbs to the backbone.

There is one big difference. The frame of a building must stay still, but the skeleton must also move. Its joints can bend, and muscles pull the bones into new positions.

The class now builds a model skeleton of a dog. They use drinking straws for the long bones and pipe cleaners to join them at the joints. One long straw is the backbone. Four shorter pieces, bent in the middle, are the legs. A ball of modelling clay is the skull, and curved pipe cleaners make the ribs. When they push down gently on the back, the model holds its shape. When they make the legs too long and thin, it wobbles and sags. They learn that a frame needs the right length of members and strong joints to carry a load.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking only buildings are structures; trees, nests and skeletons are structures too. Two: thinking a frame structure is solid; it has open spaces between its parts. Three: thinking a skeleton is a shell structure; a vertebrate skeleton is a frame of bones inside the body. Four: thinking a square frame is stiffer than a triangle; the triangle holds its shape best.

The questions for this section are with you now: why a skeleton is a frame structure, and how to build a model of one.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the building next to the school — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Shapes That Carry a Load

A structure has a shape that can carry a load.

Solid structures are solid all the way through, like a wall.

Shell structures are hollow, like an egg. Frame structures are parts joined together, like a pylon.

Keep the building site in mind and try the questions for this part: kinds of structures.

## Subtopic: Strong and Light

A frame is strong parts joined together.

There is empty space between the parts, so frames are light.

Triangles make frames stiff.

Hold the steel columns and beams in mind and try this part's questions: frame structures.

## Subtopic: A Frame Inside

The skeleton is a frame. Bones are the parts. Joints join them.

Muscles and skin cover the frame.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A structure has a shape that supports a load. Structures can be solid, shell or frame structures. A frame structure is made of strong members joined together, with spaces in between, which makes it strong but light. A vertebrate skeleton is a frame structure made of bones joined at joints and covered by muscles and skin.

Hold the straw-and-pipe-cleaner dog in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: the skeleton as a frame.
