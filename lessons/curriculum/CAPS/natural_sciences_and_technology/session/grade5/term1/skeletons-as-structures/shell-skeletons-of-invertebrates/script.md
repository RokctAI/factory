# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers shell structures, and how some invertebrates, such as crabs, have a skeleton on the outside of the body that works as a shell structure. The anchor is a visit to the small fishing harbour at Lambert's Bay on the West Coast. A Grade 5 class watches a fishing boat unload West Coast rock lobster, which local people call crayfish. On the rocks they find black mussels, sea snails, limpets and a spiky sea urchin. In a rock pool, a fisherman shows them an empty crab shell, perfect and complete, but with no crab inside. Back at school, they test the strength of eggs and paper domes. The big idea is that a shell structure is hollow, with a strong, usually curved outer covering that carries the load and protects what is inside, and that the hard outer skeleton of a crab is a shell structure. Describing invertebrate outer skeletons as shell structures, and comparing them with frame structures, are examinable.

## Subtopic: What Is a Shell Structure?

A shell structure is a hollow structure with a strong outer covering. The covering itself carries the load. There is no frame inside to hold it up.

Natural shell structures include eggs, snail shells, mussel shells, tortoise shells, coconuts, nutshells and the hard covering of beetles and crabs. Human-made shell structures include bicycle helmets, buckets, cooldrink cans, plastic bottles, car bodies, boats, tents shaped like domes and the curved roofs of some buildings and stadiums.

Shell structures have three big advantages. First, they are strong for their mass, because the whole covering shares the load. Second, they are hollow, so they leave a large space inside to hold or protect something, like the chick inside an egg or the head inside a helmet. Third, they are usually light, because they use only a thin layer of material.

Curves make shells strong. When you press on a curved shell, the push is spread out over the whole covering instead of bending one spot. That is why so many shells are round, domed or arched.

The class tests this with an egg. Holding a raw egg in the palm of one hand, with fingers wrapped all the way around it, a learner squeezes evenly as hard as he can. To everyone's surprise, the egg does not break. The push is spread around the curved shell. But a sharp tap on the edge of a bowl, on one small spot, cracks it easily.

The questions for this section are with you now: what a shell structure is, examples, and why curves make shells strong.

## Subtopic: Skeletons on the Outside

Vertebrates have their skeleton on the inside, as a frame of bones. Many invertebrates have no bones, but some have a hard covering on the outside of their bodies instead. This outer covering is called an outer skeleton. It is a shell structure.

A crab is a good example. Its whole body, including its legs and claws, is covered in a hard shell. The crayfish unloaded at the harbour are covered in the same kind of hard shell. Inside the shell are the soft body parts and the muscles. The muscles are attached to the inside of the shell. Where the legs need to bend, the shell is divided into sections, with thinner, bendy joints between them, so the legs can move.

This outer skeleton does the same jobs as our inside skeleton. It supports the body, gives it shape, protects the soft parts and gives the muscles something to pull on.

Insects such as beetles, ants and grasshoppers, and spiders and scorpions, also have outer skeletons. A beetle's hard, shiny wing covers are part of its outer skeleton.

Mussels, limpets and snails are different again. They have soft bodies and live inside a hard shell that they make themselves. A snail's shell is a spiral shell structure that it carries on its back. When there is danger or when it is very dry, the snail pulls its whole body inside.

The questions for this section are with you now: outer skeletons of crabs and insects, and the shells of snails and mussels.

## Subtopic: Growing Inside a Shell

A hard outer skeleton has one big problem: it cannot grow. A crab's shell does not get bigger as the crab grows bigger.

So a crab must moult. When its shell becomes too tight, the crab makes a new, soft shell underneath. Then the old shell splits open along the back, and the crab slowly pulls itself out, legs, claws and all. It swells up with water while the new shell is still soft, and then the new shell hardens, a size bigger. The empty crab shell in the rock pool was a moult, left behind by a growing crab. While its new shell is soft, a crab is in danger, so it hides under rocks until the shell hardens. Crayfish, insects and spiders moult in the same way.

Snails and mussels do not moult. They make their shells bigger by adding new shell material around the open edge. The lines on a mussel shell show where it has grown.

Frames and shells each have strengths. A frame skeleton can grow with the animal and allows very large bodies, like an elephant or a whale. A shell skeleton gives excellent all-round armour, but the animal must moult to grow, and big shells would be very heavy on land. That is one reason why most animals with outer skeletons are quite small.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking a crab has bones inside; its hard parts are on the outside, and it has no bones. Two: thinking an empty crab shell always means a dead crab; it may be a moult. Three: thinking a shell must be thick to be strong; a curved shell can be thin and still strong. Four: thinking a tortoise is an invertebrate because it has a shell; a tortoise has a backbone, so it is a vertebrate.

The questions for this section are with you now: moulting, how snail shells grow, and comparing shell and frame skeletons.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the harbour at Lambert's Bay — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Hollow and Strong

A shell structure is hollow, with a strong outer covering.

Curves spread the push, so shells are strong.

An egg, a helmet and a can are shells.

Keep the squeezed egg in mind and try the questions for this part: shell structures.

## Subtopic: Armour on the Outside

Crabs and crayfish have a hard skeleton on the outside.

It supports, protects and holds the muscles.

Insects and spiders have one too. Snails live in shells.

Hold the crayfish in mind and try this part's questions: outer skeletons.

## Subtopic: Too Tight? Moult!

A hard shell cannot grow.

A crab climbs out of its old shell and grows a bigger one.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A shell structure is hollow with a strong, curved outer covering that carries the load. Crabs, crayfish and insects have outer skeletons that are shell structures. These support and protect the soft body, but they cannot grow, so the animal must moult. A vertebrate skeleton is a frame inside; a crab's skeleton is a shell outside.

Hold the empty crab shell in the rock pool in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: growing inside a shell.
