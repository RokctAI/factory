# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers how energy can be stored in cells and batteries, and how an electric circuit transfers that energy to where it is needed, such as a bulb. The anchor is a Grade 5 classroom in Soshanguve, north of Pretoria. The night before, the electricity was off for a few hours, and many learners did their homework by torchlight or with the light of a cell phone. The teacher asks: where did the light come from if the electricity was off? Then she unscrews a torch, tips out two cells, and gives each group a box with cells, cell holders, wires with crocodile clips, small bulbs in holders and a switch. Their task is to make the bulb light up. The big idea is that cells store energy, and that a complete circuit is needed to carry the energy from the cell to the bulb. Explaining cells, batteries and simple circuits is examinable.

## Subtopic: Energy Stored in Cells

The small, round objects from the torch are called cells. Many people call them batteries, but in science, a single unit is a cell.

A cell stores energy in chemicals inside it. When the cell is connected in a circuit, the chemicals slowly change, and the stored energy is released as electrical energy, which flows out of the cell along the wires. When all the chemicals have changed, the cell is flat, or dead, and cannot give any more energy.

Every cell has two ends, called terminals. One end is marked with a plus sign and is called the positive terminal. The other is marked with a minus sign and is called the negative terminal. On a torch cell, the positive end has a small bump.

A battery is two or more cells joined together. The torch holds two cells, one behind the other, joined positive to negative, which makes a battery. Joining cells this way pushes the energy around the circuit more strongly, so the torch shines more brightly. A car battery has six cells inside one box.

Cells come in many sizes. Small, flat button cells are used in watches and calculators. Long cells are used in torches, remote controls and toys. Some cells, like those in cell phones and tablets, are rechargeable. When they are flat, you plug them into a charger, and the energy is stored in them again.

Used cells must never be thrown into a fire, because they can burst. They contain chemicals that can pollute soil and water, so they should be taken to a battery recycling bin at a shop.

The questions for this section are with you now: cells, terminals, batteries and rechargeable cells.

## Subtopic: Making a Circuit

Now the groups try to light the bulb.

Group 1 touches one wire from the cell to the bulb. Nothing happens. Group 2 joins two wires, one from each end of the cell, but both to the same side of the bulb holder. Still nothing. Group 3 joins one wire from the positive terminal of the cell to one side of the bulb holder, and a second wire from the other side of the bulb holder back to the negative terminal. The bulb lights up!

The learners have made a complete circuit. A circuit is a closed path that electricity can flow around, from one terminal of the cell, through the wires and the bulb, and back to the other terminal. The word circuit is like circle: the path must go all the way round.

If there is a gap anywhere in the path, the circuit is broken, or open, and no electricity flows, so the bulb does not light. A loose wire, a broken bulb, a flat cell or a wire joined to the wrong place can all leave a gap.

A switch is a device that opens and closes a gap in the circuit on purpose. When the switch is on, it is closed: its metal parts touch, and the circuit is complete, so the bulb lights. When the switch is off, it is open: a gap is made, and the bulb goes out. The light switch on your classroom wall works the same way.

Wires are made of copper, a metal that lets electricity flow through it easily, covered in plastic for safety.

The questions for this section are with you now: complete and broken circuits, and how a switch works.

## Subtopic: Energy on the Move

A circuit is a way of transferring energy from where it is stored to where it is needed.

In the torch, the energy starts as stored chemical energy in the cells. When the switch is closed, it is transferred as electrical energy through the metal strips and the bulb. In the bulb, it is changed into light energy, which lets us see, and some heat energy. If you touch an old-style bulb after it has been on for a while, it feels warm.

Different devices in a circuit change electrical energy into different useful forms. A bulb gives light. A buzzer or a speaker gives sound. A small electric motor gives movement, and can turn a fan. A heater or a kettle element gives heat.

When the torch is switched on for a long time, the cells slowly run flat, and the bulb gets dimmer. The energy has been transferred from the cells to the bulb and out into the room as light and heat.

Cells and batteries are very useful because they are portable: we can carry stored energy with us to places where there is no electricity, in torches, cell phones, radios and toys. But they store only small amounts of energy, so a fridge, a stove or a geyser needs mains electricity.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking one wire from the cell to the bulb is enough; a circuit needs a complete loop back to the cell. Two: thinking a switch makes electricity; it only opens or closes a gap in the circuit. Three: calling a single cell a battery; a battery is two or more cells joined together. Four: throwing used cells into a fire; they can burst and pollute.

The questions for this section are with you now: how energy is transferred around a circuit and changed into light, heat, sound and movement.
