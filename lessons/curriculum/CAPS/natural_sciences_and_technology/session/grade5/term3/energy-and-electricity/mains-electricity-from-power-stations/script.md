# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers mains electricity: the electricity that comes from the plug points and light switches in our homes and schools. It is made in power stations, such as coal-fired power stations, and transferred in a circuit along power lines to our homes and back. The anchor is a primary school near Kriel in Mpumalanga. From the playground, the Grade 5 class can see the tall chimneys of coal-fired power stations on the horizon, and the steel pylons marching across the fields, carrying thick cables. Their teacher draws a long road across the whiteboard and says: today we follow the journey of electricity, all the way from a lump of coal underground to the light above your head. The big idea is that a power station changes the energy stored in a fuel into electrical energy, and a giant circuit carries it to where it is needed. Describing how electricity is generated in a coal-fired power station and transferred to our homes is examinable.

## Subtopic: Inside a Power Station

The journey starts at a coal mine next to the power station. The coal is carried on long conveyor belts straight to the power station, where it is crushed into a fine powder, like flour.

The coal powder is blown into a huge furnace inside a boiler, where it burns. Burning releases the energy stored in the coal as heat. The heat boils water in thousands of metal pipes running through the boiler, turning it into very hot steam at high pressure.

The steam rushes through pipes to a turbine. A turbine is like a giant fan with many blades on a shaft. The force of the steam pushing on the blades makes the turbine spin very fast. The heat energy has now been changed into movement energy.

The spinning turbine is joined to a generator. Inside the generator, huge magnets spin inside coils of copper wire, and this produces electricity. The generator changes movement energy into electrical energy.

After the steam has passed through the turbine, it is cooled and turned back into water, which is pumped back into the boiler to be used again. The smoke and gases from the burning coal go up the tall chimney. Filters remove much of the ash and dust, but the gases still pollute the air.

So the energy chain is: stored energy in coal, to heat, to movement, to electrical energy.

Most of South Africa's electricity is made in coal-fired power stations, many of them here in Mpumalanga, close to the coal mines.

The questions for this section are with you now: the boiler, the turbine and the generator, and the energy chain in a coal-fired power station.

## Subtopic: Along the Power Lines

The electricity leaves the power station along thick cables held high on steel pylons. These are called transmission lines. They carry electricity hundreds of kilometres across the country, from the power stations to cities and towns. All the power lines are linked together in a big network called the national grid, so that electricity from many power stations can reach every part of the country.

The electricity in these lines is pushed very strongly, which is called a very high voltage, because this wastes less energy over long distances. That is also what makes these lines so dangerous.

Near a town, the electricity reaches a substation, a fenced yard full of large machines called transformers. They lower the voltage, so that the electricity is safer to send along smaller lines on wooden or concrete poles, or in cables under the ground, through the streets.

Near your home, a smaller transformer, often in a green metal box on the pavement or on a pole, lowers the voltage again. Then a cable carries the electricity to your home, through an electricity meter, which measures how much electricity you use. Many South African homes have prepaid meters, where you buy electricity in advance. From there, it goes to the distribution board, with its switches and safety fuses, and along wires inside the walls to every light and plug point.

The questions for this section are with you now: pylons, transmission lines, the national grid, substations, transformers and meters.

## Subtopic: A Giant Circuit

Remember that electricity only flows in a complete circuit. Mains electricity is no different. It travels in a giant circuit from the power station to our homes, and back to the power station.

That is why every plug has more than one pin, and every electrical cable has more than one wire inside. One wire carries the electricity to the appliance, and another carries it back. When you switch on a kettle, you close the circuit, and electricity flows from the power station, through the lines, into the kettle element and back again. When you switch it off, you open the circuit.

The electrical energy is then changed into other useful forms in our homes: light in bulbs, heat in kettles and stoves, sound in radios, and movement in fans and fridge motors.

Not all electricity in South Africa comes from coal. The Koeberg nuclear power station near Cape Town uses a different fuel, uranium, to heat water into steam. Wind farms, many of them in the Eastern Cape and Western Cape, use the wind to spin turbines. Solar power stations, especially in the sunny Northern Cape, change sunlight into electricity. And dams, such as the Gariep Dam, use falling water to spin turbines.

When people use more electricity than the power stations can make, there is not enough to go round. To protect the whole grid, the power company switches off electricity in different areas in turn. South Africans call this load shedding. We can all help by saving electricity: switching off lights, using less hot water, and not leaving appliances on.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking electricity is made inside the plug point; it is made in power stations far away. Two: thinking electricity flows only one way, to our homes; it flows in a circuit, to our homes and back. Three: thinking coal is turned straight into electricity; its energy is changed to heat, then movement, then electrical energy. Four: thinking all our electricity comes from coal; we also use nuclear, wind, solar and water power.

The questions for this section are with you now: the mains circuit, energy in the home, other kinds of power stations, and saving electricity.
