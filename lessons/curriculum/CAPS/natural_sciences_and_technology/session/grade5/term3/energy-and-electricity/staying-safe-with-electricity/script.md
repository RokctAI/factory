# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers safety precautions when using electricity. Mains electricity is very useful, but it pushes strongly enough to cause serious burns, start fires and even kill. The anchor is a visit to a primary school in Tembisa, on the East Rand, by an electrician named Mr Nkosi. He brings a box of things he has removed from homes: a melted multiplug, a kettle cord with the plastic worn through, a burnt plug, and a tangle of thin wire from an illegal connection. Then he sends the Grade 5 class on a safety hunt around the school, and asks them to repeat it at home with an adult, ticking off every danger they find. The big idea is that electricity must always be treated with respect, and that a few simple rules can prevent shocks, burns and fires. Knowing and explaining electrical safety precautions is examinable.

## Subtopic: Why Mains Electricity Is Dangerous

The small cells we use to build circuits in class give such a gentle push that we cannot feel them. Mains electricity is very different. The electricity from a plug point in South Africa pushes about one hundred and fifty times harder than a torch cell.

Our bodies can conduct electricity, especially when our skin is wet. If a person touches a live wire, or a faulty appliance, the electricity can flow through their body to the ground. This is called an electric shock. A shock can cause burns, make muscles jerk or lock tight so the person cannot let go, and stop the heart. Mains electricity can kill.

Electricity can also start fires. When too much electricity flows through a wire, the wire gets very hot. If the plastic covering melts, it can set fire to curtains, carpets or furniture.

Water makes electricity much more dangerous. Water, especially dirty or salty water, conducts electricity. Wet hands, wet floors, or appliances near basins and baths can let electricity reach a person.

Homes with proper wiring have safety switches in the distribution board. A circuit breaker switches off the electricity if too much is flowing. An earth leakage switch switches it off very quickly if electricity is leaking somewhere it should not, such as through a person. But these switches do not replace being careful.

## Subtopic: Safe Habits Indoors

The class's safety hunt finds several dangers, and Mr Nkosi explains the rules.

Never touch switches, plugs or appliances with wet hands, and never use a hairdryer, radio or heater near a bath or basin.

Never push anything into a plug point except a plug. Never poke a knife or fork into a toaster to get bread out, even if it is switched off at the toaster, because parts may still be live. Unplug it first, and let it cool.

Do not overload multiplugs or adaptors by plugging too many appliances into one point, especially heaters and kettles. The melted multiplug in Mr Nkosi's box was overloaded.

Check cords and plugs. If the plastic covering is cracked, worn or melted, or bare wire is showing, do not use the appliance. Tell an adult so that it can be fixed or replaced. Do not run cords under carpets, where they can be damaged without anyone noticing.

Pull out a plug by holding the plug itself, not by yanking the cord.

Switch off and unplug appliances when you are not using them. This saves electricity too.

Never try to repair an appliance or open a plug point yourself. Only a qualified electrician should work on wiring.

Keep small children away from plug points. Plastic safety covers can block plug points that are not being used.

The questions for this section are with you now: safe habits with plugs, cords, appliances and water at home.

## Subtopic: Safe Habits Outdoors

Outdoors, the dangers are bigger, because the voltages are much higher.

Never climb an electricity pylon, a pole, or a tree near power lines. Never fly kites or toy drones near power lines. If a kite gets caught in the lines, leave it there. The very high voltage in some lines can jump through the air to a person who gets too close.

Never go into a substation or touch a transformer box, even to fetch a ball. The warning signs are there for a reason.

If you see a power line lying on the ground, stay far away. It may still be live, and the ground around it can be dangerous. Warn others and tell an adult to call the electricity supplier.

Illegal connections, where people hook thin wires to the supply to get free electricity, are very dangerous. The wires are often bare, lying on the ground or in puddles, or hanging low across paths. Every year, children and adults in South Africa are killed by touching them. Never touch any loose wire.

If you see someone getting an electric shock, do not touch them, or the electricity can flow through you too. If it is safe, switch off the electricity at the switch or distribution board. If you cannot switch it off, push the person away from the wire with something dry that does not conduct electricity, like a wooden broom handle. Then call 10177 or 112 for help.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking a switched-off toaster is always safe to poke with a knife; unplug it first. Two: thinking you can rescue someone by grabbing them during a shock; switch off the power or use a dry wooden stick. Three: thinking a fallen power line is dead; always treat it as live. Four: thinking illegal connections are only against the law; they are also deadly.

The questions for this section are with you now: power lines, substations, fallen lines, illegal connections and helping someone safely.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with Mr Nkosi's safety hunt — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Strong and Dangerous

Mains electricity is much stronger than a cell.

It can shock, burn, start fires and kill.

Water makes it more dangerous.

Keep the melted multiplug in mind and try the questions for this part: why electricity is dangerous.

## Subtopic: Safe at Home

Dry hands only. Nothing in plug points but plugs.

Do not overload multiplugs. Do not use damaged cords.

Unplug the toaster before you take out the bread.

Hold the safety hunt checklist in mind and try this part's questions: safe habits at home.

## Subtopic: Safe Outside

Stay away from pylons, substations and fallen lines.

Never touch loose wires. Do not grab a person who is getting a shock.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Mains electricity is strong enough to shock, burn, start fires and kill, and water makes it more dangerous. At home: keep hands dry, do not overload multiplugs, check cords, and unplug appliances before touching inside them. Outside: never climb pylons or fly kites near lines, stay away from substations, fallen lines and illegal connections. To help someone getting a shock, switch off the power first.
