### subtopic_1 — Energy Stored in Cells
**Assistant:** Why does the electricity need to go all the way round the circuit?

**Tutor:** Think of a circuit like a bicycle chain. The pedals push the chain, and the chain turns the back wheel. The chain only works if it is a complete loop. If you cut the chain, pushing the pedals does nothing to the wheel. In a circuit, tiny particles in the metal wire are pushed along by the cell, like the links of a chain. The cell is like the pedals: it pushes from one terminal, and the particles move all the way round the loop, through the bulb, and back to the other terminal. As they pass through the thin wire inside the bulb, they transfer energy to it, which makes it glow. If there is a gap anywhere, the particles cannot move round, so nothing flows, and no energy reaches the bulb. That is why one wire is never enough. The cell does not send electricity out like water from a tap. It pushes it round a loop, and the loop must be complete.

### subtopic_2 — Making a Circuit
**Assistant:** Why does joining two cells make a torch brighter?

**Tutor:** Each cell gives a push to the electricity in the circuit. A torch cell gives a push of about one and a half volts. A volt is a measure of how strongly a cell pushes. When you join two cells end to end, positive to negative, their pushes add together, giving about three volts. The stronger push makes more electricity flow through the bulb each second, so more energy is transferred to it, and it glows more brightly. But be careful. Every bulb is made for a certain push. If you join too many cells to a small bulb, the push is too strong, the thin wire inside gets too hot and melts, and the bulb blows. Also, if one cell is put in the wrong way round, its push works against the other one, and the torch may hardly light at all. That is why torches and remote controls have little pictures inside, showing which way each cell must go.

### subtopic_3 — Energy on the Move
**Assistant:** Is it safe to play with the electricity from cells?

**Tutor:** The small cells we use in class, like torch cells, give such a gentle push that you cannot feel the electricity at all, so it is safe to build circuits with them, as long as you follow a few rules. Never join the two terminals of a cell directly with a bare wire and nothing else in the circuit. This is called a short circuit, and it makes the cell and wire get very hot, which can burn your fingers and damage the cell. Never cut open, crush or heat a cell, because the chemicals inside can leak, burn your skin or even catch fire. Small button cells are very dangerous if swallowed, so keep them away from little children. And remember that the electricity from the plug points in your home, called mains electricity, is completely different. It pushes far more strongly and can kill. Never experiment with plugs, sockets or electrical appliances. Only build circuits with small cells, in class or with an adult.
