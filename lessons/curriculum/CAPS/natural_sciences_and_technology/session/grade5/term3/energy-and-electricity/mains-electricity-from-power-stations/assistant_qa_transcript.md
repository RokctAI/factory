### subtopic_1 — Inside a Power Station
**Assistant:** How does spinning a magnet make electricity?

**Tutor:** This is one of the most important discoveries in science. About two hundred years ago, a scientist named Michael Faraday found that when a magnet moves near a coil of wire, electricity flows in the wire. It does not matter whether the magnet moves or the coil moves, as long as one moves past the other. The faster the movement and the stronger the magnet, the more electricity is made. A generator uses this idea. Inside it, huge, powerful magnets spin around very fast, surrounded by enormous coils of copper wire. As the magnets sweep past the coils, they push electricity through the wire, over and over, thousands of times a minute. That is why the turbine is needed: it provides the spinning movement. The same idea is used on a small scale in a bicycle dynamo, which uses the turning wheel to light a bicycle lamp. So a power station is really just a very big machine for spinning magnets.

### subtopic_2 — Along the Power Lines
**Assistant:** Why is electricity sent along power lines at such a high voltage?

**Tutor:** When electricity flows through a wire, some of its energy is lost as heat in the wire. The more electricity flowing, the more energy is wasted. Engineers found a clever trick. They can deliver the same amount of energy with a much smaller flow of electricity if they push it much harder, at a very high voltage. A smaller flow means much less is wasted as heat in the long transmission lines, which can be hundreds of kilometres long. So transformers at the power station raise the voltage to very high levels before the electricity sets off on its journey. When it reaches a town, other transformers lower the voltage step by step, until it reaches our homes at a level that our appliances are designed for. The downside is that these high-voltage lines are extremely dangerous. Electricity at that voltage can even jump through the air to a person or a kite string that comes too close. That is why we must never climb pylons or fly kites near power lines.

### subtopic_3 — A Giant Circuit
**Assistant:** Why does South Africa have load shedding?

**Tutor:** Load shedding happens when the power stations cannot make as much electricity as people want to use at the same time. Electricity cannot easily be stored in large amounts, so it must be made at the very moment it is used. If people try to use more than is being made, the whole national grid could become unstable and shut down everywhere, which would take days to fix. To stop this, the power company switches off electricity to different areas in turn, for a few hours at a time, to reduce the load. In South Africa, this has happened for several reasons. Many of the coal-fired power stations are old and break down often. New power stations took much longer to build than planned. And the number of people and businesses using electricity has grown. The country is now adding more solar and wind power, and many homes and businesses have installed solar panels. Saving electricity at peak times, early in the morning and in the evening, also helps.
