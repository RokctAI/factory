### subtopic_1 — Fuels, Burning and Fire Safety
**Assistant:** How is food a fuel if we do not burn it?

**Tutor:** That is a very good question, because the word fuel makes most of us think of flames. A fuel is anything that stores energy that can be released. Coal, wood and paraffin release their energy quickly when they burn, with a flame, giving heat and light all at once. Food releases its energy slowly, inside our bodies, without any flame. Our bodies break down the food step by step and use oxygen from the air we breathe to release its energy, a little at a time. That is why we breathe faster when we run: our bodies need more oxygen to release more energy from our food. Some of that energy keeps us warm, which is why our bodies feel warm even on a cold day, and some lets our muscles move. So the bread roll and the lump of coal have something in common: both store energy that first came from the Sun, and both need oxygen to release it. The difference is only how fast, and whether there is a flame.

### subtopic_2 — Cells, Circuits and Mains Electricity
**Assistant:** Why can I touch a torch cell safely but not a plug socket?

**Tutor:** Because the push of the electricity is very different. A torch cell gives a push of about one and a half volts. That is far too weak to push electricity through your skin in a harmful way, so you can hold a cell, and even the ends of a small battery, without being hurt. Mains electricity in a South African home has a push of about two hundred and thirty volts, more than a hundred times stronger. That push is strong enough to drive electricity through your body, especially if your skin is wet, and it can stop your heart, burn you badly or kill you. Overhead power lines are even more dangerous, because their voltages are much higher still. So the rule is simple: torch cells are safe for experiments, but mains electricity is never for playing with. Never open a plug, push anything into a socket, or use electrical appliances with wet hands.

### subtopic_3 — Elastic, Springs and Movement
**Assistant:** Is the energy in a spring the same as the energy in a fuel?

**Tutor:** Both are stored energy, which is the big idea that ties Term 3 together, but they are stored in different ways. In a fuel, the energy is locked inside the material itself, and we release it by burning, which changes the fuel into ash, smoke and gases. Once a fuel is burnt, it is used up. In a spring or a rubber band, the energy is stored because you changed its shape: you squashed the spring or stretched the band. When you let it go, it springs back to its normal shape and the stored energy becomes movement. The spring is not used up, so you can squash it and release it again and again. But notice where the energy in the spring came from: your arm pushed it, and your arm got its energy from food. So even a spring's energy can be traced back to food, to plants and to the Sun. Energy is never made from nothing; it is only stored and changed from one form into another.
