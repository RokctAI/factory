# Part 1 — Expert

This session revises the Term 3 topics of Energy and Change: stored energy in fuels; energy and electricity; and energy and movement. The anchor is a revision lesson in a Grade 5 classroom in Mthatha in the Eastern Cape, set up as an energy trail in the school hall. There are three tables. The fuel table holds a lump of coal, a piece of wood, a candle, a small bottle labelled paraffin, a picture of a petrol pump, a gas bottle and a bread roll. The electricity table holds torch cells, a bulb in a holder, wires, a switch, a plug and a picture of a power station. The springs and elastic table holds rubber bands, a ballpoint pen spring, a wind-up toy, a catapult made of a forked stick and a cotton-reel tractor. Learners move from table to table in groups, and at each one they must answer three questions: where is the energy stored, how is it released, and how do we stay safe? The big idea is that energy can be stored, and when it is released it can be changed into useful heat, light, electrical energy or movement. The whole of Term 3 is examinable.

## Subtopic: Fuels, Burning and Fire Safety

At the fuel table, the group starts with a definition. A fuel is a material that stores energy, which is released when the fuel is burnt. Everyday fuels include coal, wood, petrol, diesel, paraffin, gas and candle wax. Food is a fuel too, for our bodies: we do not set it alight, but our bodies release its stored energy slowly, so we can move, grow and keep warm. The bread roll on the table is a fuel for a learner, just as the coal is a fuel for a power station.

Most of the energy in fuels first came from the Sun. Green plants use sunlight to make their food, and that stored energy passes to animals that eat them. Wood comes from trees. Coal formed over millions of years from the remains of plants buried in swamps. Petrol, diesel, paraffin and gas come from oil, which formed from the remains of tiny sea plants and animals.

When a fuel burns, it gives us useful heat and light. Heat cooks food, warms homes and boils water; light lets us see at night. To start burning, a fuel needs heat to ignite it, like the small flame of a match. To keep burning, it needs oxygen from the air. A big log will not catch from one match; we start with small, dry kindling. Take away the fuel, the heat or the oxygen, and the fire goes out. Burning also makes smoke and gases, which can harm our lungs, so we burn fuels where there is fresh air.

Then the group turns to safety. Fires in homes are often caused by candles, paraffin stoves, open fires and overloaded plugs. Stand candles in a tin of sand, keep stoves steady and away from curtains, never fill a paraffin stove while it burns, store paraffin in labelled, child-proof containers and never in a cooldrink bottle, and keep matches and lighters out of reach of small children. If there is a fire, shout to warn others, get out, crawl low under smoke, never go back inside, and call 10177, or 112 from a cell phone. If your clothes catch fire: stop, drop and roll.

The questions for this section are with you now: fuels, what fuels need to burn, and fire safety.

## Subtopic: Cells, Circuits and Mains Electricity

At the electricity table, the group explains that energy can be stored in cells. A torch cell stores energy and gives a push of about one and a half volts. Two or more cells joined together make a battery. A cell on its own does nothing; it releases its energy only when it is part of a complete circuit.

A circuit is a complete path that electricity can flow around: from one end of the cell, along a wire, through the bulb, and back along another wire to the other end of the cell. The circuit transfers electrical energy from the cell to the bulb, and the bulb changes it into light and heat. If there is a gap anywhere, the bulb goes out. A switch makes and breaks the circuit: closed means on, open means off. The switch does not make electricity; it only opens or closes the path.

Then the group points to the picture of the power station. Mains electricity, the electricity in our plugs, comes from power stations. Most of South Africa's power stations burn coal, many of them in Mpumalanga. Burning coal boils water into steam, the steam spins a turbine, and the turbine turns a generator, which changes movement energy into electrical energy. Transmission lines on tall pylons carry the electricity across the country, and transformers lower the voltage before it reaches our homes. Mains electricity travels in a giant circuit: out from the power station to our homes along one wire, and back along another. Some electricity also comes from wind, the Sun, falling water and a nuclear power station near Cape Town.

Mains electricity is much stronger than a torch cell and can shock, burn and kill. Keep hands dry, never push anything into a plug socket, do not overload multiplugs, pull a plug out by the plug and not by the cord, and stay far away from fallen power lines and pylons. Never touch a person who is getting a shock; switch off the power if it is safe, push them away from the wire with something dry and non-metal, like a wooden broom handle, and call 10177 or 112.

The questions for this section are with you now: cells and circuits, mains electricity and electrical safety.

## Subtopic: Elastic, Springs and Movement

At the last table, the group shows that elastic and springs can store energy too. When you stretch a rubber band, or twist it, it stores energy. When you compress, or squash, a spring, it stores energy. Some springs store energy when they are stretched as well. The more you stretch, twist or squash, the more energy is stored, up to a limit: a band stretched too far snaps, and a spring pulled too far stays bent. An unstretched band stores no energy.

When the elastic or spring is released, the stored energy is changed into movement energy. A catapult throws a paper ball when the stretched band is let go. The spring in a ballpoint pen pushes the tip back in. A wind-up toy walks as its spring unwinds. The cotton-reel tractor crawls along the desk as its twisted band untwists, and the more twists you give it, the further it goes. Not all the stored energy becomes movement; some becomes sound and heat.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking food is not a fuel; food stores energy for our bodies. Two: thinking a fire only needs fuel; it also needs heat to start and oxygen to keep burning. Three: thinking a switch makes electricity; it only opens and closes the circuit. Four: thinking an unstretched band stores energy; it stores energy only when it is stretched or twisted.

At the end of the trail, the teacher asks one last question: where did the energy in the cotton-reel tractor really come from? The groups work backwards: from the twisted band, to the learner's arm, to the learner's breakfast, to the plants that grew the food, and finally to the Sun.

The questions for this section are with you now: elastic, springs and movement energy.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the Mthatha energy trail — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Fuel and Fire

A fuel stores energy. Burn it to get heat and light.

Fire needs fuel, heat and oxygen.

Candles in sand. Get out, stay low, call 10177.

Keep the candle and the coal in mind and try the questions for this part: fuels and fire.

## Subtopic: Electricity

Cells store energy. A full circuit lights the bulb.

Power stations burn coal to make mains electricity.

Dry hands, safe plugs, far from power lines.

Hold the bulb and the wires in mind and try this part's questions: electricity.

## Subtopic: Springs and Elastic

Stretch, twist or squash to store energy.

Let go, and it moves.

Pocket summary of the term. Fuels such as coal, wood, paraffin, gas, candle wax and food store energy. Burning releases heat and light, and a fire needs fuel, heat and oxygen. Cells store energy, and a complete circuit transfers electrical energy to a bulb. Coal-fired power stations make mains electricity, which travels in a circuit to our homes and back. Fire and electricity must be used safely. Stretched or twisted elastic and compressed springs store energy, which becomes movement when they are released.

Hold the whole energy trail in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: elastic, springs and movement.
