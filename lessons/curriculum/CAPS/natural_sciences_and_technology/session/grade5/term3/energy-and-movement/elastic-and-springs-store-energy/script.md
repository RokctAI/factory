# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers how stretched or twisted elastic, and compressed springs, store energy. The anchor is a Grade 5 classroom in Klerksdorp in North West. The teacher tips a box of springy things onto the front desk: a handful of elastic bands, a clicky ballpoint pen, a wooden clothes peg with a metal spring, a stapler, a big coil spring from an old mattress, a hair tie, a toy jack-in-the-box and an old wind-up alarm clock. She says: every one of these can hold energy without a battery or a fuel. Let us find out how. The learners stretch, twist and squash each object and describe what they feel. The big idea is that elastic materials and springs store energy when we change their shape by stretching, twisting or squashing them, and that this energy comes from the work our muscles do. Explaining how elastic and springs store energy is examinable.

## Subtopic: Stretchy Materials

Some materials can be stretched, twisted or squashed, and then spring back to their original shape when they are let go. These materials are called elastic.

Rubber is very elastic. An elastic band can be stretched to two or three times its length, and when you let go, it snaps back. A hair tie, the waistband of your tracksuit pants, and a balloon are also elastic. Some materials are only a little elastic, like a plastic ruler that bends and springs back. Other materials, like play dough, clay and plasticine, are not elastic. If you stretch them, they stay stretched.

When you stretch an elastic band, you can feel it pulling back against your fingers. You have to use force, a push or a pull, to change its shape. Your muscles are doing work, using energy from the food you ate. Some of that energy is now stored in the stretched elastic band, as long as you keep holding it. This is called stored energy, or elastic energy.

The more you stretch the band, the harder it pulls back, and the more energy it stores. But there is a limit. If you stretch it too far, it snaps, and if you keep it stretched for a very long time, or it gets old and dry, it loses some of its springiness.

Twisting also stores energy in elastic. If you hold a long elastic band at both ends and twist one end round and round, it gets tighter and tighter, and you can feel it trying to untwist. The more twists, the more energy is stored.

The questions for this section are with you now: elastic materials, and how stretching and twisting elastic stores energy.

## Subtopic: Springs

A spring is usually a coil of metal wire, made of a special steel that is very elastic. A spring can be squashed shorter, which is called compressing it, or pulled longer, which is called stretching it. Either way, it springs back to its original shape when let go.

The learners press down on the big mattress spring. They feel it pushing back up against their hands. The harder they press and the more they compress it, the harder it pushes back. A compressed spring stores energy, just like a stretched elastic band.

Springs are hidden inside many everyday objects. In a clicky pen, a small spring is compressed when you click the pen open, and it pushes the point back in when you click again. A clothes peg has a spring that is twisted when you squeeze the peg open, and its stored energy grips the washing on the line. A stapler has a spring that pushes the staples forward and lifts the top back up after you press it. A mattress and a sofa have dozens of springs that are compressed when you sit or lie on them, and they spread your weight and push back. Cars and minibus taxis have big springs, called suspension springs, that are compressed when the wheels hit a bump, so passengers feel a smoother ride. Trampolines have springs around the edge that stretch when you land.

The wind-up clock has a long, flat strip of steel wound into a tight spiral, called a mainspring. When you turn the key, you wind it tighter and tighter, storing energy.

The questions for this section are with you now: what a spring is, compressing and stretching springs, and springs in everyday objects.

## Subtopic: More Stretch, More Energy

The class does an investigation to find out how the amount of stretch affects the stored energy.

They hook an elastic band over the end of a ruler and stretch it back to different marks: five centimetres, ten centimetres and fifteen centimetres. Each time, they let go and measure how far the band flies across the hall floor. To make it a fair test, they use the same band, hold the ruler at the same height and aim in the same direction. They only change how far they stretch the band. Safety first: no one stands in front of the ruler, and they never aim at people.

The results are clear. Stretched five centimetres, the band flies about one metre. Stretched ten centimetres, it flies about three metres. Stretched fifteen centimetres, it flies much further. The more the band was stretched, the more energy it stored, and the further it flew.

The same is true for springs. The more you compress the spring in a jack-in-the-box, the higher the clown jumps out.

Where did the stored energy come from? From the person who did the stretching, twisting or squashing. Our muscles get their energy from food, and food got its energy from plants, which got it from the Sun.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking an elastic band has energy on its own; it only stores energy when it is stretched or twisted. Two: thinking springs can only be squashed; springs can be compressed or stretched. Three: thinking play dough is elastic; it stays stretched and does not spring back. Four: thinking stretching further always stores more energy; past its limit, the band snaps.

The questions for this section are with you now: the stretching investigation, fair testing, and where the stored energy comes from.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the box of springy things — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Stretch and Twist

Elastic things stretch and spring back, like elastic bands.

A stretched or twisted elastic band stores energy.

Keep the elastic band in your fingers in mind and try the questions for this part: elastic materials.

## Subtopic: Squash a Spring

A spring is a coil of steel wire.

Squash it or stretch it, and it stores energy.

Pens, pegs, staplers, mattresses and car wheels all use springs.

Hold the mattress spring in mind and try this part's questions: springs.

## Subtopic: The More, the Better

More stretch or squash means more stored energy.

The energy comes from our muscles.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Elastic materials stretch, twist or squash and then spring back. Stretched or twisted elastic stores energy, and so do compressed or stretched springs. The more an elastic band is stretched or a spring is compressed, the more energy it stores, up to its limit. The energy comes from the muscles that did the stretching or squashing.

Hold the flying elastic band in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: more stretch, more energy.
