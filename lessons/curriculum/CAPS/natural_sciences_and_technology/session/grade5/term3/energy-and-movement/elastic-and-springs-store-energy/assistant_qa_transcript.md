### subtopic_1 — Stretchy Materials
**Assistant:** Why does an elastic band get warm when I stretch it quickly?

**Tutor:** Try it carefully: stretch an elastic band quickly and touch it to your lip or the inside of your wrist. It feels slightly warm. Rubber is made of very long, tangled chains of tiny particles, a bit like a bowl of cooked spaghetti. When you stretch the band, the chains are pulled straighter and lined up. Some of the energy your muscles put in is stored in the stretched chains, ready to snap them back, but some of it is changed into heat as the chains rub and move past each other. When you let the band go back slowly, you may even feel it get a little cooler. This shows that when energy is stored or released, not all of it ends up where we want it. Some is always changed into heat. The same happens with springs that are squashed and released over and over, like the suspension springs on a taxi: they slowly warm up a little. Every time energy is transferred, a bit is lost as heat.

### subtopic_2 — Springs
**Assistant:** Why do springs eventually stop working?

**Tutor:** A spring works because its steel bends a little and then springs back. As long as it is only bent within its limit, it can do this millions of times. But if a spring is stretched or squashed too far, beyond its limit, the steel bends permanently. You may have seen this with a clicky pen spring that has been pulled out: it stays long and floppy and will not spring back properly. Springs can also get tired. After being squashed and released a huge number of times, tiny cracks can form in the metal, which slowly grow until the spring breaks. That is called metal fatigue. Rust can weaken springs too, which is why springs in cars are coated or painted. An old mattress sags because many of its springs have become weaker after years of people lying on them. Elastic bands also wear out, because the rubber slowly reacts with the air and sunlight, becoming dry, cracked and less stretchy.

### subtopic_3 — More Stretch, More Energy
**Assistant:** How does a wind-up toy keep moving for so long?

**Tutor:** Inside a wind-up toy or clock is a long, thin strip of springy steel coiled into a flat spiral, called a mainspring. When you turn the key, you wind the spring tighter and tighter around its centre. Your muscles do the work, and the energy is stored in the tightly wound spring. If the spring let go all at once, the toy would just spin wildly for a second. So the toy has a set of small gear wheels that control how fast the spring is allowed to unwind. The gears let the energy out a little at a time, turning the wheels or legs of the toy slowly and steadily. In a wind-up clock, a special part lets the spring unwind just one tiny step at a time, which is the ticking you hear, so the clock can run for a whole day or even a week on one winding. When the spring has unwound completely, all the stored energy has been used, and you must wind it up again.
