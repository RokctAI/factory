# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers fire safety in our communities: what causes fires, how to prevent them, and what to do if a fire breaks out or someone is burnt. The anchor is a visit by two firefighters, Captain Mokoena and Firefighter Daniels, to a primary school in Alexandra, a busy township in Johannesburg. They park their red fire engine on the playground, show the Grade 5 class their protective suits, helmets and breathing masks, and let them hold a fire hose. Then they sit down with the class and talk seriously. In winter, they say, they are called out to house fires and shack fires almost every day, and many could have been prevented. The big idea is that fuels are very useful, but fire is dangerous, so we must use fuels safely, know how to prevent fires, and know what to do if one starts. Fire safety rules and actions are examinable.

## Subtopic: How Fires Start

Captain Mokoena explains that most fires in homes start with things people use every day.

Paraffin stoves and heaters can be knocked over, or can flare up if they are filled while still burning, and the burning paraffin spreads quickly. Candles left burning near curtains, on a bed, or when everyone is asleep can fall over or set fabric alight. Open fires and coal braziers can send out sparks and are sometimes left burning unattended. Matches and lighters in the hands of small children can start fires by accident. Faulty electrical wiring, overloaded multiplugs and illegal electricity connections can overheat and start fires. Cooking oil left on a hot stove can catch fire.

Fires spread fastest where homes are built close together from materials that burn easily, such as wood, cardboard and plastic, and where there are strong winds.

Fires also start outdoors. Veld fires spread across grassland, especially in the dry winter on the highveld, when the grass is brown and dry. In the Western Cape, fynbos fires happen in the hot, dry, windy summers. Some are started by lightning, but many are started by people burning rubbish, careless cigarettes, or fires that get out of control.

Remember the fire triangle: fuel, heat and oxygen. Every one of these causes brings heat close to something that can burn, with plenty of air around it.

## Subtopic: Preventing Fires

Firefighter Daniels says that the best way to fight a fire is to stop it from starting. She gives the class a list of fire safety rules to take home.

Stand paraffin stoves and heaters on a flat, firm surface, away from curtains, bedding and paths where people walk. Never fill a paraffin stove while it is burning or still hot.

Put candles in a stable holder, such as a candle stuck in a tin of sand, away from anything that can burn. Never leave a candle burning when you leave the room or go to sleep.

Keep matches and lighters high up, out of reach of young children.

Never leave cooking unattended on the stove.

Keep paraffin in a properly labelled container with a child-proof lid, never in a cooldrink bottle. Every year, many small children in South Africa drink paraffin by mistake because it was stored in a cooldrink bottle.

Do not overload multiplugs, and never use illegal electricity connections.

Put out fires completely before you leave them. Never burn rubbish on windy days.

Farmers make firebreaks, which are strips of land cleared of grass, so that a veld fire cannot cross them, because there is no fuel left.

A smoke alarm on the ceiling makes a loud beeping noise when it senses smoke, and can wake a family up in time to escape.

## Subtopic: If There Is a Fire

If a fire does start, Captain Mokoena says, your life is more important than anything you own.

Shout Fire! loudly to warn everyone. Get out quickly, and help younger children out. Do not stop to collect things, and never go back inside a burning building. Smoke rises, so the cleanest air is near the floor. If there is smoke, get down low and crawl to the exit. Before opening a closed door, feel it with the back of your hand. If it is hot, there may be fire behind it, so use another way out. Once you are outside, stay out, and meet your family at a safe place you have agreed on.

Call for help. In South Africa, you can call 10177 for the fire brigade and ambulance, or 112 from any cell phone.

If your clothes catch fire, do not run, because running brings more oxygen and makes the flames bigger. Instead: stop, drop to the ground, cover your face with your hands, and roll over and over to smother the flames.

A small fire in a pot of oil on the stove must never be put out with water, because the water makes the burning oil splash and spread. Turn off the stove if it is safe to do so, and cover the pot with a lid or a damp cloth to cut off the oxygen. Small fires can also be smothered with sand.

If someone is burnt, cool the burn under cool, gently running water for about twenty minutes. Do not put butter, oil, toothpaste or ice on a burn. Cover it loosely with clean plastic cling wrap or a clean cloth, and get medical help for anything more than a small burn.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: running when your clothes are on fire; running fans the flames, so stop, drop and roll. Two: throwing water on burning oil; it makes the fire spread, so cover it with a lid. Three: going back inside for your belongings; never go back into a burning building. Four: putting butter on a burn; cool it under running water for about twenty minutes.

The questions for this section are with you now: escaping a fire, calling for help, stop, drop and roll, and first aid for burns.

We prevent fires by keeping fuels and flames safe and never leaving them unattended. If there is a fire, get out, stay low under smoke, never go back, and call 10177 or 112.
