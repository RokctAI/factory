### subtopic_1 — Heat and Light from Burning
**Assistant:** Why does a candle flame need a wick?

**Tutor:** Candle wax is a fuel, but solid wax cannot burn directly. The wax has to become a gas before it can burn. That is the job of the wick. When you light the wick, the heat of the flame melts a little pool of wax at the top of the candle. The liquid wax soaks up into the wick, the same way water soaks up into a paper towel. Near the flame, the heat turns the liquid wax into wax vapour, which is a gas. It is this wax vapour that actually burns, mixing with oxygen in the air to make the flame. As the wax is used up, more melts and soaks up the wick, so the candle keeps burning steadily. If you blow out a candle, you can see a thin trail of white smoke. That is wax vapour that did not burn. If you quickly hold a lit match in that trail, the flame can jump down it and relight the wick. Never try that without an adult, though.

### subtopic_2 — Heat to Get Started
**Assistant:** Why does fanning a fire make it bigger, but blowing out a candle puts it out?

**Tutor:** Both are about the fire triangle, but they affect different sides. When you gently fan glowing coals or a camp fire, you push fresh air into the fire. That brings more oxygen, so the fuel burns faster and hotter, and the fire grows. The coals are big and hot, so the moving air does not cool them down much. A candle flame is very small and does not have much heat. When you blow hard on it, the fast-moving air pushes the flame away from the wick and blows away the hot wax vapour that is burning. It also cools the wick below the temperature it needs to keep the wax burning. So you take away the heat and the fuel vapour together, and the flame goes out. If you blow very gently on a candle, it just flickers. And if you blow too hard on a camp fire, you can scatter sparks, which is dangerous. So always be careful with how you add air to a fire.

### subtopic_3 — Oxygen to Keep Burning
**Assistant:** Is it safe to burn coal or paraffin inside a house?

**Tutor:** It can be very dangerous, especially in a closed room. When fuels burn with plenty of oxygen, they mostly produce carbon dioxide and water vapour. But when there is not enough oxygen, for example in a closed room with a brazier or a stove, they also produce a poisonous gas called carbon monoxide. You cannot see it or smell it. When people breathe it in, it stops their blood from carrying oxygen around the body. People can fall asleep and never wake up. Every winter in South Africa, sadly, people die this way, especially when they bring a coal brazier indoors to keep warm at night. Burning also produces smoke, which damages the lungs, especially for babies and old people. If fuels must be burnt indoors, the room must have fresh air coming in, through an open window or a gap, and fires should be put out before people go to sleep. A carbon monoxide alarm can also warn people. Electric heaters, warm blankets and good insulation are safer ways to stay warm.
