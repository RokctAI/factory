# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers what happens when fuels burn. Burning releases the energy stored in a fuel as heat and light, which are useful to us. But a fuel will only start burning if it is heated enough, and it will only keep burning if it has oxygen from the air. The anchor is a school camp in the foothills of the Drakensberg for a Grade 5 class from Bergville in KwaZulu-Natal. In the evening, the mountain air turns cold. The camp leader, Mr Zulu, gathers the learners around a stone-lined fire pit, well away from the tents and the dry grass, with a bucket of water and a bucket of sand ready. He shows them how to build a fire step by step, and asks them to work out why each step matters. Later, back at school, they do a candle and jar experiment. The big idea is that a fire needs three things, fuel, heat and oxygen, and that if we take away any one of them, the fire goes out. Explaining that fuels need heat to ignite and oxygen to keep burning is examinable.

## Subtopic: Heat and Light from Burning

When a fuel burns, the energy stored in it is released as heat and light.

We use the heat from burning fuels to cook food, boil water, warm our homes and bodies, and dry things. Mr Zulu will use the camp fire to cook pap and to keep everyone warm while they tell stories. In factories and power stations, the heat from burning coal is used to make steam and then electricity. In car engines, the heat from burning petrol makes hot gases that push parts of the engine and make the car move.

We use the light from burning fuels to see in the dark. Before electric lights, people used candles, oil lamps and paraffin lamps. Many people still use them today, especially during power cuts, and in homes that do not have electricity. A candle flame gives a small, steady light.

Some fuels give more light than heat, and some give more heat than light. A candle gives useful light but only a little heat. Burning coal in a stove gives lots of heat but only a dull, red glow.

Burning also produces other things. Smoke is made of tiny bits of unburnt fuel and other particles, and it is harmful to breathe. Ash is the powdery material left behind after wood or coal burns. Burning also produces invisible gases, mainly carbon dioxide and water vapour.

So burning fuels is useful, but we must burn them safely and with plenty of fresh air.

The questions for this section are with you now: the heat and light we get from burning fuels, and what else burning produces.

## Subtopic: Heat to Get Started

Mr Zulu holds up a big log. He says: if I hold a match to this log, what will happen? The class guesses correctly: almost nothing. The log is too big and thick to catch fire from one small match.

A fuel must be heated to a high enough temperature before it starts to burn. Starting to burn is called igniting, or catching fire. Different fuels need different amounts of heat to ignite. Paper and dry grass ignite easily. Thin twigs, called kindling, need a bit more heat. Thick logs and coal need a lot of heat to ignite.

So Mr Zulu builds the fire in layers. First, he crumples some dry newspaper at the bottom. On top, he builds a small tent of dry twigs. Then he leans a few thicker sticks around them. The big logs come last, once the fire is going.

To start the fire, he strikes a match. Rubbing the match head on the side of the box causes friction, which makes enough heat to ignite the chemicals in the match head. The burning match ignites the paper. The burning paper heats the twigs until they ignite. The burning twigs heat the thicker sticks, and those heat the logs. Each fuel heats the next one until it is hot enough to catch fire.

Wet wood is very hard to light, because the heat is used up drying the water before the wood can get hot enough to ignite. That is why campers keep their firewood dry.

The questions for this section are with you now: why fuels need heat to ignite, and how to build a fire in layers.

## Subtopic: Oxygen to Keep Burning

The fire is crackling now. Mr Zulu asks: what else does the fire need? He leaves a gap at the bottom of the fire so that air can get in. Air contains oxygen, and fuels need oxygen to keep burning. When he gently blows on the glowing coals, the fire burns brighter, because blowing brings more oxygen.

Back at school, the class tests this. They stand three small candles on a tray of sand and light them. Then, carefully, they place a glass jar upside down over one candle, leaving the others uncovered. After a few seconds, the flame under the jar gets smaller and goes out. The candles in the open air keep burning. The covered candle used up much of the oxygen in the jar until there was too little left, and with no fresh air coming in, it could not keep burning.

So a fire needs three things: fuel to burn, heat to ignite it, and oxygen to keep it burning. Firefighters draw this as the fire triangle, with one of the three on each side. Take away any side, and the fire goes out.

Mr Zulu puts out the camp fire at bedtime. He spreads the logs apart, which takes away some of the fuel. He pours water over it, which cools it down, taking away heat. Then he covers it with sand, which keeps out oxygen. He checks that it is completely cold before anyone leaves.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking a big log lights easily from a match; thick fuels need a lot of heat to ignite. Two: thinking a fire only needs fuel; it also needs heat and oxygen. Three: thinking blowing on a fire puts it out; gently blowing on glowing coals adds oxygen and makes it burn brighter. Four: thinking smoke is harmless; smoke is harmful to breathe.

The questions for this section are with you now: why fires need oxygen, the candle and jar test, and the fire triangle.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the Drakensberg camp fire — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Warm and Bright

Burning a fuel gives heat and light.

We use the heat to cook and keep warm, and the light to see.

Burning also makes smoke, ash and gases.

Keep the cosy camp fire in mind and try the questions for this part: heat and light.

## Subtopic: Get It Hot

A fuel must be heated before it catches fire.

Paper lights easily. Big logs need lots of heat.

Build the fire in layers: paper, twigs, sticks, logs.

Hold the striking match in mind and try this part's questions: heat to ignite.

## Subtopic: Give It Air

Fire needs oxygen from the air to keep burning.

Fuel, heat and oxygen: the fire triangle.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Burning fuels gives us useful heat and light, but also smoke, ash and gases. A fuel must be heated before it ignites, which is why we start fires with paper and kindling. A fire needs oxygen to keep burning. The fire triangle shows that fire needs fuel, heat and oxygen, and taking away any one puts the fire out.

Hold the candle under the jar in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: oxygen and the fire triangle.
