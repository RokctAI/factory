# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers energy stored in fuels. A fuel is a material that has energy stored in it, which can be released, usually by burning. Everyday fuels include coal, wood, petrol, diesel, paraffin, gas and candle wax. Food is also a fuel, for our bodies. The anchor is a Grade 5 class in eMalahleni in Mpumalanga. The town's name means place of coal, and it is surrounded by coal mines and power stations. The teacher asks every learner to bring an example of a fuel from home, safely sealed or wrapped. On the table, they lay out a lump of coal, a few pieces of firewood, a sealed bottle of paraffin with a clear label, a small, closed gas canister for a camping stove, a candle and a packet of peanuts. The big idea is that fuels store energy, that we use this energy every day, and that the energy in most fuels first came from the Sun. Naming fuels and explaining that energy is stored in them is examinable.

## Subtopic: What Is a Fuel?

Energy is what makes things happen. We need energy to move, to cook, to keep warm, to see in the dark and to make machines work. Energy cannot be seen, but we can see what it does.

Energy can be stored. A fuel is a material with energy stored inside it. The energy stays locked up until we release it. With most fuels, we release the energy by burning them. Burning gives us heat and light, which we can use.

The class sorts the fuels on the table.

Solid fuels: coal, wood, charcoal and candle wax. Coal is dug out of mines. Wood is collected from trees. Candle wax is made from substances that come from crude oil.

Liquid fuels: petrol, diesel and paraffin. Petrol and diesel are used in cars, taxis, buses and trucks. Paraffin is used in many South African homes for stoves, heaters and lamps.

Gas fuels: liquid petroleum gas, called LP gas, stored under pressure in metal bottles and canisters. It is used for gas stoves, heaters and braais. Natural gas is piped to some factories.

Each fuel stores a different amount of energy. A kilogram of coal or petrol stores much more energy than a kilogram of wood.

We use the stored energy in fuels for cooking, heating homes and water, lighting, making electricity in power stations, and running vehicles and machines.

The questions for this section are with you now: what a fuel is, examples of solid, liquid and gas fuels, and what fuels are used for.

## Subtopic: Food Is a Fuel

The packet of peanuts surprises the class. How can food be a fuel?

Food is a fuel for living things. It has energy stored in it. Our bodies release this energy slowly, not by burning it in flames, but by using it inside our cells, with the oxygen we breathe. We use the energy from food to move, to grow, to keep warm, to think and to keep our hearts beating.

The amount of energy in food is measured in units called kilojoules. You can find it on the label of most packaged food in South Africa. The peanut packet says that one small packet has about one thousand kilojoules. A bottle of water has zero kilojoules, because water does not contain energy for our bodies.

Foods that are high in fats and oils, such as peanuts, butter and cooking oil, store the most energy. Foods that are high in starch and sugar, such as bread, maize meal, rice and potatoes, also give us lots of energy.

The teacher shows a famous demonstration on a video. A single peanut on a pin is set alight, and it burns with a small, steady flame for about a minute. This shows that the energy really is stored in the peanut.

If we eat more food energy than our bodies use, the body stores the extra as fat. If we eat too little, we feel tired and weak, and children do not grow properly.

The questions for this section are with you now: why food is a fuel, kilojoules, and how our bodies use food energy.

## Subtopic: Where the Energy Came From

Where did the energy in fuels come from? For almost all of them, the answer is the Sun.

Green plants capture energy from sunlight and store it in the food they make. Wood is part of a tree, so the energy in firewood is energy from the Sun that the tree stored while it grew. The energy in peanuts, maize and bread also came from sunlight captured by plants. Even the energy in meat came from plants, because animals eat plants.

Coal, crude oil and natural gas are called fossil fuels. Coal formed from huge swamp forests that grew hundreds of millions of years ago. When the plants died, they were buried under mud and sand, squashed and heated for millions of years until they turned into coal. Much of South Africa's coal formed from plants that grew in swamps in the time before the dinosaurs. Crude oil and gas formed in a similar way from tiny sea plants and animals. So the energy in a lump of coal is ancient sunlight, stored for hundreds of millions of years.

Fossil fuels took millions of years to form, and we are using them up very quickly. Once they are used, they cannot be replaced, so they are called non-renewable. Wood can be regrown by planting new trees, so it can be renewable if we do not cut trees faster than they grow back.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking a fuel is only petrol; coal, wood, paraffin, gas, candle wax and food are all fuels. Two: thinking food is not a fuel because we do not burn it in flames; our bodies release its energy slowly. Three: thinking coal is a kind of rock with no link to living things; coal formed from ancient plants. Four: thinking water is a fuel because we need it; water has no stored energy for burning or for our bodies.

The questions for this section are with you now: how the energy in fuels came from the Sun, fossil fuels, and renewable and non-renewable fuels.

Plants capture energy from the Sun.
