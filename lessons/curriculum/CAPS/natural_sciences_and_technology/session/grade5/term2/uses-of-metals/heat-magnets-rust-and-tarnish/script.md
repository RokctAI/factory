# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers more properties of metals: metals conduct heat; some metals are magnetic; iron rusts; and some other metals tarnish. The anchor is a Grade 5 class in Mossel Bay, a harbour town on the Garden Route. On a walk along the seafront, they notice that the old steel railings are flaking with orange-brown rust, and so is an old anchor on display, while a bronze statue nearby has turned green. Back in the classroom, the teacher sets up three tests: spoons of different materials standing in a mug of hot water, a fridge magnet and a tray of metal objects, and a black, tarnished silver teaspoon from a learner's grandmother. The big idea is that these properties explain how we use metals and how we must look after them. Explaining heat conduction, magnetism, rusting and tarnishing in metals is examinable.

## Subtopic: Metals Conduct Heat

When something hot touches something cooler, heat moves from the hot thing to the cooler thing. A material that lets heat move through it easily is called a good conductor of heat.

Metals are good conductors of heat. In the test, the teacher stands a metal teaspoon, a plastic teaspoon and a wooden spoon in a mug of hot water. After two minutes, the learners carefully touch the tops. The metal spoon is hot right up to the handle. The plastic and wooden spoons are only slightly warm. Heat travelled quickly up the metal.

This is why pots, pans, kettles, irons and the plates on a stove are made of metal. The metal carries heat from the stove or flame quickly to the food or water. Copper and aluminium are especially good conductors, so some pots have a copper or aluminium base.

Materials that do not let heat move through them easily are called insulators. Wood, plastic, cloth, rubber, cork and air are insulators. Pot handles are often made of wood or plastic, and we use cloth oven gloves, so that we do not burn our hands.

Metals are also good conductors of electricity. That is why electrical wires are made of copper. Electricity flows easily along the metal.

Be careful. Because metals conduct heat so well, a metal spoon left in a pot on the stove, or a metal slide in the hot midday sun, can burn your skin.

The questions for this section are with you now: conductors and insulators of heat, and why pots are metal and handles are not.

## Subtopic: Some Metals Are Magnetic

A magnet pulls, or attracts, some materials towards it. A material that a magnet attracts is called magnetic.

The class tests a tray of objects with a fridge magnet. A steel paper clip jumps to the magnet. So do an iron nail, a steel bottle cap and a pair of steel scissors. But an aluminium cooldrink can, a copper wire, a brass key, a gold-coloured ring and a silver teaspoon do not move at all. Neither do the plastic ruler, the eraser or the wooden pencil.

So not all metals are magnetic. Only a few metals are attracted to a magnet. The most common one is iron, and steel, which is mostly iron. Two other magnetic metals are nickel and cobalt.

Some coins stick to a magnet and some do not, because some are made of steel coated with another metal, and others are made of non-magnetic metals.

This property is very useful. At a scrapyard, a giant electromagnet on a crane lifts old cars and steel scrap, and lets go when the electricity is switched off. At recycling centres, magnets are used to separate steel food tins from aluminium cooldrink cans. Magnets are also used in fridge doors, speakers, electric motors and some cupboard catches.

The questions for this section are with you now: which metals are magnetic, and how magnets are used.

## Subtopic: Rusting and Tarnishing

Back on the seafront, the railings and the anchor were covered in orange-brown, flaky rust.

Rust forms when iron, or steel, which is mostly iron, is in contact with both water and the oxygen in the air for a long time. Only iron and steel rust. Rust is weak and crumbly. It flakes off, showing fresh metal underneath that then rusts too, so rust can eat right through a car body, a gate or a roof. Salty water and salty sea air make rusting happen faster, which is why things rust so quickly in coastal towns like Mossel Bay.

We can stop or slow down rusting by keeping water and air away from the iron. We can paint it, cover it in oil or grease, coat it with plastic, or cover it with a thin layer of another metal called zinc. Corrugated iron roofs are steel sheets coated in zinc, which is called galvanising. Stainless steel, used for sinks and cutlery, has other metals mixed in so that it does not rust easily.

Other metals do not rust, but some of them tarnish. Tarnishing means the surface of the metal slowly becomes dull or changes colour, because the metal reacts with substances in the air. Silver turns dark grey or black, like the grandmother's teaspoon. Copper turns dark brown, and after many years outdoors, copper and bronze turn green, like the statue. Tarnish only affects a thin layer on the surface, and it can usually be polished off. The class polishes the silver teaspoon with silver polish, and it shines again. Gold does not tarnish at all.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking all metals are magnetic; only a few, like iron, steel, nickel and cobalt, are. Two: thinking all metals rust; only iron and steel rust. Three: thinking tarnish is the same as rust; tarnish is a thin dull layer on other metals that can be polished off. Four: thinking metal handles are best for pots; metals conduct heat, so the handle would burn your hand.

The questions for this section are with you now: rusting, preventing rust, and tarnishing.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the Mossel Bay seafront — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Hot Spoons

Metals let heat through quickly. They are good conductors.

Wood and plastic do not. They are insulators.

Pots are metal. Handles are wood or plastic.

Keep the hot metal spoon in mind and try the questions for this part: conducting heat.

## Subtopic: Sticks or Not?

Magnets attract iron, steel, nickel and cobalt.

Aluminium, copper, gold and silver are not magnetic.

Hold the jumping paper clip in mind and try this part's questions: magnetic metals.

## Subtopic: Rust and Tarnish

Only iron and steel rust. Water and air cause it. Paint and oil stop it.

Silver and copper tarnish. Polish makes them shine again.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Metals are good conductors of heat and electricity, so pots are metal and handles are insulators. Only some metals are magnetic: iron, steel, nickel and cobalt. Only iron and steel rust, when water and air reach them, and we stop rust with paint, oil or zinc. Some other metals, like silver and copper, tarnish, which can be polished off.

Hold the orange railings and the green statue in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: rust and tarnish.
