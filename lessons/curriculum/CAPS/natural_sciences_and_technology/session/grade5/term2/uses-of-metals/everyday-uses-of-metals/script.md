# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the everyday uses of metals, and how the properties of a metal make it suitable for a particular job. Metals are used for coins, wire, jewellery, furniture, buildings, bridges, cars, kitchen utensils and roofs. The anchor is a visit by a Grade 5 class in Welkom to a scrap metal recycling yard. Huge piles of old metal lie everywhere: rusty car bodies, sheets of corrugated roofing, old school chairs, broken bicycles, copper pipes, coils of wire, dented pots and bags of crushed cooldrink cans. The manager shows them how each kind of metal is sorted, weighed and sent off to be melted down and made into new things. The learners make a chart of every object they see, the metal it is made of, and why that metal was chosen. The big idea is that we choose a metal for a job because its properties suit that job. Matching the uses of metals to their properties is examinable.

## Subtopic: Metals at Home

Look around a home and you will find metals everywhere.

In the kitchen, pots, pans, kettles and cutlery are made of metal, usually stainless steel or aluminium. These are called utensils. Metals are chosen because they conduct heat well, they are strong and hard so they do not break when dropped, they have high melting points so they do not melt on the stove, and stainless steel does not rust easily when it is washed.

Furniture often has metal parts. School chairs and desks have steel frames, which are strong enough to carry the weight of a learner for many years. Garden chairs may be made of aluminium, which is light and does not rust, so it can stay outside. Beds may have steel springs, and doors have steel or brass hinges and handles.

Jewellery is made of gold, silver and platinum. These metals are chosen because they are shiny and beautiful, do not rust, and are malleable, so jewellers can shape them into fine designs. South Africa produces much of the world's platinum, which is used for jewellery and also in car parts.

Coins are made of metal because metal is hard and strong, so coins last for many years, even when they are handled every day, dropped and rattled in pockets. Metal can also be stamped with a clear design. Some South African coins are made of steel coated with a thin layer of another metal, and others are made of mixtures of metals.

The questions for this section are with you now: metals used for utensils, furniture, jewellery and coins, and why.

## Subtopic: Metals in Wires and Roofs

The scrapyard has a whole section for wire.

Copper is used for electrical wire because it is ductile, so it can be drawn into thin wire, and it conducts electricity very well. That is why stolen copper cables are a serious problem in South Africa: when thieves cut cables, trains stop and whole neighbourhoods lose their electricity.

Steel wire is used for fences, washing lines, coat hangers and the strong cables that hold up lifts. It is strong and ductile. Barbed wire and the wire in diamond mesh fences are made of galvanised steel, coated with zinc so that they do not rust quickly.

Roofs on many South African homes, schools and farm buildings are made of corrugated steel sheets. The steel is strong, and because it is malleable, it can be pressed into waves, which makes thin sheets much stiffer. The sheets are galvanised or painted so they do not rust in the rain. Metal roofs are also light compared with tiles, so the roof frame does not have to be as heavy. On the down side, metal roofs conduct heat, so a building under a bare metal roof can get very hot in summer. Ceilings and insulation under the roof help keep the heat out.

Gutters, downpipes and water tanks can also be made of metal.

The questions for this section are with you now: copper and steel wire, and metal roofs.

## Subtopic: Metals in Big Structures and Cars

The biggest pile in the scrapyard is old car bodies.

Cars are made mostly of steel, because it is strong and malleable. Flat sheets of steel are pressed into the curved shapes of doors, roofs and bonnets. Aluminium is used for the wheels and engine parts of many cars, because it is lighter, which saves fuel. The engine block must have a high melting point, because burning fuel inside it makes it very hot.

Buildings use metals in many places. Steel beams and columns form the frames of tall buildings. Steel rods called reinforcing bars are placed inside concrete before it sets, to make concrete floors, pillars and bridges much stronger. Window frames are often made of aluminium, which does not rust and is light.

Bridges are made with steel because steel is very strong and can carry heavy loads, like trucks and trains, without bending. Steel cables can hold up the deck of a bridge. Railway lines are steel too, because they must be hard enough not to wear away under heavy steel wheels and strong enough to carry long trains full of coal and iron ore. Electricity pylons, water towers and the tall cranes on building sites are made of steel for the same reasons.

After their use, metals can be recycled. They are melted down and made into new things, again and again, without losing their properties. Recycling saves energy, saves ore in the ground and reduces waste.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking copper is used for wires because it is shiny; it is used because it is ductile and conducts electricity well. Two: thinking roofing sheets are pure iron; they are steel coated with zinc or paint to stop rust. Three: thinking metal can only be used once; metals can be recycled again and again. Four: thinking jewellery is made of gold only because it is valuable; gold is also malleable, shiny and does not rust.

The questions for this section are with you now: metals in cars, buildings and bridges, and recycling metals.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the Welkom scrapyard — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Pots, Chairs, Rings and Coins

Pots are metal because metal carries heat and does not melt.

Chairs have steel frames because steel is strong.

Rings are gold, silver or platinum because they shine and do not rust.

Coins are metal because metal lasts.

Keep the dented pots in mind and try the questions for this part: metals at home.

## Subtopic: Wires and Roofs

Copper wire carries electricity.

Steel wire is strong for fences.

Roofs are steel sheets, coated so they do not rust.

Hold the coil of copper wire in mind and try this part's questions: wires and roofs.

## Subtopic: Cars, Bridges and Buildings

Cars, bridges and building frames use strong steel.

Old metal can be melted and used again.

Pocket summary of the lesson. We choose metals for jobs because of their properties. Pots and utensils conduct heat and do not melt. Steel is strong for furniture, buildings, bridges and cars. Copper is ductile and conducts electricity for wires. Gold, silver and platinum are shiny and do not rust for jewellery. Coins and roofs need hard, strong, long-lasting metal. Metals can be recycled again and again.

Hold the giant pile of old cars in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: cars, bridges and buildings.
