### subtopic_1 — Metals Conduct Heat
**Assistant:** Why does salty sea air make things rust faster?

**Tutor:** Rusting needs iron, water and oxygen. Near the sea, the air is always damp, and it carries tiny drops of salty sea spray that land on everything. When salt dissolves in water on the metal, it makes the water much better at helping the chemical change that turns iron into rust. So the rust forms faster and spreads more quickly. That is why a steel gate in Mossel Bay or Durban may rust in a few years, while the same gate far inland in a dry place like Upington could last much longer. Cars in coastal towns often rust underneath, and the salt from the sea gets into every gap. To protect things near the sea, people use stainless steel, aluminium or plastic where they can, paint steel often, and rinse things like bicycles and fishing rods with fresh water to wash off the salt. Ships are painted with special thick paints, and some have blocks of zinc attached, which slowly wear away instead of the steel.

### subtopic_2 — Some Metals Are Magnetic
**Assistant:** Why is a metal spoon hot after a few minutes, but a wooden spoon is not?

**Tutor:** Everything is made of tiny particles that are always jiggling. When something is hot, its particles jiggle faster. In the hot water, the particles at the bottom of each spoon start jiggling faster, and they bump into the particles next to them, passing the jiggling, which is heat, along the spoon. In metals, this happens very quickly, because metals also have tiny, free-moving parts inside them that can carry the energy along fast, like a relay team passing a baton. So heat races up the metal spoon to the handle. In wood and plastic, the particles are held in a way that passes the jiggling along very slowly, and wood also has tiny pockets of air, which is a very poor conductor. So the heat stays mostly at the bottom, and the handle stays cool. This same reason explains why metals also conduct electricity well, while wood and plastic do not.

### subtopic_3 — Rusting and Tarnishing
**Assistant:** If gold does not tarnish, why do some gold rings go dull?

**Tutor:** Pure gold does not react with the air, so it does not tarnish, and it stays bright for thousands of years. But most gold jewellery is not pure gold. Pure gold is soft, so it is mixed with other metals, like copper, silver or zinc, to make it harder. This mixture is called an alloy. Gold jewellery is often marked to show how much gold is in it. Nine-carat gold is only about three-eighths gold, and the rest is other metals. Those other metals can tarnish a little, especially when they touch sweat, soap, perfume or chlorine from a swimming pool. So cheaper gold jewellery can slowly become dull or slightly dark. Purer gold, like eighteen-carat or twenty-four-carat, tarnishes much less. Rings also go dull from everyday dirt, hand cream and tiny scratches. A gentle wash with warm, soapy water and a soft cloth will usually bring the shine back.
