### subtopic_1 — Metals at Home
**Assistant:** Why do some pots have copper bottoms but stainless steel sides?

**Tutor:** Each metal is chosen for what it does best. Copper is one of the best conductors of heat of all the metals. A layer of copper at the bottom of a pot spreads the heat from the stove quickly and evenly across the whole base, so the food cooks evenly and does not burn in one hot spot. But copper reacts with some foods, it tarnishes, and it is expensive. Stainless steel is strong, hard, easy to clean and does not rust easily or react with food, but it does not spread heat as well as copper. So pot makers combine them. The inside and sides of the pot are stainless steel, which touches the food, and the bottom has a copper or aluminium layer to spread the heat. The handle is often plastic or wood, which are insulators, so you can hold it. This is a good example of engineers choosing materials by their properties and using them together.

### subtopic_2 — Metals in Wires and Roofs
**Assistant:** Why are steel bars put inside concrete?

**Tutor:** Concrete is very strong when it is squashed. You can stack a huge weight on top of a concrete pillar and it will not crush. But concrete is weak when it is pulled or bent. If you lay a long concrete beam across two supports and put a heavy load in the middle, the bottom of the beam is stretched, and the concrete cracks there. Steel is very strong when it is pulled. So builders place steel rods, called reinforcing bars, inside the wooden moulds, and then pour wet concrete around them. When the concrete sets, the steel and concrete are locked together. The concrete carries the squashing forces, and the steel carries the pulling forces. Together they make reinforced concrete, which is used for floors, beams, pillars, bridges and dams. The concrete also covers the steel and protects it from water and air, so it does not rust. You can often see the bumpy steel bars sticking out of a building that is still being built.

### subtopic_3 — Metals in Big Structures and Cars
**Assistant:** How does a scrapyard know which metal is which?

**Tutor:** Scrapyard workers use several tests, many of them the same as the ones we use in class. First, they use a magnet. Iron and steel are strongly attracted, so they are separated straight away. Aluminium, copper, brass and stainless steel are not attracted, or only weakly. Next, they look at the colour and shine. Copper is reddish-brown, brass is yellow, aluminium is silvery-white and light, and lead is dull grey and very heavy. They also feel how heavy a piece is for its size. Aluminium is much lighter than steel. Some yards use special machines that shine X-rays at a piece of metal and read out exactly what it is made of. Sorting matters because each metal is melted down separately and sold at a different price. Copper and brass are worth much more than steel, which is why scrap dealers must check that the copper they buy has not been stolen from electricity or railway cables.
