### subtopic_1 — Metals and Non-Metals
**Assistant:** How do I remember the difference between malleable and ductile?

**Tutor:** Here are two memory tricks. For malleable, think of a mallet, which is a kind of hammer. Malleable and mallet start the same way. A malleable metal can be hammered with a mallet into a new shape, like flattening a lead sinker or rolling aluminium into foil. For ductile, think of a duct, which is a long, narrow tube or pipe. A ductile metal can be pulled out into something long and thin, like a wire. Copper wire inside an electrical cable is the classic example. Another way is to picture what you do with your hands. Malleable: press and flatten, like squashing play dough into a pancake. Ductile: pull and stretch, like stretching play dough into a long snake. Most metals are both malleable and ductile, so in a test, if you are asked about foil or sheets, the answer is malleable, and if you are asked about wire, the answer is ductile. Brittle materials, like charcoal, are neither.

### subtopic_2 — Uses of Metals
**Assistant:** How can I tell which method was used to make a new material?

**Tutor:** Ask yourself what happened after the ingredients were mixed. If the mixture was simply left alone and turned hard by itself, without heating or cooling, that is mixing and setting, as with plaster of Paris and concrete. If nothing else happened after mixing, like flour and water stirred into glue paste, that is just mixing. If the mixture was put in an oven, a pot or a pan, that is mixing and cooking, as with dough baked into bread, or vetkoek fried in oil. If the mixture was put in the fridge or left to cool until it went firm, that is mixing and cooling, as with jelly. And if the mixture was shaped, left to dry, and then heated in a kiln at a very high temperature, that is mixing, drying and firing, as with clay bricks, pots and tiles. A good trick is to look for the key word in the question: set, cook, cool or fire. Each one points to a different method.

### subtopic_3 — Processing and Processed Materials
**Assistant:** Why is it important to know the properties of materials?

**Tutor:** Because everything that people make, from a teaspoon to a bridge, must be made of a material that suits its job. If you make a pot handle from metal, you will burn your hand, because metal conducts heat. If you make a raincoat from cotton, you will get soaked, because cotton is absorbent. If you build a home from cardboard, it will fall apart in the rain and burn easily. Engineers, builders, designers, cooks and even artists all think about properties before they choose a material. Knowing properties also helps you look after things. If you know that only iron and steel rust, you will paint the gate but not worry about the aluminium window frames. If you know that plastics do not rot, you will understand why we must recycle them. And it helps you stay safe, like knowing that wood, paper and many plastics burn, while bricks and concrete do not. So properties are not just words for a test. They are the reasons behind every material choice around you.
