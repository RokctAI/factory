# Part 1 — Expert

This session revises the Term 2 topics of Matter and Materials: metals and non-metals; uses of metals; processing materials; and processed materials. The anchor is a revision lesson in a Grade 5 classroom in Polokwane in Limpopo, set up as a pretend hardware shop called Grade 5 Building Supplies. One shelf holds metals and non-metals: copper wire, a steel nail, aluminium foil, charcoal and a pencil. One holds metal goods: a pot, a roofing sheet, a chain, some coins. One holds things for mixing: plaster of Paris, cement, sand, flour and clay. And one holds processed materials: tiles, glass, plastic pipes, fabric and paint. Learners take turns as customers with a problem, and as shop assistants who must recommend a material and explain why, using its properties. The big idea is that we choose and make materials for a job because of their properties. The whole of Term 2 is examinable.

## Subtopic: Metals and Non-Metals

The first customer asks: how can I tell if something is a metal?

The shop assistant explains the properties of metals. Metals are shiny when clean. Most are hard and strong. They are malleable, which means they can be hammered or pressed into new shapes, like aluminium foil. They are ductile, which means they can be drawn into wire, like copper wire. Most have high melting points, so they do not melt on a stove. Metals are good conductors of heat and electricity. They feel cold to touch and ring when tapped. Metals are mined from rocks called ores, and South Africa has gold, platinum, iron, copper, manganese and chrome.

Solid non-metals, like carbon and sulfur, are different. They are dull, not shiny. They are brittle, which means they crack and crumble when hit, so they cannot be hammered into shape or drawn into wire. Most are poor conductors of heat and electricity. Charcoal and pencil graphite are forms of carbon, and sulfur is yellow. Many everyday materials, such as wood, plastic and glass, are not metals either.

To sort a material, test several properties: shine, hammer, bend and heat. Mostly yes means metal. Mostly no means non-metal.

Remember that metals and non-metals often work as a team. A pot has a metal base to carry heat and a plastic handle to stay cool. An electrical cable has copper inside to carry electricity and plastic outside to keep people safe.

There are a few exceptions to remember. Mercury is a metal that is liquid at room temperature. Lead and pure gold are soft metals. Graphite is a non-metal that conducts electricity. And glass can be shiny, but it is not a metal.

The questions for this section are with you now: the properties of metals and non-metals, and how to sort them.

## Subtopic: Uses of Metals

The second customer needs to protect a steel gate near the sea. The assistant explains that only iron and steel rust, when water and oxygen reach them, and salty air makes it faster. Rust is orange-brown, flaky and weak. Paint, oil, grease, plastic coating or galvanising with zinc keep water and air away. Other metals, such as silver and copper, do not rust, but they tarnish: their surface becomes dull or changes colour, and it can be polished off. Gold does not tarnish.

The third customer wants to pick up dropped nails with a magnet. The assistant explains that only a few metals are magnetic: iron, steel, nickel and cobalt. Aluminium, copper, gold and silver are not.

The fourth customer needs a pot. The assistant explains that metals conduct heat, so pots are metal, but the handle should be wood or plastic, which are insulators.

Then the shop assistants match metals to uses. Copper for electrical wire, because it is ductile and conducts electricity. Steel for bridges, buildings, cars, fences and furniture, because it is strong. Galvanised steel for roofs, because it is strong, malleable and will not rust. Gold, silver and platinum for jewellery, because they are shiny and do not rust. Hard, long-lasting metals for coins. Stainless steel and aluminium for utensils, because they conduct heat and do not rust easily. Metals can be recycled again and again.

The questions for this section are with you now: conducting heat, magnetism, rust and tarnish, and the uses of metals.

## Subtopic: Processing and Processed Materials

The last customers want to make things.

Materials can be combined to make new materials in five ways. Mix and set: plaster of Paris for casts, and concrete, made of cement, sand, stone and water, for floors and bridges. Mix: flour and water for glue paste. Mix and cook: dough baked into bread. Mix and cool: jelly. Mix, dry and fire: clay bricks, fired in a kiln.

The new materials often have very different properties from the starting materials. Powder becomes a hard solid. Soft clay becomes hard and does not soften in water. Many of these changes, like baking and firing, cannot be undone.

Processed materials include plaster of Paris, concrete, fabrics, ceramics, glass, plastics and paints. They are useful because they can be strong, durable, waterproof, fire resistant, colourful or textured. Bricks and concrete are strong and fire resistant. Glass is see-through and waterproof. Plastics are light and waterproof but cause pollution because they do not rot. Fabrics are woven from cotton, wool, mohair or synthetic fibres. Paint colours and protects.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking all metals are magnetic; only iron, steel, nickel and cobalt are. Two: saying copper rusts; only iron and steel rust, and copper tarnishes. Three: thinking brittle means soft; brittle means it breaks or crumbles easily. Four: thinking a sun-dried brick is as good as a fired brick in the rain; only firing makes clay waterproof.

At the end of the lesson, the shop has one last, tricky customer, who wants a material that is strong, fire resistant and waterproof for a kitchen wall. The assistants agree: fired bricks for strength and fire resistance, covered with glazed ceramic tiles, which are waterproof and easy to clean.

The questions for this section are with you now: the five ways of combining materials, new properties, and processed materials.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with Grade 5 Building Supplies — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Metal or Not

Metals: shiny, strong, malleable, ductile and good at carrying heat.

Non-metals like charcoal: dull and brittle.

Keep the copper wire and the charcoal in mind and try the questions for this part: metals and non-metals.

## Subtopic: Using Metals

Only iron and steel rust. Paint stops it. Silver and copper tarnish.

Magnets attract iron and steel.

Copper for wire, steel for strength, gold for rings.

Hold the rusty gate in mind and try this part's questions: uses of metals.

## Subtopic: Making New Materials

Mix and set, mix, cook, cool, or dry and fire.

New materials have new properties.

Pocket summary of the term. Metals are shiny, hard, strong, malleable and ductile, with high melting points, and they conduct heat. Solid non-metals are dull and brittle. Only iron rusts; some other metals tarnish; only some metals are magnetic. We use metals because of their properties. Mixing and processing materials makes new materials with new properties, like concrete, ceramics, glass, plastics, fabrics and paint.

Hold the whole hardware shop in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: processing and processed materials.
