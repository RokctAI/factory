# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers seven important processed materials: plaster of Paris, concrete, fabrics, ceramics, glass, plastics and paints. A processed material is made by changing raw materials, such as rock, sand, clay, oil, plants or animal fibres, in a factory or workshop. Each processed material has special properties that make it useful. The anchor is a new community library being built right next to a primary school in Mahikeng in North West. For weeks, the Grade 5 class has watched from the playground. Now the building is nearly finished, and the site manager gives them a tour. Every few steps, she stops, taps a surface and asks: what is this made of, and why? The big idea is that almost every part of a modern building is made of processed materials, each chosen for its special properties. Naming processed materials and describing how they are made and what they are used for is examinable.

## Subtopic: Concrete, Plaster and Ceramics

The tour starts underground. The foundations, the floor slab and the pillars are made of concrete. As we learnt, concrete is made by mixing cement, sand, stone and water, which then sets hard. It is very strong, very hard, long-lasting and does not burn. Steel bars inside make it even stronger.

Inside, the walls are smooth and white. The bricks have been covered with a layer of cement plaster to make them smooth and flat. Around the top of the walls, where they meet the ceiling, there is a decorative strip called a cornice, made of plaster of Paris. Plaster of Paris is made by heating a soft rock called gypsum and grinding it into powder. Mixed with water, it can be poured into moulds of any shape, and it sets quickly into a smooth, hard solid. It is used for decorations, ceiling boards, moulds and medical casts.

On the floor are large, shiny tiles. In the bathrooms are white basins and toilets. These are ceramics. Ceramics are made by shaping clay, drying it and firing it in a kiln at a very high temperature. Many are then covered in a glaze and fired again. Ceramics are hard, do not burn, do not rot, are easy to clean, and with a glaze, are waterproof. Pots, mugs, plates, bricks, tiles, basins and toilets are all ceramics. People in southern Africa have made beautiful clay pots for thousands of years.

The questions for this section are with you now: concrete, plaster of Paris and ceramics, how they are made and used.

## Subtopic: Glass and Plastics

Next, the class looks at the windows. Glass is made by heating a mixture of sand, a substance called soda ash and crushed limestone to a very high temperature, well over a thousand degrees Celsius, until it melts into a glowing, runny liquid. The liquid glass can be floated out into flat sheets for windows, or blown and pressed into bottles, jars and drinking glasses. When it cools, it becomes hard and see-through. Glass is transparent, hard, waterproof, easy to clean and does not rot. But ordinary glass is brittle, so for doors and car windscreens, special safety glass is used. Glass can be recycled by melting it down again.

Then the class spots plastics everywhere: the water pipes, the light switches, the plug points, the chairs in the reading room and the covering on the electrical wires. Most plastics are made from crude oil, a thick, black liquid pumped from deep under the ground. In South Africa, some of the substances used to make plastics are also made from coal, at a big factory in Secunda in Mpumalanga. Plastics can be melted and moulded into almost any shape and colour. They are light, waterproof, do not rot, are cheap to make, and most do not carry heat or electricity, which makes them safe insulators.

But because plastics do not rot, they cause a lot of pollution. Plastic bags and bottles that are thrown away can stay in the environment for hundreds of years, choking rivers and harming sea animals. That is why we should reduce, reuse and recycle plastics.

The questions for this section are with you now: how glass and plastics are made, their properties and the problem of plastic pollution.

## Subtopic: Fabrics and Paints

In the children's corner, there are colourful curtains, a soft carpet and cushions. These are made of fabrics. A fabric is made by spinning fibres into threads, then weaving or knitting the threads together. Natural fibres come from plants and animals. Cotton comes from the fluffy seed pods of the cotton plant. Wool comes from sheep, and South Africa produces a lot of wool, especially in the Eastern Cape and the Karoo. Mohair comes from Angora goats, and about half of the world's mohair comes from South Africa. Synthetic fibres, such as nylon and polyester, are made from oil in factories. Fabrics can be soft, warm, flexible, strong and colourful. Some are absorbent, like cotton towels, and some are made waterproof, like raincoats.

Finally, the painters are finishing the walls. Paint is a mixture of a colouring powder called a pigment, a substance that makes it stick to the surface and harden, and a liquid, such as water or a solvent, that makes it easy to spread. When the liquid evaporates, a thin, hard, coloured layer is left. Paint makes surfaces colourful and attractive, and it protects them. It stops steel from rusting, stops wood from rotting and keeps rain out of walls.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking plastics grow naturally; they are made in factories, mostly from oil, and in South Africa also from coal. Two: thinking glass is made from water; it is made from sand, soda ash and limestone. Three: thinking all fabrics come from plants; wool and mohair come from animals, and nylon and polyester are made from oil. Four: thinking paint is only for colour; it also protects surfaces from rust, rot and rain.

The questions for this section are with you now: natural and synthetic fabrics, and what paint is made of and why we use it.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the new library in Mahikeng — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Strong, Smooth and Hard

Concrete makes strong floors and pillars.

Plaster of Paris makes smooth decorations and casts.

Ceramics, like tiles and toilets, are fired clay.

Keep the shiny floor tiles in mind and try the questions for this part: concrete, plaster and ceramics.

## Subtopic: See-Through and Mouldable

Glass is made from melted sand. It is see-through and hard.

Plastics come mostly from oil. They are light and waterproof.

Hold the new windows in mind and try this part's questions: glass and plastics.

## Subtopic: Soft and Colourful

Fabrics are woven or knitted from cotton, wool, mohair or nylon.

Paint adds colour and protects surfaces.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Processed materials are made by changing raw materials. Concrete is strong and hard. Plaster of Paris sets quickly into any shape. Ceramics are fired clay, hard and easy to clean. Glass is made from sand and is see-through. Plastics are made mostly from oil, are light and waterproof, but cause pollution. Fabrics are woven from natural or synthetic fibres. Paint colours and protects.

Hold the finished library in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: fabrics and paints.
