### subtopic_1 — Concrete, Plaster and Ceramics
**Assistant:** How can sand become something see-through like glass?

**Tutor:** Sand is made of tiny grains, mostly of a hard mineral called silica. Each grain is see-through or slightly cloudy, but a pile of sand looks pale and not see-through, because light bounces off the edges of millions of grains in every direction. When sand is heated with soda ash and limestone to well over a thousand degrees, the grains melt and mix into one smooth, glowing liquid with no separate grains and no edges. When this liquid cools quickly, its particles do not have time to line up into crystals, as they would in a rock. They get stuck in a jumbled arrangement, a bit like a liquid frozen in time. Because there are no grains or crystal edges to scatter the light, light passes straight through, and the glass is transparent. Soda ash is added because it makes the sand melt at a lower temperature, saving energy, and limestone is added to make the glass strong and stop it dissolving in water.

### subtopic_2 — Glass and Plastics
**Assistant:** Why does South Africa make some of its plastics from coal instead of oil?

**Tutor:** South Africa has very little oil of its own under the ground, but it has huge amounts of coal. Many years ago, the country wanted to make its own fuels and chemicals instead of relying only on oil from other countries. So scientists and engineers built a large factory at Secunda in Mpumalanga that turns coal into liquid fuels and many chemicals. Some of these chemicals are then used to make plastics, waxes and other products. The process works by breaking the coal down into simple gases and then building those gases up into new substances. It is a clever process, but it uses a lot of energy and water, and burning coal releases a great deal of carbon dioxide, which adds to climate change. Other countries mostly make plastics from oil or natural gas. Today, scientists everywhere are also working on plastics made from plants, and on better ways to recycle plastics, so that we need less oil and coal.

### subtopic_3 — Fabrics and Paints
**Assistant:** Why is wool from sheep good for keeping warm?

**Tutor:** Wool fibres are wavy and crinkly, so when they are spun and knitted into a jersey or woven into a blanket, they cannot pack tightly together. Lots of tiny pockets of air are trapped between them. Air is a very poor conductor of heat, so the trapped air acts as an insulator, slowing down the heat escaping from your body. Wool also soaks up some water from sweat or damp air without feeling wet straight away, so it stays warm even when it is a little damp. It is also naturally hard to set alight compared with many other fabrics, and it tends to stop burning by itself. That is why the thick woollen Basotho blanket is so well suited to the cold winters of Lesotho and the Eastern Free State. Mohair from Angora goats, which South Africa produces a lot of, is also warm and very strong, with a lovely shine. Sheep and goats grow new wool and mohair every year, so these fibres can be harvested again and again.
