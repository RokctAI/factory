# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers how the properties of a new material can be very different from the properties of the materials used to make it, and how we can test and compare those properties. The anchor is Technology Day at a primary school in Upington in the Northern Cape. Each group of Grade 5 learners makes something new. Group 1 presses their hands into wet plaster of Paris to make handprint plaques. Group 2 mixes concrete in an old ice-cream tub to make a stepping stone for the school garden. Group 3 shapes clay into small pinch pots, which a local potter dries and fires in her kiln. Group 4 bakes bread rolls in the school kitchen. A week later, every group brings back its product, and the whole class tests them. The big idea is that processing materials changes their properties, which is exactly why we do it. Comparing the properties of starting materials and new materials is examinable.

## Subtopic: Before and After

The class makes a big before-and-after table on the board.

Plaster of Paris. Before: a soft, white powder that pours, and runs through your fingers. After setting: a hard, white solid that keeps its shape, with a perfect print of a hand. It cannot be poured, and it does not turn back into powder.

Concrete. Before: cement is a fine grey powder, sand is loose grains, and stones are separate hard lumps. None of them can hold a shape by themselves. After setting: one solid, heavy block that is hard and very strong. You can stand on the stepping stone, and it does not crack.

Clay. Before: soft, sticky and easy to squash into any shape. After drying: hard, but it turns soft again in water. After firing: very hard, rings a little when tapped, and does not soften in water.

Dough. Before: soft, sticky, stretchy and pale. After baking: a brown crust outside, and a light, spongy inside full of holes. It smells good, and it can be sliced.

The class also adds a jelly to the table, made the day before. Before: a dry, sweet powder and a liquid, water. After cooling: a wobbly, see-through, coloured solid that keeps the shape of its bowl.

In every case, the new material has properties that the starting materials did not have. Powders and loose grains became hard solids. A soft, squashy material became hard and waterproof. A sticky dough became light and spongy.

The questions for this section are with you now: comparing the properties of the starting materials and the new materials.

## Subtopic: Testing the New Materials

To compare materials fairly, the class does tests and records the results.

Hardness test: try to scratch each product with a steel nail. The fired clay pot and the concrete stepping stone are hardest. The plaster scratches more easily, leaving a white mark. The bread is soft and squashes.

Strength test: put the products on a table and add weights on top until something cracks. The concrete stepping stone holds the most. The plaster plaque cracks under less weight. Plaster is hard, but it is not as strong as concrete, and it is brittle.

Waterproof test: drip water onto each product and watch for one minute. On the fired clay pot, the water soaks in a little, unless the pot has a shiny glaze. On the concrete, it soaks in slowly. On the plaster, it soaks in quickly, and the plaster gets darker. A piece of unfired, dried clay put in a glass of water slowly softens and falls apart.

The class also notices that the recipe matters. Group 2 made two stepping stones: one with the right amount of water, and one with far too much water. The runny one is weaker and crumbles at the edges. Using the right amounts of each material, and giving it time to set properly, gives the best properties. Builders follow a recipe, called a mix, just as Gogo follows a recipe for bread. A common concrete mix is one bucket of cement, two buckets of sand and three buckets of stone, with just enough water to make it workable.

The questions for this section are with you now: testing hardness, strength and waterproofness, and why the recipe matters.

## Subtopic: Changes That Cannot Be Undone

Some changes to materials can be undone, and some cannot.

When jelly sets in the fridge, it can melt again on a hot day, and set again when it is cooled. When ice melts, it can be frozen again. These are changes that can be reversed, or undone.

But you cannot un-bake a bread roll back into dough. You cannot turn a fired clay pot back into soft clay, or a concrete stepping stone back into cement powder, sand and stones. These changes cannot be reversed. The materials have changed into completely new materials.

This is useful, because it means the new materials keep their new properties. A fired brick stays hard in the rain for a hundred years. A concrete bridge does not turn back into sand. A plaster cast stays firm until the doctor cuts it off.

People choose to process materials because the new properties suit a job. Concrete is used for floors and bridges because it is hard, strong and long-lasting. Plaster is used for casts and moulds because it can be poured into any shape and sets quickly. Fired clay is used for bricks, tiles and pots because it is hard, strong and does not soften in water. Bread is made because it is tastier, softer and easier to eat than raw flour.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking a new material has the same properties as its ingredients; it often has very different properties. Two: thinking all changes can be undone; you cannot un-bake bread or un-fire a pot. Three: thinking hard always means strong; the plaster was hard but cracked easily. Four: thinking more water makes concrete stronger; too much water makes it weaker.

The questions for this section are with you now: changes that can and cannot be undone, and why we process materials.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with Technology Day in Upington — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Not the Same as Before

Powder plus water becomes a hard plaster plaque.

Cement, sand and stones become a strong stepping stone.

Soft clay becomes a hard pot.

Keep the handprint plaques in mind and try the questions for this part: before and after.

## Subtopic: Scratch, Load and Drip

Scratch it to test hardness.

Add weights to test strength.

Drip water to test if it is waterproof.

Hold the stepping stone in mind and try this part's questions: testing new materials.

## Subtopic: No Going Back

Melted jelly can set again.

Baked bread and fired clay cannot go back.

Pocket summary of the lesson. When materials are combined and processed, the new material often has very different properties from the starting materials. Powders become hard solids, and soft clay becomes hard and waterproof. We test new materials for hardness, strength and waterproofness. Many of these changes, like baking and firing, cannot be undone, which is why the new materials are so useful.

Hold the bread rolls and the fired pots in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: changes that cannot be undone.
