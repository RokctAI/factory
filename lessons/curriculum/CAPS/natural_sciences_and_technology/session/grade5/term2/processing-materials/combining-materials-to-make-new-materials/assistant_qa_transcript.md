### subtopic_1 — Mixing and Setting
**Assistant:** Why does plaster of Paris get warm when it sets?

**Tutor:** Plaster of Paris is made by heating a soft white rock called gypsum. Gypsum naturally has water locked inside its tiny crystals. When it is heated, most of this water is driven out, and the gypsum becomes a fine, dry powder. That powder is plaster of Paris. When you add water again, the powder takes the water back into its crystals, and new crystals grow and lock together into a hard solid. That is what setting is. Taking the water back in gives off some of the energy that was used to drive the water out, and that energy comes out as heat. So the plaster gets warm. You can feel it with your hand on the outside of the mixing bowl. In a large amount, plaster can get hot enough to burn skin, so you should never put your whole hand inside a big block of setting plaster. Doctors use thin layers, which only get pleasantly warm. Cement also gives off some heat as it sets, which is why very thick concrete in big dams must be cooled carefully.

### subtopic_2 — Mixing, Cooking and Cooling
**Assistant:** Why do bakers knead dough for so long?

**Tutor:** Flour contains substances that, when mixed with water, form long, stretchy strands called gluten. When you first mix flour and water, the strands are short and tangled, and the dough is lumpy and breaks easily. Kneading means pushing, folding and stretching the dough over and over. This lines up the gluten strands and links them into a strong, stretchy net, a bit like a rubber band. You can tell the dough is ready when it becomes smooth and springs back when you poke it. This stretchy net is very important. When the yeast makes tiny bubbles of gas, the gluten net traps them, so the dough rises and puffs up instead of the gas just escaping. When the bread is baked, the heat sets the net firmly in place around the bubbles, so the bread stays light and full of holes. If the dough is not kneaded enough, the bread comes out flat and heavy. That is why Gogo spends ten minutes kneading.

### subtopic_3 — Mixing, Drying and Firing
**Assistant:** What is the difference between a mud brick and a fired brick?

**Tutor:** Both start as clay or soil mixed with water and shaped in a mould, and both are dried. The big difference is the last step. A mud brick is only dried in the sun. The water evaporates, and the brick becomes hard, but the clay particles are only stuck together loosely. If it gets soaked by rain, water gets back in between the particles, and the brick turns soft and slowly crumbles. Builders protect mud brick walls with a thick coat of plaster and paint, and with wide roof overhangs. A fired brick is heated in a kiln to around a thousand degrees Celsius. At this temperature, the clay particles partly melt and fuse together, and the material changes completely into a hard, stone-like ceramic. A fired brick cannot turn back into soft clay, even if it is soaked for years. Mud bricks are cheaper and use less energy, because no kiln is needed, and they keep houses cool in summer. Fired bricks are stronger and last much longer in the rain.
