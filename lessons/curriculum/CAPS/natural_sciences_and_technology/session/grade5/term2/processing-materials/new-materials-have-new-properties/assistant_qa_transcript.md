### subtopic_1 — Before and After
**Assistant:** Why do some clay pots have a shiny coating?

**Tutor:** That shiny coating is called a glaze. Fired clay that has no glaze is hard, but it still has tiny holes, so water can slowly soak into it. Unglazed pots are useful in some ways. In many traditional homes, water is kept in an unglazed clay pot, because a little water seeps through and evaporates from the outside, which keeps the water inside cool. But for a mug, a plate or a bathroom tile, we want the surface to be completely waterproof and easy to clean. So the potter paints or dips the pot in a glaze, which is a mixture of fine powdered glass-making materials and water, and fires it again. In the kiln, the glaze melts into a thin layer of glass that covers the clay and seals the tiny holes. Glazes can be made in many colours. So a glazed pot gets new properties: shiny, colourful, smooth and fully waterproof. This is another example of processing a material to change its properties.

### subtopic_2 — Testing the New Materials
**Assistant:** If plaster is hard, why did it crack so easily in the strength test?

**Tutor:** Hardness and strength are two different properties. Hardness means a material is difficult to scratch or dent. Strength means it can carry a heavy load, or be pulled or bent, without breaking. Plaster is fairly hard on its surface, but inside it is made of tiny crystals stuck together, with many small air holes. When a heavy weight presses on a thin plaster plaque, the plaque bends a tiny bit. Plaster is brittle, so instead of bending, it cracks, and the crack spreads quickly through the weak spots. Concrete is much stronger, because the sand and stones inside it are locked together by the cement, and thick blocks of concrete are very good at carrying weight pressing down on them. Glass is another example: it is very hard, but it is brittle and breaks easily if dropped. When engineers choose a material, they think about both properties, and many others too, before deciding.

### subtopic_3 — Changes That Cannot Be Undone
**Assistant:** Why can melted jelly set again, but baked bread cannot become dough again?

**Tutor:** It depends on what happens to the material inside. In jelly, there are long, thin strands of a substance called gelatine. When jelly is cold, these strands tangle together into a net that traps the water, so the jelly wobbles but holds its shape. When it is warmed, the strands untangle and the jelly becomes a liquid. When it cools again, they tangle up again. The strands themselves have not changed, so the change can be reversed. In bread, the heat of the oven causes changes that make completely new substances. The yeast dies, the starch in the flour swells and sets, the gluten net is set firm, water is driven off, and the outside turns brown as new substances form, which also give the lovely smell. Those new substances cannot turn back into the old ones. It is the same with fired clay and set concrete. Their particles have joined in new ways that cannot be undone.
