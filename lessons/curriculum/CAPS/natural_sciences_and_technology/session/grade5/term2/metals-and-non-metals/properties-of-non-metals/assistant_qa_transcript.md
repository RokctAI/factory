### subtopic_1 — What Are Non-Metals?
**Assistant:** If pencil lead is not lead, why do people call it lead?

**Tutor:** It is an old mistake that stuck. About five hundred years ago, people in England found a large amount of a soft, dark grey material in the ground that left marks when it was rubbed on paper. It looked a bit like the metal lead, which also leaves grey marks, so people called it black lead or plumbago, which comes from the old word for lead. Later, scientists discovered that it was not lead at all, but a form of carbon. They named it graphite, from a Greek word meaning to write. By then, everyone already called pencil cores leads, and the name has stayed until today. This is actually good news, because the metal lead is poisonous, and graphite is not. Modern pencil leads are made of graphite powder mixed with clay and baked. More clay makes a harder, lighter pencil, and more graphite makes a softer, darker one. That is what the letters and numbers on pencils, like HB and 2B, tell you.

### subtopic_2 — Dull and Brittle
**Assistant:** Why does charcoal crumble when metal bends?

**Tutor:** It is all about how the tiny particles inside the material hold on to each other. In a metal, the particles are packed in neat layers, and they are held together in a way that lets the layers slide over each other without letting go. So when you hammer or bend a metal, the layers just slide into a new shape, and the metal stays in one piece. That is why metals are malleable and ductile. In a brittle solid like charcoal or sulfur, the particles are held together in a fixed pattern that cannot slide. When you hit it, the particles cannot move into new places, so the bonds between them break instead. A crack forms and spreads, and the solid crumbles or shatters. Charcoal is also full of tiny holes left behind from the wood it was made from, which makes it even weaker. Glass and chalk are brittle for the same reason, even though they are not non-metal elements.

### subtopic_3 — Sorting Metals and Non-Metals
**Assistant:** Are non-metals less useful than metals?

**Tutor:** Not at all. They are just useful in different ways. Without non-metals, we could not live. Oxygen, a non-metal gas, is what we breathe. Carbon is in every living thing, including your body, and it is in the food you eat and the fuels that cook it. Nitrogen in the air is used to make fertilisers that help farmers grow food. Sulfur is used to make fertilisers and medicines, and to toughen the rubber in car tyres. Graphite is used in pencils and in some batteries. Because most non-metals and non-metal materials do not carry heat or electricity well, they are excellent for keeping us safe. The plastic around a wire, the wooden handle on a pot and the rubber soles of an electrician's boots all protect people. Metals and non-metals work best as a team. A good engineer chooses each material because its properties suit the job, whether that is a shiny, strong steel bridge or a dull, safe plastic plug.
