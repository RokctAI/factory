# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the properties of metals and where metals come from. Metals are shiny, hard and strong. They are malleable, which means they can be hammered or pressed into new shapes, and ductile, which means they can be pulled into wire. Most have high melting points. And metals are mined from the Earth. The anchor is a school trip by a Grade 5 class in Johannesburg to an old gold mine that is now open for tours. They put on hard hats, ride a lift cage deep underground and walk along a tunnel where miners once dug out gold-bearing rock. Back at school, the teacher hands out samples: a piece of copper wire, a steel nail, a sheet of aluminium foil, a lead fishing sinker and a cheap gold-coloured ring. The big idea is that metals share a set of properties that make them very useful, and that they come from rocks deep in the Earth. Describing the properties of metals is examinable.

## Subtopic: Shiny, Hard and Strong

A property is a quality that describes what a material is like or how it behaves.

The first property the class notices is that metals are shiny. Scientists call this lustre. A new coin, a polished pot and a piece of aluminium foil all reflect light, so they shine. Some metals lose their shine when they are left in the air or get dirty, but if you rub or scratch them, the shine shows underneath.

Most metals are hard. They do not scratch or dent easily. The class tries to scratch the steel nail with a fingernail and cannot. Hard metals, like steel, are used for tools such as hammers, chisels and spades. Some metals are softer. Lead and pure gold are soft enough to scratch with a nail. That is why gold for jewellery is often mixed with other metals to make it harder.

Metals are strong. A strong material can carry a heavy load or be pulled without breaking. Thin steel cables hold up lifts in mines and tall buildings, and steel beams hold up bridges.

Metals also feel cold to the touch, even when they are at room temperature. That is because they carry heat away from your warm fingers quickly. We will learn more about this in the next topic.

Metals make a ringing sound when you tap them. A metal pot lid or a school bell rings clearly, while a wooden spoon just knocks.

The questions for this section are with you now: what a property is, and why we say metals are shiny, hard and strong.

## Subtopic: Bend, Hammer and Stretch

Metals have two more very useful properties.

Metals are malleable. This means they can be hammered, pressed or rolled into new shapes without breaking. The teacher gently taps the lead sinker with a hammer on a block of wood, and it flattens into a disc instead of shattering. Aluminium foil is aluminium that has been rolled out very thin in a factory. Car bodies, pots and roofing sheets are made by pressing flat sheets of metal into shape. Gold is the most malleable metal of all. It can be beaten into sheets so thin that light shines through them. This is called gold leaf.

Metals are ductile. This means they can be pulled, or drawn, into long, thin wires. The class looks at a piece of electrical cable. Inside the plastic covering is fine, shiny copper wire. In a factory, a thick rod of copper is pulled through smaller and smaller holes until it becomes a thin wire. Steel is drawn into wire for fences and coat hangers.

Most metals have high melting points. A melting point is the temperature at which a solid turns into a liquid. Candle wax melts in a candle flame, but a metal spoon held in the same flame does not melt. Iron only melts at about 1 500 degrees Celsius, and water boils at only 100 degrees Celsius. So metals are used for pots, stoves and car engines, which get very hot.

There is one surprising exception. Mercury is a metal that is a liquid at room temperature. It was used in old thermometers.

The questions for this section are with you now: malleable, ductile and high melting points.

## Subtopic: Metals from the Earth

Metals do not grow, and we cannot make them in a factory from nothing. They come from rocks in the Earth's crust.

A rock that contains enough metal to be worth digging out is called an ore. Miners dig ore out of the ground, either in deep underground mines or in huge open pits. Then the ore is crushed and heated in a furnace to separate the metal from the rock. This is called smelting.

South Africa is one of the richest countries in the world for metals. Gold was discovered on the Witwatersrand in 1886, and Johannesburg grew up around the mines. Some of our gold mines are among the deepest mines in the world, more than three kilometres underground. South Africa also has most of the world's known platinum, mined near Rustenburg and in Limpopo. Iron ore is mined at Sishen in the Northern Cape. Copper is mined at Phalaborwa, and our country has huge amounts of manganese and chrome.

Gold and a few other metals can be found as pure metal in the rock, but most metals are found joined with other substances in ores.

Mining provides jobs and money, but it can also scar the land, pollute water and be dangerous for miners. That is why old metal should be recycled. Recycling a cooldrink can uses much less energy than making a new can from ore.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking all metals are hard; lead and pure gold are soft. Two: thinking all metals are solid at room temperature; mercury is a liquid. Three: thinking metals are made in factories; they are mined from ores in the Earth. Four: confusing malleable and ductile; malleable means it can be hammered into shape, and ductile means it can be drawn into wire.

The questions for this section are with you now: ores, mining and smelting, and South Africa's metals.
