### subtopic_1 — A Ball of Rock
**Assistant:** Why does the Moon sometimes look bigger when it is low near the horizon?

**Tutor:** This is one of the most famous tricks our eyes and brain play on us. When the full Moon rises over the hills near Mthatha, it looks huge and often orange. A few hours later, high in the sky, it looks smaller. But if you measure it, it is the same size in both places. Scientists call this the Moon illusion. Nobody is completely sure why our brains do this, but one idea is that when the Moon is near the horizon, we see it next to trees, houses and hills, and our brain compares it to them, so it seems big. High in the sky, there is nothing next to it, so it seems smaller. You can test this yourself. Hold up a small coin at arm's length next to the rising Moon, and do it again later when the Moon is high. The Moon will cover the coin the same way both times. The orange colour is real, though. It happens because the moonlight passes through more air near the horizon.

### subtopic_2 — No Air and No Water
**Assistant:** Is there really no water at all on the Moon?

**Tutor:** There are no rivers, lakes, oceans or rain on the Moon, and no liquid water on its surface. For a long time, scientists thought the Moon was completely dry. But space missions have found that there is some water ice hidden on the Moon. It is frozen in very deep, dark craters near the Moon's north and south poles, where sunlight never reaches the bottom, so the ice never melts. There are also tiny amounts of water locked inside some Moon rocks and dust. This discovery is exciting, because if people ever build a base on the Moon, they might be able to melt this ice for drinking water, and even split it to make oxygen for breathing and fuel for rockets. But this ice is frozen, buried and hard to reach. It cannot support plants or animals the way the Earth's water does. So for living things, the Moon is still a dry, airless place.

### subtopic_3 — Smaller and Closer
**Assistant:** Where did the Moon come from?

**Tutor:** Scientists have studied Moon rocks that astronauts brought back to the Earth, and they found that Moon rocks are very similar to some of the Earth's rocks. This gave them a clue. The idea most scientists agree with is that a very long time ago, when the Earth was very young, another object about the size of Mars crashed into the Earth. The crash was so huge that it threw a giant cloud of melted rock and dust out into space around the Earth. Over time, that material came together, pulled by its own gravity, and formed a ball. That ball became our Moon. This happened billions of years ago, long before there were any plants or animals on the Earth. Scientists cannot go back in time to watch, so they use clues from rocks, computers and other planets to work out what most likely happened. This is how science often works: collecting clues, like a detective.
