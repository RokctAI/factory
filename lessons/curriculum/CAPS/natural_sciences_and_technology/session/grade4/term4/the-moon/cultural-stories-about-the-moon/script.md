# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers cultural stories about the Moon. The anchor is a public library in Cape Town that holds a story evening on the night of a full Moon. A Grade 4 class from a nearby school brings blankets and sits in a circle outside in the library garden. The storyteller, Mama Thuli, has told stories at schools all over the Western Cape. The learners in the class come from many different families and cultures, and some of them have brought Moon stories from home to share too. The big idea is that people everywhere have watched the Moon for thousands of years, long before telescopes and rockets, and that many cultures have stories and traditions about it. These stories teach lessons, explain the world and keep cultures alive. Retelling a cultural story about the Moon and explaining why people told such stories is examinable.

## Subtopic: People Have Always Watched the Moon

Before there were electric lights, the Moon was the brightest light at night. On a night with a full Moon, people could walk, herd cattle and travel. On a night with no Moon, it was very dark. So people paid close attention to the Moon.

People noticed that the Moon follows a pattern. It grows from a thin crescent to a full circle and shrinks back again, and the pattern repeats about every twenty-nine and a half days. This made the Moon a very useful clock and calendar. People could count time in Moons. Farmers could plan when to plant and when to harvest. Families could plan when to hold gatherings.

That is why, in many languages, the word for moon and the word for month are the same or almost the same. In isiZulu, inyanga means both moon and month. In Setswana, kgwedi means both moon and month. In Afrikaans, the Moon is the maan, and a month is a maand. Even in English, the word month comes from the word Moon.

Because the Moon was so important and so mysterious, people also wondered about it. Why does it change shape? Why are there dark patches on its face? Where does it go when we cannot see it? Many cultures answered these questions with stories, passed on from grandparents to grandchildren around the fire.

These stories were not written in books. They were remembered and told aloud, again and again, so that each new generation learnt them by heart. Storytellers often changed small details, which is why the same story can have several versions.

The questions for this section are with you now: why people have always watched the Moon.

## Subtopic: Moon Stories from Southern Africa

Mama Thuli begins with one of the oldest stories told in Southern Africa. Different versions of it have been told by San and Khoikhoi people for a very long time.

Long ago, the story goes, the Moon wanted to send people a message of hope. The Moon said: just as I die and come back to life every month, so you too will die and come back to life. The Moon chose the hare to carry this message. But the hare was in a hurry and careless, and when it reached the people, it got the message wrong. It told them: just as the Moon dies and does not come back, so you will die and never come back. When the Moon found out, it was very angry. It struck the hare across the mouth, splitting its lip. And that, the story says, is why the hare has a split lip to this day.

The learners notice that the story uses something true about the Moon. It really does seem to disappear and then come back every month. The story also explains something people could see, the hare's split upper lip. And it teaches a lesson: be careful to pass on a message correctly.

Mama Thuli shares another San story. The Mantis, a clever trickster called Kaggen, once threw his leather shoe up into the sky, and it became the Moon. That is why, the story says, the Moon is cold and pale, not hot like the Sun.

The questions for this section are with you now: Moon stories from Southern Africa.

## Subtopic: Moon Traditions Around the World

Then the learners share stories from home. Wei's grandmother is from China. She says that in China, Japan and Korea, many people see the shape of a rabbit in the dark patches of the Moon. In China, families celebrate the Mid-Autumn Festival under the full Moon, eating round mooncakes. Chinese New Year also starts on a new Moon.

Zainab's family is Muslim. She explains that the months of the Islamic calendar follow the Moon. A new month starts when the thin crescent of the new Moon is first seen in the evening sky. In Cape Town and other cities, people gather on hills and at the beach to look for the new crescent, to know when the fasting month of Ramadan begins and when to celebrate Eid.

Priya's family is Hindu, and her grandparents live in Durban. She says that Diwali, the festival of lights, is celebrated on the night of a new Moon, one of the darkest nights of the year. Families light rows of small clay oil lamps and set off fireworks, so that light fills the dark night.

Liam says that in some European stories, people see the face of a man in the Moon.

Mr Petersen, the teacher, reminds the class that these stories and traditions are not science, and that science is not a story. Science explains the Moon using careful observations and tests. Stories explain the Moon in ways that teach lessons, bring families together and keep culture alive. We can learn from both and respect both.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: saying cultural stories are silly because they are not science; they teach lessons and keep cultures alive. Two: saying a story is a scientific fact; stories and science answer questions in different ways. Three: thinking only one culture has Moon stories; people all over the world do. Four: thinking the Moon was not useful long ago; people used it as a calendar.

The questions for this section are with you now: Moon traditions around the world, and stories and science.
