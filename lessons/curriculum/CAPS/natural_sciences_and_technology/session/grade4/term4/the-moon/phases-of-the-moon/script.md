# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the phases of the Moon: why the Moon seems to change shape, and how the pattern repeats. The anchor is Durban in KwaZulu-Natal. Ayanda, a Grade 4 learner, lives in a flat with a balcony. For a project, her teacher, Mr Naidoo, asks every learner to keep a Moon diary. Every clear night for a month, Ayanda goes onto the balcony, looks for the Moon, and draws its shape in her diary with the date. At first the Moon is a thin curved sliver. A week later it is half a circle. Then it is a full, round ball. Then it slowly shrinks again. The big idea is that the Moon does not really change shape. We see different amounts of its sunlit side as it travels around the Earth, and this pattern, called the phases of the Moon, repeats every twenty-nine and a half days. Describing and explaining the phases of the Moon is examinable.

## Subtopic: The Moon Seems to Change Shape

When Ayanda looks at her diary at the end of the month, she sees a clear pattern.

At the start, she could not see the Moon at all for a night or two. Then a thin curved sliver appeared low in the west, just after sunset. A shape like this, curved like a banana or a fingernail clipping, is called a crescent. Each night the bright part grew a little bigger. After about a week, the Moon looked like half a circle. After about two weeks, it was a full, round circle, rising in the east as the Sun set in the west. Then, night by night, the bright part got smaller again. It rose later and later at night, and soon Ayanda had to get up early in the morning to see it. After about four weeks, it disappeared again, and then the whole pattern started over.

Durban had some cloudy and rainy nights that month, so Ayanda has a few empty boxes in her diary. But there are enough drawings to see the pattern clearly, and Mr Naidoo says that is how scientists work too: they collect many observations and look for a pattern.

When the bright part of the Moon is growing, we say the Moon is waxing. When the bright part is shrinking, we say the Moon is waning.

The Moon is always a round ball. It never really changes shape. What changes is how much of its bright, sunlit part we can see from the Earth.

The questions for this section are with you now: the changing shapes that Ayanda saw.

## Subtopic: Why We See Phases

To understand why, Mr Naidoo sets up a model in a dark classroom. A bright lamp in one corner of the room is the Sun. Ayanda holds a white ball on a stick at arm's length, a little higher than her head. The ball is the Moon, and Ayanda's head is the Earth.

The lamp always lights up one half of the ball, the half facing the lamp. The other half is dark. This is true of the real Moon too. The Sun always lights up half of the Moon, the half facing the Sun.

Now Ayanda turns slowly on the spot, keeping the ball in front of her face. When the ball is between her and the lamp, the lit half faces away from her, and she sees only the dark side. That is the new Moon, when we cannot see the Moon at all. As she turns further, she starts to see a thin edge of the lit half, a crescent. A quarter of the way around, she sees half of the ball lit. When she has turned halfway, so that her back is to the lamp and the ball is on the other side of her head, she sees the whole lit half. That is the full Moon. As she keeps turning, the lit part she can see gets smaller again.

The real Moon does the same thing as it travels around the Earth. As the Moon moves, we see more or less of its sunlit half. These different shapes are called the phases of the Moon. The Moon is not always seen only at night, either. Around first quarter, Ayanda can often spot the half Moon in the blue afternoon sky on her way home from school.

The changing pattern of sunlight on the Moon is caused by the Moon's journey around the Earth, not by any shadow.

The questions for this section are with you now: why we see the Moon's phases.

## Subtopic: The Phases and the Cycle

The main phases of the Moon have names. In order, they are new Moon, waxing crescent, first quarter, waxing gibbous, full Moon, waning gibbous, last quarter and waning crescent, and then new Moon again. Gibbous means more than half lit, but not yet full. First quarter and last quarter look like half a circle. They are called quarters because the Moon has gone a quarter or three quarters of the way around the Earth.

The whole pattern, from one new Moon to the next, takes about twenty-nine and a half days. That is about one month. In fact, the word month comes from the word Moon. In isiZulu, the word inyanga means both moon and month.

In South Africa, when the Moon is waxing, the bright side is on the left as we look at it. When it is waning, the bright side is on the right. People in countries in the north of the world see it the other way around.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: saying the Moon really changes shape; it is always round, and we see different amounts of its sunlit half. Two: saying the Earth's shadow causes the phases; the phases are caused by the Moon moving around the Earth. Three: saying waxing means shrinking; waxing means growing, and waning means shrinking. Four: saying the pattern repeats every week; it repeats every twenty-nine and a half days.

The questions for this section are with you now: the names of the phases and how long the cycle takes.

## Subtopic: Growing and Shrinking

The Moon looks like it changes shape.

It grows from a thin crescent to a full circle. That is waxing.

Then it shrinks again. That is waning.
