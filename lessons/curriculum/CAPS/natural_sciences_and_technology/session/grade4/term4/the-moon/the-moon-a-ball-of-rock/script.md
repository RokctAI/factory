# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers what the Moon is made of, what it is like there, and how its size and distance compare to the Earth and the Sun. The anchor is a village near Mthatha in the Eastern Cape. On a clear night, the full Moon rises over the hills, big, round and bright. Lwazi, who is in Grade 4, sits outside with his grandmother, Gogo Nomsa. She has an old pair of binoculars, and she hands them to him. Through them, the Moon is no longer just a bright circle. Lwazi can see grey patches, bright patches and round holes. The big idea is that the Moon is a ball of rock with no air and no water, that it is much smaller than the Earth, and that it is much closer to the Earth than the Sun is. Describing the Moon and comparing it to the Earth and the Sun is examinable.

## Subtopic: A Ball of Rock

The Moon is a ball of rock. Like the Earth, it is a sphere. It travels around the Earth, taking about a month to go around once. We call it the Earth's moon.

The Moon does not make its own light. It shines because sunlight falls on it and bounces off towards us. The rocks on the Moon are grey, but in the sunlight they look bright white against the dark night sky.

Through the binoculars, Lwazi sees dark grey patches. Long ago, people thought these patches were seas of water, so they are still called seas today. But they are really wide, flat plains of dark rock that formed when melted rock flowed out and hardened, a very, very long time ago. The brighter parts are higher land and mountains.

The Moon is covered in round holes called craters. A crater is made when a rock from space crashes into the surface. Some craters are tiny, and some are wider than a whole city. On the Earth, most craters have been worn away by wind, rain and rivers, or covered by plants. On the Moon, there is nothing to wear them away, so they last for a very long time.

The surface of the Moon is covered in fine grey dust and loose rocks, a bit like powder mixed with gravel.

The Moon is the brightest thing in the night sky, but it is not always there at night. Sometimes it rises late at night, and sometimes we see it in the daytime, as a pale white shape in the blue sky. Lwazi's grandmother tells him that the Moon rises almost an hour later each night, so tomorrow night it will come up over the hills a little later than tonight.

The Moon always shows the same side to the Earth. As it travels around the Earth, it turns just enough to keep the same face towards us. So the patches Lwazi sees tonight are the same patches his grandmother saw when she was a child.

The questions for this section are with you now: what the Moon is made of and why it shines.

## Subtopic: No Air and No Water

The Moon has no air. That makes it very different from the Earth.

Without air, there is no wind on the Moon. There are no clouds, no rain and no weather. When astronauts walked on the Moon, they left footprints in the dust. With no wind or rain to wipe them away, those footprints are still there today.

Without air, there is nothing to breathe. Astronauts had to wear space suits with their own air supply. Without air, sound cannot travel, so the Moon is completely silent. And without air, the sky on the Moon is always black, even in the daytime, when the Sun is shining. Astronauts standing on the Moon could see the Earth hanging in that black sky, a bright blue and white ball.

The Moon has no water like the Earth's. There are no rivers, no lakes, no oceans and no rain. Without air and water, nothing can live on the Moon. There are no plants, no animals and no people living there.

Without a blanket of air, the Moon gets extremely hot in the sunshine, much hotter than boiling water, and extremely cold in the dark, much colder than any freezer.

The questions for this section are with you now: what it is like on the Moon without air and water.

## Subtopic: Smaller and Closer

The Moon is much smaller than the Earth. It is only about a quarter as wide. If the Earth were a soccer ball, the Moon would be about the size of a tennis ball. About fifty Moons could fit inside the Earth.

Because the Moon is smaller, it has a weaker pull. On the Moon, you would weigh only about one sixth of what you weigh on the Earth. You could jump much higher, and astronauts on the Moon bounced along in big, slow hops. A heavy backpack that is hard to lift on the Earth would feel light enough to carry with one hand.

The Moon is our closest neighbour in space. It is about three hundred and eighty-four thousand kilometres away. That sounds far, but about thirty Earths lined up in a row would fill the gap between the Earth and the Moon. The Moon is so close that it was the first place in space that people visited. The Sun is about four hundred times further away than the Moon.

The Sun is about four hundred times wider than the Moon, but it is also about four hundred times further away. That is why the Sun and the Moon look about the same size in our sky.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: saying the Moon makes its own light; it reflects sunlight. Two: saying the dark patches are seas of water; they are plains of dark rock. Three: saying the Moon is as big as the Earth; it is only about a quarter as wide. Four: saying the Moon is as far as the Sun because they look the same size; the Sun is about four hundred times further away.

The questions for this section are with you now: the Moon's size and distance.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with Lwazi and Gogo Nomsa — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Rock, Not Light

The Moon is a ball of rock. It has craters made by space rocks.

It does not make light. It reflects sunlight.

Keep the binoculars in mind and try the questions for this part: a ball of rock.

## Subtopic: Nothing to Breathe

The Moon has no air and no water.

No wind, no rain, no sound and no life.

Hold the footprints in the dust in mind and try this part's questions: no air and no water.

## Subtopic: Small and Near

The Moon is much smaller than the Earth, like a tennis ball next to a soccer ball.

Pocket summary of the lesson. The Moon is a ball of rock covered with craters. It has no air and no water, so nothing lives there. It does not make its own light but reflects sunlight. It is much smaller than the Earth, and much closer to the Earth than the Sun is.
