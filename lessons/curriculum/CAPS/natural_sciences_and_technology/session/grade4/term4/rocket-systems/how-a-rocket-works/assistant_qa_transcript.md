### subtopic_1 — Push Back, Move Forward
**Assistant:** How fast does a rocket have to go to get into space?

**Tutor:** Very, very fast. To get into space and stay there, travelling around the Earth like a satellite, a rocket must reach a speed of about twenty-eight thousand kilometres an hour. That is fast enough to go from Cape Town to Johannesburg in about three minutes. If it goes slower than that, the Earth's pull will drag it back down. To travel to the Moon, a spacecraft must go even faster, about forty thousand kilometres an hour, to break free from the Earth's pull. That is why rockets need so much fuel. Most of the weight of a rocket on the launch pad is fuel, and it burns almost all of it in just a few minutes. The rocket starts off slowly, rising gently from the launch pad, and then speeds up faster and faster as it climbs and burns off its fuel, getting lighter all the time. Astronauts feel pressed back hard into their seats as the rocket speeds up.

### subtopic_2 — The Parts of a Rocket
**Assistant:** Can I make a rocket at home?

**Tutor:** Yes, you can make safe rockets that use air or water instead of fuel, but always ask an adult to help. The balloon rocket on a string is the easiest. You need a long string, a drinking straw, a balloon and some sticky tape. Thread the straw onto the string, tie the string tightly between two chairs, blow up the balloon, tape it to the straw and let go. Try different balloon shapes and sizes to see which goes furthest. With an adult, you can also make a water bottle rocket from an empty plastic cold drink bottle, a little water, a cork and a bicycle pump. The pump pushes air into the bottle until the pressure pops it off its launcher, and the water is pushed out of the back so the bottle shoots up. This must be done outside, in an open space, with everyone standing well back. Never, ever try to make a rocket that uses fire, fuel or fireworks.

### subtopic_3 — A Rocket Is a System
**Assistant:** What happens to the parts of a rocket after the launch?

**Tutor:** It depends on the rocket. For most of the history of space travel, rockets could only be used once. The lower stages fell into the ocean after their fuel was used up, and the upper stage often stayed in space or burnt up when it fell back through the air. That made space travel very expensive, a bit like throwing away an aeroplane after one flight. Today some companies have built rockets whose bottom stage can fly back down and land upright on a landing pad or on a ship at sea, using its engines to slow down. These stages can be checked, refuelled and flown again many times, which saves a lot of money. The capsules that carry astronauts come back to the Earth too, slowed down by parachutes and protected from the heat by a special shield. The payload, such as a satellite, stays in space to do its job, sometimes for many years.
