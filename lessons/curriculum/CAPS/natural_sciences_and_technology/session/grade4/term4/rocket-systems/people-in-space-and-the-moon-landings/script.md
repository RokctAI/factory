# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers how people have used rockets to travel into space and to the Moon. The anchor is a science centre in Cape Town. A Grade 4 class sits in a dark room and watches a big screen. On it is old black and white film from 1969. A huge rocket roars off its launch pad. Days later, a strange spider-like spacecraft lands on the grey dust of the Moon. A person in a bulky white space suit climbs slowly down a ladder and steps onto the Moon. The class guide, Mr Abrahams, tells them that more than six hundred million people around the world watched that moment live on television. The big idea is that people have used rockets to go into space and to travel to the Moon, and that space travel has grown from one small satellite to astronauts living in space today. Describing the main events in the story of space travel is examinable.

## Subtopic: The First Steps into Space

For thousands of years, people looked at the Moon and stars and wondered if they could go there. To leave the Earth, you need something very powerful, because the Earth's pull, gravity, holds everything down. The answer was the rocket. A rocket is a machine that pushes itself upwards very fast, fast enough to escape into space. We will learn how a rocket works in the next lesson.

In 1957, a country called the Soviet Union used a rocket to send the first satellite into space. A satellite is an object that travels around the Earth in space. The first one was a small metal ball with long aerials. It sent out beeping radio signals that people could hear on radios all over the world.

Soon, animals were sent into space to test whether living things could survive there. Later in 1957, a dog named Laika became the first animal to travel around the Earth. Scientists learnt from these flights how to keep living things alive in space, with air to breathe, the right temperature and protection from the shaking of the launch. Then, in 1961, a man from the Soviet Union named Yuri Gagarin became the first person to travel into space. He flew once around the Earth in a small round spacecraft at the top of a rocket. The trip took less than two hours. When he came back, he was a hero all over the world. Two years later, Valentina Tereshkova became the first woman to travel into space.

The questions for this section are with you now: the first satellite and the first people in space.

## Subtopic: Landing on the Moon

The United States of America then set a huge goal: to land people on the Moon and bring them safely home. To do this, they built the Saturn Five, one of the most powerful rockets ever built. It was taller than a thirty-storey building.

In July 1969, the Apollo Eleven mission set off with three astronauts: Neil Armstrong, Buzz Aldrin and Michael Collins. The rocket carried them into space, and they travelled for about three days to reach the Moon. Michael Collins stayed in the main spacecraft, flying around and around the Moon. Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin flew down to the surface in a small landing craft called the Eagle. The landing was difficult. The computer was guiding them towards an area full of large boulders, so Neil Armstrong took control and flew the Eagle to a flat, safe spot, with very little fuel left. Then he radioed the Earth with the famous words: the Eagle has landed.

On the twentieth of July 1969, Neil Armstrong became the first person to step onto the Moon. He said: that is one small step for a man, one giant leap for mankind. Buzz Aldrin joined him soon after. They collected Moon rocks and dust, took photographs, set up science experiments and planted a flag. Then they flew back up to Michael Collins, and all three returned safely to the Earth. Their spacecraft splashed down in the Pacific Ocean.

There were six Moon landings in all, between 1969 and 1972. Twelve astronauts walked on the Moon. On the later trips, astronauts even drove a small electric car called a Moon buggy across the surface. Nobody has walked on the Moon since 1972. The Moon rocks the astronauts brought back are still studied by scientists today, and they have taught us a great deal about how the Moon formed.

The questions for this section are with you now: the Moon landings.

## Subtopic: People in Space Today

Today, people live and work in space all the time. The International Space Station is a large spacecraft that travels around the Earth. Astronauts from many countries live on it for months at a time. They do experiments, look after the station, and exercise every day to keep their muscles strong. The station goes all the way around the Earth about every one and a half hours. On a clear evening, you can sometimes see it from South Africa, as a bright, steady dot moving quickly across the sky.

In 2002, Mark Shuttleworth became the first South African, and the first person from Africa, to travel into space. He flew on a Russian rocket to the International Space Station and spent about eight days there doing experiments.

Rockets also launch thousands of satellites. Satellites help us with weather reports, television, phone calls and maps that show where you are. South African engineers and students have built small satellites too. Rockets have also sent robot spacecraft to every planet in the solar system, and robot cars called rovers drive across the surface of Mars.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: saying the first person in space walked on the Moon; Yuri Gagarin flew around the Earth in 1961, and Neil Armstrong walked on the Moon in 1969. Two: saying only one person has walked on the Moon; twelve astronauts have. Three: saying all three Apollo Eleven astronauts walked on the Moon; Michael Collins stayed in orbit. Four: saying no South African has been to space; Mark Shuttleworth went in 2002.
