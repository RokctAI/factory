# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers how a rocket works. The anchor is a primary school in Pretoria, Gauteng. Mrs Botha's Grade 4 class has strung long pieces of fishing line from one side of the classroom to the other. Each group threads a drinking straw onto a line. They blow up a balloon, hold the neck closed, and tape the balloon to the straw. On the count of three, they let go. Air rushes out of the back of the balloon, and the balloon zooms forward along the string. The fastest balloon crosses the whole classroom in about two seconds. The big idea is that a rocket is a system that moves forward by pushing gases out of its back end. The faster and harder it pushes gas backwards, the harder it is pushed forwards. Explaining how a rocket moves and naming its main parts is examinable.

## Subtopic: Push Back, Move Forward

Why did the balloon move? When you blow up a balloon, you squeeze lots of air into it, and the stretchy rubber squeezes that air hard. When you let go of the neck, the rubber pushes the air out of the back of the balloon. As the air is pushed out backwards, the balloon is pushed forwards.

This is a rule that works everywhere: when something pushes, it is pushed back the other way. Think about these examples. When you stand on a skateboard and throw a heavy ball forwards, you roll backwards. When you jump off a small boat onto the shore, the boat moves away from the shore. When a fire hose sprays water forwards, it pushes back so hard that firefighters must hold it firmly. When you swim, you push water backwards with your arms, and the water pushes you forwards.

A rocket works in exactly the same way. It pushes huge amounts of gas out of its back end, very fast. The gas is pushed down, so the rocket is pushed up. The faster the gas shoots out, and the more gas there is, the harder the rocket is pushed.

The gas that comes out of the back of a rocket is called exhaust gas. A car also has exhaust gas coming out of its exhaust pipe, but a car moves because its engine turns its wheels. A rocket has no wheels. It moves only because of the push of its exhaust gas.

The questions for this section are with you now: how pushing gas backwards moves a rocket forwards.

## Subtopic: The Parts of a Rocket

A real rocket is much more powerful than a balloon. Instead of squeezed air, it uses fuel.

The main part of a rocket is a long tube called the body. Inside the body are big tanks that hold the fuel. At the bottom is the engine. In the engine, the fuel is burnt. Burning makes huge amounts of very hot gas, which shoots out of a bell-shaped opening called the nozzle at the bottom of the rocket. This pushes the rocket upwards.

At the top of the rocket is the pointed nose cone. Its pointed shape helps the rocket cut through the air smoothly. The nose cone protects the payload. The payload is what the rocket is carrying into space, such as a satellite, a robot spacecraft, or a capsule with astronauts inside.

Many rockets have fins near the bottom. Fins are like the feathers on an arrow. They help to keep the rocket flying straight.

Big space rockets are usually built in parts called stages, stacked on top of each other. The bottom stage fires first. When its fuel is used up, it drops off and falls away, so the rocket is lighter. Then the next stage fires. This way the rocket does not have to carry empty, heavy tanks all the way to space.

The questions for this section are with you now: the parts of a rocket and what each one does.

## Subtopic: A Rocket Is a System

In Technology, a system is a set of parts that work together to do a job. Every system has an input, a process and an output. A rocket is a system.

The input is the fuel stored in the rocket's tanks. The process is burning the fuel in the engine, which makes hot gas, and pushing that gas out of the nozzle. The output is movement: the rocket is pushed up into space.

All the parts must work together. If the fuel tanks leak, the engine has nothing to burn. If the nozzle is blocked, the gas cannot escape. If the fins are bent, the rocket wobbles off course.

The system also has controls. Computers on board check the rocket's direction many times every second and turn the engine nozzle a tiny bit to steer, the way you steer a bicycle by turning the handlebars. Engineers on the ground watch every part of the flight on their screens.

Fire needs air to burn. But there is no air in space. So how can a rocket burn its fuel in space? A rocket carries its own supply of oxygen, the part of air that fire needs, in a separate tank. It mixes the fuel and the oxygen in the engine. That is why a rocket can work where there is no air at all.

People sometimes think a rocket moves by pushing against the ground or the air. It does not. It moves by pushing its own exhaust gas out backwards. That is why it works even better in empty space.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: saying a rocket pushes against the ground to fly; it pushes its own exhaust gas backwards. Two: saying rockets cannot work in space because there is no air; rockets carry their own oxygen. Three: saying the gas comes out of the nose; it comes out of the nozzle at the back end. Four: saying the payload is the fuel; the payload is what the rocket carries, like a satellite or astronauts.

The questions for this section are with you now: a rocket as a system.
