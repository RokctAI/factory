### subtopic_1 — The First Steps into Space
**Assistant:** How do astronauts eat, sleep and go to the toilet in space?

**Tutor:** Life on the space station is very different, because everything floats. Astronauts eat food from packets and tins. Some food is dried and they add water to it. They avoid crumbly food like bread, because crumbs would float around and could get into their eyes or the machines, so they often use flat tortillas instead. Drinks come in pouches with straws. To sleep, astronauts zip themselves into sleeping bags that are tied to the wall, so they do not drift around while they sleep. Up and down do not matter, so they can sleep standing up or upside down. The toilet uses a fan that sucks air, a bit like a vacuum cleaner, to pull waste away, because without gravity nothing would fall down. Water is very precious, so the station even cleans and recycles the water from the astronauts' sweat and urine to use again. Astronauts say the hardest part is not the toilet, but missing their families.

### subtopic_2 — Landing on the Moon
**Assistant:** Why has nobody walked on the Moon since 1972?

**Tutor:** Going to the Moon was extremely expensive and dangerous. In the 1960s, the United States spent huge amounts of money on the Apollo missions because it was in a race with the Soviet Union to be first. Once they had won the race and landed six times, the government decided to spend the money on other things. Space agencies then focused on closer goals, like building space stations around the Earth, launching satellites and sending robot spacecraft to explore other planets, which is cheaper and safer than sending people. But that is changing. Several countries and companies are now building new rockets and spacecraft and have plans to send people back to the Moon, and this time to stay longer and even build bases there. Some of the astronauts who will one day walk on the Moon or Mars may be children today, perhaps even someone in a Grade 4 class in South Africa.

### subtopic_3 — People in Space Today
**Assistant:** How did people on Earth watch the Moon landing live?

**Tutor:** The Apollo Eleven astronauts carried a special television camera. When Neil Armstrong climbed down the ladder, the camera was fixed to the side of the landing craft, pointing at him. It sent pictures as radio signals across the three hundred and eighty-four thousand kilometres of space back to the Earth. Big dish-shaped radio antennas on the Earth caught the signals. Because the Earth spins, there were antennas in different countries so that one of them could always face the Moon. The pictures were black and white and quite blurry, but people around the world were amazed. Some people in South Africa heard about the landing on the radio, because television only started in South Africa in 1976. Even today, the radio signals from spacecraft are caught by big dishes on the Earth, including one at Hartebeesthoek, near Pretoria, which helps to track satellites and spacecraft.
