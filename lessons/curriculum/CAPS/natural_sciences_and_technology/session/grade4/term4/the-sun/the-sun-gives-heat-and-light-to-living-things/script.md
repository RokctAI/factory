# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers how the Sun gives heat and light to living things. The anchor is a grape farm near Upington in the Northern Cape, on the banks of the Orange River. Upington is one of the sunniest places in South Africa, with clear blue skies on most days of the year. The river water lets farmers grow green vineyards in the middle of a dry land. A Grade 4 class visits the farm in summer. Mr Visser, the farmer, shows them rows of grapes laid out on racks in the hot sun. After a few weeks, the sun will dry the grapes into raisins. The big idea is that the Sun gives living things the light and heat they need, and that without the Sun there could be no life on Earth. Explaining how plants, animals and people use the Sun's heat and light is examinable.

## Subtopic: Plants Need Sunlight

In Term 3 we learnt that plants make their own food in their leaves using sunlight, water and a gas from the air. Sunlight is the energy that powers this. Without light, a plant cannot make food, and it will die.

The grape vines on Mr Visser's farm grow long, leafy branches in summer. The leaves catch the sunlight and make food, and the plant stores some of that food as sugar in the grapes. Lots of sunshine makes very sweet grapes.

Plants grow towards the light. If you put a pot plant on a windowsill, after a few days its stems bend towards the window. Young sunflower plants even turn their heads to follow the Sun across the sky during the day.

Because plants need light, they grow best in sunny places. In a thick forest, tall trees catch most of the light, and only plants that can live in the shade grow on the dark forest floor.

Animals and people need plants for food, and plants need the Sun. So the Sun's light keeps all of us alive. It also gives us the light to see by during the day.

Farmers know that seasons with more sunshine and longer days help crops grow. In summer, the days in Upington are long, with many hours of strong sunshine, so the vines grow quickly. In winter, the days are shorter and the vines drop their leaves and rest until spring.

The questions for this section are with you now: why plants need the Sun's light.

## Subtopic: Animals Need the Sun's Heat

The Sun's heat warms the land, the water and the air. Without it, the Earth would be frozen solid, and nothing could live here.

Some animals cannot make their own body heat. Lizards, snakes, tortoises, frogs and crocodiles depend on the Sun to warm their bodies. On a cold morning, a lizard is slow and sleepy. It climbs onto a sunny rock to warm up, and once it is warm, it can run fast and catch insects. When it gets too hot at midday, the lizard moves into the shade or under a rock to cool down.

Other animals, like birds and mammals, make their own body heat from the food they eat. But many of them still use the Sun. In the Kalahari, meerkats stand up on their back legs in the morning sun with their bellies facing the Sun to warm up after a cold night. Dassies lie on warm rocks in the sun.

Nile crocodiles in the rivers of Mpumalanga and KwaZulu-Natal crawl out onto sandbanks to warm up in the sun. On a very hot day, they lie with their mouths wide open, which helps them cool down. The Sun warms the water too. Warm, shallow water near the shore is where many fish lay their eggs and where tadpoles grow quickly into frogs.

The Sun's heat and light also tell animals when to do things. Many birds sing at sunrise, and owls hunt after sunset. In winter, when the days are short and cold, some animals, like some kinds of bats, sleep for long periods because there is less food.

The questions for this section are with you now: how animals use the Sun's heat.

## Subtopic: People Use the Sun, and Stay Safe

People use the Sun's heat and light in many ways. Mr Visser uses the Sun to dry grapes into raisins. Long ago, and still today, people dry fruit, fish and washing in the sun. Salt farmers let the Sun dry up sea water or salty lake water, leaving the salt behind.

People also use the Sun to make electricity. Solar panels on roofs change sunlight into electricity. Near Upington there are big solar power stations with thousands of panels and mirrors that make electricity for many homes. Solar water heaters on roofs use the Sun's heat to warm water for washing. Some families cook food in solar cookers, boxes that trap the Sun's heat.

Builders use the Sun too. In South Africa, the winter Sun shines from the north side of the sky. So a house with big windows facing north lets in warm winter sunshine and needs less heating. A roof that sticks out over those windows shades them from the high summer Sun, keeping the house cooler in summer.

And every day, the Sun gives us light to see by. Schools, farms and shops use daylight for most of their work, which saves electricity.

But too much sun can harm us. The Sun's rays can burn our skin, causing painful sunburn. Too much heat can make us thirsty, dizzy and ill. To stay safe, wear a hat, put on sunscreen, drink plenty of water and stay in the shade at midday, when the Sun is strongest. Never leave children or pets in a car parked in the sun.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: saying only plants need the Sun; animals and people need its heat, and they eat plants that need its light. Two: thinking a lizard is lazy when it lies on a rock; it is warming its body. Three: saying the Sun only gives light; it gives heat and light. Four: thinking sunburn only happens in summer; the Sun can burn skin in winter too.

The questions for this section are with you now: how people use the Sun and stay safe.
