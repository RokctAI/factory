# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers why the Earth gets the right amount of light and heat from the Sun to support life. The anchor is a Grade 4 school camp in the Drakensberg in KwaZulu-Natal. On a clear winter night, the air in the mountains is icy, and the learners gather around a big campfire. Bongani sits right up against the fire, and soon his face is so hot that he has to move back. Amahle sits far away at the edge of the circle, and she is shivering. Thabo sits in the middle, not too close and not too far, and he is just warm enough. Their teacher, Mrs Zungu, smiles and says that the Earth sits around the Sun just like Thabo sits around the fire. The big idea is that the Earth is at just the right distance from the Sun, so it gets enough light and heat for life, but not too much. Explaining why the Earth can support life is examinable.

## Subtopic: Closer Is Hotter, Further Is Colder

Like a campfire, the Sun gives out heat and light in all directions. The closer you are to the Sun, the more heat and light you get. The further away you are, the less you get.

Venus is closer to the Sun than the Earth. It is extremely hot, hotter than the inside of a kitchen oven, hot enough to melt some metals. Thick clouds wrap around Venus and trap the heat. No water can stay on Venus, and no living thing could survive there.

Mercury is the closest planet to the Sun. In the day, its ground is burning hot. Mercury has almost no air, so at night the heat escapes into space and it becomes freezing cold, much colder than any freezer.

Mars is further from the Sun than the Earth. It is very cold, much colder than the coldest winter night in the Drakensberg. The water on Mars is frozen as ice. Mars also has very thin air that people could not breathe.

The giant planets further out, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune, are even colder, because they are so far away that the Sun looks like a small, bright star from there.

So, just like the campfire, being too close is too hot, and being too far is too cold.

The questions for this section are with you now: why some planets are too hot and others too cold.

## Subtopic: The Earth Is Just Right

The Earth is in the middle zone, not too close and not too far. It gets just the right amount of heat and light from the Sun.

The most important result is water. On the Earth, most of the water is liquid, in oceans, rivers, lakes and rain. If the Earth were too close to the Sun, the water would all boil away into gas. If it were too far, the water would all freeze into ice. All living things need liquid water to live, so this is very important.

The right amount of sunlight also means plants can make food. Remember, plants need light to make food, and animals and people need plants. Not too much heat means plants do not dry out and burn. Not too little heat means they do not freeze.

The Earth also spins once a day. This means no part of the Earth faces the Sun all the time, so no side gets far too hot, and no side stays dark and cold for too long. Day warms the land, and night lets it cool down.

Scientists call this middle zone around a star, where water can stay liquid, the habitable zone. Habitable means a place where living things can live. The Earth is right inside the Sun's habitable zone.

Even on the Earth, some places are very hot, like the Kalahari in summer, and some are very cold, like the top of the Drakensberg in winter or the ice of Antarctica. But even the hottest and coldest places on Earth are mild compared to Venus or Mars, and living things that are suited to them can survive there.

The questions for this section are with you now: why the Earth's distance from the Sun is just right for life.

## Subtopic: Air and Water Help

The distance from the Sun is not the only reason the Earth is just right. The Earth's layer of air helps too.

The air acts like a blanket around the Earth. During the day, the Sun warms the land and the sea. At night, the blanket of air holds some of that heat in, so it does not all escape into space. Without the air, the Earth would be much colder, especially at night. The Moon is the same distance from the Sun as the Earth, but it has no air. Its days are boiling hot and its nights are freezing cold, and nothing lives there.

The air also protects living things. It blocks some of the Sun's harmful rays that could hurt plants, animals and people. And it contains oxygen, the gas that animals and people breathe in, and the gas that plants need to make food.

The oceans help too. Water warms up slowly and cools down slowly, so the oceans help to keep the temperature of the Earth steady.

As far as scientists know, the Earth is the only planet where living things are found. Light and heat from the Sun, liquid water and a layer of air all work together to make it our home.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking the closer to the Sun, the better for life; Venus is closer and far too hot. Two: thinking that because the Moon is as close to the Sun as the Earth, it must be just right; it has no air, so its days are boiling and its nights freezing. Three: thinking only heat matters; living things also need liquid water, light and air. Four: thinking the air does nothing; it keeps us warm at night and blocks harmful rays.

The questions for this section are with you now: how the air and water help the Earth support life.
