### subtopic_1 — The Earth Orbits the Sun
**Assistant:** What happened to Pluto?

**Tutor:** For many years, children learnt that there were nine planets, and Pluto was the ninth, furthest from the Sun. Pluto is a small, icy world, even smaller than our Moon. As telescopes got better, scientists started finding more icy worlds far out past Neptune, and some of them were about the same size as Pluto. Scientists had to decide whether to call them all planets, which would mean many more planets, or to make a new group. In 2006, a big meeting of astronomers decided on a clear meaning for the word planet. A planet must travel around the Sun, be big enough to pull itself into a round shape, and be big enough to have cleared other objects out of its path. Pluto does the first two, but not the third, because it shares its path with many other icy objects. So Pluto is now called a dwarf planet. It is still out there, and it is still interesting. Only its name changed.

### subtopic_2 — The Sun at the Centre
**Assistant:** Can we see other planets without a telescope?

**Tutor:** Yes, five of them. Mercury, Venus, Mars, Jupiter and Saturn can all be seen with just your eyes, if you know where and when to look. They look like bright stars, but they usually do not twinkle as much as stars do. They also slowly change their position among the stars over weeks and months, because they are moving along their orbits. The ancient Greek word for planet means wanderer, because of this. Venus is the brightest. It is often seen low in the west just after sunset, or low in the east just before sunrise, so people sometimes call it the evening star or the morning star, even though it is a planet. Mars looks slightly reddish, and Jupiter is a bright, steady white light. Uranus and Neptune are so far away and so faint that you need a telescope to see them. On a clear night, ask an adult to help you find a planet using a sky map or a phone app.

### subtopic_3 — Eight Planets
**Assistant:** Is the Earth's orbit a perfect circle?

**Tutor:** Not quite. The Earth's orbit is very slightly squashed, so it is a little bit like an oval. But it is so close to a circle that if you drew it on a page, it would look just like a circle to your eye. The Earth is a little closer to the Sun at the start of January, and a little further away at the start of July. Some people think that this is what causes summer and winter, but it is not. In South Africa it is actually summer in January, when the Earth is closest to the Sun, but in Europe and North America it is winter at that same time. If being close to the Sun caused summer, everyone on Earth would have summer at the same time. Seasons are really caused by the way the Earth is tilted as it travels around the Sun, which you will learn more about in later grades.
