# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers how the Earth moves around the Sun, and the eight planets of the solar system. The anchor is a primary school in Bloemfontein in the Free State. It is Naledi's tenth birthday, and her name means star in Sesotho. Her teacher, Mr Mofokeng, tells the class that Naledi has just finished her tenth trip around the Sun. To show what he means, the class goes out to the playground. Naledi stands in the middle holding a yellow sign that says Sun. Eight classmates hold signs with the names of the planets and walk in big circles around her. The big idea is that the Earth travels around the Sun in a path called an orbit, that the Sun is at the centre of the solar system, and that the Earth is one of eight planets. Describing the Earth's orbit and naming the eight planets in order is examinable.

## Subtopic: The Earth Orbits the Sun

The Earth moves around the Sun all the time. The path it follows is called an orbit. The Earth's orbit is almost a circle, with the Sun near the middle.

One complete trip around the Sun takes one year. That is about three hundred and sixty-five days. So every time you have a birthday, the Earth has gone all the way around the Sun once since your last birthday. When Naledi turns ten, she has travelled around the Sun ten times.

In fact, one trip takes about three hundred and sixty-five and a quarter days. Those extra quarter days add up. Every four years, we add one extra day to the calendar, the twenty-ninth of February. A year with that extra day is called a leap year, and it has three hundred and sixty-six days. A person born on the twenty-ninth of February only has a real birthday once every four years.

The Earth's orbit is enormous, and the Earth moves very fast to get around it in one year, about thirty kilometres every second. That is much faster than any aeroplane. We do not feel it, because everything around us is moving with us.

Remember that the Earth also spins, once every day. So the Earth is doing two things at the same time. It spins around once a day, which gives us day and night, and it travels around the Sun once a year. Naledi shows this on the playground by turning around slowly while she walks in a circle around the Sun sign.

The questions for this section are with you now: the Earth's orbit and what makes a year.

## Subtopic: The Sun at the Centre

The Sun is at the centre of the solar system. The solar system is the Sun and everything that travels around it: the planets, their moons and many smaller rocks and lumps of ice. The word solar means to do with the Sun.

Why do the planets go around the Sun and not fly off into space? The Sun is so big that it has a very strong pull, called gravity. Gravity pulls the planets towards the Sun. At the same time, the planets are moving fast along their orbits. The pull and the movement together keep each planet going around and around the Sun, like a ball whirled on a string.

The solar system has many other travellers too. Our Moon goes around the Earth, and most of the other planets have moons of their own. Jupiter and Saturn each have dozens. Between Mars and Jupiter there is a wide belt of rocks of many sizes, called asteroids. Further out there are icy lumps called comets. When a comet comes close to the Sun, some of its ice turns to gas and dust, and it grows a long, glowing tail.

Long ago, many people thought the Earth was at the centre, and that the Sun moved around the Earth. It looks that way, because we see the Sun rise and set. But careful study of the sky showed that the Sun is at the centre, and the Earth is one of the planets moving around it.

The questions for this section are with you now: why the Sun is at the centre of the solar system.

## Subtopic: Eight Planets

There are eight planets in the solar system. In order from the Sun, they are Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune. A sentence can help you remember the order: My Very Easy Method Just Speeds Up Naming. The first letter of each word is the first letter of a planet.

The four planets closest to the Sun are smaller and made of rock. Mercury is the smallest planet and the closest to the Sun. Venus is the hottest planet, covered in thick clouds. Earth is the third planet from the Sun and the only one we know of with living things. Mars is a cold, dusty planet that looks reddish, so it is called the red planet.

The four planets further out are giant planets with no solid ground to stand on. Jupiter is the biggest planet of all. More than a thousand Earths could fit inside it. Saturn has beautiful bright rings made of pieces of ice and rock. Uranus and Neptune are very far away and very cold. Neptune is the furthest planet from the Sun and takes about one hundred and sixty-five of our years to go around the Sun once.

The further a planet is from the Sun, the longer its orbit and the longer its year. Mercury's year is less than three of our months.

In Bloemfontein, Mr Mofokeng shows this on the playground. The learner holding the Mercury sign walks a tiny circle close to Naledi and gets around quickly. The learner with the Neptune sign has to walk a huge circle at the edge of the field and is still walking long after the others.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: saying the Sun moves around the Earth; the Earth moves around the Sun. Two: saying one orbit takes one day; one orbit takes one year, and one spin takes one day. Three: saying there are nine planets; there are eight, since Pluto is now called a dwarf planet. Four: saying the Earth is the biggest planet; Jupiter is the biggest.

The questions for this section are with you now: the eight planets and their order.
