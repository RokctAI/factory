# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the shape of the Earth and what its surface is made of: land, water and air. The anchor is Cape Agulhas, the most southern point of Africa, near the town of Bredasdorp in the Western Cape. Mr Jacobs takes his Grade 4 class to the lighthouse there. A stone sign on the rocks marks the place where the Atlantic Ocean and the Indian Ocean meet. Lerato holds a globe, and the class finds the tiny tip of Africa where they are standing. To the south there is nothing but water until the frozen land of Antarctica, thousands of kilometres away. The big idea is that the Earth is a huge ball of rock, that most of its surface is covered by water, that the land is made of continents and islands, and that a thin layer of air surrounds it all. Describing the shape of the Earth and naming its main surface features is examinable.

## Subtopic: The Earth Is a Sphere of Rock

The Earth is shaped like a ball. A ball shape is called a sphere. A globe is a small model of the Earth, so a globe is a sphere too.

The Earth is enormous. It is about twelve thousand seven hundred kilometres across. If you could drive a car straight through the middle at one hundred kilometres an hour, it would take more than five days without stopping. The Earth is so big that when we stand on it, the ground looks flat. That is why, long ago, many people thought the Earth was flat. Today we have photographs taken from space that show the whole round Earth.

The Earth is made mostly of rock. The outside layer, the ground we walk on, is a thin crust of solid rock and soil. Deep inside, the Earth is very hot. In some places the rock is so hot that it is melted, and it can come out of volcanoes as lava.

The rocky surface is not smooth. It has high mountains, like the Drakensberg in KwaZulu-Natal and Lesotho, flat plains, like the Highveld, and deep valleys. Even the floor under the ocean is rock, with its own mountains and valleys hidden under the water. Some parts of the rocky crust are covered by soil, where plants grow, and some parts are bare rock, like the cliffs of Table Mountain. Sand on a beach is also rock, broken into tiny grains by waves over a very long time.

The questions for this section are with you now: the shape of the Earth and what it is made of.

## Subtopic: Oceans, Continents, Islands and Air

The surface of the Earth has three main parts: land, water and air.

Water covers most of the Earth's surface. About seven out of every ten parts of the surface are covered by oceans and seas. That is why the Earth looks blue from space, and why it is sometimes called the blue planet. An ocean is a huge body of salty water. There are five oceans: the Pacific, the Atlantic, the Indian, the Southern and the Arctic Ocean. The Pacific is the biggest. South Africa has the Atlantic Ocean on its west coast and the Indian Ocean on its east coast. A sea is a smaller part of an ocean, often partly closed in by land. The Earth also has fresh water in rivers, lakes and ice, like the Orange River and the Vaal Dam, but most water on Earth is salty sea water.

Land covers the rest of the surface. The biggest pieces of land are called continents. There are seven continents: Africa, Asia, Europe, North America, South America, Australia and Antarctica. Asia is the biggest, and Africa is the second biggest. South Africa is a country on the continent of Africa. A smaller piece of land with water all around it is called an island. Robben Island near Cape Town is a small island. Madagascar, off the east coast of Africa, is a very big island.

Around the whole Earth is a thin layer of air. We cannot see air, but we feel it as wind and we breathe it. The layer of air is very thin compared to the size of the Earth, about as thin as the skin on an apple. The higher you go, the thinner the air becomes. That is why climbers on very high mountains often carry bottles of oxygen. This layer of air keeps the Earth warm and protects living things.

The questions for this section are with you now: oceans, seas, continents, islands and the layer of air.

## Subtopic: Land, Water and Air Together

At Cape Agulhas, the class can see all three parts of the Earth's surface at once. They stand on rocks, which are land. They look at the ocean, which is water. The wind blows their caps around, which is air.

Land, water and air work together. Water from the ocean evaporates into the air, forms clouds and falls as rain on the land. Rivers carry the rain water back to the sea. Remember the water cycle from Term 2: it moves water between the land, the oceans and the air all the time. Living things need all three parts. Plants grow in the soil on land, fish swim in water, and almost every living thing needs the air.

A map is a flat picture of the Earth, while a globe shows the real round shape. On both, water is usually coloured blue. Lerato spins the globe and sees that it is mostly blue. She can find Africa easily, but Antarctica is a white patch at the bottom, covered in thick ice.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: saying the Earth is flat because the ground looks flat; it is a sphere, so big that we cannot see the curve. Two: saying land covers most of the Earth; oceans and seas cover about seven out of ten parts. Three: mixing up continents and countries; South Africa is a country, Africa is the continent. Four: thinking the air goes on forever; it is a thin layer around the Earth.

The questions for this section are with you now: how land, water and air make up the Earth's surface.

A globe is a small model of the Earth.

The land is made of seven continents and many islands. Africa is a continent.

A thin layer of air covers the Earth.

Hold the blue globe in mind and try this part's questions: oceans, land and air.
