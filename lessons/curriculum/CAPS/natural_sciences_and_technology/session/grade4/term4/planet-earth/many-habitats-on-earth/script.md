# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the many different habitats on Earth. The anchor is a road trip by the Ndlovu family during the school holidays. They start near Upington in the Northern Cape, at the edge of the Kalahari, and drive across the country through grasslands and mountains, all the way to the forests near Knysna and the sea at the coast. Sipho, who is in Grade 4, keeps a notebook. At every stop he writes down what the land looks like, how hot or wet it is, and which plants and animals he sees. In Term 1 we learnt that a habitat is the place where a plant or animal lives and finds everything it needs. The big idea now is that the Earth has many different habitats, and that each one has plants and animals suited to it. Naming different habitats and describing how they differ is examinable.

## Subtopic: What Makes Habitats Different

The Earth's surface is made of land, water and air, but it is not the same everywhere. Some places are hot and some are cold. Some places get lots of rain and some get very little. Some places are high up on mountains and some are deep under the sea.

These differences make different habitats. When Sipho looks out of the car window, he notices the land changing. Near Upington the ground is red sand, the sun is hot, and there are only a few thorny trees and dry grasses. Further east the land turns into wide, grassy plains. In the mountains it is cooler, and there are streams and rocks. Near Knysna it is green and wet, with tall trees.

Things that make a habitat different include how hot or cold it is, how much water there is, what kind of soil or ground it has, and how much sunlight reaches it. These things decide which plants can grow there, and the plants decide which animals can find food and shelter there.

A habitat can be very big, like a whole desert or an ocean. A habitat can also be very small, like a rotting log, a rock pool at the beach or the soil under a stone. A rock pool is home to small fish, sea snails and anemones. The soil under a stone is home to earthworms, ants and woodlice. Big or small, every habitat must give its living things food, water, shelter and a safe place to have young.

The questions for this section are with you now: what makes one habitat different from another.

## Subtopic: Habitats on Land

The first stop is the Kalahari. It is very dry, with hot days and cold nights. Meerkats live in burrows under the sand, where it stays cooler. Gemsbok can go a long time without drinking, getting water from the plants they eat, like wild melons. Camel thorn trees have very long roots that reach water deep underground.

The next habitat is grassland. On the Highveld of the Free State and Mpumalanga, there are wide plains covered in grass, with cold, frosty winters and summer thunderstorms. Grass-eating animals like blesbok and black wildebeest graze here. There are very few trees, so many birds nest on the ground.

In the north and east of the country, in places like the Kruger National Park, there is bushveld, also called savanna. It is grassland with many scattered trees and thorn bushes, and it is hot, with rain in summer. Giraffes eat the leaves of tall thorn trees, zebras and buffalo graze the grass, and lions hunt the grazers. Elephants push down small trees to reach the leaves and roots.

In the Drakensberg mountains it is cooler and wetter, with snow on the high peaks in winter. Eland feed on the mountain grasslands, and big birds like vultures nest on the high cliffs.

Near Knysna, the family walks into a forest. It is shady, cool and damp, because there is a lot of rain. Tall yellowwood trees grow here. The Knysna turaco, a bright green bird, flies between the branches, eating fruit.

In the Western Cape, Sipho sees fynbos, a special habitat of small bushes with thin, tough leaves and colourful flowers like proteas. Sugarbirds drink nectar from the protea flowers.

The questions for this section are with you now: deserts, grasslands, mountains, forests and fynbos.

## Subtopic: Habitats in Water and Around the World

Water habitats are just as different. Freshwater habitats include rivers, dams and wetlands. At the iSimangaliso Wetland Park in KwaZulu-Natal, hippos and crocodiles live in the lakes and swamps, and flamingos wade in the shallow water. Reeds and water lilies grow in the wetland, giving frogs, fish and water birds food and places to hide.

The ocean is the biggest habitat on Earth. Along the cold Atlantic coast, Cape fur seals and African penguins live on the rocks and catch fish in the sea. At Sodwana Bay, in the warm Indian Ocean, colourful fish live among coral reefs. Even the ocean has many habitats inside it. Sunny water near the surface, dark water far below, sandy beaches, rocky shores and kelp forests along the Cape coast are all different homes, each with its own living things.

Other places on Earth have habitats we do not have in South Africa. Antarctica is the coldest place on Earth, covered in ice, where emperor penguins and seals live. In the rainforests of central Africa, where it is hot and rains almost every day, gorillas and chimpanzees live among the trees.

Every animal is suited to its own habitat. A penguin could not survive in the Kalahari, and a meerkat could not survive on the ice of Antarctica. That is why, when a habitat is destroyed, the plants and animals that live there cannot simply move somewhere else.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: saying a habitat is always a big place; a rotting log or a rock pool is a habitat too. Two: thinking any animal can live in any habitat; each animal is suited to its own habitat. Three: saying deserts have no living things; meerkats, gemsbok and camel thorn trees live in the Kalahari. Four: saying the ocean is one place with the same animals everywhere; cold and warm seas have different animals.

The questions for this section are with you now: water habitats and habitats around the world.
