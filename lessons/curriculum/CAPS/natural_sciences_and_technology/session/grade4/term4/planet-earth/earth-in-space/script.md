# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the Earth as a planet in space, and the Sun, Moon and stars that we can see from Earth. The anchor is Sutherland, a small town in the Karoo in the Northern Cape. Sutherland is far from big cities, so its nights are very dark and the air is dry and clear. On a hill outside the town are big telescopes, including one of the largest telescopes in the world, called SALT, the Southern African Large Telescope. A Grade 4 class from the town spends an evening with Ms Pieterse, an astronomer, which is a scientist who studies the sky and space. The big idea is that the Earth is a planet travelling through space, and that when we look up, we see the Sun, the Moon and the stars. Describing the Earth as a planet in space and naming what we see in the sky is examinable.

## Subtopic: The Earth Is a Planet in Space

Space is the huge, dark emptiness outside the Earth's layer of air. In space there is no air to breathe, and away from the Sun it is extremely cold.

The Earth is a planet. A planet is a big, round object in space that travels around a star. The Earth travels around the Sun, which is our star. We will learn more about this later in the term. The Earth is not the only planet. There are other planets that also travel around the Sun, like Mars and Jupiter.

From space, the Earth looks like a blue and white ball, with swirls of white cloud over blue oceans and patches of brown and green land. It floats in the darkness of space. Nothing holds it up. It is not resting on anything. The Moon travels around the Earth, and together the Earth and the Moon travel around the Sun.

The Earth also spins around, like a top, once every day. As it spins, our side of the Earth turns to face the Sun and then turns away from it. When our side faces the Sun, it is day. When our side faces away from the Sun, it is night. That is why the Sun seems to rise in the east in the morning, move across the sky and set in the west in the evening. The Sun is not really moving across the sky. We are turning.

## Subtopic: What We See in the Day and Night Sky

In the daytime, the brightest thing in the sky is the Sun. It is so bright that it lights up the whole sky and makes it look blue. Never look straight at the Sun, not even with sunglasses. Its light can damage your eyes forever.

At night, when our side of the Earth faces away from the Sun, the sky turns dark and we can see the Moon and the stars. The Moon is the biggest and brightest thing in the night sky. Sometimes we can also see the Moon in the daytime, as a pale shape in the blue sky.

Are the stars gone during the day? No. The stars are always there, but in the day the Sun's light is so bright that it hides them. It is like trying to see a torch shining far away on a sunny afternoon.

In Sutherland, Ms Pieterse shows the class a pale, cloudy band of light that stretches across the whole sky. This is the Milky Way. It is made of billions of stars so far away that they blur together. Our Sun is one of the stars in the Milky Way.

She also points to the Southern Cross, a group of four bright stars shaped like a kite. Next to it are two very bright stars called the Pointers. People in the southern half of the Earth, like South Africans, use the Southern Cross to find the direction south. Some of the bright dots that do not twinkle much are not stars at all, but planets, like Venus or Jupiter.

The questions for this section are with you now: the Sun, the Moon and the stars in our sky.

## Subtopic: Stars Are Suns Far Away

Each star in the sky is a giant ball of hot, glowing gas, like our Sun. Some stars are even bigger than the Sun. They look like tiny dots only because they are so far away. Our Sun looks big and bright because it is the closest star to the Earth.

How far away are the stars? Light is the fastest thing there is. Light from the Sun takes about eight minutes to reach the Earth. Light from the next closest star takes more than four years. Light from many of the stars we see at night has been travelling for hundreds or even thousands of years. So when you look at the stars, you are seeing light that left them long before you were born.

The Sun and the stars make their own light. The Moon does not. The Moon shines because sunlight falls on it and bounces off towards us, a bit like a mirror. Planets shine in the same way.

A telescope makes faraway things look bigger and brighter. With the giant telescope at Sutherland, scientists can see stars and groups of stars that are much too faint to see with our eyes alone. The darker the sky, the more stars we can see. In a big city, the bright street lights and buildings make the sky glow, so many stars disappear.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: saying the stars disappear in the daytime; they are still there, but the Sun's light hides them. Two: saying the Sun moves around the sky each day; the Earth spins, so the Sun only seems to move. Three: saying the Moon makes its own light; it reflects sunlight. Four: saying stars are small; they are huge, but very far away.

The questions for this section are with you now: why stars look small and what makes its own light.
