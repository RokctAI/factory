### subtopic_1 — Making Hollow Pillars
**Assistant:** Why does a hollow pillar hold more than a solid stick of the same paper?

**Tutor:** Imagine crumpling a sheet of paper into a tight, solid stick and standing it up. It would be thin and would bend over easily. Now roll the same sheet into a wide, hollow tube. The paper is spread out in a ring, further from the middle. A wider shape is much harder to bend over or buckle, because to bend it, one side must squash and the other side must stretch, and they are far apart. So the hollow tube stands straight and carries the load down through its walls. That is why so many strong things in nature and building are hollow tubes: bamboo, the stems of grasses and reeds, the bones in a bird's wing, scaffolding poles and the steel frames of bicycles. They give lots of strength for very little material, which also keeps them light. A solid pillar of the same width would be stronger still, but it would need far more material and would be much heavier.

### subtopic_2 — Testing the Pillars
**Assistant:** If circle pillars are strongest, why do we see so many square pillars on buildings?

**Tutor:** Strength is not the only thing builders think about. Square and rectangular pillars are easier to make with flat moulds or with bricks, and they fit neatly against square walls, corners and doors. A brick pillar on a stoep is square because bricks are rectangular, so they stack neatly into square shapes. Square pillars are also easier to join to flat beams and walls. In real buildings, pillars are made from concrete with steel bars inside, or from solid bricks, not thin hollow paper. These materials are so strong that a square pillar can easily hold the roof up, even if a round one of the same material would hold a bit more. Round pillars are still used in many places, such as under bridges, in big shopping centres and at the front of grand old buildings, partly for strength and partly because they look good. So builders choose a shape for strength, cost, ease of building and appearance, all together.

### subtopic_3 — Struts from Rolled Paper
**Assistant:** How can we join paper struts to make a frame?

**Tutor:** There are a few easy ways. The simplest is to squash the end of each strut flat for about two centimetres, lay the flat ends on top of each other and wrap them firmly with sticky tape. You can also glue the flattened ends together and hold them with a peg while the glue dries. For a joint that can turn, push a split pin, also called a paper fastener, through both flattened ends and open its legs at the back. Another method is to cut small gussets, which are little triangles of thick cardboard, and glue the ends of the struts onto them. Gussets make the joints much stiffer. Whatever method you use, joints are often the weakest part of a frame, so take care to make them firm. In the next lesson, the class will join struts into triangles and discover that a frame of triangles is much stronger than a frame of squares.
