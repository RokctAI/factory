### subtopic_1 — A Flat Sheet Is Weak
**Assistant:** Why does a folded sheet hold more weight when the folds run along the bridge, not across it?

**Tutor:** Think of each fold as a tiny wall standing on its edge. When the folds run from one pile of books to the other, each little wall stretches all the way across the gap, and it is supported at both ends. A wall standing on its edge is very hard to bend downwards, so the bridge stays stiff and holds lots of coins. If you turn the same folded paper the other way, so that the folds run across the bridge from side to side, none of the little walls reaches from one book to the other. The paper between the folds can bend easily along the fold lines, a bit like a door swinging on its hinges, and the bridge flops down into the gap. It is the same paper with the same folds, but the direction makes all the difference. Builders think about this too. A wooden plank is much stiffer when it stands on its narrow edge than when it lies flat, which is why the beams that hold up floors and roofs are placed on their edges.

### subtopic_2 — Folding and Rolling
**Assistant:** Why is cardboard light but strong?

**Tutor:** Corrugated cardboard is clever because most of it is air. It has a flat sheet on top, a flat sheet underneath, and a wavy sheet glued between them. The wavy sheet holds the two flat sheets apart, so the board is thick, but it uses very little paper. Thickness makes a material much stiffer, so the board resists bending. The waves also act like lots of tiny arches and pillars standing side by side, which hold up weight pressing down on the top sheet. Because so much of the board is air, it stays light, which is very important for boxes that are carried and sent all over the country. A solid block of paper as thick as the cardboard would be strong too, but it would be very heavy and would use far more paper. Corrugated cardboard is a great example of using shape to get strength without adding weight. The same idea is used in corrugated iron roofs and in some aeroplane parts.

### subtopic_3 — Layering and Corrugating
**Assistant:** Can we use these ideas to make other materials stronger, not just paper?

**Tutor:** Yes, and people do it all the time. Metal is a good example. A thin, flat sheet of metal bends easily, but bent into waves it becomes corrugated iron, strong enough for a roof. Bent into a U or an L shape along its length, it becomes a strong beam. Rolled into a tube, it becomes a pipe or a scaffolding pole. Plastic is shaped in the same way. A plastic cool drink bottle has ridges and curves that make its thin walls stiffer. A plastic crate has ribs along its sides. Wood is made stronger by layering thin sheets into plywood. Even nature uses these tricks. A blade of grass is folded along its middle, which helps it stand up. Bamboo is a hollow tube, so it is light and strong. Shells of snails and seeds are curved. The rule is the same for every material: the right shape can make a thin, weak material into a strong one.
