# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers folding and rolling paper into hollow pillars with circular, triangular and square shapes, testing their strength, and rolling paper into tubes called struts that can be used to build structures. The anchor is a pillar contest in a Grade 4 classroom in Bethlehem in the Free State. Each group gets three sheets of A4 paper, sticky tape, a ruler and a stack of the same textbooks. They make one pillar with a circle shape, one with a triangle shape and one with a square shape, all the same height. A thin board is placed on top of each pillar and books are added one at a time until it collapses. The big idea is that paper becomes very strong when it is made into a hollow pillar that is pushed down along its length, and that the shape of the pillar affects how much it can hold. Making and testing paper pillars and making struts are examinable.

## Subtopic: Making Hollow Pillars

A pillar is a tall, upright support that holds up a load from below, like the pillars that hold up a stoep roof or a bridge. A hollow pillar is empty inside, like a tube.

To make a circle pillar, roll a sheet of paper into a tube, overlapping the edges by about two centimetres, and tape the join from top to bottom. Stand it on its end. Its cross-section, the shape you see when you look down on it, is a circle.

To make a triangle pillar, fold a sheet of paper lengthways into three equal strips, plus a narrow flap. Fold along the lines, bring the edges together and tape the flap so that the pillar stands up with three flat sides. Looking down on it, you see a triangle.

To make a square pillar, fold the sheet into four equal strips plus a narrow flap, fold along the lines and tape. Looking down on it, you see a square.

The class must make a fair test. All three pillars use the same size and kind of paper, the same amount of tape, and are the same height. The folds must be sharp and straight, and the top and bottom ends must be cut flat, so the board sits evenly on each pillar. If one pillar were taller, or made from thicker paper, the test would not be fair.

A single sheet of paper standing on its edge flops over at once. The same sheet folded or rolled into a pillar stands up straight and can hold a surprising load. Once again, changing the shape of the paper makes it strong.

The questions for this section are with you now: what a pillar is and how to make circle, triangle and square pillars.

## Subtopic: Testing the Pillars

The groups place a thin board on top of each pillar and add books one at a time, gently, in the middle. They record how many books each pillar holds before it crumples.

In most groups, the circle pillar holds the most books. Some circle pillars hold the weight of a whole stack of textbooks, many times heavier than the paper itself. The triangle and square pillars usually hold less, and different groups find that one or the other does a little better.

Why is the circle strongest? A round tube has no corners and no folds. The weight pressing down is shared evenly all the way around the paper. In a square or triangle pillar, the weight is carried mostly along the folded corners, and the flat sides between them can bend inwards and buckle. Once one side buckles, the whole pillar collapses.

Notice how the pillars fail. They do not tear apart. They crumple and fold, usually starting at a weak spot such as a bent edge, a crease or an uneven top. Neat, careful making helps a pillar hold more.

Pillars made of stronger materials work the same way. Round concrete pillars hold up bridges and the roofs of shopping centres, and steel tubes hold up stadium roofs. Tree trunks are round pillars too.

The class also tries making the circle pillar fatter and thinner. A wide tube made from one sheet has thin walls and can crumple, while a very thin tube is wobbly and bends over. A tube about as wide as a toilet roll works well.

The questions for this section are with you now: testing the pillars and why the circle shape is usually strongest.

## Subtopic: Struts from Rolled Paper

A strut is a stiff part of a structure that holds other parts apart or holds them up. The class learns to make struts from paper. Take a sheet of paper and a thin stick, such as a kebab stick or a pencil. Start at one corner and roll the paper tightly and diagonally around the stick. Put a little glue on the last corner and press it down. Slide out the stick. You now have a thin, light tube that is surprisingly stiff.

Paper struts are strong when they are pushed along their length, like the pillars. But if you push on the middle of a long strut from the side, it bends. Short struts are stiffer than long ones. Two struts taped side by side are stiffer than one.

Struts can be joined to build frame structures. The ends can be squashed flat and joined with tape, glue or a split pin. In the next topic, the class will join struts into triangles to build strong frames, like the roof trusses and pylons they see around Bethlehem.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: making the pillars different heights; that is not a fair test. Two: thinking a square pillar is strongest because it looks solid; its flat sides buckle, and the circle usually wins. Three: dropping books onto the pillars; a sudden knock breaks them early, so add each book gently. Four: rolling struts loosely; a loose roll is floppy, so roll tightly around a stick.

## Subtopic: Stand It Up

A pillar holds up a load from below.

Roll paper for a circle pillar. Fold it in three for a triangle and in four for a square.

Make them all the same height for a fair test.

## Subtopic: Which One Wins?

Add books one at a time.

The circle pillar usually holds the most.

It has no corners, so the weight is shared all the way round. Flat sides buckle.

Hold the winning circle in mind and try this part's questions: testing pillars.

## Subtopic: Thin Strong Tubes

Roll paper tightly around a kebab stick. Glue the end. Slide out the stick.

That is a strut, a stiff tube for building frames.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Paper can be folded or rolled into hollow pillars with circle, triangle or square shapes.
