# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers ways to strengthen materials such as paper so that they can be used to build strong structures. The anchor is a paper bridge challenge in a Grade 4 classroom in Mahikeng in the North West. Each group gets one sheet of A4 paper, some sticky tape and a pile of five rand coins. Two piles of books are set up with a gap between them, about the width of a ruler. The bridge must cross the gap and hold as many coins as possible in the middle. The big idea is that a weak material like paper can be made much stronger and stiffer by changing its shape, by folding, rolling, layering or corrugating it, without adding any new material. Describing and demonstrating ways to strengthen paper is examinable.

## Subtopic: A Flat Sheet Is Weak

The first group lays a flat sheet of paper across the gap. Before they even add a coin, it sags in the middle. They place one coin on it and the paper bends down and the coin slides into the gap. A flat sheet of paper is flexible and weak. It bends as soon as a load is put on it.

Why? Paper is thin. When it lies flat, there is nothing to stop it bending downwards. Think of a flat sheet of cardboard, a flat ruler or a slice of bread lying across a gap: they all sag in the middle, because thin, flat things bend easily.

But paper is not useless. The same sheet can hold a surprising load if we change its shape. Engineers who design bridges, roofs and buildings use this idea all the time. They do not only choose strong materials. They also choose strong shapes. A thin sheet of steel bends easily, but steel shaped into a beam or a tube can hold up a building.

The class makes a list of ideas to try: folding the paper into a zigzag, rolling it into tubes, sticking several layers together, and copying the wavy layer inside a cardboard box. They agree to test each idea fairly. Each bridge uses the same size of paper, the same gap and the same kind of coin, and coins are added one at a time in the middle until the bridge collapses.

The questions for this section are with you now: why a flat sheet of paper is weak and how changing its shape can help.

## Subtopic: Folding and Rolling

Folding. Group two folds the sheet backwards and forwards into narrow pleats, like a fan or the bellows of an accordion. This zigzag shape is called a concertina fold. They lay the folded paper across the gap with the folds running from one book to the other. It does not sag. Coin after coin goes on top, and it holds more than twenty coins before it buckles. The folds make the paper stiff along their length, because each fold stands up like a little wall.

Folding the edges also helps. If you fold up the two long edges of a strip of paper to make a U shape, like a gutter, it becomes much stiffer. Gutters on houses, the rails of a ladder and the edges of metal roof sheets are folded for the same reason.

Rolling. Group three rolls the sheet tightly into a tube and tapes it. One tube makes a narrow bridge that holds several coins. They roll more tubes from another sheet and tape them side by side, and the bridge becomes stronger. A tube is strong because the paper curves all the way around, so there is no flat part that can sag easily. Rolled newspaper tubes are strong enough to build tables and chairs from.

Curving. One group bends a sheet into a curved arch and tucks its ends against the books, then lays a flat sheet on top. The arch pushes up under the flat sheet and holds several coins. Old stone bridges and many doorways use arches for the same reason: a curve carries a load better than a flat sheet.

The questions for this section are with you now: folding and rolling paper to make it stronger.

## Subtopic: Layering and Corrugating

Layering. Group four glues three sheets of paper on top of each other, one sheet at a time, pressing them flat. The layered sheet is thicker and stiffer, and it holds more coins than one flat sheet. Thin layers glued together become a strong board. This is how cardboard for cereal boxes and the thick covers of some books are made. Plywood is made the same way, from thin layers of wood glued together.

Corrugating. Group five looks at the torn edge of a cardboard box. Inside, between two flat sheets, is a wavy sheet of paper. This wavy shape is called corrugated. The waves hold the two flat sheets apart and make the cardboard stiff and strong, but still light. The group copies the idea. They fold a sheet into small even waves, glue a flat sheet above and below, and get a light, stiff board that holds many coins. Corrugated iron roofs on many South African homes use the same idea: the waves make the thin metal sheets strong enough to span between the roof beams.

The class compares the results. The flat sheet held no coins. The folded, rolled, layered and corrugated bridges all held many more. Paper was not changed into a new material. Only its shape changed.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking you need a stronger material to make a strong structure; changing the shape of paper makes it much stronger. Two: laying the folded paper with the folds running across the gap; the folds must run from one side of the gap to the other. Three: comparing bridges with different gaps or different coins; that is not a fair test. Four: thinking a cardboard box is just thick paper; it has a wavy corrugated layer inside that makes it strong.

The questions for this section are with you now: layering and corrugating paper, and comparing the methods fairly.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the paper bridge challenge — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Floppy Paper

A flat sheet of paper bends and sags. It is weak.

You do not need new material to make it strong.

You just change its shape.

Keep the saggy sheet in mind and try the questions for this part: flat paper.

## Subtopic: Fold It, Roll It

Fold paper into a zigzag, like a fan. It becomes stiff.

Roll paper into a tube. It becomes strong.

The folds must run across from one side of the gap to the other.

Hold the zigzag bridge in mind and try this part's questions: folding and rolling.

## Subtopic: Stack It, Wave It

Glue layers on top of each other. Thick layers are stiff.

Make a wavy layer between two flat sheets. That is corrugated cardboard.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A flat sheet of paper is weak and bends easily. We can make paper much stronger by changing its shape: folding it into pleats, rolling it into tubes, gluing layers together, or corrugating it with a wavy layer. Strong structures need strong shapes, not only strong materials.

Hold the cardboard box in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: layering and corrugating.
