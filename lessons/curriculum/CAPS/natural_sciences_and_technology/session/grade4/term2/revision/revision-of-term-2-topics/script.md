# Part 1 — Expert

This session revises the Term 2 topics of Matter and Materials: materials around us, including solids, liquids and gases, changes of state and the water cycle; solid materials, including raw and manufactured materials and their properties; strengthening materials; and strong frame structures. The anchor is a revision poster wall in a Grade 4 classroom in Mbombela, Mpumalanga. The class has made four panels. Panel 1 shows an ice cube, a glass of water and a balloon. Panel 2 shows the water cycle over the Crocodile River. Panel 3 shows raw materials with arrows to the things made from them, and a table of properties. Panel 4 shows a paper bridge, three paper pillars, a roof truss and a pylon. The big idea is that materials behave in ways we can observe and test, that heating and cooling change their state, and that we can choose and shape materials to make strong structures. The whole of Term 2 is examinable.

## Subtopic: States of Matter and the Water Cycle

Panel 1. All materials are solids, liquids or gases. A solid keeps its own shape and amount, like a brick or an ice cube. Sugar and sand can be poured, but each grain is a solid. A liquid flows and takes the shape of its container, but keeps the same amount, like water, milk or honey. A gas spreads out to fill any space, like air or the smell of a braai. Most gases are invisible, and an empty bottle is full of air.

Heating and cooling change the state of a material. Heating makes a solid melt into a liquid, like an ice lolly in the sun, and makes a liquid evaporate into a gas, like washing drying on the line. Cooling makes a gas condense into a liquid, like the drops on a cold glass or a pot lid, and makes a liquid freeze, or solidify, into a solid, like water in the freezer or candle wax cooling. Ice melts and water freezes at 0 degrees Celsius. Water boils at about 100 degrees Celsius. The material stays the same: ice, water and water vapour are all water.

Panel 2. In the water cycle, the Sun heats water in the sea, rivers and dams, and it evaporates into invisible water vapour. The salt stays behind in the sea. High in the cold sky, the vapour condenses into tiny drops that make clouds. The drops join and fall as rain, or freeze and fall as hail or snow. Snow melts in spring, rivers carry water back to the sea, and the cycle repeats. No new water is made. Because South Africa is a dry country, every drop of fresh water is precious.

The questions for this section are with you now: solids, liquids and gases, changes of state, and the water cycle.

## Subtopic: Raw Materials and Properties

Panel 3. Raw materials come from nature: sand, clay, coal and oil from the ground, wood and cotton from plants, wool and hide from animals. Manufactured materials are made by people from raw materials. Sand is melted in a furnace to make glass. Clay is shaped and fired in a kiln to make ceramics like mugs and bricks. Oil and coal are used to make plastics. Wood is mashed into pulp to make paper. Wool is spun into yarn and knitted or woven into fabric. Hide is tanned to make leather.

Materials have properties, which come in pairs: hard or soft, stiff or flexible, strong or weak, light or heavy, and waterproof or absorbent. We test properties fairly, for example by dripping the same amount of water on same-sized pieces to see which are waterproof and which are absorbent, or by weighing blocks of the same size. We choose a material for a job because its properties suit the job. A raincoat must be waterproof and flexible. A towel must be absorbent and soft. A window must be see-through, hard and waterproof.

Remember that one material can have many properties at once. Rubber is soft, flexible and waterproof. A brick is hard, stiff, strong and heavy. Remember too that hard and strong are different. Glass is hard but breaks easily. And light does not mean weak: aluminium and bamboo are light but strong.

The questions for this section are with you now: raw and manufactured materials and their properties.

## Subtopic: Strengthening Materials and Frames

Panel 4. A flat sheet of paper is weak and sags. We can make it much stronger by changing its shape: folding it into a concertina, rolling it into tubes, gluing layers together, or corrugating it with a wavy layer, like cardboard. The folds of a paper bridge must run from one side of the gap to the other.

Paper can be folded or rolled into hollow pillars with circle, triangle or square shapes. In a fair test, the circle pillar usually holds the most, because it has no corners and the load is shared all the way round. Paper rolled tightly around a stick makes a strut, a stiff tube for building frames.

Struts joined into a square can be pushed into a diamond shape. Struts joined into a triangle keep their shape. Adding a diagonal strut to a square makes two triangles and a strong frame. Roof trusses, bridges, cranes and pylons are frames built from triangles, and a skeleton is a natural frame of bones. Indigenous homes such as the Nama matjieshuis, the Zulu uguqa and the Xhosa rontabile and ungqu-phantsi are built on a framework of poles tied together and covered with reed mats, thatch or mud.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: calling the white cloud above a kettle water vapour; water vapour is invisible. Two: calling glass a raw material; it is made from sand. Three: saying the square pillar is strongest; the circle usually wins. Four: thinking a frame needs thicker struts to stop wobbling; triangles stop it.

The questions for this section are with you now: strengthening paper, pillars and struts, and frames built from triangles.

## Subtopic: Pour, Melt and Rain

Solids keep their shape. Liquids pour. Gases spread out.

Heat: melt and evaporate. Cool: condense and freeze.

Water goes up, makes clouds, comes down as rain, and goes round again.

## Subtopic: From Nature to Things

Sand to glass. Clay to mugs. Oil and coal to plastic. Wood to paper. Wool to jerseys. Hide to shoes.

Hard or soft. Stiff or flexible. Strong or weak. Light or heavy. Waterproof or absorbent.

Hold panel 3 in mind and try this part's questions: materials and properties.

## Subtopic: Fold, Roll and Triangles

Fold, roll, layer and wave paper to make it strong.

Circle pillars hold the most. Triangles make frames strong.

Pocket summary of the term. Materials are solids, liquids or gases, and heating and cooling change their state, as in the water cycle. Raw materials from nature are made into manufactured materials, and we choose materials by their properties. Changing the shape of a material makes it stronger, and triangles make frames strong, from pylons to traditional homes.
