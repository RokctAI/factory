### subtopic_1 — States of Matter and the Water Cycle
**Assistant:** How can I remember which changes of state need heating and which need cooling?

**Tutor:** Think about going up and coming down. Heating gives a material more energy, so it moves up the ladder from solid to liquid to gas. Solid to liquid is melting, and liquid to gas is evaporating. So melting and evaporating need heating, like an ice lolly in the sun or water boiling in a pot. Cooling takes energy away, so the material comes down the ladder from gas to liquid to solid. Gas to liquid is condensing, and liquid to solid is freezing or solidifying. So condensing and freezing need cooling, like drops forming on a cold glass or water turning to ice in the freezer. A quick check is to think of the water cycle. The Sun heats the sea, so water goes up as vapour. The cold sky cools it, so it comes down as cloud drops and rain. Up needs heat, down needs cold.

### subtopic_2 — Raw Materials and Properties
**Assistant:** Why does changing the shape of paper make it stronger when it is still the same paper?

**Tutor:** The paper itself does not change. It is the same material with the same thickness. What changes is the way the paper carries the load. A flat sheet across a gap has nothing to stop it bending down, because it is so thin. When you fold it into a concertina, each fold stands up on its edge like a small wall, and a wall standing on its edge is very hard to bend. When you roll it into a tube or a circle pillar, the paper curves all the way around, and the load is shared evenly with no flat parts to sag or buckle. When you glue layers together or add a wavy corrugated layer, the board becomes thicker, and thicker things are much stiffer. Engineers use these same tricks with steel, plastic and wood. That is why roof sheets are corrugated, scaffolding poles are tubes, and floor beams stand on their edges.

### subtopic_3 — Strengthening Materials and Frames
**Assistant:** Are indigenous homes built from triangles like pylons?

**Tutor:** They use some of the same ideas, even though they look very different. A pylon is made of straight steel struts bolted into triangles. A traditional dome-shaped home, like the Nama matjieshuis or a Zulu beehive-style home, is made of thin, bendy poles pushed into the ground in a circle, bent over and tied together. More poles are tied around in rings, crossing the first poles. Wherever the poles cross and are tied, they form many small shapes, including triangles, and the curved dome shape helps to spread the weight evenly down to the ground. So the frame is strong and stable even though each pole is thin. The big lesson from Term 2 is that the way struts are arranged and joined matters as much as the material they are made of. Whether it is a steel pylon in eMalahleni or a reed-mat house in Namaqualand, a well-designed frame can be strong, light and stable.
