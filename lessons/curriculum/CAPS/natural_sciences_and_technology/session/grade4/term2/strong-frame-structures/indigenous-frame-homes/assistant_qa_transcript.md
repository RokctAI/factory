### subtopic_1 — A Framework of Poles
**Assistant:** Why are so many traditional homes round instead of square?

**Tutor:** A round, dome-shaped home has several advantages, especially when it is built from thin poles and grass. First, bending poles into a dome makes a strong, stable shape. The curved poles push against each other, and the weight is spread evenly all the way round to the ground. A square frame of thin poles would wobble unless it had many diagonal braces. Second, a round shape handles wind well. Wind slides around a curve instead of pushing hard against one flat wall. Third, a dome encloses a lot of space using fewer poles and less covering than a square house of the same floor size, which saves materials. Fourth, a curved thatched roof lets rain run off quickly in every direction. Round homes are also easy to keep warm, because a fire in the middle heats the whole space evenly. People across Africa and in many other parts of the world discovered these advantages and built round homes for thousands of years.

### subtopic_2 — The Nama Matjieshuis
**Assistant:** How did the Nama carry their homes when they moved?

**Tutor:** The matjieshuis was designed to be packed up. When a family decided to move to fresh grazing, the women untied the mats from the frame and rolled them up into bundles. Then they untied the bent sticks and bundled them together. The sticks kept their curved shape, which made it quicker to build the frame again later. In the past, these bundles were loaded onto the backs of pack oxen, which carried them across the veld to the new place. Later, many families used donkey carts. At the new place, the sticks were pushed into the ground in a circle again, bent over and tied, and the mats were tied on top. A family could have their home standing again in a few hours. This was very important in the dry Northern Cape, where grazing and water are scattered, and people and their animals needed to move with the seasons. It is a great example of a frame structure that is strong, light and portable.

### subtopic_3 — Zulu and Xhosa Homes
**Assistant:** How is thatch able to keep out rain if it is just grass?

**Tutor:** Thatch is not just a loose pile of grass. Thatchers choose long, strong, straight grass and tie it in thick bundles. The bundles are laid on the roof in overlapping layers, starting at the bottom and working upwards, like the scales on a fish or the feathers on a bird. Each layer covers the tops of the layer below. The grass stems all point downwards, and the roof is steep. When rain falls on the thatch, it runs down along the outside stems from one stem to the next, all the way to the edge, and drips off. Only the outer few centimetres get wet. The thick inside of the thatch stays dry. Thatch also traps lots of air between the grass stems, and trapped air slows down heat moving through. That is why thatched homes stay cool on hot days and warm on cold nights. Many modern lodges and houses in South Africa still have thatched roofs for exactly these reasons.
