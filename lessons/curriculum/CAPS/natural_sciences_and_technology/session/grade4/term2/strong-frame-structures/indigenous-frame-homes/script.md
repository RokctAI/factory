# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers indigenous homes of South Africa that are built on a framework of struts, such as the Zulu uguqa, the Xhosa rontabile and ungqu-phantsi, and the Nama matjieshuis. Indigenous means belonging to the people who have lived in a place for a very long time. The anchor is a Grade 4 class in Springbok in Namaqualand, in the Northern Cape. They visit a heritage site where an elder shows them a matjieshuis, a dome-shaped mat house, and explains how it is put up. Back at school, they look at photographs of traditional Zulu and Xhosa homes, and build their own small model frames. The big idea is that people built strong, comfortable homes using a frame of thin poles joined together, then covered the frame with local materials, long before factories made bricks and steel. Describing how indigenous homes use a framework of struts is examinable.

## Subtopic: A Framework of Poles

Many traditional homes in South Africa begin in the same way: with a frame. Builders collect long, thin, bendy poles from young trees or shrubs that grow nearby. These poles act as struts.

The builders mark out a circle on the ground. They push the thick ends of the poles firmly into the ground around the circle. Then they bend the tops of the poles over towards the middle and tie them together where they cross, using strips of bark, grass rope or leather thongs. Next, they tie more poles around the outside in rings, from the bottom to the top, crossing the upright poles. Wherever two poles cross, they are tied. A gap is left between two of the upright poles for a doorway. Fresh, green poles are used because they bend without snapping, and as they dry they keep their curved shape.

The result is a dome-shaped frame that looks a bit like an upside-down basket. All those crossing poles make many small triangles and curved shapes, and the curved dome shape spreads the weight evenly to the ground. This makes the frame strong and stable, even though each pole is thin and light. The round shape also stands up well against wind, because wind slides around a curved shape more easily than it pushes against a flat wall.

Once the frame is finished, it is covered. The covering keeps out sun, wind, rain and cold, but it is the frame underneath that holds the shape of the home. This is just like the paper frames the class built from struts: the struts give the strength, and the covering gives the shelter.

Using local materials made sense. People used whatever grew nearby, so they did not need to buy or carry materials far.

## Subtopic: The Nama Matjieshuis

The Nama people are herders who have lived in Namaqualand and further north for a very long time. Traditionally, they moved with their sheep and goats to find fresh grazing and water in this dry land, so they needed homes that could be taken down, carried and put up again.

The matjieshuis, which means mat house, solved this problem. Its frame is made of bent sticks tied into a dome. The frame is covered with mats woven by hand from reeds or rushes. In the past, Nama women built and owned the houses, and they wove the mats themselves.

The mats are clever. In hot, dry weather, the reeds shrink a little and let air flow through, which keeps the inside cool. When it rains, the reeds soak up a little water and swell, which closes the gaps and helps keep the rain out. In cold weather, extra mats can be added for warmth.

When it is time to move, the mats are rolled up and the sticks are untied. Everything is light enough to load onto pack animals. At the new place, the frame is put up again, and the mats are tied on. The elder in Springbok tells the class that some families in Namaqualand and the Richtersveld still build matjieshuise today.

## Subtopic: Zulu and Xhosa Homes

Traditional Zulu homes, such as the uguqa, were also built on a framework of thin, bendy poles. Many traditional Zulu homes had a rounded beehive shape. The poles were pushed into the ground in a circle, bent over and tied together, and then the whole frame was covered with thick layers of thatch grass, tied down with grass ropes. The thatch kept the inside warm in winter and cool in summer, and the steep, curved surface let rain run off quickly.

Traditional Xhosa homes, such as the rontabile and the ungqu-phantsi, were also built using a framework of wooden poles tied together, which was covered with thatch grass. In some homes, the walls were plastered with mud, which dried hard and helped keep the inside cool in summer and warm in winter. Many families in the Eastern Cape and KwaZulu-Natal still build round homes with thatched roofs today, held up by a frame of poles.

The class builds model frames from kebab sticks and string, and from paper struts and tape. They find that the frames with poles crossing many times are much stronger than those with only a few poles.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking traditional homes were weak because they were not made of bricks; their frames of tied poles are strong and stable. Two: thinking the covering holds the home up; the frame of struts holds the shape, and the covering gives shelter. Three: thinking the matjieshuis stayed in one place; it was designed to be taken apart and moved. Four: thinking these homes belong only to the past; many are still built and lived in today.
