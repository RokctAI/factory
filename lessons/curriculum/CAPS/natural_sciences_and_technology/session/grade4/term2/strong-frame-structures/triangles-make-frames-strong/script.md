# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers how struts joined into triangular shapes make strong, stable frame structures, such as roof trusses, bridges, cranes, pylons and skeletons. The anchor is a Grade 4 class from eMalahleni in Mpumalanga, a town surrounded by power stations. On a bus trip they see rows of tall steel Eskom pylons carrying electricity across the veld, a yellow tower crane lifting bricks on a building site, and builders lifting wooden roof trusses onto the walls of a new house. Back in class, they make frames from the paper struts they rolled last week. The big idea is that a frame made of squares can easily be pushed out of shape, but a frame made of triangles keeps its shape, so engineers build strong frames out of triangles. Explaining why triangles make frames strong and identifying triangles in real structures are examinable.

## Subtopic: Squares Wobble, Triangles Do Not

A frame structure is made of struts joined together at their ends. The places where struts are joined are called joints.

The class joins four paper struts into a square, with one split pin at each corner so that the joints are like simple hinges. They stand the square up and push its top corner sideways. The square leans over and squashes into a diamond shape. Push harder and it folds flat. None of the struts bent or broke. Only the angles at the corners changed. A square frame is not stable, because its shape can change.

Next, the class joins three struts into a triangle, using split pins in the same way. They push one corner as hard as they did with the square. The triangle does not change shape at all. It keeps its shape because the only way a triangle can change shape is if one of its sides gets longer or shorter, and the struts cannot stretch or shrink.

Then they go back to the wobbly square and add one more strut across it, from one corner to the opposite corner. This strut is called a diagonal brace. Now the square is made of two triangles, and when they push it, it stays firm. One extra strut turned a weak frame into a strong one.

This is the most important rule for building frames: triangles make frames strong. Builders call it triangulation, which means dividing a frame into triangles.

You can find this idea at school too. Look under a school desk or a table and you may see small diagonal pieces of metal or wood in the corners where the legs meet the top. They make little triangles that stop the table wobbling. Bookshelves often have a thin board nailed across the back for the same reason.

The questions for this section are with you now: why square frames wobble and how triangles make them strong.

## Subtopic: Triangles in Roofs, Bridges and Cranes

A roof truss is the triangle-shaped wooden frame that holds up the roof of a house. The builders in eMalahleni lift one truss after another onto the walls, spaced along the house. Each truss is a big triangle, with smaller triangles of struts inside it. The trusses carry the weight of the roof tiles or iron sheets, and hold their shape in strong wind.

Many bridges are frame structures. Look at an old steel railway bridge and you will see a pattern of triangles along its sides. The triangles let a long, light bridge carry heavy trains without sagging. Footbridges over highways are often built the same way.

A tower crane on a building site has a tall mast and a long arm, called the jib. Both are made of steel struts joined in a zigzag pattern of triangles. This lets the crane be very tall and reach far, while staying light and strong enough to lift heavy loads of bricks and steel.

A strong frame must also be stable, which means it does not fall over. Many frames are wider at the bottom than at the top, which helps them stand firmly. The struts are joined firmly at the joints with bolts, nails, welds or gussets, because a frame is only as strong as its joints.

The questions for this section are with you now: triangles in roof trusses, bridges and cranes.

## Subtopic: Pylons and Skeletons

An electricity pylon is a tall steel frame that holds power lines high above the ground. Pylons near eMalahleni carry electricity from the power stations to cities and towns. Look closely and you will see that a pylon is made of steel struts joined into triangles, over and over again, from the bottom to the top. A pylon is wide at its base and narrower at the top, which makes it stable. Its open frame of triangles lets the wind blow through, so it does not get pushed over in a storm. Cell phone towers are built in the same way.

Living things have frames too. A skeleton is a frame of bones that holds up an animal's body, gives it shape and protects soft parts inside. The bones are joined at joints, such as the knee and elbow. Unlike a pylon, many joints in a skeleton can bend, so that the animal can move. Muscles pull on the bones and hold them firmly in place, which keeps the body stable. Your ribs form a curved cage of bones that protects your heart and lungs. A giraffe's long leg bones and an elephant's thick, pillar-like leg bones show how a skeleton's frame suits the size and weight of the animal.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking a square frame is strong because squares look neat; a square can be pushed into a diamond. Two: thinking you need thicker struts to stop a frame wobbling; one diagonal strut that makes triangles works much better. Three: thinking a pylon is solid metal; it is an open frame of steel struts in triangles. Four: forgetting the joints; a frame with weak joints falls apart even if its struts are strong.

The questions for this section are with you now: triangles in pylons and how a skeleton is a frame.
