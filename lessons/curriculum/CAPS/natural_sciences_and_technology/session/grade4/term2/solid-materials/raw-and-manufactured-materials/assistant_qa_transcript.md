### subtopic_1 — Raw Materials from Nature
**Assistant:** Why does glass let light through when sand does not?

**Tutor:** Sand is made of many tiny grains with gaps of air between them, and each grain has rough edges and many surfaces. When light hits a pile of sand, it bounces off all those surfaces in every direction, so the light cannot pass straight through, and we cannot see through it. When sand is heated in a furnace, the grains melt and flow together into one smooth liquid with no gaps and no separate grains. When that liquid cools slowly and becomes solid glass, it stays smooth and even all the way through. Light can travel straight through it without bouncing around, so the glass is see-through. This is a good example of how a manufactured material can have very different properties from the raw material it came from. People choose glass for windows, spectacles and bottles exactly because it lets light through, keeps out wind and rain, and does not rot or rust.

### subtopic_2 — Making Glass, Ceramics and Plastics
**Assistant:** Why must hide be tanned before we can use it?

**Tutor:** Fresh hide is the skin of an animal, and like any part of a dead animal, it is a once-living material. If it is left as it is, tiny living things like bacteria start to feed on it. It begins to rot, it smells terrible, and it falls apart. If it is simply dried in the sun, it becomes stiff and hard like cardboard, and cracks when you bend it. Tanning is a process that changes the hide so that it no longer rots and stays soft and bendable. In the past, people tanned hides by soaking them for weeks in water with crushed tree bark, which contains special substances. Today, most tanning is done in factories called tanneries, using chemicals. After tanning, the leather is dried, softened, smoothed and coloured. Good leather can last for many years, which is why it is used for shoes, belts, bags and saddles.

### subtopic_3 — Making Paper, Fabrics and Leather
**Assistant:** Is recycling paper really better than making new paper from trees?

**Tutor:** Recycling paper has several good points. When we recycle old newspapers, boxes and office paper, they are soaked and mashed back into pulp, cleaned, and made into new paper or cardboard. That means fewer trees need to be cut down for the same amount of paper. Recycling also uses less water and less energy than making paper from fresh wood, and it keeps old paper out of rubbish dumps, where it takes up space. Paper cannot be recycled forever, though. Each time it is recycled, its fibres get a little shorter and weaker, so some new wood pulp is usually mixed in. In South Africa, much of the wood for paper comes from plantations, where trees are planted especially to be cut, and new trees are planted again. Even so, recycling helps. You can help by putting clean paper and cardboard in the recycling bin instead of the rubbish bin.
