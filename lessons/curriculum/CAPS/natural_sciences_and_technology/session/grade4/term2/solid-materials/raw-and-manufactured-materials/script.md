# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers raw materials and how people use them to make manufactured materials: sand into glass, clay into ceramics, coal and oil into plastics, wood into paper, wool into fabrics and hide into leather. The anchor is a Grade 4 classroom in Vereeniging, a town on the Vaal River with many factories. The class sets up a table called Where Does It Come From? On the left side are six raw materials: a jar of sand, a lump of wet clay, a piece of coal, a log of wood, a bag of sheep's wool and a piece of dried cow hide. On the right side are six products: a glass jar, a clay mug, a plastic bottle, a newspaper, a wool jersey and a leather shoe. The big idea is that raw materials come straight from nature, and people change them, often using heat, into manufactured materials that are more useful. Naming raw materials and the materials made from them is examinable.

## Subtopic: Raw Materials from Nature

A raw material is a material that comes from nature and has not been changed much by people. We dig it out of the ground, grow it, or get it from animals.

Some raw materials come from the ground. Sand is found on beaches, in rivers and in deserts. Clay is a sticky, fine soil found near rivers and in some fields. Coal is a black rock dug out of mines, and South Africa has huge coal mines in Mpumalanga and Limpopo. Oil is a thick, dark liquid pumped up from deep underground. Iron ore, which is used to make steel, is also dug out of mines, for example in the Northern Cape.

Some raw materials come from plants. Wood comes from trees. In South Africa, large plantations of pine and gum trees in Mpumalanga and KwaZulu-Natal are grown for wood. Cotton comes from the fluffy seed pods of the cotton plant.

Some raw materials come from animals. Wool is the thick, curly hair of sheep, cut off once a year. Mohair is the hair of Angora goats, and South Africa produces more mohair than any other country. Hide is the skin of animals such as cattle.

Some raw materials are used almost as they are. Stone can be cut into blocks for building, and thatching grass is cut, dried and tied into bundles for roofs. But most raw materials are not useful as they are. You cannot drink from a pile of sand or wear a bag of wool to school. People change raw materials to make them useful.

The questions for this section are with you now: what raw materials are and where they come from.

## Subtopic: Making Glass, Ceramics and Plastics

A manufactured material is made by people from raw materials. Manufactured means made, usually in a factory or a workshop.

Glass is made from sand. The sand is mixed with a few other materials and heated in a furnace until it melts into a hot, glowing, runny liquid. The furnace is much hotter than any kitchen oven, more than a thousand degrees Celsius. While the glass is hot and soft, it is blown or pressed into moulds to make jars, bottles, drinking glasses and windows. When it cools, it becomes a hard, see-through solid. Sand is not see-through, but glass is. Heating has changed it.

Ceramics are made from clay. Wet clay is soft and can be shaped with your hands or on a potter's wheel into mugs, pots, plates, tiles and bricks. Then it is dried and baked, or fired, at a very high temperature in an oven called a kiln. Firing changes the clay so that it becomes hard and will not soften in water again. Zulu potters have made beautiful round clay pots for hundreds of years, firing them in open fires. Bricks for houses are made in huge brickworks near many South African towns.

Plastics are made from oil and coal. In factories, chemicals taken from oil or coal are changed into plastic. The plastic is melted and shaped into bottles, buckets, chairs, toys, pipes and packets. In South Africa, factories at Sasolburg make plastics from chemicals that come from coal.

The questions for this section are with you now: how glass, ceramics and plastics are made from raw materials.

## Subtopic: Making Paper, Fabrics and Leather

Paper is made from wood. Logs are cut into small chips and cooked with water and chemicals until the wood breaks down into a soft, wet mush called pulp. The pulp is spread out into thin sheets, pressed flat and dried on hot rollers. The result is paper, used for newspapers, books, boxes and toilet paper. Old paper can be soaked and turned back into pulp to make recycled paper, which saves trees.

Fabrics are made from wool and other fibres. Wool is cut from sheep, washed to remove dirt and grease, combed and then spun into long threads called yarn. The yarn is knitted into jerseys and beanies or woven into cloth for blankets and coats. Cotton is spun and woven in the same way to make T-shirts and jeans. The Eastern Cape is famous for its sheep farms and wool.

Leather is made from hide. When an animal such as a cow is slaughtered for meat, its skin is kept. Fresh hide would rot and smell, so it is treated in a process called tanning, which makes it strong, soft and long-lasting. Leather is used for shoes, belts, bags, car seats and soccer balls.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: calling glass a raw material; it is manufactured from sand. Two: thinking plastic comes from plants; most plastic is made from oil and coal. Three: saying clay and ceramics are the same; firing changes soft clay into hard ceramic. Four: thinking leather is made in a factory from chemicals only; it starts as animal hide.

The questions for this section are with you now: how paper, fabrics and leather are made from raw materials.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the Where Does It Come From? table — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Straight from Nature

Raw materials come from nature.

Sand, clay, coal and oil come from the ground. Wood and cotton come from plants. Wool and hide come from animals.

Keep the left side of the table in mind and try the questions for this part: raw materials.

## Subtopic: Sand to Glass, Clay to Pots

Melt sand in a very hot furnace and you get glass.

Shape clay and bake it in a kiln and you get ceramics, like mugs and bricks.

Plastic is made from oil and coal.

Hold the glass jar in mind and try this part's questions: glass, ceramics and plastic.

## Subtopic: Wood to Paper, Wool to Jerseys

Wood is mashed into pulp to make paper.

Wool is spun into yarn and knitted into jerseys.

Hide is tanned to make leather shoes.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Raw materials come from nature. Manufactured materials are made by people from raw materials. Sand becomes glass, clay becomes ceramics, coal and oil become plastics, wood becomes paper, wool becomes fabric and hide becomes leather. Heat often helps to make the change.

Hold the whole table in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: paper, fabrics and leather.
