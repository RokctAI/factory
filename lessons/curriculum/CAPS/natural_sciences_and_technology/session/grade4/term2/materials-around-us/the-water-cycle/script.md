# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the water cycle, in which water evaporates, condenses, freezes and melts as it moves between the land, the sea and the sky. The anchor is the journey of one drop of water through KwaZulu-Natal. It begins as snow on the high peaks of the Drakensberg in winter. In spring it melts and trickles into the Tugela River, tumbles over the Tugela Falls, flows past farms and towns and reaches the warm Indian Ocean near the coast. From there, the Sun lifts it back into the sky. The big idea is that the same water goes round and round, changing state again and again, driven by heating from the Sun and cooling high in the sky. Describing the water cycle and naming the changes of state in it are examinable.

## Subtopic: Evaporating Water Rises

The journey of our drop can start anywhere, because a cycle goes round and round with no real beginning or end. Let us start at the sea. Our drop has already come a long way. It fell as snow, melted, and plunged over the Tugela Falls in the Drakensberg, one of the highest waterfalls in the world, before the river carried it all the way down to the coast.

The Sun shines on the Indian Ocean and heats the water at the surface. Some of the water evaporates and turns into water vapour, an invisible gas. The water vapour rises into the air with the warm air. Our drop has left the sea and is floating upwards as a gas.

Water evaporates from many places, not only the sea. It evaporates from rivers, lakes and dams like the Vaal Dam, from puddles, from wet soil, from washing on the line and even from our skin when we sweat. Plants give off water vapour through the tiny holes in their leaves. A big tree can give off a lot of water on a hot day.

When water evaporates from the sea, the salt stays behind. Only the water rises. That is why rain is fresh water, not salty, even though most of it came from the sea.

The Sun's heat is the engine that drives the water cycle. Without the Sun, water would not evaporate and rise, and the cycle would stop. Hot, sunny and windy days make water evaporate faster, which is why farmers' dams lose lots of water in the hot summer months.

The questions for this section are with you now: how and where water evaporates in the water cycle.

## Subtopic: Clouds, Rain, Hail and Snow

As the water vapour rises higher and higher, it reaches air that is much colder. High in the sky the air is cold even on a hot summer day. The cold makes the water vapour condense into tiny drops of liquid water. Millions and millions of these tiny drops together make a cloud. Our drop is now part of a cloud, floating over the coast.

Inside the cloud, the tiny drops bump into each other and join together, making bigger drops. When the drops become too heavy to float, they fall to the ground as rain. Rain, hail and snow that fall from clouds are called precipitation.

Sometimes the clouds are so high and so cold that the drops freeze into ice. On the highveld, tall thunderclouds in summer can make lumps of ice that fall as hail and can damage cars and crops. In winter, when it is very cold on the Drakensberg and in parts of the Eastern Cape, water in the clouds freezes into delicate ice crystals that fall gently as snow.

The rain that falls on the land soaks into the soil, runs into streams and rivers, or collects in dams. Some soaks deep underground, where people can reach it with boreholes. Plants take it up through their roots. Rivers carry the water back to the sea.

Condensing also happens close to the ground. On a cool, clear night, water vapour in the air condenses on cold grass and leaves, and in the morning they are covered in drops of dew. On very cold highveld nights, the dew freezes into white frost. Mist and fog are clouds that form right at ground level, often in valleys and along the coast in the early morning.

The questions for this section are with you now: how clouds form and how water falls as rain, hail and snow.

## Subtopic: Round and Round

Now put the whole journey together. Our drop fell as snow on the Drakensberg. The snow was frozen water. In spring, the Sun warmed it and the snow melted into liquid water, which ran into the Tugela River. The river carried it to the sea. The Sun heated the sea and the water evaporated into water vapour. High up, the vapour cooled and condensed into cloud drops. The drops joined and fell as rain, or froze and fell as hail or snow. And then the journey started again.

So the water cycle uses all four changes of state. Evaporating and melting need heating, which comes from the Sun. Condensing and freezing need cooling, which happens high in the sky and in cold places.

The water cycle means that Earth keeps using the same water over and over. No new water is made. The water you drink today has been round the cycle millions of times. Because fresh water is so precious in South Africa, a dry country, we must not waste it or pollute it. Polluted water in a river does not get clean just by flowing to the sea.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: saying clouds are made of water vapour; clouds are made of tiny drops of liquid water or ice. Two: saying rain is salty because it came from the sea; the salt stays behind when water evaporates. Three: thinking the water cycle makes new water; it moves the same water round and round. Four: forgetting the Sun; the Sun's heat drives evaporation and melting.

The questions for this section are with you now: the whole water cycle and the four changes of state in it.
