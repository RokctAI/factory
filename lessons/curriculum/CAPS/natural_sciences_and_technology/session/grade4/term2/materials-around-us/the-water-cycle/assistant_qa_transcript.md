### subtopic_1 — Evaporating Water Rises
**Assistant:** Why is it cold high up in the sky if it is closer to the Sun?

**Tutor:** It seems strange, because we might think that going up brings us closer to the Sun's heat. But the Sun is extremely far away, about 150 million kilometres. Climbing a mountain or flying in an aeroplane brings you only a few kilometres closer, which makes no real difference. What matters is how the air gets warm. Sunlight passes through the air and warms the ground and the sea first. The warm ground then warms the air just above it, like a stove plate warming a pot. Higher up, the air is far from the warm ground, and it is also thinner, so it is colder. That is why there is snow on the top of the Drakensberg in winter while people in the valleys below are only a little chilly, and why the air outside an aeroplane flying high in the sky is far below freezing. That cold air high up is exactly what makes water vapour condense into clouds.

### subtopic_2 — Clouds, Rain, Hail and Snow
**Assistant:** Why do clouds float if they are made of water?

**Tutor:** Clouds are made of drops of water, but the drops are tiny, far smaller than a raindrop. Many thousands of them could fit on the head of a pin. Each one is so small and light that even a gentle movement of air holds it up, a bit like dust floating in a sunbeam. Air in the sky is always moving, and warm air rising from below keeps pushing the tiny drops upwards. So the cloud floats. Inside the cloud, the tiny drops bump into each other and join together. Bit by bit, some drops grow bigger and heavier. When a drop grows too heavy for the moving air to hold up, it falls as a raindrop. Dark, heavy rain clouds are full of bigger drops that are almost ready to fall. That is why the sky often goes grey or dark before a storm, and why light, fluffy white clouds on a sunny day usually do not bring rain.

### subtopic_3 — Round and Round
**Assistant:** Is the water I drink really old?

**Tutor:** In a way, yes. The water on Earth has been going round the water cycle for an extremely long time. No new water is being made, and very little is being lost. The same water evaporates, condenses, falls as rain or snow, flows in rivers and evaporates again, over and over. So the water in your glass this morning has been on a very long journey. Some of it may once have been snow on a mountain, part of a cloud over the ocean, or water in a river where dinosaurs drank. Before it reached your tap, though, people cleaned it at a water treatment works, because along the way water picks up dirt and germs. That is why we must look after rivers and dams and not throw rubbish or chemicals into them. Water is old and precious, and in a dry country like South Africa every drop counts.
