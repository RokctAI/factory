### subtopic_1 — Melting and Freezing
**Assistant:** Why does ice melt faster in the sun than in the shade?

**Tutor:** Melting needs heat. Ice melts when it takes in enough heat to warm up past 0 degrees Celsius. In direct sunlight, the ice gets heat from two places: from the warm air all around it, and from the sunshine shining straight onto it. In the shade, it only gets heat from the air, which is slower. That is why an ice lolly in the Upington sun drips almost at once, while one kept in the shade of a tree lasts a little longer. Things that keep heat away from ice slow down melting. A cooler box has thick walls that do not let heat through easily, so the ice inside stays frozen for hours. Wrapping a bottle of frozen water in newspaper or a towel also slows the melting. People in hot places sometimes put a damp cloth around a clay pot to keep water cool, because as the water in the cloth evaporates, it takes heat away from the pot.

### subtopic_2 — Evaporating and Condensing
**Assistant:** Where does the water in a puddle go when it dries up?

**Tutor:** It does not disappear. It evaporates. The water in the puddle slowly changes from a liquid into water vapour, which is an invisible gas, and that gas spreads out into the air. Water at the top of the puddle evaporates first, so the puddle gets shallower and smaller each hour. The sun's heat and the wind make it happen faster. The water vapour does not stay near the ground. It rises with warm air, and high up in the sky, where it is much colder, it cools down and condenses into tiny drops of water that make clouds. Later, those drops may fall again as rain, perhaps far away from where the puddle was. So the water from the puddle on your school playground might one day fall as rain in the Drakensberg or over the sea. Water keeps moving around like this, changing state again and again. In the next lesson, we will follow that journey in the water cycle.

### subtopic_3 — Heating and Cooling Change the State
**Assistant:** Why does butter melt in a pan but a metal spoon does not?

**Tutor:** Different materials melt at different temperatures. Butter melts at quite a low temperature, a little higher than the temperature of a warm room. That is why butter goes soft on a hot day and melts quickly in a warm pan. Ice melts at an even lower temperature, 0 degrees Celsius. A metal spoon is also a solid, and metals can melt too, but only at very, very high temperatures, far hotter than any kitchen stove can reach. Steel, for example, needs a temperature of well over a thousand degrees Celsius, which is only reached inside huge furnaces in factories. That is how metal is shaped into car parts, pots and spoons: it is melted, poured into a mould, and left to cool and solidify. Rocks also melt deep inside the Earth, where it is extremely hot, and when that melted rock comes out of a volcano and cools, it becomes solid rock again. So melting depends on how much heat a material needs.
