# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the three states of materials: solids, liquids and gases, and the properties of each. Solids keep their shape, liquids flow and take the shape of their container, and gases spread out to fill any space. The anchor is a family braai in Soweto on a Saturday afternoon. The braai stand is built of bricks, the meat sits on a metal grid, Mama pours cool drink from a bottle into glasses of different shapes, and the smell of boerewors drifts across the yard to the neighbours. Uncle Sipho pumps up a soccer ball for the children. The big idea is that every material is a solid, a liquid or a gas, and we can tell which by watching how it behaves. Describing the properties of solids, liquids and gases and sorting materials into the three groups are examinable.

## Subtopic: Solids

A solid keeps its own shape. A brick from the braai stand is a brick shape whether it is on the ground, in a wheelbarrow or in your hands. It does not flow and it does not spread out. You can hold a solid and pick it up.

A solid also takes up a fixed amount of space. If you move the brick into a bigger box, it does not grow to fill the box. Scientists say a solid has a fixed shape and a fixed volume, which means a fixed amount of space.

Solids at the braai include the bricks, the metal grid, the wooden table, the plastic plates, the charcoal, the ice cubes in the cooler box and the boerewors itself. Some solids are hard, like a brick. Some are soft, like a sponge or bread. Some solids can be bent or squashed, like a rubber band or play dough, but they still keep their new shape until something else changes it. They do not flow on their own.

What about sand, sugar and salt? They can be poured, a bit like a liquid, so they can trick us. But look closely: each tiny grain of sand or sugar is a solid with its own shape. A pile of sugar makes a heap. A liquid would spread out flat. So sand, sugar and salt are solids made of many tiny pieces.

Solids are useful because they keep their shape. We build houses, make tools, furniture and clothes from solid materials.

## Subtopic: Liquids

A liquid flows. It can be poured. Mama pours cool drink from a bottle into a tall glass, then into a short wide glass, then into a mug. Each time, the liquid takes the shape of whatever it is in. A liquid has no shape of its own.

But a liquid keeps the same amount. If Mama pours one cup of cool drink into the tall glass and then into the wide glass, there is still one cup. It looks higher in the tall glass and lower in the wide glass, but the amount has not changed. Scientists say a liquid has a fixed volume, but no fixed shape.

A liquid always has a flat, level surface at the top when it is still, and it flows downwards if it can. Spill water on the table and it spreads out into a puddle and drips off the edge.

Liquids at the braai include water, cool drink, milk, cooking oil, tomato sauce and the fat dripping from the meat. Some liquids flow quickly, like water. Others flow slowly, like honey, tomato sauce or syrup. We say these slow liquids are thick. They are still liquids, because they flow and take the shape of their container.

Because liquids flow, we keep them in containers such as bottles, jugs, cups, buckets and tanks. A liquid cannot be stacked like bricks or carried in your bare hands. Try to carry water in your cupped hands from the tap to the braai and most of it will drip through your fingers.

## Subtopic: Gases and Sorting Materials

A gas spreads out to fill any space it is in. The smell of boerewors cooking is carried by gases that spread out from the braai, all through the yard and over the wall to the neighbours. Air is a mixture of gases. It fills every room, every box and every empty bottle.

Most gases cannot be seen. We cannot see air, but we can feel it when the wind blows, and we can see what it does when it fills a balloon or makes washing flap on the line. A gas has no fixed shape and no fixed volume. Uncle Sipho can squash lots of air into the soccer ball with a pump, and when he lets it out, it spreads into the whole yard.

Gases at the braai include the air, the smell of the food, and the gas inside a gas cylinder used for cooking. When we breathe in, we take in air, and when we breathe out, we let out gas. Push an empty bottle upside down into a bucket of water and then tilt it. Bubbles of air escape and rise to the top, which shows that the bottle was full of gas all along.

To sort materials, ask questions. Does it keep its own shape? Then it is a solid. Does it flow and take the shape of its container, but keep the same amount? Then it is a liquid. Does it spread out to fill all the space? Then it is a gas.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: saying sugar is a liquid because it can be poured; each grain is a solid. Two: saying a liquid gets more when you pour it into a tall glass; the amount stays the same. Three: saying an empty bottle has nothing in it; it is full of air. Four: saying honey is a solid because it is thick; it flows, so it is a liquid.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the Soweto braai — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Keeps Its Shape

A solid keeps its own shape. You can pick it up.

Bricks, plates, wood, ice and the boerewors are solids.

Sugar and sand pour, but each tiny grain is a solid.

Keep the brick in mind and try the questions for this part: solids.

## Subtopic: Flows and Pours

A liquid flows. It takes the shape of its container.

The amount stays the same when you pour it.

Water, milk, oil and honey are liquids.

Hold the glass of cool drink in mind and try this part's questions: liquids.

## Subtopic: Spreads Out Everywhere

A gas spreads out to fill all the space.

Air is a gas. The smell of boerewors travels as a gas.

An empty bottle is full of air.

Pocket summary of the lesson. All materials are solids, liquids or gases. Solids keep their shape and their amount. Liquids flow and take the shape of their container, but keep their amount. Gases spread out to fill any space and usually cannot be seen.

Hold the smell of the braai in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: gases and sorting.
