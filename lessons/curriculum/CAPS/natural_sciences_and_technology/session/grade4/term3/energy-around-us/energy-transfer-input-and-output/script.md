# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers how energy can be transferred from a source to where it is needed, and how machines and appliances need input energy and provide useful output energy. The anchor is a sheep farm near Beaufort West in the Karoo, where Mr Jacobs farms with his family. A tall windpump stands near the dam, its wheel turning in the wind and pumping water up from deep underground for the sheep. Solar panels on the roof catch sunlight. Electricity wires stretch across the veld to the farmhouse, where a kettle boils, a radio plays the news and a fridge keeps food cold. In the shed, a tractor runs on diesel, and Mr Jacobs carries a torch at night. The big idea is that energy moves from a source to where we need it, and that every machine takes in one kind of energy and changes it into the kind of energy we want. Describing energy transfers and naming input and output energy are examinable.

## Subtopic: From a Source to Where It Is Needed

A source of energy is where the energy comes from. Energy often has to travel, or be transferred, from its source to the place where it does a job.

The Sun is the biggest source of energy for Earth. Its light and heat travel a very long way through space to reach the Karoo, where they warm the ground, help plants grow and fall on the solar panels on Mr Jacobs's roof.

The wind is a source of movement energy. The moving air pushes on the blades of the windpump and turns the wheel. That movement is passed down a long rod into the borehole, where it works a pump that lifts water up into the dam.

A power station is a source of electricity. In South Africa, most power stations burn coal. The electricity is transferred along thick cables on tall pylons, then along smaller wires on poles, all the way to the farmhouse. When the family switches on the kettle, the energy has travelled hundreds of kilometres.

A battery is a small, portable store of energy. In the torch, energy is transferred from the battery along metal parts to the bulb.

Food is a source of energy for our bodies. Energy is transferred from the food we eat to our muscles when we walk, lift and work.

Energy can also be transferred by heating. When the kettle boils water for tea, heat moves from the hot water into the cold cup and into Mr Jacobs's hands as he holds it.

In each case, there is a source, a way for the energy to travel, and a place where the energy is used.

The questions for this section are with you now: sources of energy and how energy is transferred to where it is needed.

## Subtopic: Input and Output Energy

A machine or appliance is something people have made to do a job. Every machine needs energy to work. The energy that goes into a machine is called input energy. The energy that comes out of the machine to do the job is called output energy. The machine changes the input energy into the output energy we want.

The kettle: input energy is electricity. Output energy is heat, which boils the water.

The radio: input energy is electricity. Output energy is sound, so the family can hear the news.

The torch: input energy is the stored energy in the battery, which gives electricity. Output energy is light.

The windpump: input energy is the movement energy of the wind. Output energy is movement, which pumps water up.

The tractor: input energy is the stored energy in diesel. Output energy is movement, so the tractor can pull a trailer.

The solar panel: input energy is light from the Sun. Output energy is electricity, which Mr Jacobs uses to charge batteries for lights in the shearing shed.

A bicycle is a machine too. Its input energy is the movement of your legs pushing the pedals, and that energy came from your food. Its output energy is movement of the wheels.

A useful way to show this is a simple diagram: input energy, arrow, machine, arrow, output energy. For the kettle: electricity, arrow, kettle, arrow, heat.

## Subtopic: Useful and Wasted Output

The output energy we want is called useful output energy. But most machines also give out some energy we do not want. That energy is wasted.

An old-fashioned light bulb is a good example. Its useful output is light. But touch it after it has been on for a while and it is very hot. Most of the electricity it uses is turned into heat, not light, so most of the energy is wasted. Modern LED bulbs give the same light with much less waste, which is why they save electricity.

The tractor's useful output is movement, but it also gives out lots of heat from its engine and loud noise. The radio gives out sound, but it gets a little warm. The fridge keeps food cold, but its back gets warm. The windpump creaks and squeaks as it turns, so some energy becomes sound.

Wasted energy is not destroyed. It spreads out into the surroundings, usually as heat and sound, where it is no longer useful. Engineers try to design machines that waste as little energy as possible. Saving energy at home, by switching off lights and appliances we are not using, also saves money and means less coal needs to be burnt.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: saying the input energy of a kettle is heat; the input is electricity and the output is heat. Two: thinking all the input energy becomes useful output; some is always wasted, often as heat and sound. Three: saying wasted energy disappears; it spreads out into the surroundings. Four: forgetting that a bicycle has input energy; it comes from your legs, and that energy came from food.

The questions for this section are with you now: useful and wasted output energy and saving energy.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the Karoo farm — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Energy on the Move

Energy comes from a source and travels to where it is needed.

Sunlight travels to Earth. Electricity travels along wires from a power station. Wind turns the windpump.

Keep the wires across the veld in mind and try the questions for this part: energy transfer.

## Subtopic: In and Out

Input energy goes into a machine. Output energy comes out.

Kettle: electricity in, heat out. Radio: electricity in, sound out. Torch: battery in, light out.

Hold the kettle in mind and try this part's questions: input and output.

## Subtopic: Not All Useful

Some output energy is wasted, usually as heat or sound.

Switch things off to save energy.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Energy is transferred from a source, like the Sun, the wind, a power station, a battery or food, to where it is needed. Machines and appliances take in input energy and give out useful output energy. Some output energy is always wasted, usually as heat and sound, so we try to save energy.
