### subtopic_1 — From a Source to Where It Is Needed
**Assistant:** How does a windpump get water out of the ground?

**Tutor:** A windpump has a big wheel with many metal blades at the top of a tall steel tower. A tail fin behind the wheel keeps it facing into the wind. When the wind blows, it pushes against the angled blades and makes the wheel spin. The spinning wheel is connected to a set of gears that change the turning movement into an up-and-down movement. This moves a long metal rod that goes straight down the middle of the tower and deep into a narrow hole drilled into the ground, called a borehole. At the bottom of the rod is a pump. Each time the rod goes up, the pump lifts some water. Stroke after stroke, the water is pushed up a pipe and into a dam or tank. Windpumps have been used on Karoo farms for over a hundred years, because the Karoo has little surface water but lots of wind and underground water. They need no fuel and no electricity: their input energy is free movement energy from the wind.

### subtopic_2 — Input and Output Energy
**Assistant:** Why does a fridge feel warm at the back if its job is to make things cold?

**Tutor:** A fridge does not make cold. It moves heat. Inside the fridge, a special liquid flows through pipes and takes in heat from the food and the air in the fridge, which makes the inside cold. A pump called a compressor, which runs on electricity, pushes that liquid to the pipes at the back of the fridge. There, the heat that was taken from inside is given out into the kitchen. That is why the back of a fridge feels warm. So the input energy is electricity, and the useful output is a cold inside, but the heat removed from the food, plus some extra heat from the compressor, comes out at the back. That is also why you should never stand a fridge right against a hot stove or in direct sunlight, and why there must be space behind it for air to move. A fridge with a door left open has to work much harder and uses more electricity.

### subtopic_3 — Useful and Wasted Output
**Assistant:** Why should we save electricity if we can just make more?

**Tutor:** Making electricity is not free, and in South Africa it comes mostly from burning coal. Coal takes millions of years to form, so once we burn it, it cannot be replaced. Burning coal also puts smoke and gases into the air, which can make people sick and add to the warming of the Earth's climate. Mining coal changes the land. Power stations also use large amounts of water, which is precious in a dry country. And there is only so much electricity that power stations can make at once. When everyone uses a lot at the same time, there may not be enough, which is one reason for load shedding. Saving electricity at home helps in many ways: it saves your family money, it means less coal has to be burnt, and it helps make sure there is enough for everyone. Switching off lights in empty rooms, using LED bulbs, boiling only the water you need and turning off appliances at the wall all make a difference.
