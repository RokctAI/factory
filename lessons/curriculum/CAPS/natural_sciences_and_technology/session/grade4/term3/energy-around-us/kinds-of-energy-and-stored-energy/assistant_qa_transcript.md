### subtopic_1 — Movement, Heat, Light and Sound
**Assistant:** How did energy from the Sun get into coal?

**Tutor:** Millions of years ago, long before there were people, large parts of South Africa were covered in swampy forests full of giant plants. Those plants grew in the sunshine and stored the Sun's energy in their leaves, stems and trunks, just as plants do today. When the plants died, they fell into the swamp water and mud. Because they were under water, they did not rot away completely. Layer upon layer of dead plants piled up over thousands of years and were buried under sand and mud. Over millions of years, the weight of the rock above squashed them and the heat underground slowly changed them into hard, black coal. The energy that the plants stored from the Sun stayed locked inside. When a power station in Mpumalanga burns coal today, it is releasing sunshine that was captured by plants millions of years ago. Coal takes so long to form that once we burn it, it cannot be replaced, which is one reason people are building solar and wind power too.

### subtopic_2 — Stored Energy in Food and Fuels
**Assistant:** Is electricity a kind of energy too?

**Tutor:** Yes, electricity is a very useful kind of energy that we use every day, even though this lesson focuses on movement, heat, light and sound. Electricity is special because it can be carried long distances through wires, from a power station to your home, and then changed easily into other kinds of energy. In your home, electricity is changed into light by light bulbs, into heat by the stove and kettle, into movement by a fan, and into sound by a radio. But electricity is not a store of energy like food or coal. It has to be made at a power station, usually by burning coal, or by using the movement of wind or water, or the light of the Sun. Batteries are small stores of energy that can give out electricity when you need it, which is how Lindiwe's torch works at the campsite, far from any plug. In the next lesson, we will see how energy moves from one place to another.

### subtopic_3 — Releasing Stored Energy Safely
**Assistant:** Why is the campfire hot even a short distance away?

**Tutor:** The campfire sends out its energy in different ways. Some of the heat travels outwards in all directions as invisible rays, a bit like the light from the flames. When those rays reach your face and hands, they warm them, even if you are standing a few steps away. That is why the side of you facing the fire feels hot while your back feels cold on a winter night. The Sun warms the Earth in the same way, sending its heat and light across empty space. The fire also heats the air around it. Hot air rises, which is why sparks and smoke float upwards and why the air directly above a fire is very hot. Never lean over a fire. The heat from a fire also travels into anything touching it, like the metal handle of a pot, which is why you should always use a cloth or oven glove. Feeling heat from a distance is a sign that energy is moving out from the fire to you.
