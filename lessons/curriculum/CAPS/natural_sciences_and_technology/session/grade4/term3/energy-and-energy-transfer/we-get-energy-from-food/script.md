# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the idea that we use energy for everything we do, and that we get our energy from food. The anchor is a school sports day in Bloemfontein. Thandeka, a Grade 4 learner, runs the 100 metres, takes part in the long jump, carries a crate of water bottles to the tent and shouts herself hoarse cheering for her team. By lunchtime she is tired, slow and hungry. She eats a plate of pap and stew and a banana, drinks some water, and after a rest she is ready for the relay. The big idea is that energy is what makes things happen, that our bodies need energy all the time, and that food is the source of that energy. Explaining what we use energy for and where we get it from is examinable.

## Subtopic: We Use Energy All the Time

Energy is what we need to do work and to make things happen. Without energy, nothing could move, grow, warm up or light up.

Thandeka uses energy for everything she does on sports day. Running and jumping need lots of energy, because her muscles work hard to move her body quickly. Carrying the crate of water needs energy to lift and move it. Shouting needs energy too, because she uses muscles to push air out and make her voice loud. Even thinking about her next race uses energy, because her brain is busy.

We also use energy when we are not doing anything that looks like work. While you sit still, your heart is beating, your lungs are breathing and your body is keeping warm. When you sleep at night, your body is still using energy to keep you alive, to grow and to repair itself. Children need energy to grow taller and stronger.

Different activities need different amounts of energy. Sleeping and reading use a little. Walking uses more. Running, swimming, playing soccer and dancing use a lot. That is why Thandeka feels much more tired after the 100 metres than after sitting in the tent watching the long jump.

When we run out of energy, we feel tired, weak and sometimes grumpy, and it is hard to concentrate. That is a sign that the body needs a refill. Thandeka notices it before the relay: her legs feel heavy and she cannot think about anything except lunch.

The questions for this section are with you now: what energy is and what we use it for.

## Subtopic: Food Gives Us Energy

We get our energy from the food we eat. Food is like fuel for our bodies, in the same way that petrol is fuel for a car. When we eat, our body breaks the food down and releases the energy stored in it. That energy is carried around the body by the blood to the muscles, the brain and every other part.

Some foods give us lots of energy. Starchy foods like pap, bread, rice, potatoes, samp and pasta are excellent energy foods. Sugary foods like fruit and honey give quick energy. Fats and oils, like those in peanut butter and cooking oil, store a lot of energy too. Thandeka's plate of pap and stew and her banana give her plenty of energy for the relay.

Other foods, like vegetables, beans, eggs, milk, fish and meat, also give energy, and they help the body to grow, repair itself and fight illness. So we need a balanced mix of foods, not only energy foods.

The amount of energy we need depends on what we do. An athlete training for a marathon needs much more food than a person who sits at a desk all day. Growing children need plenty of good food. If we eat much more energy food than we use, the body stores the extra as fat. If we eat too little, we become tired and weak.

Breakfast matters too. After a whole night without food, the body needs a refill. Learners who eat breakfast, even a bowl of porridge or a slice of bread, usually find it easier to concentrate in class than learners who come to school hungry. That is one reason many schools in South Africa run feeding schemes.

Water does not give us energy, but our bodies cannot use food properly without it. On a hot sports day, drinking water is very important.

The questions for this section are with you now: how food gives us energy and which foods give us the most.

## Subtopic: Animals Need Food for Energy Too

All animals need energy, and all animals get their energy from food. The cattle on a farm near Bloemfontein graze on grass all day to get the energy they need to walk, grow and make milk. A cheetah needs huge amounts of energy for its short, very fast chase, and it gets that energy by eating the meat of an antelope. A bird flapping its wings uses energy from the seeds and insects it eats.

Some animals eat a lot compared with their size because they use energy quickly. A tiny sunbird must drink nectar from hundreds of flowers every day. Other animals, like crocodiles and snakes, can go for weeks without eating, because they move less and do not use their energy to keep their bodies warm.

People, cattle and cheetahs all get energy from food, but they eat different foods. Young animals get their first food energy from their mothers. A calf drinks its mother's milk, and a baby bird is fed insects or seeds by its parents until it can find food itself. Some animals eat only plants. Some eat only other animals. Some, like people, eat both.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: saying we only use energy when we move; we use energy even when we sleep, for breathing, our heartbeat and keeping warm. Two: saying water gives us energy; water is vital, but energy comes from food. Three: thinking only sweets give energy; starchy foods like pap and bread are excellent energy foods. Four: thinking animals do not need energy when resting; all living things need energy all the time.

The questions for this section are with you now: how animals get energy from food.

## Subtopic: Energy for Everything

Energy makes things happen.

We use energy to run, jump, carry, shout and think.

We even use energy when we sleep, to breathe and keep our heart beating.

We get energy from food.

Pap, bread, rice and potatoes give lots of energy.

Fruit and honey give quick energy. Water helps, but it does not give energy.
