# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers noise pollution and how it can cause permanent hearing damage. The anchor is a primary school in central Johannesburg, right next to a busy taxi rank. All day long the Grade 4 class hears minibus taxis hooting, drivers calling for passengers, engines revving and loud music from passing cars. Their teacher, Mrs Dlamini, notices that learners struggle to concentrate when the noise is worst. On Monday, Kabelo tells the class that after watching a soccer match at the stadium on Saturday, surrounded by vuvuzelas, his ears rang and everything sounded muffled until Sunday evening. The big idea is that very loud or unwanted sound is a kind of pollution that can harm our health, and that loud sounds can damage our hearing for life, so we must protect our ears. Explaining noise pollution and how to protect hearing is examinable.

## Subtopic: What Is Noise Pollution?

Pollution is anything harmful or unwanted that people put into the environment. We know about dirty air, dirty water and litter. Sound can be pollution too. Noise pollution is loud or unwanted sound that disturbs people or animals or harms their health.

Not all sound is noise. A song you love, the sound of birds or a friend's voice can be pleasant. A sound becomes noise when it is unwanted or too loud. The same music can be enjoyable at a low volume and noise pollution when it blasts from a car at midnight.

Noise pollution comes from many sources. In cities it comes from traffic, hooting taxis, buses, trucks, aeroplanes, building sites, factories and loud music. Even in quieter places there can be noise from generators, machines on farms or a neighbour's party. Mines and factories can be very noisy places to work.

Noise pollution harms people in several ways. It makes it hard to concentrate, which is why Mrs Dlamini's class struggles during the busiest times at the taxi rank. It can stop people from sleeping well, which makes them tired and grumpy. It can cause headaches and make people feel stressed. Noise also harms animals. It can scare birds away from their nests and make it hard for animals to hear each other or to hear danger coming.

Many towns and cities have rules, called by-laws, that limit how loud people may be, especially at night.

The school finds ways to reduce the noise in the classroom. They close the windows that face the taxi rank during tests, hang thick curtains, and plant a row of trees and bushes along the fence. Soft materials and plants take in some of the sound vibrations, so less noise reaches the learners.

The questions for this section are with you now: what noise pollution is, where it comes from and how it affects people and animals.

## Subtopic: How Loud Sounds Damage Hearing

Our ears are delicate. Deep inside each ear, in a part shaped like a snail shell, are thousands of tiny hair cells. When sound vibrations reach them, the tiny hairs bend, and they send messages to the brain, which we hear as sound.

Very loud sounds make huge vibrations. These can bend the tiny hairs so much that they are damaged or broken. A short, very loud sound, like an explosion or a firecracker right next to your ear, can damage them at once. Loud sounds that go on for a long time, like music turned up high in earphones for hours, or working next to a noisy machine every day, damage them slowly.

The scary part is that these tiny hair cells do not grow back. Once they are damaged, the hearing loss is permanent. It cannot be fixed with medicine or an operation. People with hearing loss may need hearing aids, and they may find it hard to follow conversations, especially in noisy places.

There are warning signs. After a very loud event, your ears may ring, buzz or hiss, and sounds may seem muffled, as Kabelo found after the soccer match. This means your ears have been hurt. The ringing may fade after a day, but if it happens again and again, some damage can become permanent.

Loudness is measured in units called decibels. Normal talking is safe. A vuvuzela blown close to your ear can be as loud as a jet engine nearby, loud enough to start damaging hearing very quickly.

## Subtopic: Protecting Our Ears

The good news is that hearing damage from noise can be prevented. The class makes a list of ways to protect their ears.

Turn it down. Keep the volume of radios, TVs, cell phones and earphones at a comfortable level. A good rule is that if someone next to you can hear the music from your earphones, it is too loud.

Take breaks. Give your ears quiet time after being in a noisy place, and do not listen to music through earphones for hours without stopping.

Move away. The further you are from a loud sound, the softer it is. Do not stand right next to speakers at a party or a concert.

Cover your ears. Wear earplugs or earmuffs in very loud places, like stadiums, concerts and workshops. Workers in mines, factories and on building sites wear ear protection to keep their hearing safe. If you have nothing else, put your fingers in your ears when a loud sound comes.

Be a good neighbour. Do not blow a vuvuzela or hoot right next to someone's ear, and keep music down at night.

Two: thinking damaged hearing grows back; the tiny hair cells do not recover, so the damage is permanent. Three: thinking only sudden bangs harm hearing; long hours of loud music can damage it too. Four: thinking noise only harms people; it also disturbs animals.
