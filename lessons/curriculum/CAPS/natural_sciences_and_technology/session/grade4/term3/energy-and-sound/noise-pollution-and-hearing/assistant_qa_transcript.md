### subtopic_1 — What Is Noise Pollution?
**Assistant:** Why did Kabelo's ears ring after the soccer match?

**Tutor:** At the stadium, Kabelo was surrounded by thousands of fans blowing vuvuzelas, shouting and singing for hours. The sound was very loud and went on for a long time. Inside his ears, the tiny hair cells that help him hear were bent over and over again by huge vibrations. They became tired and some may have been slightly hurt. When the hair cells are overloaded like this, they can send confused signals to the brain even after the noise stops. The brain hears this as a ringing, buzzing or hissing sound that is not really there. Sounds also seem muffled, because the tired hair cells are not working properly. In Kabelo's case, the ringing faded after a day, which means his ears mostly recovered. But each time this happens, a few hair cells may be damaged for good. If it happens often, the damage adds up. Next time, Kabelo should wear earplugs at the stadium, or take breaks away from the loudest vuvuzelas.

### subtopic_2 — How Loud Sounds Damage Hearing
**Assistant:** Are earphones dangerous?

**Tutor:** Earphones are not dangerous on their own, but they can be if the volume is too high or they are used for too long. Earphones sit right inside or over your ears, so the sound goes straight into them. On a noisy taxi ride or in a busy place, people often turn the volume up very high to drown out the noise around them, without noticing how loud it really is. Listening at a high volume every day for hours can slowly damage the tiny hair cells in your ears, and because it happens gradually, you might not notice until some of your hearing is already gone. A simple rule is to keep the volume at about half or less, and to take a break after about an hour. If someone next to you can hear your music, or if you cannot hear a person talking to you from an arm's length away, it is too loud. Headphones that block outside noise can help, because then you do not need to turn the volume up as much.

### subtopic_3 — Protecting Our Ears
**Assistant:** Can loud noise harm animals in the wild?

**Tutor:** Yes, noise pollution affects animals in many ways. Many animals depend on their hearing to survive. Prey animals listen for predators sneaking up on them, and predators listen for prey. Birds sing to find mates and to mark their territory, and frogs call to each other at night. When there is a lot of noise from roads, machines, aeroplanes or loud music, animals may not hear these important sounds. Some birds that live near busy roads have been found to sing louder or at different times to be heard. Noise can also frighten animals away from good feeding and nesting places. In the sea, noise from ships' engines and from machines exploring the seabed travels a long way through the water. It can confuse whales and dolphins, which use sound to find food and to talk to each other over long distances. That is why some national parks have rules about noise, and why people should keep their voices and music down when visiting nature.
