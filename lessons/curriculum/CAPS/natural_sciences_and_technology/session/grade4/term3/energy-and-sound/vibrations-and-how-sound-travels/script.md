# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers how musical instruments and other things make sounds through vibrations, and how those vibrations travel outwards through materials such as air, water, plastic, metal and wood. The anchor is a Grade 4 science lesson in Kimberley in the Northern Cape. The teacher, Ms Pieterse, sets up five stations: a drum with rice on it, rulers to twang on desks, a long wooden table, a metal fence pole outside, and string telephones made from yoghurt cups. Later, one learner tells the class what she heard when she put her head under water in the swimming pool. The big idea is that every sound is made by something vibrating, and that the vibrations spread out from the source in all directions through solids, liquids and gases until they reach our ears. Explaining how sounds are made and how they travel through different materials is examinable.

## Subtopic: Sounds Are Made by Vibrations

To vibrate means to move quickly backwards and forwards, over and over. Every sound we hear is made by something vibrating.

The class starts with their own voices. They put their fingers gently on the front of their throats and hum. They feel a buzzing under their fingers: their vocal cords are vibrating. When they stop humming, the buzzing stops, and so does the sound.

Next, they hold one end of a plastic ruler flat on the edge of the desk, with most of the ruler sticking out over the edge. They push the free end down and let go. The ruler moves up and down so fast that it looks blurry, and it makes a twanging sound. As the ruler slows down and stops, the sound fades away.

At the drum station, they sprinkle rice on the drum skin and tap the drum gently. The rice jumps and dances, showing that the skin is moving up and down. The harder they tap, the higher the rice jumps. They cannot see the skin moving with their eyes, because the vibrations are very small and very fast, but the rice shows it.

Musical instruments make sounds in the same way. A guitar string vibrates when it is plucked. A marimba key vibrates when it is struck. The air inside a recorder vibrates when it is blown. Other sounds are made by vibrations too. A bee's wings vibrate so fast that they buzz. A slammed door vibrates, and so does a cell phone set to vibrate.

The questions for this section are with you now: how vibrations make sounds.

## Subtopic: Vibrations Travel Outwards

When something vibrates, it pushes and pulls on the material around it. In air, the vibrating drum skin pushes the air next to it, which pushes the air next to that, and so on. The vibration is passed along from one tiny bit of air to the next, spreading outwards in all directions, like ripples on a pond when you drop in a stone.

When the vibrations reach your ear, they make a thin part inside your ear, called the eardrum, vibrate too. Tiny parts inside your ear pass these vibrations on, and your brain turns them into the sound you hear.

Because the vibrations spread out in all directions, everyone in the classroom can hear the drum, whether they sit in front of it, behind it or next to it. As the vibrations spread further, they get weaker, which is why sounds are softer when you are far away. Thunder from a storm far away is a low rumble, but thunder from a storm overhead is a loud crack.

Sound needs something to travel through. It can travel through gases like air, through liquids like water, and through solids like wood, plastic and metal. In empty space, where there is no air or any other material, sound cannot travel at all. On the Moon, which has no air, astronauts have to talk to each other using radios.

The questions for this section are with you now: how vibrations travel outwards and reach our ears.

## Subtopic: Sound Through Different Materials

Sound travels through solids and liquids, and often better than through air.

Wood: one learner puts her ear flat on one end of the long wooden table while another taps gently at the other end with a pencil. The tapping sounds much louder through the table than through the air.

Metal: outside, one learner presses an ear to a metal fence pole while another taps the pole further along with a stone. The sound comes clearly along the metal. Long ago, people put their ears to railway lines to hear a train coming from far away.

Plastic and string: in the string telephone, one learner speaks into a yoghurt cup. The bottom of the cup vibrates, the vibrations travel along the tight string, and the other cup vibrates and passes the sound to the listener's ear. If the string is loose or someone pinches it, the sound stops.

Water: the learner who swam in the pool heard her friends' voices and the splashing, muffled but clear, while her head was under water. Whales and dolphins in the sea use sounds to talk to each other over long distances under water.

Sound passes through walls and windows too, which is how you can hear the neighbour's music or a taxi hooting outside while the classroom door is closed. Thick, soft materials like curtains, carpets and foam take in some of the vibrations, which is why a room with carpets and curtains sounds quieter than an empty room with bare walls.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking sound only travels through air; it travels through water, wood, plastic and metal too. Two: thinking sound travels in one direction only; it spreads out in all directions. Three: thinking sound can travel through empty space; it needs a material to travel through. Four: thinking a sound can be made without anything moving; every sound is made by a vibration.

The questions for this section are with you now: how sound travels through air, water, plastic, metal and wood.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the sound stations — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Feel the Buzz

Every sound comes from something vibrating, moving back and forth very fast.

Hum and feel your throat buzz. Twang a ruler. Watch rice jump on a drum.

Stop the vibrating and the sound stops.

Keep the jumping rice in mind and try the questions for this part: vibrations.

## Subtopic: Ripples of Sound

Vibrations spread out in all directions, like ripples on a pond.

They reach your eardrum and you hear the sound.

Far away, sounds are softer. In empty space, there is no sound.

Hold the pond ripples in mind and try this part's questions: how sound travels.

## Subtopic: Through Wood, Water and Metal

Sound travels through air, water, plastic, metal and wood.

A tap sounds louder with your ear on the desk. A string telephone carries your voice.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Sounds are made when something vibrates. The vibrations travel outwards in all directions through materials, until they reach our ears. Sound can travel through air, water, plastic, metal and wood, often better through solids than through air, but it cannot travel through empty space.
