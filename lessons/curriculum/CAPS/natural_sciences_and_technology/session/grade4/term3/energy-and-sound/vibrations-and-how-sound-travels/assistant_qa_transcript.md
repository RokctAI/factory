### subtopic_1 — Sounds Are Made by Vibrations
**Assistant:** Why can we see lightning before we hear the thunder?

**Tutor:** Lightning and thunder happen at exactly the same moment. The lightning is a giant electric spark, and it heats the air around it so suddenly that the air bursts outwards with a huge bang. That bang is the thunder. We see the flash first because light travels unbelievably fast. It reaches our eyes almost instantly, even from far away. Sound travels much more slowly through air. It takes about three seconds to travel one kilometre. So if a storm is a few kilometres away, the flash reaches you straight away, but the thunder arrives several seconds later. You can use this to estimate how far away a storm is. When you see the flash, start counting seconds slowly. When you hear the thunder, stop. Divide the number of seconds by three, and you have roughly how many kilometres away the lightning was. If the thunder comes almost immediately after the flash, the storm is very close, and you should already be safely indoors.

### subtopic_2 — Vibrations Travel Outwards
**Assistant:** Why does sound travel better through a desk than through the air?

**Tutor:** In a solid like wood, the tiny particles that make up the material are packed very closely together and are joined to each other. When one part of the desk vibrates, it pushes strongly on its neighbours straight away, and the vibration is passed along quickly and without spreading out much. In air, the particles are far apart and move about freely. The vibration has to pass from one particle to another across gaps, and it spreads out in all directions, so it gets weaker more quickly. That is why the tapping of a pencil at the far end of a long table sounds so much clearer when your ear is pressed to the wood. Sound also travels faster through solids and water than through air. In water, sound travels about four times faster than in air, and in steel it travels even faster. This is why whales can hear each other across huge distances in the ocean.

### subtopic_3 — Sound Through Different Materials
**Assistant:** How does a string telephone work?

**Tutor:** A string telephone uses vibrations travelling through solids. When you speak into one cup, the vibrations from your voice travel through the air inside the cup and hit the bottom of the cup. The thin bottom of the cup starts to vibrate, just like a drum skin. The string is attached to the middle of the bottom of the cup, so the vibrations pass into the string. As long as the string is pulled tight, the vibrations travel along it to the other cup. There, they make the bottom of the second cup vibrate. That cup bottom pushes on the air inside the cup, and the vibrations reach your friend's ear, so they hear your voice. If the string is slack, or touches something, or someone pinches it, the vibrations cannot pass along, and the line goes quiet. Real telephones work differently, using electricity and radio waves, but the string telephone is a great way to see that sound can travel through solid materials.
