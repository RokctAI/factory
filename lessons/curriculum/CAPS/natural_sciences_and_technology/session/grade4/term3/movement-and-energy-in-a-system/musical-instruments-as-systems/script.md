# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers musical instruments as systems that use movement input energy, such as blowing, beating and plucking, and have parts that move or vibrate to produce sound as the main output energy. The anchor is a primary school in Thohoyandou in Limpopo, practising for its Heritage Day concert. The school's marimba band plays on wooden marimbas. Two learners beat big drums made of wood and cow hide. One plucks a guitar, one blows a kudu horn, a few play recorders, and the youngest learners shake rattles made of dried seed pods. The music teacher, Mr Netshiongolwe, asks the class what all these instruments have in common. The big idea is that every musical instrument is a system that needs input energy from a person's movement and changes it into sound. Explaining instruments as systems and identifying their input and output energy are examinable.

## Subtopic: Instruments Are Systems

A system is a set of parts that work together to do a job. A bicycle is a system: the pedals, chain, gears and wheels work together to make it move. A kettle is a system too. If one part is missing or broken, the system does not work properly. A bicycle without its chain cannot move forward, however hard you pedal.

A musical instrument is a system whose job is to make sound. Look at the drum. It has a hollow wooden body, a skin of cow hide stretched tightly over the top, and pegs or strings that hold the skin tight. Each part has a job. The skin moves when it is hit. The hollow body makes the sound louder and richer. The pegs keep the skin tight so it makes a clear sound. If the skin is loose or torn, the drum sounds dull or makes no proper sound at all.

Like all systems that use energy, an instrument has an input and an output. The input energy is the movement of the player: the drummer's hands moving down and striking the skin. The output energy is sound. We can show it simply: movement in, arrow, drum, arrow, sound out.

Instruments cannot play themselves. A guitar lying on a chair is silent until someone plucks it. Without input energy from a player, they are silent. The more energy the player puts in, by hitting, blowing or plucking harder, the more energy comes out as sound.

Some energy is wasted, as with any system. The drum skin gets a tiny bit warm, and the drummer's hands sting a little. But the main, useful output of every instrument is sound.

The questions for this section are with you now: what a system is and how an instrument works as a system.

## Subtopic: Blowing, Beating and Plucking

Instruments can be grouped by how the player gives them movement energy.

Blowing. Some instruments are played by blowing air into them. The recorder, the flute, the trumpet, the vuvuzela and the kudu horn are blown. The moving air from the player's lungs is the input energy. Traditional horns made from the horns of kudu or other antelope have been used in Southern Africa for a very long time, for music and to send signals.

Beating. Some instruments are played by beating, hitting or striking them. Drums, marimbas, xylophones, triangles and cymbals are beaten, with hands or with sticks. The marimba has wooden keys of different lengths, and the players strike them with beaters that have soft rubber heads. The Zulu umakhweyana is a musical bow with a string that is struck with a thin stick, and a hollow calabash attached to the bow makes the sound louder.

Plucking. Some instruments are played by plucking, which means pulling a string or a metal strip and letting it go. The guitar, the harp and the banjo are plucked. The mbira, sometimes called a thumb piano, has thin metal strips that are plucked with the thumbs.

There are other ways too. A violin's strings are rubbed with a bow. Rattles and shakers are shaken, so the seeds inside hit the sides.

In every case, the input energy is movement from the player, and the main output energy is sound.

The questions for this section are with you now: blowing, beating and plucking as input energy for instruments.

## Subtopic: Parts That Vibrate

Every instrument has a part that moves when it is played. That part moves very quickly backwards and forwards, many times a second. This fast back-and-forth movement is called vibrating, and it is what makes the sound.

When the drum skin is hit, the skin vibrates up and down. Sprinkle a few grains of rice on a drum and tap it gently: the rice jumps, showing that the skin is moving. When a guitar string is plucked, the string vibrates so fast that it looks blurry. When a marimba key is struck, the wooden key vibrates. In a recorder or a kudu horn, there is no string or skin; the air inside the tube vibrates. In the mbira, the metal strips vibrate.

When the vibrating stops, the sound stops. Put your hand flat on a vibrating drum skin or guitar string, and the sound dies at once.

The class makes its own instrument system. They stretch rubber bands of different thicknesses over an open shoebox and pluck them. The rubber bands are the vibrating parts, the box is the hollow body that makes the sound louder, and their fingers give the input energy. When they push a pencil under the bands to lift them off the box, the sound becomes clearer. Every part of their shoebox guitar has a job, just like a real guitar.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking an instrument makes sound by itself; it needs input energy from the player's movement. Two: saying the output energy of a drum is movement; the main output is sound. Three: thinking only strings vibrate; drum skins, wooden keys, metal strips and air all vibrate. Four: thinking a system is just one object; a system is many parts working together.

The questions for this section are with you now: the parts of instruments that move or vibrate to make sound.

A part of every instrument vibrates. It moves back and forth very fast.
