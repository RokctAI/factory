### subtopic_1 — Instruments Are Systems
**Assistant:** Why does a drum have a hollow body if only the skin vibrates?

**Tutor:** The skin is the part that is hit and starts vibrating, but on its own, a stretched skin makes a fairly quiet, thin sound. The hollow body underneath makes the sound bigger and richer. When the skin vibrates, it pushes and pulls on the air inside the hollow body, and that air starts vibrating too. The body is shaped so that the air inside helps the sound grow louder and gives the drum its special deep tone. That is why a big drum with a large hollow body sounds deep and booming, while a small drum sounds higher and sharper. Many instruments use a hollow body in the same way. A guitar has a hollow wooden box with a round sound hole. The strings on their own would be very quiet, but the hollow box makes them loud enough to fill a room. The umakhweyana has a hollow calabash for the same reason. In a system, every part helps the instrument do its job well.

### subtopic_2 — Blowing, Beating and Plucking
**Assistant:** How is a marimba able to play different notes?

**Tutor:** A marimba has a row of wooden keys, or bars, of different lengths, arranged from long to short. Long keys vibrate more slowly when they are struck, and that makes a low, deep note. Short keys vibrate more quickly and make a high note. So by striking different keys, the player can play a tune, just like on a piano. Under each key hangs a tube, which is longer under the long keys and shorter under the short ones. The air in each tube vibrates along with its key and makes the note louder and clearer. Marimba bands are very popular in South African schools. Often several marimbas of different sizes play together. The big bass marimbas have very long keys and play the deep notes, while the smaller ones play the higher tune. In the next topic, you will learn more about how the size and speed of vibrations change whether a note is high or low.

### subtopic_3 — Parts That Vibrate
**Assistant:** Is my voice an instrument too?

**Tutor:** In a way, yes. Your voice is part of a wonderful natural system. Inside your throat is your voice box, which has two small folds of tissue called vocal cords. When you breathe out and decide to speak or sing, muscles pull the vocal cords close together. The air rushing up from your lungs makes them vibrate very quickly. That vibration makes a sound. Your throat, mouth and nose then shape that sound into words and notes, using your tongue, lips and teeth. You can feel the vibration yourself. Put your fingers gently on the front of your throat and hum. You will feel a buzzing. Stop humming and the buzzing stops. The input energy for your voice is the movement of air pushed out by your lungs, using energy from your food, and the output is sound. Choirs in South Africa, singing together in many voices, show how amazing this human instrument can be.
