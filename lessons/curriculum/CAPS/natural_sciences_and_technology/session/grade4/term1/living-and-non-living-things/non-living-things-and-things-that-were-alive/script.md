# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers non-living things, which cannot carry out all seven life processes, and things that were once living and are now dead. The anchor is a Grade 4 class on a beach clean-up in Durban. Along the sand they find a live crab, seaweed washed up by the waves, empty shells, a piece of driftwood, a feather, sand, rocks, a plastic bottle and a rusty tin. The big idea is that everything can be sorted into three groups: living, once living, and never living. The test is always the seven life processes. Sorting things into these groups and giving reasons are examinable.

## Subtopic: Things That Were Never Alive

A non-living thing cannot carry out all seven life processes. The sand, the rocks, the sea water, the air, the plastic bottle and the rusty tin have never fed, never grown and never had babies. They were never alive.

Some non-living things seem to do one or two life processes, and this can trick us. The sea moves, but it does not feed or reproduce. A cloud moves and gets bigger, but it does not breathe or sense anything. A fire seems to feed on wood, grow bigger and move, but it cannot reproduce in the way living things do, and it does not sense. A car moves and uses fuel, but it cannot grow or make baby cars.

So the rule is strict. To be living, a thing must carry out all seven life processes: feeding, growing, reproducing, breathing, excreting, sensing and moving. If even one is missing, it is not living.

Think about the rusty tin. Rust spreads over the metal and makes the tin look as if it is changing, and even growing. But rust is just the metal reacting with water and air. The tin is not feeding, breathing or sensing anything. It is still non-living.

Many non-living things are made by people, like the bottle, the tin, a cell phone and a school desk. Others are natural, like sand, rocks, water and air. Natural or made by people, they have never been alive. Some non-living things are still very important to living things. The crab needs sea water and air. Plants need sunlight and soil. Living things depend on non-living things every single day, but that does not make the water or the soil alive. Plastic is a special problem on the beach. It never rots, and sea birds, turtles and fish can mistake small pieces of it for food.

The questions for this section are with you now: what makes a thing non-living, and things that only seem alive.

## Subtopic: Things That Were Once Alive

Some things on the beach were alive in the past, but they are now dead. The empty shell was once part of a living sea animal, which built it to protect its soft body. When the animal died, the shell was left behind. The driftwood was once part of a living tree. The feather was once growing on a living bird. The dry seaweed was once a living plant-like thing in the sea.

Dead things cannot carry out the seven life processes any more, but they are different from things that were never alive, because they came from a living thing.

Many things we use every day were once living. A wooden desk was a tree. Paper is made from wood. A cotton T-shirt comes from the cotton plant. A leather shoe was the skin of a cow. Wool comes from sheep. Even coal, which we burn to make electricity in South Africa, formed from plants that died millions of years ago.

Bones and teeth are once living too. Scientists who dig up the bones of dinosaurs in the Karoo know that those animals lived there about two hundred million years ago. The bones have turned to stone over time, but they still tell us that something was once alive.

Dead things do not last forever. Tiny living things like fungi and bacteria break them down slowly, so a dead leaf rots and becomes part of the soil. That is why a fallen log goes soft and crumbly, while a plastic bottle stays the same for hundreds of years.

The questions for this section are with you now: things that were once alive and are now dead.

## Subtopic: Sorting into Three Groups

The class makes three piles. Living: the crab, which feeds, grows, breathes, senses danger and scuttles away. Once living: the shells, driftwood, feather and dry seaweed. Never living: sand, rocks, sea water, the bottle and the tin.

To sort anything, ask two questions. First: is it carrying out all seven life processes now? If yes, it is living. If no, ask the second question: did it come from something that was alive? If yes, it is once living, or dead. If no, it was never alive.

Some things need careful thinking. A wooden spoon is once living, because wood comes from a tree. A metal spoon was never alive. An apple on a tree is part of a living plant, and its seeds are still alive even after it is picked. A piece of firewood is once living, because it was part of a tree.

The class adds a few things from their school bags to the piles. A pencil is mostly wood, so it goes with the once-living things. A plastic ruler and a metal sharpener go with the never-living things. A ham sandwich is once living, because bread comes from wheat plants and ham comes from a pig. The class is surprised how many everyday things came from living things.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: saying fire is alive because it moves and grows; it cannot carry out all seven life processes. Two: putting a shell with never-living things; a shell came from a living animal, so it is once living. Three: saying a wooden table was never alive; wood comes from a tree. Four: calling any thing that does not move non-living; a resting seed is alive.

The questions for this section are with you now: sorting things into living, once living and never living.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the beach clean-up — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Never Alive

Sand, rocks, water, plastic and metal were never alive.

They cannot do all seven life processes.

Fire, clouds and cars move, but they are still not alive.

Keep the bottle in mind and try the questions for this part: never alive.

## Subtopic: Once Alive

A shell, a feather and driftwood were part of living things. Now they are dead.

Wood, paper, cotton, leather and wool all came from living things.

Dead things slowly rot away.

Hold the driftwood in mind and try this part's questions: once alive.

## Subtopic: Three Baskets

Basket one: living. Basket two: once living. Basket three: never living.

Ask: does it do all seven now? Did it come from a living thing?

Pocket summary of the lesson. Living things carry out all seven life processes. Things that were once living, like shells, wood and feathers, are now dead. Non-living things, like sand, rocks, water and plastic, were never alive. Doing one or two life processes is not enough to be alive.

Hold the three baskets in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: sorting.
