### subtopic_1 — Head, Body, Tail and Limbs
**Assistant:** Why can an ostrich not fly if it has wings?

**Tutor:** An ostrich is the biggest bird in the world. A grown ostrich is taller than an adult person and much heavier. To fly, a bird must be light, with big, strong wings compared with the size of its body. The ostrich's wings are far too small to lift its heavy body off the ground. Instead, the ostrich has long, powerful legs. It can run very fast across the open veld, faster than most animals that might hunt it, and its kick is strong enough to hurt a lion. It still uses its wings, though. It spreads them to help it balance and turn when it runs, to shade its chicks from the hot sun, and to show off to other ostriches. So the ostrich has the same body plan as other birds, with a head, a body, a tail, two legs, two wings and feathers, but its parts are suited to running, not flying.

### subtopic_2 — Sense Organs
**Assistant:** Why do animals have different body coverings?

**Tutor:** Each body covering helps an animal live in its own way and in its own place. Fur and hair trap warm air close to the body, which keeps mammals like lions and dogs warm on cold nights. Feathers keep birds warm too, and the long feathers on the wings help them fly. Dry, tough scales stop reptiles like crocodiles and snakes from drying out in the hot sun. Smooth, slippery scales help fish slide through water. A hard shell protects a tortoise from being bitten, and it can pull its head and legs inside. A frog's smooth, wet skin is so thin that the frog can take in some air through it, which is why frogs need to stay near water. Many coverings also help with hiding. A zebra's stripes make it hard for a lion to pick out one zebra in a running herd, and a frog's green skin blends in with the leaves.

### subtopic_3 — Animals Look Different
**Assistant:** How can a bat find insects in the dark if it cannot see well?

**Tutor:** Bats that hunt insects at night use their ears, not their eyes. As a bat flies, it makes very high squeaking sounds, so high that people cannot hear them. The sounds travel out through the air and bounce off anything in front of the bat, like a moth or a tree branch. The bounced sound comes back to the bat as an echo. The bat has large ears that are very good at catching these echoes. From how quickly the echo comes back, the bat can tell how far away the moth is, and which way it is moving. This is called echolocation. A bat can catch hundreds of tiny insects in one night this way, in complete darkness. It is a good example of how animals differ in their sense organs. People mostly trust their eyes, while a bat mostly trusts its ears, because each animal's senses are suited to the way it lives.
