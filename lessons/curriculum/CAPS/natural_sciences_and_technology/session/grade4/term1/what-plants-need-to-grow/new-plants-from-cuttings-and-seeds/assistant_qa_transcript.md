### subtopic_1 — New Plants from Cuttings
**Assistant:** How does a cutting know to grow roots if it has never had roots before?

**Tutor:** A plant stem is full of living parts that can change into other parts when they need to. Normally, the stem's job is to hold the plant up and carry water. But when a stem is cut off and pushed into damp soil, the bottom part of the stem notices that it is now in the dark and wet, and it starts to grow roots there. Some plants are very good at this, like spekboom, mint, geraniums and willow trees. Others hardly ever grow roots from a cutting, so gardeners grow them from seeds instead. The cutting does need to stay alive while it makes its roots. That is why we pull off some of the leaves, so it does not lose too much water before it has roots to drink with, and why we keep it out of the hottest sun. Gardeners sometimes dip the cut end in a special powder that helps roots grow faster.

### subtopic_2 — New Plants from Seeds
**Assistant:** Why does the root always grow down, even if the seed is upside down?

**Tutor:** A germinating seed can sense which way is down. Plants feel the pull of the Earth, the same pull that makes a ball fall when you drop it. The root grows towards that pull, downwards into the soil, where it will find water and something to hold on to. The shoot grows the other way, upwards, away from the pull and towards the light. If you plant a bean seed upside down, the root will first poke out pointing up, but it soon curves around and bends downwards. The shoot will also turn and curve upwards. You can test this in a clear jar with wet paper towel. Put a few seeds in different positions, some on their sides and some upside down. After a few days, every root will be pointing down and every shoot will be pointing up. This is a great example of sensing, one of the seven life processes, in a plant.

### subtopic_3 — Water and Warmth for Germination
**Assistant:** Why should we not water seeds so much that they sit under water?

**Tutor:** Seeds need water to germinate, but they also need air. A germinating seed is alive and breathing, and it is very busy using its stored food to grow. That takes air. In damp soil there are tiny spaces between the grains, filled with air, so the seed gets both water and air. If a seed is sitting deep under water, the water fills all the spaces and pushes the air out. The seed cannot get enough air, so it struggles to germinate, and fungi and bacteria in the water may make it rot. That is why we use damp cotton wool, not a jar full of water, for our bean test. When you plant seeds in a garden, water the soil so it is damp like a squeezed-out sponge, not a muddy puddle. Then the seed has water, warmth from the spring sunshine, and air, which is everything it needs to start growing.
