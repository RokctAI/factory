### subtopic_1 — Shelters Animals Make
**Assistant:** How can so many sociable weavers share one nest without fighting?

**Tutor:** A sociable weaver nest is a bit like a block of flats. The birds work together to build one big thatched roof out of dry grass and sticks, which can be several metres wide and very heavy. Under the roof, each pair of birds has its own small room, with its own entrance tube opening downwards. So the birds share the roof but have their own private spaces. Many hands, or in this case many beaks, make the work easier. Each bird keeps adding grass, and the nest grows over many years. Some nests have been in use for a very long time. The thick grass walls keep the rooms cool on hot Kalahari days and warm on freezing winter nights, so the shared nest helps every bird survive. Other animals use the nest too. Pygmy falcons sometimes live in some of the rooms, and snakes may try to climb in to steal eggs, so the birds keep watch together.

### subtopic_2 — Shelters People Make for Animals
**Assistant:** Why do traditional kraals use thorn branches?

**Tutor:** Thorn branches come from acacia trees and other thorny bushes that grow in many parts of South Africa. They have long, sharp thorns that hurt any animal trying to push through them. When the branches are packed tightly together into a fence, they make a strong barrier. Cattle and goats inside cannot push their way out, and predators like jackals and leopards do not want to force their way in through the thorns. The branches are also free, because they grow nearby, and people can cut them with simple tools. Some kraals are built with wooden poles or stacked stones instead, which last longer. In a traditional homestead, the cattle kraal is often at the centre, with the houses around it. Cattle were very important for wealth and for ceremonies, so the kraal was a special place. Today, many farmers also use wire and steel fences, but the job of the kraal is the same: keeping animals safe at night.

### subtopic_3 — What a Good Shelter Does
**Assistant:** Why do bees and wasps make six-sided cells?

**Tutor:** Bees and wasps build their cells in the shape of a hexagon, which is a shape with six sides. Hexagons fit together perfectly, side by side, with no gaps in between. Circles would leave little empty spaces between them, which would waste room. Hexagons also share their walls with the cells next to them, so the insects need less building material. For a bee, that material is wax, which takes a lot of energy to make. For a paper wasp, it is chewed wood paste. Using as little material as possible, while still making a strong shelter, is very clever building. The hexagon shape is also strong, because the walls hold each other up. The cells hold eggs and growing young, and in a beehive they also store honey and pollen. People who design buildings and packaging sometimes copy this hexagon idea because it is strong and light.
