### subtopic_1 — Shell Structures
**Assistant:** Is a weaver bird's nest a shell or a frame?

**Tutor:** It is a bit of both, and that is why it is a good question. The weaver starts by tying a ring of green grass strips between two hanging twigs. That ring is a frame, and it gives the nest its shape. Then the bird weaves more and more grass strips onto the ring, in and out, until it has made a round, hollow ball with an entrance at the bottom. When the nest is finished, the woven grass forms a closed outer skin around a hollow space, so it works like a shell. Many real structures mix the two ideas. A traditional grass hut has a frame of bent poles inside and a shell-like covering of thatch outside. A dome tent has a frame of bendy poles, with fabric stretched over it to make a skin. When you describe a structure, look at what holds its shape. If it is the outer skin, call it a shell. If it is the parts joined together inside, call it a frame.

### subtopic_2 — Frame Structures
**Assistant:** Why are triangles so strong in a frame?

**Tutor:** Make a square from four straws joined at the corners and push one corner sideways. The square leans over and turns into a squashed diamond shape. All four straws stay the same length, but the corners change their angles, so the shape collapses. Now make a triangle from three straws and push one corner. It does not change shape at all. A triangle cannot change its shape unless one of its sides gets longer or shorter, and the straws cannot stretch or shrink. That is why builders add diagonal parts to frames, turning squares into triangles. Look at a roof truss in a house, a bridge, an electricity pylon or the frame of a bicycle and you will see triangles everywhere. For the rabbit shelter, the class can add a diagonal stick in each side of the wooden frame. Then the shelter will not lean over when the wind blows or when a dog bumps into it.

### subtopic_3 — Shapes, Sizes and Materials
**Assistant:** Why should the rabbit shelter not be completely closed?

**Tutor:** A completely closed shelter, like a sealed plastic box, would be dangerous for the rabbits. Animals need fresh air to breathe, and a closed box would soon become stuffy. In the hot Kimberley summer, a closed box in the sun would also become very hot inside, like a car parked in the sun, and the rabbits could overheat. That is why the class chose wire mesh for the sides. The mesh lets air flow through and lets in light, while the small holes stop dogs and cats from getting in and stop the rabbits from getting out. The sloping roof gives shade and keeps rain off. The class also decided to put the shelter under a tree, so it is in the shade in the afternoon. A good design always thinks about all of the animal's needs, not just one. The best shelter keeps the animal safe, dry, cool and able to breathe.
