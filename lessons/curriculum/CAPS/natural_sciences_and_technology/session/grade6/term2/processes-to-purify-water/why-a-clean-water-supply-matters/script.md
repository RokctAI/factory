# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers why a clean water supply is important for people, plants and animals. The anchor is a primary school in a village near Lusikisiki in the Eastern Cape. Until last year, there was no tap at the school. Every morning, some learners walked down to a stream with buckets and carried water back up the hill for drinking, cooking the school lunch and cleaning. Cows and goats drank from the same stream upstream, and in summer many children got diarrhoea. Then the community and the municipality put in a borehole, a pump and a tank, and now there is a tap right outside the kitchen. The Grade 6 class, with their teacher, Mrs Ngcobo, writes a report for the opening day about what clean water has changed. The big idea is that all living things need water, that people need water that is clean and safe, and that because South Africa is a dry country, we must protect our water and use it carefully. The importance of a clean water supply is examinable.

## Subtopic: Water for People

People cannot live without water. You can survive for weeks without food, but only for a few days without water. About two thirds of your body is water, and you lose some every day by sweating, breathing and going to the toilet. You must replace it by drinking.

We need water for much more than drinking. We cook with it, wash ourselves, our clothes and our dishes, flush toilets, clean our homes and water our gardens.

The water we drink and cook with must be clean. Clean water has no harmful germs, no poisons and no dirt in it. Dirty water can carry the germs that cause water-borne illnesses like cholera, typhoid and diarrhoea. Before the tap came, the learners at the Lusikisiki school drank stream water that had been in contact with animal dung. After the tap came, the nurse at the local clinic noticed that fewer children from the school came in with diarrhoea.

Clean water also helps with hygiene. Washing hands with soap and clean water after using the toilet and before eating is one of the best ways to stop germs from spreading.

A clean water supply also saves time. The learners who used to carry water now spend that time in class. In many places, it is women and girls who walk long distances to fetch water, sometimes for hours a day, which takes them away from school and work.

Clean water is so important that the Constitution of South Africa says that everyone has the right to have access to sufficient water. Government and municipalities must work to provide it.

The questions for this section are with you now: why people need water, and why it must be clean.

## Subtopic: Water for Plants, Animals and Farms

Plants need water too. A plant takes in water through its roots and uses it in photosynthesis to make food. Water also carries dissolved minerals up from the soil to the leaves, and keeps the plant firm. A plant without water wilts, and soon dies.

Animals need water to drink, and many live in water. Fish, frogs, crabs and water insects need clean water with enough oxygen in it. If a river is polluted, these animals get sick or die, and the animals that eat them go hungry. Farm animals like the cows and goats near the school need clean water to stay healthy and give good milk and meat.

Most of the water used in South Africa goes to farming. Farmers pump water from rivers, dams and boreholes to irrigate crops like maize, vegetables, citrus and sugar cane. If the water is polluted with salts or chemicals, it can damage the crops and the soil, and germs in irrigation water can make people ill when they eat raw vegetables.

Factories, mines and power stations also need large amounts of water. A coal power station, for example, uses water to make steam.

Even the soil depends on water: earthworms and the decomposers that keep soil fertile need damp soil to live.

So water connects everything. Clean water in rivers keeps ecosystems healthy, feeds the crops we eat, and supplies our homes.

The questions for this section are with you now: why plants, animals and farms need clean water.

## Subtopic: A Water-Scarce Country

Mrs Ngcobo shows the class a map of rainfall in South Africa. The east, including Lusikisiki, gets good summer rain, but much of the west, like the Northern Cape, gets very little. On average, South Africa gets much less rain than most other countries, and much of the water evaporates in the heat before it reaches rivers. South Africa is called a water-scarce country, which means there is not much water to share for everyone's needs.

Droughts make this worse. In 2017 and 2018, Cape Town came very close to Day Zero, the day when the dams would be too low to supply the city's taps, and people had to cut their water use to fifty litres each per day.

Because our water is scarce, every person must help to save it and keep it clean. At home and at school, we can:

Turn off the tap while brushing our teeth.

Fix leaking taps and toilets quickly. A dripping tap can waste thousands of litres a year.

Take short showers instead of full baths.

Use a bucket instead of a hosepipe to wash a car, and water gardens in the cool evening.

Collect rainwater in tanks for gardens.

Never throw litter, oil or chemicals into rivers or drains.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking water that looks clear is always safe to drink; it can carry invisible germs and chemicals. Two: thinking South Africa has plenty of water; it is a water-scarce country. Three: thinking only people need clean water; plants, animals and farms need it too. Four: thinking a small drip does not matter; it can waste thousands of litres.

The questions for this section are with you now: why South Africa must save water, and how we can save it.
