### subtopic_1 — Cleaning Water Before We Use It
**Assistant:** If our tap water has chlorine in it, is it safe to drink every day?

**Tutor:** Yes. The amount of chlorine in treated tap water is very small and carefully controlled. It is enough to kill germs, but far too little to harm people. Water works test it many times a day to make sure the amount is right. In fact, adding chlorine to drinking water is one of the most important health discoveries ever made. Before cities treated their water, diseases like cholera and typhoid killed huge numbers of people. Today, in towns where the water works and pipes are properly looked after, those diseases have become rare. Some people notice a faint smell or taste of chlorine, especially in water straight from the tap. If you fill a jug and leave it in the fridge for a few hours, most of the smell goes away. In South Africa, the water in most cities meets high safety standards, but in some places pipes and works are broken, so if your municipality warns that the water is unsafe, boil it first.

### subtopic_2 — Cleaning Water After We Use It
**Assistant:** Why does the water works add chemicals to make flocs instead of just filtering the river water?

**Tutor:** Because the bits of clay and mud in river water are incredibly tiny. Many of them are so small that they would take days or even weeks to settle on their own, and they would slip straight through the sand filters or quickly block them. The chemicals that are added work a bit like a magnet for dirt. They make the tiny particles stop pushing each other away, so that they bump together and stick, forming soft, fluffy clumps called flocs. As the flocs grow, they also trap other tiny bits, including many germs. Big, heavy flocs settle in hours instead of weeks. This means that most of the dirt is removed in the settling tanks, and the sand filters only have to catch what is left, so they work much better and last longer. You can see something similar if you add a pinch of alum, which is sold at some chemists, to a jar of muddy water: the mud clumps and settles much faster.

### subtopic_3 — Why Both Matter
**Assistant:** Where does the water in a toilet go in a village with no sewers?

**Tutor:** Many homes and schools in rural areas, and some in towns, are not connected to sewers. They use other kinds of toilets. A pit latrine is a toilet built over a deep pit in the ground. The waste collects in the pit, where bacteria slowly break it down, and when the pit is nearly full, it must be emptied or a new one dug. A well-built ventilated pit latrine has a pipe with a fly screen to remove smells and keep flies away. A septic tank is a large underground tank that collects waste from flush toilets. Solids settle and are broken down by bacteria, and the liquid soaks slowly into the ground through pipes. It must be emptied by a truck every few years. Some places use composting toilets. All these systems must be built away from boreholes, springs and rivers, because if waste seeps into groundwater, it can carry germs into drinking water. Safe toilets are one of the most important ways to prevent water-borne illnesses.
