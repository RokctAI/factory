# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the different ways water can be cleaned, or purified. The anchor is a Grade 6 class on a three-day camp near Hogsback, a village high in the forested Amathole mountains of the Eastern Cape. On the second morning, the camp's water pipe breaks after a storm, and the tap runs dry. The camp leader, Mr Mbatha, turns the problem into a lesson. The class collects buckets of water from the stream behind the camp. After the rain, it is brown and cloudy, with leaves, twigs and grit in it. It looks clean enough to some learners once the twigs are removed, but Mr Mbatha says that nobody may drink a drop until it has been through every step. The big idea is that water can be cleaned by sieving, settling, decanting and filtering, which remove insoluble dirt, and by boiling or adding chemicals, which kill germs. Only after the germs are killed is the water safe to drink. The methods of cleaning water are examinable.

## Subtopic: Removing the Dirt You Can See

The first steps remove the insoluble substances that we can see: leaves, twigs, sand, grit and mud. These are the same methods we used to separate mixtures.

Sieving. Mr Mbatha ties a clean cloth over the top of a bucket and pours the stream water through it. The cloth acts like a sieve. Leaves, twigs, insects and bigger bits of grit stay on the cloth. A kitchen sieve or a piece of fine netting works too.

Settling. Next, the water is left to stand still in the bucket for about an hour. The heavy sand and much of the mud sink slowly to the bottom and form a layer of sediment. The water on top becomes clearer.

Decanting. Very carefully, without shaking the bucket, a learner pours the clearer water off the top into a clean container, leaving the muddy sediment behind.

Mr Mbatha explains why the order matters. If they poured the muddy water straight into a filter, the filter would quickly clog with mud and stop working. Removing the bigger dirt first lets each step do its own job.

These three steps make the water look much better. But it is still slightly cloudy, because very fine bits of mud are still floating in it.

The questions for this section are with you now: sieving, settling and decanting water.

## Subtopic: Filtering the Water

To remove the fine bits, the class builds a filter. They cut the bottom off a large plastic bottle, take off the cap, and stretch a piece of clean cloth over the neck. Then they turn the bottle upside down and fill it with layers, from the bottom up: a layer of cotton wool, a layer of crushed charcoal from the camp fire, a thick layer of fine sand, a layer of coarse sand, and a layer of small gravel on top.

They pour the decanted water in at the top. It trickles slowly down through the layers and drips out of the neck into a clean jug. The first water to come out is a bit dirty, from the sand itself, so they pour it away. After that, the water dripping out is clear.

Each layer catches smaller and smaller bits. The gravel catches small grit, the sand catches fine mud, and the charcoal helps to remove some bad smells and tastes. This is the same idea used in the huge sand filters at water treatment works.

Mr Mbatha holds the jug up to the light. The water is beautifully clear. Can they drink it now? No. Filtering removes insoluble dirt, but it does not remove all germs, and germs are too small to see. Clear water can still make you very ill. The filter also cannot remove dissolved substances, like salt or chemicals.

The questions for this section are with you now: how to build and use a water filter, and what it cannot do.

## Subtopic: Killing the Germs

The last and most important step is to kill the germs. There are two main ways.

Boiling. Mr Mbatha pours the filtered water into a pot and heats it over the gas stove until it bubbles hard all over, which is called a rolling boil. He keeps it boiling for at least one minute. The great heat kills the germs that cause cholera, typhoid and diarrhoea. Then he leaves it to cool with a lid on. Boiling is very effective, but it needs fuel, which costs money, and it takes time.

Adding chemicals. The other way is to add a small amount of a chemical that kills germs. In South Africa, people are taught to add one teaspoon of plain, unscented household bleach to twenty-five litres of water, stir it, and leave it to stand for at least two hours before drinking. Only an adult should measure the bleach, because bleach is a poison in large amounts and must be kept away from children. If the water is still cloudy, it should be settled and filtered again before the bleach is added, because dirt can protect germs from the chemical. Water purification tablets, sold at pharmacies and camping shops, work in the same way. Water treatment works use a chemical called chlorine.

After cleaning, the water must be kept in a clean container with a lid, so that dirt and germs cannot get back in. It should be poured out or scooped with a clean cup, not with hands.

At last, the class drinks the water they cleaned themselves. It tastes a little flat, because boiling drives out some air, but it is safe.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking clear water is safe to drink; germs are invisible. Two: thinking filtering kills germs; it removes dirt, but not all germs. Three: thinking a quick warm-up is the same as boiling; it must reach a rolling boil. Four: thinking more bleach is better; too much is poisonous, so use the right amount.

The questions for this section are with you now: boiling, chemicals and storing clean water safely.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the camp near Hogsback — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Kill the Germs

Boil the water, or add the right amount of bleach.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Water can be cleaned in steps. Sieving through a cloth removes leaves and twigs. Settling lets mud sink, and decanting pours off the clearer water. Filtering through sand, gravel and charcoal removes fine dirt. These steps remove insoluble dirt but not all germs. To make water safe to drink, the germs must be killed, by boiling it at a rolling boil for at least a minute or by adding the right amount of bleach or purification tablets. Store clean water in a clean, covered container.
