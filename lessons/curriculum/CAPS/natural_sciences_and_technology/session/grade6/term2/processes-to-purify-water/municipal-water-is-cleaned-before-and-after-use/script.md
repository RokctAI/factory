# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers how the water in our taps is cleaned by the municipality before we use it, and how it is cleaned again after we use it. The anchor is a Grade 6 class from Vereeniging in the south of Gauteng, on the banks of the Vaal River. Most of the tap water for Johannesburg, Pretoria and many other towns in Gauteng comes from the Vaal. The class visits a water treatment works, where a guide, Ms Khumalo, shows them giant tanks, pipes and pumps. She explains that every day, enormous amounts of river water are cleaned here and pumped up to the reservoirs that feed homes, schools and hospitals. The next day, the class visits a wastewater treatment works, where the dirty water from people's sinks, baths and toilets arrives through underground pipes to be cleaned again. The big idea is that municipal water is cleaned in several steps before it reaches our taps, and that used water is cleaned again before it is returned to the river, because people further downstream will use it too. Municipal water treatment is examinable.

## Subtopic: Cleaning Water Before We Use It

River water is a mixture. It contains sand, mud, leaves, tiny living things, germs and some dissolved substances. Ms Khumalo walks the class through the steps that turn it into safe tap water.

Screening. Water is pumped from the river through big metal screens, like giant sieves. They stop fish, branches, plastic and other large objects from getting in.

Adding chemicals to clump the dirt. Next, special chemicals are mixed into the water. They make the tiny bits of mud and other fine particles stick together into bigger, heavier clumps called flocs. Flocs are much easier to remove than tiny particles.

Settling. The water flows slowly through huge settling tanks. The heavy flocs sink to the bottom as sludge, which is removed. The clearer water flows out over the top, like decanting on a giant scale.

Filtering. The water then trickles down through deep beds of sand and gravel, which filter out the last fine particles.

Killing germs. Finally, a small, carefully measured amount of chlorine is added to kill any germs that are left. A little chlorine stays in the water as it travels through the pipes, so that germs cannot grow on the way to our taps. That is why tap water sometimes smells faintly of a swimming pool.

Ms Khumalo holds up a glass of water from the start of the works and one from the end. The first is brown and cloudy. The second is so clear that the class can read the words on a page through it.

Storing and testing. The clean water is pumped to reservoirs and water towers, and then through pipes to homes. Scientists in laboratories test samples every day, to check that the water is safe.

The questions for this section are with you now: the steps that clean water before it reaches our taps.

## Subtopic: Cleaning Water After We Use It

When water goes down a sink, a bath or a toilet, it becomes wastewater, also called sewage. It is full of human waste, soap, food scraps, fat and germs. In towns, it flows through underground pipes, called sewers, to a wastewater treatment works.

At the wastewater works, the class watches the steps that clean it.

Screening. Screens remove rags, plastic, wet wipes, nappies and other rubbish that should never have been flushed.

Removing grit. The water flows slowly through channels where sand and grit settle to the bottom.

Settling. In large round tanks, much of the solid waste settles to the bottom as sludge, and fat and oil float to the top and are scraped off.

Using bacteria. The water then goes into big tanks where air is bubbled through it. Huge numbers of helpful bacteria live in these tanks. They feed on the dissolved waste and break it down, just as decomposers do in nature.

Settling again and disinfecting. The bacteria settle out in another tank, and the cleaned water is often disinfected to kill remaining germs. Then it is released back into the river.

The sludge is treated and dried, and some can be used as fertiliser on farms.

The questions for this section are with you now: how wastewater is cleaned before it goes back to the river.

## Subtopic: Why Both Matter

Why clean water twice? Because water is used again and again. The cleaned wastewater from one town flows down the river to the next town, where it is taken in and cleaned to become someone else's drinking water. Farms, fish and wild animals along the river depend on it too.

Scientists test the water leaving the wastewater works too, to check that it is clean enough before it goes back into the Vaal.

If wastewater works break down, are overloaded, or are not looked after, raw sewage flows into rivers. The germs can spread water-borne illnesses like cholera to people downstream, and the waste feeds algae that use up oxygen and kill fish. Keeping both kinds of works running well protects everyone's health.

Cleaning water costs money, for chemicals, electricity, pumps, pipes and skilled workers. That is one reason we pay for municipal water, and why wasting it wastes money as well as water.

Everyone can help the works do their job. Never flush wet wipes, nappies, sanitary pads, plastic or rags; they block pipes and pumps. Never pour cooking fat or oil down the sink; it hardens and blocks sewers. Report leaking pipes and overflowing manholes to the municipality.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking tap water comes straight from the river; it is cleaned in several steps first. Two: thinking chlorine is added to make water taste nice; it kills germs. Three: thinking sewage just disappears down the drain; it is cleaned at a wastewater works. Four: thinking wet wipes can be flushed; they block pipes.

The questions for this section are with you now: why water is cleaned before and after use, and how we can help.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Municipal water is cleaned before we use it: screens remove large objects, chemicals clump fine dirt into flocs, the flocs settle, sand filters remove the last particles, and chlorine kills germs. The water is stored, tested and piped to homes. After use, wastewater flows through sewers to a wastewater works, where screens, settling and bacteria clean it before it returns to the river. Both matter, because people downstream use the same water. Never flush wipes or pour fat down the drain.
