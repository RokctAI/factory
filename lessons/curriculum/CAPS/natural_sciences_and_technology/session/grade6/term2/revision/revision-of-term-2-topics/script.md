# Part 1 — Expert

This session revises the Term 2 topics of Matter and Materials: solids, liquids and gases; mixtures; solutions as special mixtures; dissolving; mixtures and water resources; and processes to purify water. The anchor is a Grade 6 class in Mbombela, the capital of Mpumalanga, near the Crocodile River. For Water Week, the class turns the school hall into an exhibition with three stations. At the first station, there is a tray of ice, a glass of water and a steaming kettle, beside a spilled mixture of beans, rice and sand, a sieve and a gold pan. At the second, there are glasses of sugar water, salt water, sand water and oil water, a scale, a saucer of drying salt water and a stopwatch. At the third, there is a jar of brown river water, a bottle filter, a model wetland and a poster of a water treatment works. The learners take turns to explain each station to parents and visiting classes. The big idea of the term is that matter is made of particles, that mixtures can be separated, that solutions form when particles of a solute spread between particles of a solvent, and that our water must be kept clean and purified. The whole of Term 2 is examinable.

## Subtopic: Particles and Mixtures

At the first station, Thabo explains the particle model. All matter is made of tiny particles that are always moving, with spaces between them. In a solid, like the ice, the particles are packed closely in a regular pattern and vibrate on the spot, so a solid has a fixed shape and volume. In a liquid, like the water, the particles are close together but slide past each other, so a liquid flows and takes the shape of its container. In a gas, like the water vapour from the kettle, the particles are far apart and move fast in all directions, so a gas spreads out to fill any space. Heating makes particles move faster, so solids melt and liquids boil. Thabo points to the cold window near the kettle, where water vapour has condensed into drops, because its particles slowed down and came closer together.

Then Naledi explains mixtures. A mixture contains at least two different substances that are not joined, so each keeps its own properties and they can be separated. She picks the beans out of the spilled mixture by hand, which is hand sorting. She shakes the rest through a sieve, so the fine sand falls through and the rice stays behind, which is sieving. With muddy water, she lets the mud sink, which is settling, pours off the clearer water, which is decanting, and pours it through filter paper, which is filtering. The gold pan shows settling and decanting together, because heavy gold sinks quickly.

The questions for this section are with you now: particles in solids, liquids and gases, and separating mixtures.

## Subtopic: Solutions and Dissolving

At the second station, Lindiwe explains solutions. When sugar is stirred into water, it dissolves: water particles pull the sugar particles away, layer by layer, and they spread out evenly into the spaces between the water particles. The mixture is a solution, a uniform mixture that is the same all the way through. A solution is clear, though it may be coloured; nothing settles out; and a filter cannot separate it. Sand in water is not a solution.

The sugar is the solute, the substance that dissolves. The water is the solvent, the liquid that does the dissolving. A substance that dissolves is soluble. Lindiwe puts a jug of water on the scale, adds salt and stirs: the mass goes up by exactly the mass of the salt, so the dissolved salt is still there. On the windowsill, the saucer of salt water has dried, leaving salt crystals. Evaporation recovers a dissolved solute.

She also shows a glass where the colour from a tea bag has spread all through the water without any stirring, because the moving water particles carry the tea particles about.

When no more solute can dissolve, the solution is saturated, and extra solute stays at the bottom. Hot water can dissolve more of most solids. Insoluble substances, like sand, chalk, flour and oil, do not dissolve in water.

Finally, the stopwatch: a solid dissolves faster in hot water, when it is stirred or shaken, and when it is in small pieces. A fair test changes only one thing at a time.

The questions for this section are with you now: solutions, saturation and the rate of dissolving.

## Subtopic: Clean Water

At the third station, Sibusiso holds up the jar of brown river water. Water can be polluted by insoluble substances like plastic, litter, oil and mud; by soluble substances like fertilisers, poisons, detergents and salts, which cannot be seen or filtered out; and by germs from sewage, which cause water-borne illnesses like cholera, typhoid and diarrhoea.

Wetlands help. Their plants slow the water so that mud settles, filter out bits, and take in some dissolved substances, while bacteria break down waste. Like a sponge, wetlands soak up floods and release water slowly in dry times.

A clean water supply is important for people, plants and animals, and South Africa is a water-scarce country, so we must save water. Water can be cleaned by sieving, settling, decanting and filtering, which remove dirt, and by boiling or adding the right amount of bleach, which kill germs. At the municipal water works, river water is screened, clumped, settled, filtered and treated with chlorine before it reaches our taps. After we use it, wastewater goes through sewers to a wastewater works, where screens, settling and bacteria clean it before it returns to the river.

Sibusiso ends by reminding the visitors to save water at home, because cleaning water costs money and South Africa's water is scarce.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking particles in a solid do not move; they vibrate. Two: thinking sugar melts in water; it dissolves. Three: thinking a filter can remove dissolved salt; only evaporation can. Four: thinking clear water is always safe; germs and dissolved poisons are invisible.

The questions for this section are with you now: water pollution, wetlands and purifying water.

## Subtopic: Particles and Separating

Solids are packed, liquids slide, gases spread out.

Sort, sieve, settle, decant and filter to separate mixtures.

Keep the gold pan and the sieve in mind and try the questions for this part: particles and separating.

## Subtopic: Dissolving

A solute dissolves in a solvent to make a clear solution.

Hot water, stirring and small grains make it faster.

Hold the glass of sugar water in mind and try this part's questions: dissolving.

## Subtopic: Safe Water

Keep water clean, and purify it before drinking.

Pocket summary of the term. All matter is made of moving particles: packed in solids, sliding in liquids and spread out in gases. Mixtures can be separated by hand sorting, sieving, settling, decanting and filtering. A solution forms when a solute dissolves in a solvent, and the solute can be recovered by evaporation. A saturated solution can dissolve no more. Hot water, stirring and small grains speed up dissolving. Water can be polluted by insoluble substances, soluble substances and germs. Wetlands clean water, and water works purify it before and after we use it.

Hold all three exhibition stations in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: safe water.
