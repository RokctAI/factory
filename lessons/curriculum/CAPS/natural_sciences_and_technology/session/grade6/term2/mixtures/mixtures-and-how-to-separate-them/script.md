# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers what a mixture is, and how we can separate mixtures that we can see into their parts. The anchor is a Grade 6 class from Graskop on a trip to Pilgrim's Rest in Mpumalanga, a town that grew up in the 1870s when gold was found in the streams there. At the old diggings, a guide hands each learner a round metal pan. They scoop up gravel and water from the stream, swirl it round and round, and tip the muddy water out over the edge. The light sand washes away, and the heavy specks at the bottom of the pan are left behind. One learner, Precious, finds a tiny shining fleck. Back at school the next day, their teacher, Mr Mashego, has a different problem: a bag in the storeroom has split, and dried beans, rice, sand and small stones have spilled together on the floor. The big idea is that a mixture contains two or more different substances that are not joined together, so they can be separated again, and that we choose a method of separating to suit the mixture. Mixtures and methods of separating them are examinable.

## Subtopic: What a Mixture Is

A mixture is made of at least two different substances that are mixed together but not joined to each other. Each substance in the mixture keeps its own properties.

The spilled bag is a mixture of four things: beans, rice, sand and stones. If you look closely, you can still see each one. The beans are still big and brown, the rice is still small and white, the sand is still fine and gritty. Nothing new has been made.

The gravel in the stream at Pilgrim's Rest is a mixture too. It contains water, mud, sand, small stones and, if you are lucky, tiny pieces of gold.

Mixtures are all around us. Soil is a mixture of tiny bits of rock, dead plant material, water and air. A fruit salad is a mixture. Muddy water, the air we breathe, sea water, and the mealie and bean mixture called umngqusho are all mixtures.

In a mixture, the amounts can change. You can put more beans or fewer beans in the bag, and it is still a mixture of beans and rice. This is different from a substance like pure water, which is always the same throughout.

Some mixtures are easy to see, because we can see the different parts. These are called visible mixtures. In this lesson we focus on them. Other mixtures, like sugar dissolved in water, look the same all the way through, and we will look at those in the next lessons.

The questions for this section are with you now: what a mixture is, with examples.

## Subtopic: Sorting and Sieving

Because the substances in a mixture are not joined together, we can separate them. We choose a method that uses a difference between the parts, such as their size, or whether they sink or float.

Hand sorting. The simplest method is to pick out the parts by hand. The class picks the big beans and the stones out of the spilled mixture one by one, using their fingers. Hand sorting works well when the pieces are big, few and easy to tell apart. It is slow for small pieces. People hand sort every day: picking stones out of dried beans before cooking, or sorting litter into glass, plastic, paper and cans for recycling.

Sieving. A sieve is a container with a mesh, a net of small holes. Small pieces fall through the holes, and big pieces stay behind. Mr Mashego shakes the rest of the spilled mixture through a kitchen sieve. The fine sand falls through, and the rice stays in the sieve. With a sieve that has slightly bigger holes, the rice falls through and the beans stay behind. Sieving separates a mixture of solids by the size of their pieces. Builders sieve sand to take out the stones before they make concrete. Bakers sieve flour to remove lumps. Gold miners long ago used sieves to remove large stones from gravel before panning.

## Subtopic: Filtering, Settling and Decanting

Some mixtures contain a solid mixed into a liquid, like muddy water. Sieves have holes that are far too big to catch mud. We need other methods.

Settling. If you leave muddy water to stand still for a while, the heavier bits of sand and mud slowly sink to the bottom. This is called settling. The bits that sink form a layer at the bottom, called sediment. The water above becomes clearer.

Decanting. Once the mud has settled, you can carefully pour the clearer water off the top into another container, leaving the sediment behind. This is called decanting. You must pour slowly and stop before the mud starts to flow out. Panning for gold works by settling and decanting together: gold is very heavy, so it settles quickly to the bottom of the pan, while the lighter sand and water are swirled and poured away.

Filtering. To remove finer bits from water, we use a filter. A filter has holes so tiny that water can pass through, but small solid bits cannot. The class folds a coffee filter, puts it in a funnel over a jar, and pours in the decanted water. Clear water drips into the jar, and a thin layer of mud is left on the filter paper. A cloth, cotton wool or a layer of fine sand can also be used as a filter.

Often we use more than one method. To clean the muddy water, the class first lets it settle, then decants it, then filters it.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking a mixture makes a new substance; each part keeps its own properties. Two: thinking a sieve can separate mud from water; its holes are far too big. Three: thinking decanting means pouring everything out; you pour off only the liquid and leave the sediment. Four: thinking filtered water is safe to drink; it can still contain germs and dissolved substances.

The questions for this section are with you now: settling, decanting and filtering.
