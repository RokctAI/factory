### subtopic_1 — What a Mixture Is
**Assistant:** Is gold really found in streams, and why?

**Tutor:** Yes. Gold forms deep underground in cracks in rocks, often mixed with a white mineral called quartz. Over thousands and millions of years, rain, rivers and frost slowly break down the rocks on hillsides. The pieces of rock, sand and gold are washed down into streams. Because gold is one of the heaviest substances there is, much heavier than sand or ordinary stones, it sinks quickly and collects in low places in the stream bed, behind boulders and in cracks in the rock. That is why miners in places like Pilgrim's Rest and Barberton in the 1800s panned the streams. When they found gold in the streams, they followed it uphill to find the rocks it came from. Today, most of South Africa's gold has been mined deep underground on the Witwatersrand around Johannesburg, where very old riverbeds have been buried and turned to rock. Gold panning at Pilgrim's Rest is now mostly done for fun by visitors.

### subtopic_2 — Sorting and Sieving
**Assistant:** How do recycling plants separate a mixture of rubbish?

**Tutor:** Recycling plants use the same ideas we learnt, but on a giant scale, and they use differences between the materials. First, workers hand sort the rubbish on long moving belts, picking out big items and things that should not be there. Then the rubbish passes over big rotating sieves, called screens, that let small pieces fall through and send big flat pieces like cardboard another way. Strong magnets pull out steel cans, because steel is magnetic. Aluminium cans are not magnetic, so another machine uses a clever trick with electricity and magnets to make them jump off the belt. Blowers push light paper and plastic away from heavy glass. Some plants even use cameras and puffs of air to sort different kinds of plastic. At home, the best help we can give is to separate our own waste into paper, plastic, glass and cans, and to rinse them, so the recycling plant has an easier job.

### subtopic_3 — Filtering, Settling and Decanting
**Assistant:** What is the difference between filtering and sieving if both use holes?

**Tutor:** They are very alike, because both let small things through and hold big things back. The main difference is the size of the holes and what we are separating. A sieve has holes we can see, and we usually use it to separate pieces of different solids from each other, like sand from rice, or lumps from flour. A filter has holes so tiny that we cannot see them, and we usually use it to separate very fine solid bits from a liquid or a gas, like mud from water. Filter paper, a coffee filter, a cloth or a thick layer of fine sand all have tiny gaps between their fibres or grains that only water, or air, can get through. A car has an air filter that stops dust getting into the engine, and a vacuum cleaner has a filter too. So you can think of a filter as a very, very fine sieve that is made to separate solids from liquids or gases.
