# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the parts of a solution, called the solute and the solvent, what it means for a substance to be soluble, and how a dissolved solute can be recovered by evaporation. The anchor is a Grade 6 class from Velddrif, a fishing town where the Berg River meets the sea on the West Coast of the Western Cape. Near the town there are salt pans: wide, shallow ponds where sea water is let in and left to stand in the hot sun and the strong wind. Over the summer, the water slowly disappears, and a thick white crust of salt is left behind, which workers rake into big white heaps. The class walks along the edge of the pans, crunching salt under their shoes. Back at school, their teacher, Ms Cloete, sets up two experiments: one with a scale, and one with saucers on the windowsill. The big idea is that a solution is made of a solute dissolved in a solvent, and that when the solvent evaporates, the solute is left behind. Solutes, solvents, soluble substances and recovering a solute by evaporation are examinable.

## Subtopic: Solute, Solvent and Solution

Scientists use special words for the parts of a solution.

The solute is the substance that dissolves. In salt water, the salt is the solute. In sugar water, the sugar is the solute.

The solvent is the liquid that the solute dissolves in. In salt water and sugar water, the water is the solvent.

The solution is the mixture of the solute and the solvent together.

So we can write: solute plus solvent gives solution. Salt plus water gives salt water.

Water is the most common solvent on Earth. It can dissolve more substances than any other everyday liquid, which is why it is sometimes called the universal solvent. That is very useful: water carries dissolved minerals to plant roots, dissolved food around our bodies in the blood, and dissolved salt in the sea. But there are other solvents too. Nail polish does not dissolve in water, but it does dissolve in nail polish remover. Some paints and inks dissolve only in special solvents.

A substance that dissolves in a solvent is called soluble. Salt is soluble in water. Sugar is soluble in water. Instant coffee, the colour in food colouring, and powdered cooldrink are soluble in water too.

The questions for this section are with you now: solute, solvent, solution and soluble.

## Subtopic: The Solute Is Still There

When salt dissolves, it seems to vanish. Ms Cloete uses a kitchen scale to prove that it does not.

She puts a jug of water on the scale. It shows exactly five hundred grams. Then she weighs out twenty grams of salt on a piece of paper. Before she adds the salt, she asks the class to predict what the scale will show once the salt has dissolved. Some learners say five hundred grams, because the salt will disappear. Others say five hundred and twenty.

She tips in the salt and stirs until the water is clear again. The scale shows five hundred and twenty grams. The salt has not disappeared at all. Its mass is still there, even though we can no longer see it.

This shows an important rule: when a solute dissolves, nothing is lost. The mass of the solution equals the mass of the solvent plus the mass of the solute.

The learners also notice that the salty water tastes salty everywhere in the jug, from the top to the bottom. The dissolved salt has spread evenly all through the water, as we expect in a solution.

The questions for this section are with you now: why the mass of a solution equals the solute plus the solvent.

## Subtopic: Getting the Solute Back by Evaporation

If the salt is still in the water, can we get it out again? Not by filtering, because the dissolved pieces pass through the filter. But we can get it back by evaporation.

Evaporation is when a liquid slowly changes into a gas, called vapour. Water evaporates all the time from puddles, from washing on a line and from the sea, and it evaporates faster when it is warm, windy and the air is dry.

When salt water evaporates, only the water turns into vapour and goes into the air. The salt cannot evaporate, so it stays behind. As more and more water leaves, there is not enough water left to keep all the salt dissolved, and the salt comes out as small, solid crystals.

On Monday, Ms Cloete pours a little salt water into three saucers and places them on the sunny windowsill. On Tuesday, the water is lower. By Thursday, the saucers are dry, and their bottoms are covered in tiny white crystals that taste salty. In another experiment, she heats some salt water gently in a small pan on a hot plate, while the learners stand back. The water bubbles away in minutes, and salt is left in the pan. Heating makes evaporation much faster.

This is exactly what happens at the salt pans near Velddrif, but on a giant scale. The sun and the wind evaporate the sea water, and the salt is left behind to be harvested. The same method is used at salt pans in other parts of South Africa, where salty underground water is pumped into pans.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking the solute is the liquid; the solute is the substance that dissolves, and the liquid is the solvent. Two: thinking a dissolved solute has no mass; the scale shows it is still there. Three: thinking a filter can get the salt back; only evaporation can. Four: thinking the salt evaporates with the water; only the water evaporates, and the salt is left behind.

The questions for this section are with you now: evaporation and recovering salt from salt water.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A solution is made of a solute, the substance that dissolves, and a solvent, the liquid it dissolves in. Water is the most common solvent. A substance that dissolves is soluble. When a solute dissolves, its mass is still there.
