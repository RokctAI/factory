# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers saturated solutions and insoluble substances. The anchor is a Grade 6 class in Paarl in the Western Cape, getting ready for the school fair. Mrs Adams, a parent, is going to make koeksisters, the plaited doughnuts that are dipped into ice-cold, sticky syrup. She shows the class how to make the syrup and lets them help. First she pours some cold water into a glass jug, and the learners take turns to add one level spoon of sugar at a time and stir until it dissolves. Spoon after spoon disappears, until, at last, the sugar stops dissolving and a layer of white grains stays at the bottom, however hard they stir. Then their teacher, Mr Daniels, sets out four more glasses of water, with sand, chalk dust, flour and cooking oil. The big idea is that there is a limit to how much solute can dissolve in a solvent, and that some substances do not dissolve at all. Saturated solutions and insoluble substances are examinable.

## Subtopic: A Solution That Is Full

The first spoons of sugar dissolve quickly. As more sugar is added, it takes longer and longer to dissolve, and the solution becomes thick and syrupy. Finally, no amount of stirring makes the last spoon disappear. The grains swirl around and then sink to the bottom.

The solution is now saturated. A saturated solution is a solution in which no more solute can dissolve at that temperature. It is full.

Using the particle model, Mr Daniels explains why. Each bit of water can only hold so many sugar particles between its own particles. Once all the water is carrying as much sugar as it can, any extra sugar has nowhere to go, so it stays as solid grains.

Different solutes have different limits. The class repeats the test with salt in the same amount of cold water. The salt solution becomes saturated much sooner than the sugar solution. Far more sugar than salt can dissolve in the same amount of water.

The amount of solvent matters too. If you add more water to the saturated sugar solution, the grains at the bottom start to dissolve again, because now there is more water to carry them. A solution that can still dissolve more solute is called unsaturated.

The questions for this section are with you now: what a saturated solution is, and why it cannot dissolve more.

## Subtopic: Hot Water Holds More

Mrs Adams now pours the saturated mixture, with the sugar grains still at the bottom, into a pot and heats it on the stove while the learners watch from a safe distance. As the solution gets hotter, the grains at the bottom disappear. She keeps adding more sugar, and it keeps dissolving.

For most solids, hot water can dissolve more solute than cold water. When the water is hot, its particles move faster and are further apart, so they can pull more solute particles away and fit more of them in. That is how Mrs Adams can make syrup that is much thicker and sweeter than anything the class could make with cold water.

She adds a little lemon juice and a pinch of ginger and cinnamon, boils the syrup for a few minutes, and then lets it cool and puts it in the fridge overnight. Koeksister syrup must be very cold when the hot doughnuts are dipped in it, so that they soak it up and become crunchy on the outside.

What happens when a hot saturated solution cools? The cold water cannot hold as much sugar as the hot water did. If there is too much sugar, some of it may come out of the solution as crystals. People use this to grow sugar crystals on a string. Honey, which is mostly sugar dissolved in a little water, sometimes forms crystals in a cold cupboard for the same reason.

The questions for this section are with you now: how temperature changes how much solute can dissolve.

## Subtopic: Insoluble Substances

Now the class looks at Mr Daniels's four glasses, each stirred for a full minute.

The sand glass: the sand swirls and then sinks. The water stays clear on top. Sand does not dissolve.

The chalk glass: the water turns milky white, but after some time the chalk dust settles at the bottom. Chalk does not dissolve.

The flour glass: the water turns cloudy and white, and the flour slowly settles to the bottom. Flour does not dissolve in cold water.

The oil glass: the oil breaks into droplets while stirring, but then floats up and forms a separate yellow layer on top of the water. Oil does not dissolve in water.

These substances are insoluble in water. An insoluble substance is one that does not dissolve in a solvent. Other examples are stones, plastic, wax, most metals and wood.

Insoluble substances form mixtures with water that are not solutions. They are not uniform, they are often cloudy, they settle or float, and they can be separated by settling, decanting or filtering. When the class pours the chalky water through filter paper, the chalk stays on the paper and clear water comes through.

Being insoluble is often useful. A plastic bottle can hold water because plastic does not dissolve in it, and the sand in a water filter stays in place for the same reason. A cup made of sugar would not last long!

Some substances are insoluble in water but soluble in another solvent. Oil and grease do not dissolve in water, but they can be removed with soap, which helps them mix with water, and some paints dissolve in turpentine.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking you can dissolve endless amounts of sugar; a solution becomes saturated. Two: thinking cold water dissolves more sugar than hot water; hot water dissolves more. Three: thinking cloudy chalky water is a solution; chalk is insoluble and settles. Four: thinking oil dissolves in water; it forms a separate layer.

The questions for this section are with you now: insoluble substances and how they behave in water.
