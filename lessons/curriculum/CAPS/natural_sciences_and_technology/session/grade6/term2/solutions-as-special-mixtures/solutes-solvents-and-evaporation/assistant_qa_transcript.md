### subtopic_1 — Solute, Solvent and Solution
**Assistant:** If the salt is left behind when sea water evaporates, why don't rivers and lakes become salty too?

**Tutor:** Rivers do carry small amounts of dissolved salts and minerals, which they pick up from rocks and soil as the water flows over and through them. But river water keeps moving and is always being replaced by fresh rainwater, so the salts never build up. The river carries them down to the sea. In the sea, the water evaporates into the air, but the salts stay behind, because salts cannot evaporate. The evaporated water forms clouds and falls as fresh rain, which runs into the rivers again. This has been happening for millions of years, so the sea has slowly become salty. Some lakes that have no river flowing out of them, in very dry areas, become salty in the same way, because water only leaves by evaporation. Pans in the dry parts of South Africa, like those in the Northern Cape, often have salty water or a crust of salt for the same reason.

### subtopic_2 — The Solute Is Still There
**Assistant:** Why does salt water leave crystals with flat sides and square shapes?

**Tutor:** That is a sharp observation. If you look at grains of table salt with a magnifying glass, many of them are tiny cubes. This shape comes from the way the salt particles fit together. As the water evaporates and there is less and less water to keep the salt dissolved, the dissolved salt pieces start to join up again into a solid. They join in a very regular pattern, a bit like building blocks stacked neatly in rows and columns. Because the pattern repeats in the same way in all directions, the crystal grows with flat faces and square corners. Other substances form crystals with other shapes. Sugar crystals have a different shape, and the crystals of some minerals are long and thin, or shaped like six-sided columns. If the water evaporates slowly, the crystals have more time to grow and can become quite large. If it evaporates quickly, like when it is heated, many small crystals form.

### subtopic_3 — Getting the Solute Back by Evaporation
**Assistant:** Can we get drinking water from sea water?

**Tutor:** Yes, but not just by filtering it, because dissolved salt passes straight through ordinary filters. One way is to boil the sea water and collect the steam. When the vapour cools, it turns back into liquid water, and that water has no salt, because the salt stayed behind in the pot. This is called distillation. It works, but it uses a lot of energy to boil large amounts of water. Another way is to push sea water at very high pressure through special thin sheets that let water particles through but hold back the salt. This is called reverse osmosis. Plants that turn sea water into drinking water are called desalination plants. Some have been built along the South African coast, for example in towns that ran very short of water during droughts. Desalination is expensive and uses a lot of electricity, so it is usually used only where there is not enough fresh water from rivers, dams and underground.
