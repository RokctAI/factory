### subtopic_1 — A Solution That Is Full
**Assistant:** Why does salt reach saturation so much sooner than sugar?

**Tutor:** Every solute has its own limit, which depends on how strongly its particles hold on to each other and how strongly water particles can pull them away. Sugar particles are pulled into water very easily, and a lot of them can fit in. At room temperature, about two hundred grams of sugar can dissolve in one hundred millilitres of water, which is more than the mass of the water itself. That is why syrup can be so thick and sweet. Salt particles are held together more strongly in their crystal, and only about thirty-six grams of salt can dissolve in the same amount of water. So the salt solution fills up much sooner. The two also behave differently when heated. Much more sugar dissolves in hot water than in cold, but salt dissolves only a little more in hot water than in cold. Scientists measure these limits for thousands of substances and write them in tables, so that cooks, chemists and factories know exactly how much will dissolve.

### subtopic_2 — Hot Water Holds More
**Assistant:** Why does soap help to wash away oil if oil is insoluble in water?

**Tutor:** Soap is a clever substance. Each soap particle has two different ends. One end is attracted to water, and the other end is attracted to oil and grease, and avoids water. When you wash a greasy plate with soapy water, the oil-loving ends of the soap particles push into the grease, and the water-loving ends stick out into the water. Many soap particles surround each tiny blob of grease, so it is wrapped in a coat with water-loving ends on the outside. The water can now hold on to these coated blobs, and when you rinse, they are carried away down the drain. The oil has not really dissolved, but it has been broken into tiny droplets that stay spread out in the water. That is why oily hands do not get clean with water alone, but do with soap. This is also why washing hands with soap is so good at removing germs, which are often stuck in greasy dirt on our skin.

### subtopic_3 — Insoluble Substances
**Assistant:** Is flour insoluble even though it makes the water cloudy?

**Tutor:** Yes. When flour is stirred into cold water, the tiny pieces of flour spread out and make the water cloudy and white, but they do not dissolve. They are still separate bits, too small to see one by one, but big enough to block and scatter light. That is why the water is cloudy instead of clear. If you leave the glass to stand, the flour slowly settles to the bottom, and the water above gets clearer. If you filter it, the flour is caught on the filter paper. A true solution, like sugar water, would stay clear and never settle. Something interesting happens when flour is heated in water, as when making gravy or a white sauce. The starch grains in the flour soak up water, swell and burst, and the mixture becomes thick. But the flour still has not dissolved like sugar does. So cloudiness is a good clue: if a mixture is cloudy and settles, the solid is insoluble.
