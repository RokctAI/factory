# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers solutions, a special kind of mixture. The anchor is a Grade 6 class in Clanwilliam in the Western Cape, a town surrounded by the farms where rooibos tea is grown. In the morning, the class makes a big pot of rooibos for a parents' meeting, and someone stirs sugar into it. Then their teacher, Mr Fortuin, gives each group two clear glasses of water, a spoon of sugar and a spoon of sand. They stir the sugar into one glass and the sand into the other, and watch. In the afternoon, a nurse from the clinic visits and shows them how to make a sugar and salt drink for children who have lost a lot of water through diarrhoea. The big idea is that a solution is a mixture that is the same all the way through, made when a solid dissolves in a liquid, like sugar or salt in water. What a solution is, and how it is different from other mixtures, is examinable.

## Subtopic: A Special Kind of Mixture

The groups stir their two glasses and then stop and look.

In the sand glass, the water goes cloudy and brown while it is being stirred. When the stirring stops, the sand sinks to the bottom, and you can still see every grain. The mixture is not the same all the way through: there is sand at the bottom and clearer water at the top.

In the sugar glass, the sugar grains swirl around and then slowly disappear. After a minute, the water is clear again, and nobody can see any sugar. But when a learner takes a sip with a clean straw, it tastes sweet. The sugar is still there. It has dissolved.

When a solid dissolves in a liquid, it breaks up into pieces that are far too small to see, and they spread evenly all through the liquid. The mixture that forms is called a solution.

A solution is a uniform mixture. Uniform means the same all the way through. Every sip from the top, the middle or the bottom of the sugar water tastes equally sweet. Every part of the mixture has the same amount of sugar. The sugar does not sink to the bottom, even if the glass stands all day.

Sugar water, salt water, sweet rooibos tea and sea water are all solutions.

Mr Fortuin asks: if the sugar is still there, where is it? It has broken into pieces far too small to see, even with a microscope, and those pieces are now spread among the water. In the next lessons, we will see exactly how this happens.

The questions for this section are with you now: what dissolving is and what makes a solution a uniform mixture.

## Subtopic: Solution or Not?

How can we tell if a mixture is a solution? Mr Fortuin gives the class four checks.

Can you see separate pieces? In a solution, you cannot see the dissolved solid, even with a magnifying glass. In the sand mixture, you can see the grains.

Is it clear? A solution is clear, or transparent, so you can see through it. It may be coloured, like rooibos tea or cooldrink, but it is still see-through. Muddy water and milk are cloudy, so they are not true solutions.

Does anything settle? In a solution, the dissolved substance never settles to the bottom, however long you wait. In sand and water, the sand settles within seconds.

Can it be filtered out? When the class pours the sugar water through filter paper, the water that comes through still tastes sweet. The dissolved sugar passes straight through the filter, because its pieces are far smaller than the holes. When they filter the sand mixture, the sand stays on the paper. So filtering cannot separate a solution.

Using these checks, the class sorts some mixtures. Salt water: solution. Sugar water: solution. Rooibos tea, once the leaves are taken out: solution. Muddy water: not a solution. Sand and water: not a solution. Oil and water: not a solution, because the oil floats in a separate layer.

The questions for this section are with you now: how to tell whether a mixture is a solution.

## Subtopic: Solutions in Everyday Life

Solutions are everywhere in daily life.

Drinks. Tea and coffee with sugar, cooldrinks, and juice made from a concentrate are solutions. The colour, the flavour and the sugar have dissolved in water.

Sea water. The sea at Lambert's Bay, not far from Clanwilliam, tastes salty, because salt and other substances are dissolved in it. You cannot see the salt, and it never settles to the bottom of a bucket of sea water.

Medicine. Some medicines are sold as powders that must be dissolved in water. Cough mixtures are often solutions.

The sugar and salt drink. The nurse explains that diarrhoea makes the body lose a lot of water and salts, which can be very dangerous, especially for babies and small children. To replace them, clinics teach people to make a sugar and salt solution. An adult measures one litre of clean, boiled and cooled water, and dissolves eight level teaspoons of sugar and half a level teaspoon of salt in it. The child sips it slowly and often. It must be made carefully, with exact amounts, and a sick child must still be taken to the clinic.

In nature too. Rainwater soaks into the soil and dissolves minerals, forming a solution that plant roots take in. Our blood carries dissolved sugar, salts and other substances around our bodies.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking dissolved sugar has disappeared; it is still there, as the sweet taste shows. Two: thinking a solution must be colourless; it can be coloured, as long as it is clear. Three: thinking any mixture of a solid and water is a solution; sand and water is not. Four: thinking a filter can remove dissolved salt; the dissolved pieces pass right through.

The questions for this section are with you now: solutions at home, in nature and in health.

## Subtopic: Solutions All Around

Tea, cooldrink, sea water and the sugar and salt drink are solutions.

Pocket summary of the lesson. When a solid like sugar or salt dissolves in a liquid like water, it breaks into pieces too small to see, which spread evenly through the liquid. The mixture is called a solution. A solution is a uniform mixture: it is the same all the way through, it is clear, though it may be coloured, nothing settles out, and a filter cannot separate it.
