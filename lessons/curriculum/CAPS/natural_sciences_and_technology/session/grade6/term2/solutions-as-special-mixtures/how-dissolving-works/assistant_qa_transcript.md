### subtopic_1 — Particles Break Away
**Assistant:** Why did the colours on the plate not mix together in the middle?

**Tutor:** This surprises almost everyone who does the experiment. The stripes of colour meet in the middle, but for quite a while they stay as separate stripes with sharp edges instead of turning into a muddy brown. Each sweet is dissolving at the same rate, so the sugary, coloured water spreads out from each one at about the same speed. When two stripes meet, they push against each other a little, because both are full of dissolved sugar, and neither one can easily push into the other. Also, there is no stirring or shaking, so nothing mixes them by force. If you leave the plate for a long time, the particles do slowly spread across the boundaries, and the colours start to blur together. If you give the plate a little shake, they mix much faster. It is a beautiful way to see that dissolving and spreading happen slowly and steadily on their own, when the water is still.

### subtopic_2 — Spreading Out Between the Water Particles
**Assistant:** If dissolved particles fill the spaces between water particles, why does sugar water still take up more room than plain water?

**Tutor:** Good thinking. The spaces between water particles are real, but they are small, and they can only hold so much. A few solute particles can tuck into the gaps, but sugar particles are actually quite big compared with water particles, so most of them need room of their own. That is why a cup of water with a cup of sugar dissolved in it fills more than one cup, but less than two cups. The volume grows, but by less than you would expect if you just added the two volumes together. For salt, the effect is even more noticeable, because the salt pieces are pulled very tightly in among the water particles. The mass, though, always adds up exactly, because no particles are lost. This is a useful thing to remember: in dissolving, mass is always kept, but volume may not simply add up.

### subtopic_3 — Dissolving Is Not Melting
**Assistant:** Does everything that dissolves look clear?

**Tutor:** Yes, a true solution is always clear, which means you can see through it, but it does not have to be colourless. Rooibos tea, cooldrink, food colouring in water and copper sulfate solution in a science lab are all brightly coloured, but you can still see through them, and if you shine a torch through them, the beam is not scattered into a cloudy glow. The colour comes from the dissolved particles themselves, which absorb some colours of light and let others through. Clear and colourless are different words. Water and salt water are clear and colourless. Tea is clear and coloured. Milk and muddy water are not clear, because they contain bits that are not dissolved and that scatter light. So a quick test is to hold the mixture up to a window or a torch: if you can see through it and nothing settles, it is likely to be a solution.
