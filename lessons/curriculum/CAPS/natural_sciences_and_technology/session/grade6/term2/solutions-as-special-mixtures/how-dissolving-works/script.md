# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers what happens to the particles when a solid dissolves in a liquid. The anchor is a Grade 6 class in Mahikeng, the capital of North West. Their teacher, Mr Seleka, lays a white plate on each group's desk and asks them to place coloured sweets around the edge, like the numbers on a clock: red, orange, yellow, green and purple. Then he gently pours a little warm water into the middle of the plate until it just touches the sweets. Nobody may stir or shake the desk. Within a minute, bright stripes of colour begin to creep away from each sweet towards the centre of the plate, like the spokes of a wheel. In another corner, he drops a tea bag into a tall glass of hot water and leaves it alone. Brown swirls slowly spread down and out until the whole glass is the colour of tea. The big idea is that when a substance dissolves, its particles break away from each other and spread out evenly in the spaces between the particles of the solvent. Explaining dissolving using particles is examinable.

## Subtopic: Particles Break Away

Remember that all matter is made of tiny particles, that there are spaces between them, and that they are always moving.

A grain of sugar is a solid. Inside it, millions of sugar particles are packed tightly together, held to each other by forces of attraction.

Water is a liquid. Its particles are close together, but they slide around and move all the time, bumping into everything around them.

When a sugar grain is put into water, the water particles crowd around the outside of the grain. The water particles are attracted to the sugar particles, and they tug at them. The particles on the surface of the grain are pulled away from the grain, one by one, and carried off into the water. Then the next layer of sugar particles is exposed, and they are pulled away too. Layer by layer, the grain gets smaller, until all of its particles have been pulled into the water and the grain is gone.

Mr Seleka gives each group a magnifying glass and a single sugar grain in a drop of water. They watch its sharp edges become rounded as it shrinks and shrinks, until it is gone. They never see the particles themselves, because those are far too small.

This is dissolving. The solute particles break away from each other and mix in among the solvent particles.

On the plate, the coloured coating of each sweet is made of sugar and food colouring. The warm water particles pull the sugar and colour particles off the surface of the sweet, which is why the sweets slowly lose their colour and turn white.

The questions for this section are with you now: how solvent particles pull solute particles away.

## Subtopic: Spreading Out Between the Water Particles

Once the solute particles have broken away, they do not stay next to the grain. They spread out.

All particles in a liquid are moving and bumping into each other all the time. The solute particles are knocked about by the moving water particles, and they slowly spread from where there are many of them to where there are few. They move into the spaces between the water particles. In time, they are spread evenly all through the liquid. That is why a solution is uniform: there is the same number of solute particles in every part of it.

The tea glass shows this well. At first, the brown colour is thick and dark near the tea bag, and the water at the top is still clear. Over the next half an hour, without any stirring, the colour spreads until the whole glass is the same light brown. The coloured stripes on the plate spread towards the middle in the same way.

Because the solute particles fit into the spaces between the solvent particles, a solution can take up a little less space than the solute and solvent did before they were mixed. But no particles are lost, so the mass of the solution is the same as the mass of the solute and the solvent added together.

The dissolved particles are far too small to see, and they are spread too thinly to block light, so a solution is clear. They are also far too small to be caught by filter paper, and they are kept moving by the water particles, so they never settle to the bottom.

The questions for this section are with you now: how solute particles spread out evenly, and what this explains.

## Subtopic: Dissolving Is Not Melting

People often say that sugar melts in tea. Mr Seleka explains why that is the wrong word.

Melting happens to one substance on its own when it is heated. Ice melts into water. Butter melts in a hot pan. Candle wax melts near the flame. The particles get enough energy from heat to break out of their rows and slide past each other. No second substance is needed.

Dissolving needs two substances: a solute and a solvent. The solute particles are pulled away by the solvent particles and spread between them. Sugar dissolves in tea even if the tea is cold, although more slowly. Sugar left on a plate in the sun does not turn into a liquid. It needs very great heat to melt sugar, like when sugar is heated in a pan to make caramel.

So we say: the ice melts, but the sugar dissolves.

A quick way to tell them apart: melting turns one solid into a liquid of the same substance, and you can watch it happen with no water around. Dissolving always happens inside a liquid, and the solid seems to vanish into it.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking dissolved particles vanish; they are still there, spread between the water particles. Two: thinking sugar melts in tea; it dissolves, because it needs a solvent. Three: thinking a solution is uniform only if it is stirred; the particles spread out on their own, stirring only speeds it up. Four: thinking the dissolved particles sink to the bottom over time; they stay spread out.

The questions for this section are with you now: the difference between dissolving and melting.

Pocket summary of the lesson. When a solid dissolves, the particles of the solvent pull the particles of the solute away from each other, layer by layer.
