### subtopic_1 — Hot and Cold
**Assistant:** If stirring makes sugar dissolve faster, why can't stirring make a saturated solution dissolve more?

**Tutor:** It is important to keep two ideas apart: how fast something dissolves, and how much of it can dissolve. Stirring changes how fast. It brings fresh water to the grains, so the sugar dissolves sooner. But once all the water is holding as much sugar as it can at that temperature, the solution is saturated, and there is no fresh, unfilled water left to bring to the grains. You can stir for an hour and the extra grains will just swirl around and settle again. To make more dissolve, you have to change the solution itself, by adding more water or by heating it, because hot water can hold more sugar. So stirring and small grains are like a faster car on the road: you reach the end of the road sooner, but the road is not any longer. Temperature is different, because it changes both how fast sugar dissolves and how much can dissolve.

### subtopic_2 — Stirring and Shaking
**Assistant:** Do these rules work for things other than sugar?

**Tutor:** Yes, they work for most solids that dissolve in liquids. Salt, instant coffee, washing powder, stock cubes and bath salts all dissolve faster in hot water, with stirring, and in small pieces. Think about how a stock cube is often crumbled into a pot of hot water and stirred: all three tricks at once. In industry, factories that dissolve substances in water use huge tanks with heaters and big mixing paddles, and they grind solids into fine powder first. There are a few exceptions with temperature. A small number of substances actually dissolve less well in hot water, and gases behave the opposite way to most solids: more gas dissolves in cold water than in hot. That is why a cold cooldrink stays fizzy for longer than a warm one, which goes flat quickly as the gas bubbles out. But for the solids we use every day in the kitchen, the three rules hold well.

### subtopic_3 — Big Lumps and Fine Grains
**Assistant:** Why do we need to use the same amount of water and sugar in each glass?

**Tutor:** Because otherwise the race is not fair, and we cannot trust the result. Imagine that in the hot-water glass you used a little sugar and a lot of water, but in the cold glass you used a lot of sugar and a little water. If the hot glass finished first, you would not know whether it was because the water was hot, or because there was less sugar to dissolve, or because there was more water to dissolve it. Scientists call the thing they choose to change the variable they are testing. Everything else, like the amount of water, the amount and type of sugar, the size of the glass and the stirring, must be kept the same. These are the things kept constant. Then, if the result changes, it can only be because of the one thing that was changed. Scientists also repeat their tests a few times, because one result can be a fluke. If the hot water wins three times out of three, we can be much more confident.
