# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the factors that affect how fast a solid dissolves, called the rate of dissolving. The anchor is a Grade 6 class in Welkom in the Free State, on a cold winter morning. Their teacher, Mrs Mokoena, makes tea for the staffroom, and a learner notices that the sugar in her hot tea disappears almost at once, while the sugar he once put in his cold juice took ages. Mrs Mokoena turns this into a competition: the sugar race. Each group gets glasses, a kettle of hot water, a jug of cold water, teaspoons, a stopwatch on a cell phone, and three kinds of sugar: sugar cubes, ordinary white sugar, and very fine icing sugar. Their job is to find out what makes sugar dissolve faster. The big idea is that a solid dissolves faster when the solvent is hotter, when the mixture is stirred or shaken, and when the solute is in smaller pieces. These three factors, and how to test them fairly, are examinable.

## Subtopic: Hot and Cold

The first race tests temperature. Group 1 fills one glass with cold water from the fridge and another with hot water from the kettle, which Mrs Mokoena pours for them. They put exactly one level teaspoon of ordinary sugar into each glass at the same moment, and start the stopwatch. They do not stir either glass.

In the hot water, the sugar is gone in well under a minute. In the cold water, grains are still lying on the bottom several minutes later.

Group 1 also tries a glass of water at room temperature. Its sugar dissolves faster than in the fridge water, but slower than in the hot water.

The higher the temperature of the solvent, the faster a solid dissolves.

Why? Remember that particles move faster when they are hotter. In hot water, the water particles are moving fast and bumping hard and often into the sugar grains. They pull the sugar particles away more quickly. They also carry the sugar particles away from the grain more quickly, spreading them through the water and making room for more.

This is why we dissolve sugar in hot tea rather than cold, and why washing powder works better in warm water than in cold.

Mrs Mokoena warns that hot water can scald, so learners must not carry boiling water, and an adult must pour it. Glasses with hot water should stand in the middle of the desk.

The questions for this section are with you now: temperature and the rate of dissolving.

## Subtopic: Stirring and Shaking

The second race tests stirring. Group 2 uses two glasses of cold water, both at the same temperature, with the same amount. They add one level teaspoon of ordinary sugar to each. One glass is stirred steadily with a teaspoon. The other is left alone.

The stirred sugar disappears in about a minute. The unstirred sugar is still sitting in a pile at the bottom long after.

Stirring or shaking makes a solid dissolve faster.

Why? When a solid sits still at the bottom of a glass, the water right next to it soon fills up with dissolved sugar particles. That water becomes very sugary, so it pulls new sugar particles away more slowly. If you look closely at the unstirred glass, you can see wavy lines in the water just above the sugar pile. That is the very sugary water, which bends light differently from the plain water above it. Stirring moves this sugary water away and brings fresh water, with fewer sugar particles, into contact with the grains. It also spreads the grains out, so that water can reach all sides of them. Shaking a closed container, like a bottle of water with a sachet of powdered drink, does the same thing.

This is why a cooldrink powder in a bottle dissolves quickly when you shake the bottle, and why we stir our tea.

The questions for this section are with you now: stirring, shaking and the rate of dissolving.

## Subtopic: Big Lumps and Fine Grains

The third race tests grain size. Group 3 puts the same mass of sugar into three glasses of cold water at the same temperature: one sugar cube, a heaped spoon of ordinary sugar, and a heaped spoon of icing sugar. They stir each glass in the same way.

The icing sugar dissolves almost at once. The ordinary sugar takes longer. The sugar cube takes the longest of all.

The smaller the pieces of solute, the faster it dissolves.

Why? Dissolving happens only at the outside surface of each piece, where the water can touch it. When a sugar cube is broken into many small grains, there is much more surface touching the water, so many more sugar particles can be pulled away at the same time. Crushing the cube gives the water more places to work.

This is why instant coffee is sold as fine powder or granules, and why medicines often come as powders or tablets that are crushed before they are dissolved.

At the end, the groups put all three factors together. They crush a sugar cube into fine grains, tip it into hot water and stir. It dissolves in a few seconds, faster than any other glass in the whole competition. Each factor helps on its own, and together they help even more.

To be sure that each result is fair, Mrs Mokoena reminds the class that in each race, only one thing may be changed. In the temperature race, the amount of water, the kind and amount of sugar and the stirring stayed the same, and only the temperature changed. This is called a fair test. If two things are changed at once, nobody can tell which one made the difference.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking stirring makes more sugar dissolve in total; it makes it dissolve faster, but does not change the saturation limit by itself. Two: thinking a sugar cube and the same mass of icing sugar dissolve at the same rate; smaller pieces dissolve faster. Three: thinking hot water makes sugar melt; it makes it dissolve faster. Four: changing two things at once in a test; only one thing may change in a fair test.

The questions for this section are with you now: grain size, surface and fair tests.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the sugar race in Welkom — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Hotter Is Faster

Sugar dissolves faster in hot water than in cold water.

Hot water particles move faster.

Keep the cup of hot tea in mind and try the questions for this part: hotter is faster.

## Subtopic: Stir It Up

Stirring or shaking makes sugar dissolve faster.

It brings fresh water to the sugar.

Hold the spinning teaspoon in mind and try this part's questions: stir it up.

## Subtopic: Small Is Quick

Small grains dissolve faster than big lumps.

Pocket summary of the lesson. The rate of dissolving is how fast a solute dissolves. A solid dissolves faster when the solvent is hotter, because the particles move faster; when the mixture is stirred or shaken, because fresh solvent reaches the solute; and when the solute is in smaller pieces, because more surface touches the solvent. In a fair test, only one thing is changed at a time.

Hold the sugar cube and the icing sugar in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: small is quick.
