### subtopic_1 — Insoluble Pollution
**Assistant:** What can I do to stop rivers being polluted?

**Tutor:** There are many small things that add up when lots of people do them. Never drop litter, because rain washes it down storm water drains straight into rivers. Storm water drains are not connected to the sewage works, so whatever goes into them ends up in a river. Never pour paint, oil, petrol or chemicals down a drain or into the ground. Ask an adult to take them to a proper collection point. Use less plastic, by carrying a reusable shopping bag and a water bottle. Wash clothes, dishes and cars away from rivers, so that soap does not run into the water. Use toilets, and report leaking or overflowing sewage pipes to the municipality. At home and at school, use compost instead of too much fertiliser in the garden. Many communities in South Africa also have river clean-up groups and adopt-a-river projects, like the one on the Hennops River, which welcome school groups. Telling your family and friends why it matters helps too.

### subtopic_2 — Soluble Pollution
**Assistant:** How does a water scientist test water for germs if they are too small to see?

**Tutor:** Scientists use a clever trick. They do not look for each germ directly. Instead, they put a small amount of the water onto a special jelly, or into a special liquid, that has food in it for bacteria. Then they keep it warm for a day or so. Each single bacterium that was in the water feeds and divides again and again, until it forms a small spot, called a colony, that is big enough to see. By counting the spots, the scientist can tell roughly how many bacteria were in the water. They usually look for a kind of bacteria that lives in the gut of people and animals. If those bacteria are in the water, it means faeces have got into it, and so other dangerous germs may be there too. Simple test kits can also change colour if these bacteria are present. Municipalities test drinking water regularly in this way to make sure it is safe.

### subtopic_3 — Germs and Water-Borne Illnesses
**Assistant:** Why are plastic bags and bottles such a big problem in the sea?

**Tutor:** Most plastic does not rot like paper, wood or food does, because decomposers like bacteria and fungi cannot break it down easily. So a plastic bottle that reaches the sea can last for hundreds of years. Sunlight and waves slowly break it into smaller and smaller pieces, but it never really disappears. These tiny pieces, called microplastics, are now found in sea water, on beaches, and inside fish, sea birds and even the salt we make from sea water. Sea turtles often mistake floating plastic bags for jellyfish, which they eat, and the bags can block their stomachs. Sea birds feed bits of plastic to their chicks. Seals and dolphins get caught in plastic rope and nets. Most of the plastic in the sea comes from the land, washed down rivers from towns and cities. That is why stopping litter at a river like the Hennops, far from the coast, also helps to protect the sea.
