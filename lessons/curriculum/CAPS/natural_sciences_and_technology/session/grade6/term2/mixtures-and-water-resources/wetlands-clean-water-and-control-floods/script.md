# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers how wetlands help to clean water and control the flow of water. The anchor is a Grade 6 class on a trip to Wakkerstroom, a small town in the highlands of Mpumalanga, where the Wakkerstroom River spreads out into a wide wetland of reeds, sedges and wet grassland. Wooden walkways and bird hides run over the marsh. A guide, Mr Mavimbela, points out herons, ducks, crowned cranes and tiny reed frogs. He tells the class that the water flowing into the wetland from the farmland above is often muddy, but the water flowing out at the other end is much clearer. Then he hands each group a kitchen sponge and asks them to hold it under a running tap. The big idea is that wetlands remove substances from water, like a natural filter, and regulate the flow of water, like a giant sponge, which makes them some of the most valuable ecosystems we have. ## Subtopic: What Wetlands Are

A wetland is an area of land where the soil is soaked with water, or covered by shallow water, for all or part of the year. Marshes, vleis, swamps, reed beds, the edges of lakes, and the flat areas where rivers spread out are all wetlands.

Wetlands are found all over South Africa. Some are huge, like the iSimangaliso Wetland Park in KwaZulu-Natal and Nylsvley in Limpopo. Many are small, like a vlei on a farm or a soggy patch at the bottom of a hillside.

You can recognise a wetland by three things. First, the water: the ground is wet, even in parts of the dry season. Second, the soil: wetland soil is often dark grey, sticky and smelly, because it is waterlogged and has little air in it. Third, the plants: reeds, bulrushes, sedges and grasses that are adapted to growing with their roots in water.

Wetlands are home to many animals. Frogs and insects breed there. Water birds, like the grey crowned crane, nest in them. Fish shelter in them. Antelope such as reedbuck graze on their edges.

But wetlands also do two very important jobs for people: they clean water, and they control its flow.

## Subtopic: Nature's Water Filter

Back at school, the class builds a model wetland. They fill one end of a long tray with soil and cover it with a layer of grass sods and moss, and leave the other end bare. They tilt the tray slightly and pour a jug of muddy water mixed with a little soil and crushed leaves onto the high end.

On the bare side, the muddy water rushes straight down to the bottom of the tray, still brown. On the grassy side, the water spreads out and trickles slowly through the plants. The water that comes out at the bottom is much clearer.

A real wetland works in the same way, using several of the separating methods we have learnt.

Slowing and settling. When a river enters a wetland, it spreads out over a wide, flat area, and the dense plants slow the water down. Slow water cannot carry mud and sand, so they settle to the bottom.

Filtering. The thick mat of stems, roots and leaves acts like a filter, trapping bits of soil, litter and other insoluble substances.

Taking in dissolved substances. Wetland plants take in dissolved substances, such as fertiliser, through their roots and use them to grow. Bacteria in the wetland mud break down some harmful substances and germs from sewage. Some dissolved metals and poisons become stuck to the mud.

In this way, wetlands remove both insoluble and some soluble substances from water. Water that leaves a healthy wetland is cleaner than the water that went in. Some towns even build artificial wetlands to help clean water after it leaves the sewage works.

## Subtopic: Nature's Sponge

Now the class tries the sponge. When the sponge is held under the tap, it soaks up a lot of water. When they put it on a plate, water slowly drips out of it for a long time.

A wetland works like a giant sponge. In the rainy season, when heavy rain falls and rivers rise, the wetland soaks up and holds a lot of the extra water in its soil and plants. It also slows the water down. This means that less water rushes downstream at once, so floods are smaller and do less damage to bridges, roads, homes and farmland. The slow water also does less damage to riverbanks, so less soil is washed away.

In the dry season, the wetland slowly releases the water it stored. Water seeps out of the wetland into the river, so the river keeps flowing even when there has been no rain for months. Some water also soaks down into the ground, filling up underground water that people reach with boreholes.

This is called regulating water flow: evening out the flow, so there is less water in floods and more in droughts.

Sadly, many of South Africa's wetlands have been drained for farming, built on, or damaged by overgrazing, mining and pollution. When a wetland is destroyed, floods get worse, rivers dry up sooner, and the water downstream gets dirtier.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking a wetland is useless, smelly land; it cleans water and controls floods. Two: thinking wetlands can remove only floating litter; plants and bacteria also remove some dissolved substances and germs. Three: thinking a wetland stops all water reaching the river; it releases water slowly. Four: thinking draining a wetland gives us more water; it makes floods worse and rivers dry up.
