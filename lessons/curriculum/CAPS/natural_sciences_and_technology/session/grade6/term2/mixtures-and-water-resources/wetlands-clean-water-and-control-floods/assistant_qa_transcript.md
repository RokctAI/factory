### subtopic_1 — What Wetlands Are
**Assistant:** Can people build a wetland to clean dirty water?

**Tutor:** Yes. These are called constructed or artificial wetlands, and they copy what natural wetlands do. Engineers dig shallow ponds or channels, line them, fill them with gravel and soil, and plant reeds and other wetland plants. Dirty water, for example water that has already been partly cleaned at a sewage works, or water from a farm or a mine, is let in at one end and flows slowly through the plants. As it flows, the mud settles, the gravel and roots filter it, the plants take in dissolved nutrients, and bacteria living on the roots and gravel break down waste and kill many germs. The water that comes out at the other end is much cleaner. Constructed wetlands are cheaper to run than big machines, because they use no chemicals and very little electricity, and they also make homes for birds and frogs. They do not work as fast as a full treatment works, so they usually help other cleaning steps rather than replacing them.

### subtopic_2 — Nature's Water Filter
**Assistant:** Why do wetlands smell like rotten eggs sometimes?

**Tutor:** That smell is a natural sign that a wetland is working. Wetland soil is waterlogged, so very little air, and very little oxygen, can get into it. Dead plant material keeps falling into the water and sinking into the mud. Bacteria break it down, as decomposers always do. But because there is so little oxygen, some kinds of bacteria use other substances in the mud instead, and this makes a gas that smells like rotten eggs. When you step into the mud, the gas bubbles up and you smell it. This low-oxygen mud is also why wetland soil is dark grey instead of brown or red. Wetland plants have special air spaces in their stems to carry oxygen down to their roots, so they can grow in this mud. So although the smell is not pleasant, it does not mean the wetland is polluted. A really polluted wetland might smell of sewage or chemicals, have dead fish, or have very few plants and animals.

### subtopic_3 — Nature's Sponge
**Assistant:** Why is the grey crowned crane in trouble if it lives in wetlands?

**Tutor:** The grey crowned crane, with its golden crown of feathers, is one of the most beautiful birds in South Africa, and it depends on wetlands to breed. Pairs build large, flat nests from grasses and sedges in the middle of marshes, where the water helps to protect the eggs and chicks from predators like jackals. When wetlands are drained for crops, dammed, burnt at the wrong time, or trampled by too many cattle, the cranes lose their nesting places. They also sometimes fly into power lines, and their eggs and chicks have been taken illegally. As a result, their numbers in South Africa have dropped, and they are now an endangered species. Farmers, conservation groups and communities near places like Wakkerstroom work together to protect wetlands, mark power lines so birds can see them, and keep people and cattle away from nests in the breeding season. Protecting wetlands for the cranes also protects the clean water that people need.
