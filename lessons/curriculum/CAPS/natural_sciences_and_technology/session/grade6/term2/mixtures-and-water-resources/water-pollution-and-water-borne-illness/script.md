# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers how mixtures can pollute our water. The anchor is a Grade 6 class from Centurion, between Pretoria and Johannesburg, who join a community clean-up day on the Hennops River. After heavy summer rains, the river is brown and fast, and its banks are covered in litter caught in the reeds: plastic bags, cooldrink bottles, chip packets, polystyrene food boxes and even an old tyre. The learners wear thick gloves and boots and work with adults, filling bag after bag. A water scientist, Dr Nkosi, joins them with a test kit. She fills a small bottle with river water that looks fairly clear, and tests it. The results worry her: the water contains germs from sewage, and too much fertiliser. She tells the class that the litter is only the pollution we can see. The big idea is that water can be polluted by insoluble substances, by soluble substances and by germs, and that polluted water can make people ill. Water pollution and water-borne illnesses are examinable.

## Subtopic: Insoluble Pollution

Water pollution means adding harmful substances to water, so that it becomes dirty and unsafe for people, plants and animals. River water is always a mixture, but when people add harmful things to that mixture, the water is polluted.

Some pollution is made of insoluble substances. These do not dissolve in water, so we can see them. They float on top, are carried along by the current, or sink to the bottom.

Litter is the most common. Plastic bags, bottles and packets are blown or washed into rivers from streets and dumps. Plastic does not rot, so it can stay in rivers and the sea for hundreds of years, slowly breaking into tiny pieces. Fish, birds and turtles get tangled in it, or swallow it, thinking it is food.

Oil is another insoluble pollutant. When people pour used engine oil down drains, or when ships leak oil at sea, it floats in a thin layer on top of the water. It stops oxygen from getting into the water, and it coats the feathers of birds like penguins, so that they cannot keep warm or fly.

Soil and mud are washed into rivers when land is bare, after trees and grass have been removed. Too much mud makes the water cloudy, blocks sunlight from water plants, and covers the places where fish lay their eggs.

Insoluble pollution is the easiest kind to remove, by picking it out, sieving, settling or filtering. That is what the learners are doing by hand on the riverbank.

The questions for this section are with you now: insoluble pollutants like litter, plastic, oil and mud.

## Subtopic: Soluble Pollution

Dr Nkosi explains that some of the most harmful pollution is invisible, because it is dissolved in the water. Soluble pollutants form a solution with the river water, so the water can look clear and still be dangerous.

Fertilisers. Farmers and gardeners use fertilisers to help plants grow. When it rains, some fertiliser dissolves and washes into rivers and dams. There, it makes algae and water weeds grow far too fast. When the algae die and rot, they use up the oxygen in the water, and fish die. Hartbeespoort Dam, downstream of the Hennops River, is often covered in thick green algae for this reason.

Poisons and chemicals. Pesticides sprayed on crops can wash into rivers. Some factories have released harmful chemicals into rivers. Water that seeps out of old mines can be very acidic and full of dissolved metals, which kills water life.

Soaps and detergents. Washing clothes, dishes or cars in a river adds soap and washing powder to the water. Many washing powders contain substances that act like fertiliser and feed algae.

Salts. Water from some factories, mines and farms can be very salty, and too much salt harms freshwater plants and animals and makes water useless for drinking or watering crops.

Because these pollutants are dissolved, they cannot be removed by sieving, settling or filtering. They pass straight through. They must be stopped from reaching the water in the first place, or removed by special treatment.

The questions for this section are with you now: soluble pollutants like fertiliser, chemicals, detergents and salts.

## Subtopic: Germs and Water-Borne Illnesses

The third kind of pollution is germs. Germs are tiny living things, like bacteria and viruses, that are far too small to see and can cause disease.

Germs get into water mainly from human and animal faeces. This happens when sewage pipes leak or burst, when sewage works are broken or overloaded, when people do not have proper toilets, and when animals are kept near rivers. After rain, the faeces are washed into streams and rivers.

Illnesses that people catch by drinking or using dirty water are called water-borne illnesses. They include cholera, typhoid and many kinds of diarrhoea. Cholera causes very bad, watery diarrhoea and can kill a person within hours if they lose too much water and salts. Diarrhoea from dirty water is one of the main reasons young children become very ill in many parts of the world. People also catch these germs by eating food washed in dirty water, or by not washing their hands after using the toilet.

South Africa has had outbreaks of cholera in the past, when germs from sewage got into water that people drank. Clean water, working toilets and sewage works, and handwashing with soap prevent these diseases.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking clear water is always clean; dissolved chemicals and germs cannot be seen. Two: thinking litter is the only kind of water pollution; soluble chemicals and germs are often worse. Three: thinking filtering removes all pollution; dissolved substances and some germs pass through. Four: thinking washing in the river is harmless; soap and detergent pollute the water.

The questions for this section are with you now: germs, sewage and water-borne illnesses.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the clean-up on the Hennops River — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Pollution You Can See

Plastic, litter, oil and mud are insoluble pollution.

They float, sink or are carried along.

Keep the bags of plastic from the riverbank in mind and try the questions for this part: pollution you can see.

## Subtopic: Pollution You Can't See

Fertiliser, poisons, soap and salt can dissolve in water.

Clear water can still be polluted.

Hold Dr Nkosi's test bottle in mind and try this part's questions: pollution you can't see.

## Subtopic: Germs in the Water

Germs from sewage cause cholera, typhoid and diarrhoea.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Water pollution makes water unsafe for people, plants and animals. Insoluble pollutants, like plastic, litter, oil and mud, can be seen and removed. Soluble pollutants, like fertilisers, poisons, detergents and salts, dissolve, so the water may look clear but still be harmful, and they cannot be filtered out. Germs from sewage and faeces cause water-borne illnesses such as cholera, typhoid and diarrhoea. Toilets, sewage works, clean water and handwashing prevent them.

Hold the clear but dirty bottle of river water in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: germs in the water.
