# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the idea that all matter is made up of tiny particles, and how those particles are arranged and how they move in solids, liquids and gases. The anchor is a Grade 6 class in Pietermaritzburg in KwaZulu-Natal. On a cool morning, their teacher, Mrs Pillay, puts three things on her desk: an ice cube on a saucer, a glass of water, and a kettle that is starting to steam. She asks the class what the three things are made of. Some learners say water, ice and steam. Mrs Pillay says that all three are the same substance, water, in three different states, and that the only difference is how the tiny particles inside them are arranged and how they move. Then she takes the class out onto the school field to act it out. The big idea is that all matter is made of particles that are too small to see, and that the arrangement and movement of the particles explain why solids, liquids and gases behave so differently. The particle model of matter is examinable.

## Subtopic: All Matter Is Made of Particles

Matter is anything that has mass and takes up space. The desk, the water, the air in the room, your body and the steam from the kettle are all matter. Matter comes in three states: solid, liquid and gas.

Scientists have shown that all matter is made up of tiny particles. The particles are so small that we cannot see them, even with an ordinary microscope. A single drop of water contains more particles than there are grains of sand on all the beaches of the world.

How do we know particles are there if we cannot see them? We can see what they do. Mrs Pillay sprays a little perfume in the front corner of the classroom. After a short while, the learners at the back can smell it. The perfume particles have spread out between the particles of air, all the way across the room. She drops a little food colouring into a glass of still water. Without any stirring, the colour slowly spreads through all the water. The colouring particles are moving and mixing with the water particles.

There are also tiny spaces between particles. When she pours a cup of sugar into a cup of water and stirs, the mixture takes up less space than two full cups. The sugar particles have slipped into the spaces between the water particles.

The particles in matter are always moving. Even in a solid, they jiggle on the spot. And the hotter a substance is, the faster its particles move.

The questions for this section are with you now: matter, particles and the evidence that particles exist.

## Subtopic: Solids, Liquids and Gases

On the field, Mrs Pillay turns the class into particles.

First, she says, you are particles in a block of ice, a solid. The learners stand shoulder to shoulder in neat rows, so close that they are touching. They are not allowed to move from their places, but they may jiggle and sway on the spot. This is how particles behave in a solid. They are packed closely together in a regular pattern, held by strong forces of attraction, and they can only vibrate. That is why a solid has a fixed shape and a fixed volume. A brick stays a brick shape, and you cannot squash it into a smaller space.

Next, says Mrs Pillay, the Sun is warming you, and you are melting. You are now particles in a liquid. The learners stay close together, still touching, but now they can slide and squeeze past each other and move around the group. This is how particles behave in a liquid. They are still close together, but not in neat rows, and they can move past one another. The forces between them are weaker. That is why a liquid can flow and takes the shape of its container, but still keeps the same volume. A litre of water poured from a jug into a bowl is still a litre.

Last, she says, you are boiling: you are particles in a gas. The learners spread out across the whole field and run in straight lines in every direction, bumping into each other and bouncing off the fence. This is how particles behave in a gas. They are very far apart, with large spaces between them, and they move fast and freely in all directions. The forces between them are very weak. That is why a gas has no fixed shape and no fixed volume. It spreads out to fill any container, and it can be squashed into a smaller space, like air pumped into a bicycle tyre.

The questions for this section are with you now: how particles are arranged and move in solids, liquids and gases.

## Subtopic: Heating and Changing State

Back in the classroom, Mrs Pillay uses the particle model to explain changes of state.

When a solid is heated, its particles vibrate faster and faster. At the melting point, they vibrate so strongly that they break out of their rows and begin to slide past each other. The solid melts and becomes a liquid. The ice cube on the saucer is now a small puddle.

When a liquid is heated, its particles move faster. At the boiling point, they move so fast that they escape from the liquid and spread out as a gas. The water in the kettle boils and becomes water vapour, a gas. Water vapour itself is invisible. The white cloud we call steam is made of tiny droplets of liquid water, formed when the vapour cools in the air.

When a gas cools, its particles slow down, come closer together and become a liquid again. This is condensation. You can see it on the cold window near the kettle, which is covered in drops. When a liquid cools further, its particles slow down even more and settle into rows. This is freezing.

Notice that the particles themselves do not change. Water particles in ice, water and vapour are exactly the same. Only their arrangement, spacing and movement change.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking particles in a solid do not move at all; they vibrate on the spot. Two: thinking there is nothing between the particles of a gas except more gas; there is empty space. Three: thinking particles get bigger when a substance is heated; they move faster and further apart, but stay the same size. Four: thinking ice, water and steam are made of different particles; they are all water particles.

The questions for this section are with you now: melting, boiling, condensing and freezing in terms of particles.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the class on the field in Pietermaritzburg — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Tiny Particles

All matter is made of tiny particles.

The particles are always moving, and there are spaces between them.

Keep the spreading smell of perfume in mind and try the questions for this part: tiny particles.

## Subtopic: Packed, Sliding, Free

Solid: packed close in rows, only jiggling.

Liquid: close, but sliding past each other.

Gas: far apart, moving fast in all directions.

Hold the learners on the field in mind and try this part's questions: packed, sliding, free.

## Subtopic: Heat Makes Them Move

Heating makes particles move faster. Cooling slows them down.

Pocket summary of the lesson. All matter is made of tiny particles that are always moving, with spaces between them. In a solid, particles are packed closely in a regular pattern and vibrate on the spot, so a solid has a fixed shape and volume. In a liquid, particles are close but slide past each other, so a liquid flows and takes the shape of its container. In a gas, particles are far apart and move fast in all directions, so a gas spreads out to fill any space. Heating makes particles move faster and can melt or boil a substance.

Hold the ice, the water and the steaming kettle in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: heat makes them move.
