### subtopic_1 — All Matter Is Made of Particles
**Assistant:** If particles in a solid are fixed in place, how can I smell a solid like soap?

**Tutor:** Good thinking. In a solid, most particles are held in place, but they are always vibrating. At the surface of the solid, a few particles vibrate hard enough to break free and escape into the air. Once they are in the air, they move about like gas particles and some of them reach your nose, where special cells detect them, and you smell the soap. This happens much more slowly with solids than with liquids, because the particles in a solid are held more strongly. Some solids, like mothballs and some air fresheners, are made of substances whose particles escape quite easily, which is why they slowly get smaller and smaller over weeks until they disappear, without ever melting. This is called sublimation, when a solid turns straight into a gas. Metals and stones hold their particles so tightly that almost none escape at room temperature, which is why most of them have no smell at all.

### subtopic_2 — Solids, Liquids and Gases
**Assistant:** Why does a balloon get smaller when you put it in the fridge?

**Tutor:** The balloon is full of air, which is a gas. The gas particles inside are moving fast in all directions and keep hitting the inside of the rubber. Each hit is a tiny push, and millions of pushes every second hold the balloon out. When you put the balloon in the fridge, the air inside cools down. The particles slow down, so they hit the rubber less often and less hard. The stretchy rubber squeezes in, and the balloon gets a bit smaller. When you take it out and it warms up again, the particles speed up, push harder, and the balloon grows back to its old size. The number of particles inside never changed, and the particles did not shrink or grow. Only their speed changed. This is also why car and bicycle tyres can look a little flat on a very cold winter morning in the Highveld.

### subtopic_3 — Heating and Changing State
**Assistant:** Is fire a solid, a liquid or a gas?

**Tutor:** This is a question that even adults find tricky. A flame is not really a substance in one state. It is the place where burning is happening. When a fuel, like the wax of a candle, burns, the wax melts, then turns into a gas, and the gas reacts with oxygen from the air. This reaction gives out a lot of heat and light. The flame we see is made mostly of very hot gases glowing, together with tiny glowing specks of soot that make the yellow colour. So the closest answer is that a flame is mostly hot, glowing gas. Notice how a flame has no fixed shape and moves upward, because hot gases are lighter than the cooler air around them and rise. The smoke above a candle is mostly tiny solid soot particles and droplets being carried by those hot gases. So a flame is a mixture of a process and hot gases, rather than a single state of matter.
