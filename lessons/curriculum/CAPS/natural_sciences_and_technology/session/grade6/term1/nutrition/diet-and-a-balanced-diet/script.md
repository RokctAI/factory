# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers what a diet is and what makes a diet balanced. The anchor is a Grade 6 class in Polokwane in Limpopo. For one week, every learner keeps a food diary, a small notebook in which they write down everything they eat and drink, from breakfast until bedtime. On Friday the class sticks all the diaries onto a big chart on the wall. Lerato ate soft porridge with milk for breakfast every day. Johan had toast and tea. Tumi had nothing before school and bought chips and a cooldrink at break. For supper, most learners had pap or rice with meat, beans or morogo, but a few had only bread. The teacher, Mrs Ramaphosa, hands out paper plates, and each learner draws their best supper of the week on a plate. The big idea is that a diet is simply the foods we usually eat, and that a balanced diet contains all four nutrient groups, plus water and fibre, in the right amounts. What a diet is and what makes a diet balanced are examinable.

## Subtopic: What a Diet Is

Many people use the word diet to mean eating less to lose weight. In science, the word has a simpler meaning. Your diet is the selection of foods and drinks that you eat every day. Everybody has a diet, whether it is healthy or not.

The food diaries show that people's diets are very different. Diets depend on many things.

Culture and family. Families eat the foods they grew up with. In Limpopo, many families eat pap with morogo, mopane worms or atchar. In the Western Cape, many families eat bredie or snoek. In KwaZulu-Natal, many families eat curry with rice or roti.

Religion and beliefs. Some people do not eat certain foods because of their religion. Muslim and Jewish people do not eat pork. Many Hindu people do not eat beef. Vegetarians choose not to eat meat at all.

Money. Meat, fish and fresh fruit can cost a lot. Families with little money often eat more maize meal, bread and beans, because these are cheaper and filling.

Where we live. People near the sea may eat more fish. People on farms may grow their own vegetables and keep chickens.

Likes and dislikes. Some people do not like the taste of certain foods. Some people are allergic to foods like peanuts or milk.

The questions for this section are with you now: what a diet is and what affects the foods people eat.

## Subtopic: The Balanced Diet

A balanced diet is a diet that gives the body all the nutrients it needs, in the right amounts. It contains foods from all four nutrient groups: carbohydrates, proteins, fats and oils, and vitamins and minerals. It also contains water and fibre.

Mrs Ramaphosa draws a healthy plate on the board to show the right amounts.

About half of the plate should be vegetables and fruit, for vitamins, minerals and fibre: cabbage, spinach, morogo, carrots, butternut, tomatoes, and fruit for pudding.

About a quarter of the plate should be starchy food, for energy: pap, rice, samp, potatoes or bread.

About a quarter of the plate should be protein food, for growth and repair: beans, lentils, eggs, chicken, fish, meat or soya.

A small amount of fat or oil is used in cooking, and a glass of milk or maas adds protein and calcium.

The class looks again at the diaries. Lerato's breakfast of porridge and milk gave her energy, protein and calcium. Tumi's chips and cooldrink gave her energy from fat and sugar, but almost no protein, vitamins, minerals or fibre. A meal of only bread is mostly carbohydrate. These meals are not balanced.

A balanced diet does not mean that every single meal must be perfect. It means that, over the whole day and the whole week, we eat a good mix of foods. Eating a variety of different foods, especially vegetables and fruit of many colours, is the best way to get all the nutrients we need.

The questions for this section are with you now: the four nutrient groups on a balanced plate.

## Subtopic: Water and Fibre

Two more things are needed in a balanced diet, even though they are not in the four nutrient groups: water and fibre.

Water. About two thirds of your body is water. Water carries nutrients around the body in the blood, helps to get rid of waste in urine, and keeps the body cool through sweat. We lose water all the time when we breathe, sweat and go to the toilet, so we must drink water every day. A child should drink about six to eight glasses of liquid each day, and more on a hot day in Polokwane or after sport. Clean water is the best drink. If you do not drink enough, you can get a headache, feel tired and find it hard to concentrate.

Fibre. Fibre, also called roughage, is the tough part of plant foods that our bodies cannot digest. It is found in vegetables, fruit with the skin on, beans, lentils, brown bread, whole-wheat flour and unsifted maize meal. Fibre gives the food in our gut bulk, so that it can be pushed along easily, and it helps to prevent constipation, which is when it is hard to go to the toilet. Fibre also helps us feel full. Meat, eggs, milk, sugar and oil contain no fibre at all.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking a diet means eating less to lose weight; everyone's daily food is their diet. Two: thinking a balanced diet means equal amounts of every food; it means the right amounts, with lots of vegetables and fruit. Three: thinking cooldrink is as good as water; it adds a lot of sugar. Four: thinking fibre is not needed because we cannot digest it; it keeps the gut working well.

The questions for this section are with you now: why we need water and fibre.
