# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the relationships between living things and non-living things in an ecosystem. The anchor is a Grade 6 class from Howick in the KwaZulu-Natal Midlands, spending a morning at a farm dam owned by the family of a learner called Sipho. Next to the dam is a small vlei, a marshy area full of reeds and sedges. It is a sunny morning after rain. The learners carry clipboards, a thermometer, a spade, jars and magnifying glasses. They find tadpoles and frogs at the edge of the water, dragonflies over the reeds, water beetles, snails, small fish, a grey heron standing very still, and a herd of cows coming down to drink. Their teacher, Mr Zulu, gives them one job: draw a line between any two things that depend on each other. Soon their pages are covered in lines. The big idea is that in an ecosystem, living things depend on the non-living things around them, and on each other, so that a change to one part affects the others. These relationships are examinable.

## Subtopic: The Non-Living Parts

Every living thing in an ecosystem depends on the non-living things around it. The main ones are sunlight, air, water and soil.

Sunlight. Sunlight gives energy to green plants, so that they can make food by photosynthesis. The reeds, the grass and the tiny green algae in the dam all depend on it. Sunlight also warms the land and water. Frogs and lizards bask in the sun to warm their bodies, because they cannot make their own body heat.

Air. Air contains oxygen, which animals and plants use to release energy from food, and carbon dioxide, which plants use to make food. Fish and tadpoles take oxygen from the water, where some air is dissolved.

Water. All living things need water. Plants take it in through their roots. Animals drink it, like the cows at the dam. Some animals live in water for all of their lives, like fish, or for part of their lives, like frogs, whose tadpoles hatch and grow in water.

Soil. Soil holds plants up and gives them water and minerals. Many animals live in the soil, like earthworms, beetle larvae and moles. When the learners dig in the mud at the edge of the vlei, they find worms, small snails and the roots of reeds tangled together.

Temperature also matters. The learners measure the water: it is cooler in the deep shade of the reeds and warmer in the shallow, sunny water where the tadpoles gather.

The questions for this section are with you now: how living things depend on sunlight, air, water and soil.

## Subtopic: Living Things Depend on Each Other

Living things also depend on one another in many ways.

For food. The cows eat grass. The tadpoles eat algae. The dragonflies catch mosquitoes and other small insects. The heron catches frogs and fish.

For shelter and homes. Weaver birds build their nests hanging from the reeds over the water, which keeps them safe from snakes. Frogs hide among the sedges. Insects shelter under leaves and bark.

For pollination and spreading seeds. Bees and other insects visit flowers to drink nectar, and carry pollen from flower to flower, so that the plants can make seeds. Birds eat fruits and drop the seeds far away in their droppings, where new plants may grow.

Living things also change the non-living parts of their ecosystem. Plants give off oxygen into the air and water. Tree roots and reed roots hold the soil together, so that rain does not wash it away. Earthworms dig tunnels that let air and water into the soil, and they mix dead leaves into it. When plants and animals die, they rot and add nutrients to the soil. The cows' dung also adds nutrients, and dung beetles roll it away and bury it.

So the lines on the learners' pages go in all directions: living to non-living, non-living to living, and living to living.

The questions for this section are with you now: food, shelter, pollination, and how living things change their surroundings.

## Subtopic: One Change Affects the Whole Ecosystem

Because everything is linked, a change to one part of an ecosystem affects the other parts.

Mr Zulu asks the class to imagine some changes.

A drought. If no rain falls for months, the dam shrinks and the vlei dries out. The reeds and sedges die back. Tadpoles cannot grow without water, so there are fewer frogs, and the heron has less to eat and must fly away. The cows have to be moved or given water from a borehole.

Pollution. If fertiliser from a nearby field washes into the dam after heavy rain, the algae grow so fast that the water turns green and thick. When the algae die and rot, they use up the oxygen in the water, and the fish suffocate. If litter and plastic wash in, animals can get tangled in it or swallow it.

Too many animals. If too many cows graze around the dam, they trample the reeds and eat the grass down to the soil. The bare soil washes into the water when it rains, making it muddy, and the weaver birds lose their nesting places.

Removing an animal. If people killed all the frogs, the mosquitoes they ate would increase, and the heron would lose some of its food.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking only living things matter in an ecosystem; sunlight, air, water and soil are just as important. Two: thinking animals only depend on plants for food; they also need them for shelter and oxygen. Three: thinking a change in one part affects only that part; everything is linked. Four: thinking living things cannot change their surroundings; roots, worms and rotting plants change the soil.

The questions for this section are with you now: how drought, pollution and other changes affect an ecosystem.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the farm dam near Howick — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Sun, Air, Water and Soil

Living things need sunlight, air, water and soil.

Plants use sunlight to make food. Animals drink water and breathe air.

Keep the sunny dam in mind and try the questions for this part: sun, air, water and soil.

## Subtopic: Helping and Eating

Living things depend on each other for food, shelter and pollination.

Roots and worms change the soil.

Hold the weaver nests on the reeds in mind and try this part's questions: helping and eating.

## Subtopic: One Change, Many Effects

A drought or pollution changes the whole ecosystem.

Pocket summary of the lesson. In an ecosystem, living things depend on non-living things: sunlight for energy and warmth, air for oxygen and carbon dioxide, water for life, and soil for support, minerals and homes. Living things also depend on each other for food, shelter, pollination and spreading seeds, and they change their surroundings, for example by adding oxygen and making soil. Because everything is linked, a change like a drought or pollution affects the whole ecosystem.

Hold the drying vlei in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: one change, many effects.
