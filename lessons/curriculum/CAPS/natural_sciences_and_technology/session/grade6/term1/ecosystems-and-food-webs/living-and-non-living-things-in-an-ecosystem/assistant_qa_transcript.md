### subtopic_1 — The Non-Living Parts
**Assistant:** Why do frogs need to bask in the sun but birds do not?

**Tutor:** It is about how their bodies get heat. Birds and mammals, like us, make their own body heat from the food they eat. Our bodies stay at about the same warm temperature whether it is hot or cold outside, as long as we have enough food and can shiver, sweat or put on clothes. Frogs, lizards, snakes, fish and insects cannot make much body heat of their own. Their body temperature goes up and down with the temperature of their surroundings. When they are cold, they become slow and sleepy, and they cannot hunt or escape well. So on a cool morning, a lizard lies on a sunny rock to warm up, and a frog sits in the warm, shallow water. When they get too hot, they move into the shade or into the water. In winter, many of them hide away and become inactive until it warms up again. This is one more example of how living things depend on a non-living thing, the temperature.

### subtopic_2 — Living Things Depend on Each Other
**Assistant:** If algae are plants that give off oxygen, how can too many algae kill the fish?

**Tutor:** This is a very good question, because it seems backwards. While the algae are alive and in sunlight, they do give off oxygen. But when there is too much fertiliser in the water, the algae grow so thickly that they form a green mat on the surface. This blocks the sunlight from the plants deeper down, so those plants die. At night, the algae themselves use oxygen and give off none. Then, as the huge mass of algae dies, bacteria feed on the dead algae to break them down. These bacteria use up enormous amounts of the oxygen in the water. The oxygen level drops so low that fish, frogs and insects cannot breathe, and they die. Scientists see this in many dams and rivers in South Africa where fertiliser or sewage gets into the water. It shows that adding too much of something, even a nutrient, can upset the balance of a whole ecosystem.

### subtopic_3 — One Change Affects the Whole Ecosystem
**Assistant:** Can people help an ecosystem that has been damaged?

**Tutor:** Yes, and in South Africa many people work on this. One way is to remove the cause of the damage, for example by stopping sewage and litter from flowing into a river, or by keeping cattle away from a stream bank so that the plants can grow back. Another way is to remove alien plants, which are plants brought from other countries that spread quickly and push out the indigenous plants. Some alien trees, like certain wattles and gum trees, use a lot of water, so clearing them helps streams to flow again. People also replant indigenous plants, build small walls across dongas to stop soil washing away, and rebuild damaged wetlands. Ecosystems can often recover, but it takes time, sometimes many years. It is much easier to protect an ecosystem than to fix it. Even a school can help, by picking up litter, saving water and planting indigenous trees and flowers that feed local insects and birds.
