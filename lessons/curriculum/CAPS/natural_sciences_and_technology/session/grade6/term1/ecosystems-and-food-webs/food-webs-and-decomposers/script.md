# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers food webs, made of producers, consumers and decomposers, and how decomposers return nutrients to the soil. The anchor is a Grade 6 class from Rustenburg on a game drive in the Pilanesberg National Park in North West, a park inside the ring of an ancient volcano. The learners sit in an open vehicle with their guide, Mr Molefe, and a notebook each. In the first hour they see impala and zebra grazing on the grassland, a giraffe browsing on the leaves of an acacia tree, a troop of baboons eating fruit and digging up roots and insects, a cheetah resting under a tree after a meal, a jackal trotting along the road, and vultures circling over something far away. Then the vehicle stops for a dung beetle rolling a ball of elephant dung across the road. Mr Molefe asks the class to work out who eats whom, and what happens to everything at the end. The big idea is that energy passes from producers to consumers through many linked food chains, called a food web, and that decomposers break down dead things and return nutrients to the soil. Food webs, producers, consumers and decomposers are examinable.

## Subtopic: Producers and Consumers

Every food chain starts with a producer. A producer is a living thing that makes its own food. Green plants are producers, because they make food by photosynthesis, using energy from sunlight. In the Pilanesberg, the producers include grass, acacia trees, marula trees, shrubs and wild flowers.

A consumer is a living thing that cannot make its own food, so it must eat, or consume, other living things. All animals are consumers. We sort consumers by what they eat.

Herbivores eat only plants. The zebra and impala graze on grass, and the giraffe browses on leaves. Herbivores are often called primary consumers, because they are the first consumers in a food chain.

Carnivores eat only other animals. The cheetah hunts impala. Lions, leopards, wild dogs and eagles are carnivores too.

Omnivores eat both plants and animals. The baboon eats fruit, seeds and roots, but also insects, eggs and sometimes small animals. The jackal eats small mammals, birds and insects, but also fruit. People are omnivores too.

A food chain shows how energy passes from one living thing to the next when one is eaten by another. For example: grass, then impala, then cheetah. The arrows in a food chain always point from the food to the eater, in the direction the energy goes. So we write: grass, arrow, impala, arrow, cheetah. The arrow means is eaten by.

The questions for this section are with you now: producers, herbivores, carnivores, omnivores and food chains.

## Subtopic: Food Chains Join into Food Webs

In a real ecosystem, animals do not eat only one kind of food, and they are eaten by more than one kind of animal. The impala is eaten by cheetahs, but also by lions, leopards and wild dogs. The baboon eats fruit, grasshoppers and eggs. So the food chains in an ecosystem cross over and join up.

A food web is a set of many food chains in an ecosystem that are linked together. On the way back to the camp, the learners draw a food web on a big sheet of paper. At the bottom they draw the producers: grass, acacia and marula trees. Above them they draw the herbivores: zebra, impala, giraffe and grasshoppers. Then the omnivores and carnivores: baboons, jackals, cheetahs, lions and eagles. They draw arrows from every food to every animal that eats it. Soon the sheet is criss-crossed with arrows, like a spider's web.

A food web shows that living things depend on each other in many ways. It also shows what can happen if one living thing is removed. If disease killed most of the impala, the cheetahs and wild dogs would have less food, and they might eat more of other animals instead, like warthogs or young zebras. With fewer impala grazing, some plants might grow taller. One change spreads through the whole web.

A food web with many different kinds of living things is usually more stable, because if one food runs short, the consumers can eat something else.

The questions for this section are with you now: food webs and what happens when part of a web is removed.

## Subtopic: Decomposers Return Nutrients

What happens to the grass that the zebra did not eat, the leaves that fall from the trees, the bones of the impala, and the dung of the elephants? Mr Molefe says that nothing in nature is wasted.

Scavengers are animals that eat the bodies of animals that have already died. The vultures circling in the sky had spotted the remains of an animal killed by lions. Vultures, hyenas and jackals clean up the remains of dead animals.

Then the decomposers take over. Decomposers are living things that break down dead plants, dead animals and animal waste, like dung. The most important decomposers are fungi, like mushrooms and moulds, and bacteria, which are too small to see. As they feed, they break the dead material into simpler substances, which become part of the soil. These substances are nutrients, which plants take in through their roots to grow. So decomposers return nutrients to the soil, and the producers use them again.

Other small animals help. Dung beetles roll dung away and bury it, which adds it to the soil. Termites eat dead wood and grass. Earthworms pull dead leaves into the soil. They break dead material into smaller pieces, so the fungi and bacteria can work faster.

Without decomposers, the world would be piled high with dead leaves, dead animals and dung, and the soil would run out of nutrients, so plants could not grow.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking the arrow in a food chain points from the eater to the food; it points from the food to the eater, the way the energy goes. Two: thinking an omnivore eats everything; it eats both plants and animals. Three: thinking vultures are decomposers; they are scavengers, and fungi and bacteria are the decomposers. Four: thinking decomposers are harmful and useless; they return nutrients to the soil for plants.

The questions for this section are with you now: scavengers, decomposers and returning nutrients to the soil.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the game drive in the Pilanesberg — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Makers and Eaters

Plants are producers. They make food.

Animals are consumers. Herbivores eat plants, carnivores eat animals, and omnivores eat both.

Keep the zebra and the cheetah in mind and try the questions for this part: makers and eaters.

## Subtopic: Chains Join into Webs

Many food chains joined together make a food web.

The arrow points from the food to the eater.

Hold the criss-crossed poster in mind and try this part's questions: chains join into webs.

## Subtopic: Nature's Recyclers

Fungi and bacteria break down dead things and return nutrients to the soil.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Producers, like grass and trees, make their own food. Consumers eat other living things: herbivores eat plants, carnivores eat animals, and omnivores eat both. A food chain shows who eats whom, with arrows from the food to the eater. Many linked food chains make a food web. Decomposers, like fungi and bacteria, break down dead plants, dead animals and dung, and return nutrients to the soil so that producers can grow again.

Hold the dung beetle and its ball of dung in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: nature's recyclers.
