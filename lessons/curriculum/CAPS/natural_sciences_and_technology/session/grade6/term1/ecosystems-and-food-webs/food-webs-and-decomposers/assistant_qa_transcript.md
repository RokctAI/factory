### subtopic_1 — Producers and Consumers
**Assistant:** Why are there so many more zebras and impala than cheetahs and lions?

**Tutor:** It is because of how energy passes along a food chain. When an impala eats grass, it uses most of the energy from the grass to live: to walk, run, breathe, keep warm and escape from predators. Only a small part of that energy ends up stored in its body as new muscle and fat. So when a cheetah eats the impala, it gets only that small part. The cheetah, too, uses most of its energy to live and hunt. At each step along a food chain, a lot of energy is used up and lost as heat. This means there is much less energy available at the top of a food chain than at the bottom. A grassland can feed thousands of grazing animals, but only a few big carnivores. That is why there is far more grass than impala, far more impala than cheetahs, and why big hunters like lions and cheetahs need large areas to find enough food.

### subtopic_2 — Food Chains Join into Food Webs
**Assistant:** Are all bacteria and fungi good?

**Tutor:** No, but most of them are harmless or helpful. The bacteria and fungi that decompose dead things are some of the most useful living things on Earth, because they keep the soil fertile. Other helpful bacteria live in our gut and help us digest food. Bacteria are used to turn milk into maas and yoghurt, and a fungus called yeast makes bread rise and is used to make umqombothi and other drinks. But some bacteria and fungi cause disease. Some bacteria cause illnesses like tuberculosis or cholera, and some fungi cause ringworm and athlete's foot. Moulds that grow on old bread and fruit can make us ill if we eat them, because they are busy decomposing the food. This is why we keep food covered and cool, throw away mouldy food, and wash our hands. So bacteria and fungi are like people: most of them are useful, and a few can be harmful.

### subtopic_3 — Decomposers Return Nutrients
**Assistant:** Why do dung beetles roll dung into balls?

**Tutor:** Dung is full of nutrients and water that the dung beetle can use. Many kinds of dung beetle roll a piece of fresh dung into a neat ball and push it away from the dung pile, so that other beetles cannot steal it. They roll it with their back legs, walking backwards with their heads down. Scientists in South Africa discovered that some dung beetles use the Sun, the Moon and even the light of the Milky Way at night to keep rolling in a straight line. When the beetle finds soft soil, it buries the ball. Sometimes it eats the dung, and sometimes a female lays an egg in it, so that when the young beetle hatches, it has food all around it. By burying so much dung, dung beetles add nutrients to the soil, help water get into the ground, and remove the places where flies would breed. That is why dung beetles are so important in places like the Pilanesberg and on farms.
