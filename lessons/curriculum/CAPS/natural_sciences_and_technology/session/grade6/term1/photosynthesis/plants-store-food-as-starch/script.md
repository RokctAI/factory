# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers what plants do with the glucose they make, and how they change some of it into starch and store it in different parts of the plant. The anchor is a Grade 6 class from Durban on a walk through Warwick Junction, the huge market near the city centre. Traders sit behind piles of potatoes, sweet potatoes, madumbe, carrots, green mealies, dried beans, bananas, pumpkins and bunches of spinach. The teacher, Mr Naidoo, gives each group a shopping list and a few rand, and they buy one of everything they can afford. Back at school, they will cut the food open and test it. The big idea is that a plant makes more glucose than it needs right away, so it changes some of it into starch and stores it for later. People and animals eat these food stores. How plants store food, where they store it, and how to test for starch are examinable.

## Subtopic: Glucose Becomes Starch

In the last lesson, we learnt that a green plant makes glucose by photosynthesis. Glucose is a sugar. The plant uses some glucose straight away, for energy to stay alive and to grow.

But on a long, sunny day, a plant makes more glucose than it can use. It needs a way to keep the extra food for later. Glucose dissolves easily in water, so it is not a good way to store food. Instead, the plant joins many glucose pieces together to make a new substance called starch.

Starch does not dissolve in water, so it stays where it is put, packed away inside the plant. It is a bit like taking loose coins and changing them into notes that are easy to store in a safe place. When the plant needs energy again, at night, in winter or when it starts to grow in spring, it breaks the starch back down into glucose.

Starch is packed into tiny grains inside the cells of the plant. If you grate a raw potato, squeeze out the milky juice into a saucer and leave it to stand, a fine white powder settles at the bottom. That powder is potato starch.

So starch is the plant's food store. Like glucose, it is a carbohydrate, a food that gives energy.

The questions for this section are with you now: why plants make starch and how it is used.

## Subtopic: Food Stores All Over the Plant

Plants store starch in many different parts. The market shows them all.

Leaves. During the day, leaves store some starch for a short time, and use it up or send it to other parts at night. Spinach and cabbage are leaves we eat.

Stems. A potato is not a root. It is a swollen underground stem, called a tuber, packed with starch. You can tell it is a stem because it has eyes, which are buds that grow new shoots. Madumbe are also swollen underground stems full of starch. Sugar cane, grown on the hills around Durban, stores food in its tall stem as sugar instead of starch.

Roots. Sweet potatoes and cassava are swollen roots full of starch. Carrots and beetroot are roots too, and they store food mostly as sugar, which is why they taste sweet.

Flowers. When we eat cauliflower and broccoli, we are eating flower buds that are full of food.

Fruits. Bananas, mangoes and pumpkins are fruits. A green banana is full of starch. As it ripens and turns yellow, much of the starch changes back into sugar, which is why a ripe banana tastes sweet.

Seeds. Maize, wheat, rice, sorghum and beans are seeds. Each seed has a store of food, mostly starch, to feed the tiny new plant inside it until it grows leaves of its own.

Why do plants store food? To survive the night, to survive winter or a dry season, to grow again in spring, and to give their seeds a start in life. When we eat these stores, we take that food for ourselves. That is why so many of our staple foods, like pap, bread, rice and potatoes, are full of starch.

The questions for this section are with you now: which plant parts store food and why.

## Subtopic: Testing for Starch

Back in the classroom, Mr Naidoo shows the class how to find out if a food contains starch. He uses iodine solution, a brown liquid.

When iodine solution is dropped onto a food that has no starch, it stays brown or yellow-brown. When it is dropped onto a food that contains starch, it turns a dark blue-black colour.

The groups put a drop on a slice of potato: blue-black. On mealie meal: blue-black. On bread and on a cut green banana: blue-black. On a slice of apple and on a drop of cooking oil: it stays brown.

Then they try a spoon of white sugar dissolved in water. It also stays brown. Sugar is a carbohydrate, but it is not starch, so the iodine test does not find it. Iodine is a test for starch only.

Scientists can also test a leaf for starch, to show that it has been making food. The leaf has to be boiled to soften it and remove the green colour first, which uses hot alcohol, so this is only done by the teacher. A leaf kept in sunlight turns blue-black with iodine. A leaf from a plant kept in the dark for two days does not, because without light it could not make glucose to turn into starch.

Iodine solution can stain clothes and skin and must not be swallowed, so we use only a drop at a time and wash our hands afterwards.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking a potato is a root; it is a swollen underground stem. Two: thinking plants store food as glucose; most food is stored as starch, which does not dissolve. Three: thinking iodine turns red with starch; it turns blue-black. Four: thinking only roots store food; leaves, stems, roots, flowers, fruits and seeds all can.

The questions for this section are with you now: the iodine test and the starch in a leaf.
