### subtopic_1 — One Food, Many Nutrients
**Assistant:** Why does fat make brown paper see-through?

**Tutor:** Brown paper is made of tiny fibres from wood, pressed together with lots of tiny air gaps between them. When light hits the paper, it bounces around in all directions off the fibres and the air gaps, so very little light gets through. That is why the paper looks solid brown. When oil soaks into the paper, it fills up those tiny air gaps. Oil lets light pass through it more like the fibres do, so the light is no longer scattered as much, and much more of it can travel straight through. The spot looks lighter and see-through when you hold it up to a window. Water does the same thing for a while, but water evaporates into the air, and the air gaps come back. Oil does not evaporate at room temperature, so the greasy spot stays. That is why we let the paper dry before we decide if a food has fat in it.

### subtopic_2 — Grouping by the Main Nutrient
**Assistant:** Is there any food that has only one nutrient?

**Tutor:** Very few natural foods are like that, but some foods made by people come close. Table sugar, made from sugar cane, is almost pure sugar, so it is almost all carbohydrate, with no protein, fat, vitamins or minerals. Cooking oil is almost pure fat. Salt is almost pure mineral. These foods have been processed to take out everything else. That is one reason why nutrition experts call sugar empty energy: it gives energy, but nothing else that the body needs. Natural foods, like fruit, vegetables, grains, beans, eggs, milk and meat, almost always carry a mix of nutrients. They also carry water and, in the case of plant foods, fibre. So when you choose natural foods, you get more of what your body needs from every mouthful. This does not mean that sugar, oil and salt are banned. It means we should use only small amounts of them and get most of our food from natural foods.

### subtopic_3 — Testing Foods for Nutrients
**Assistant:** How do the people who make food labels know what is in the food?

**Tutor:** Food companies send samples of their food to a laboratory, where scientists test it. The scientists use special tests that are much more exact than our iodine and brown paper tests. They can measure how many grams of protein, fat, carbohydrate, sugar, fibre and salt are in every one hundred grams of the food, and how much energy it gives. The energy is shown in kilojoules. In South Africa, there are rules about food labels, so that companies must tell the truth about what is in their food, and they are not allowed to make false health promises. The label also lists the ingredients, starting with the one there is the most of. This helps people who are allergic to some foods, like peanuts or milk, and people who must watch how much sugar or salt they eat. Learning to read labels is a very useful skill for the rest of your life.
