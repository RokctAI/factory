### subtopic_1 — What Processed Food Is
**Assistant:** How do salt and sugar stop food from going bad?

**Tutor:** Food goes bad mainly because tiny living things, like bacteria and moulds, grow in it and feed on it. These germs need water to live and grow. When you pack a lot of salt or sugar around food, the salt or sugar draws water out of the germs, a bit like how salt on a cucumber slice makes water come out of it. The germs dry out and cannot grow, so the food keeps for much longer. That is why biltong, which is rubbed with salt and dried, can keep for weeks, and why jam, which is packed with sugar, can sit in a cupboard for months before it is opened. Vinegar works in another way: it is sour, or acidic, and most germs cannot grow in sour conditions. That is how atchar and pickled onions keep. Drying, smoking and freezing also help, because germs cannot grow well without water or in the cold.

### subtopic_2 — What Is Added and Why
**Assistant:** If maize meal is processed, why is it called a healthy food?

**Tutor:** Processing is not good or bad by itself. What matters is what is done to the food. When maize is dried and ground into meal, it keeps its starch, and some of its protein and fibre, and nothing unhealthy is added. In South Africa, there is also a law that says white maize meal and bread flour must be fortified. This means that vitamins and minerals, such as vitamin A, several B vitamins, iron and zinc, are added during processing. Many families eat pap every day, so fortifying maize meal is a clever way to give millions of people extra vitamins and minerals that they might not get enough of. This helps prevent some diseases. A packet of chips is different. It has had a lot of salt and fat added, and it is easy to eat a large amount of it without feeling full. So we look at what has been added or taken away, not only at whether a food is processed.

### subtopic_3 — Choosing Wisely
**Assistant:** Are colourings and flavourings dangerous?

**Tutor:** In South Africa, as in most countries, there are rules about which colourings and flavourings may be used in food, and how much. They are tested before they are allowed, so in small amounts they are considered safe for most people. But a few people are allergic or sensitive to certain colourings or preservatives. They may get a rash, a runny nose or trouble breathing, and some parents notice that their children become very restless after eating a lot of brightly coloured sweets and drinks. These people need to read labels carefully and avoid those ingredients. The bigger problem is usually not the colouring itself, but the food it is in. Bright colours and strong flavours are often used to make sweets, chips and cooldrinks look and taste exciting, and those foods are high in sugar, salt or fat. So when you see a very brightly coloured snack, it is a good clue to check the label and to eat it only as a small treat.
