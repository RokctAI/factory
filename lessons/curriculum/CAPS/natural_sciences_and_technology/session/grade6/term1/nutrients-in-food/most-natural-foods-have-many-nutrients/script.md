# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the idea that most natural foods contain more than one group of nutrients. The anchor is a Grade 6 classroom in Mthatha in the Eastern Cape, on the day the class holds a Food Detective Day. Each group has brought a natural food from home and placed it on their desk: a boiled egg, a glass of milk, a handful of raw peanuts, a cup of dried sugar beans, a ripe avocado, a mealie and an orange. The teacher, Mr Dlamini, has also brought empty food packets from the shop, each with a food label on the back. He tells the detectives that each food is hiding more than one nutrient, and their job is to find them. The big idea is that most natural foods contain a mix of nutrients, but usually one nutrient group is the biggest, and that is the group we place the food in. Knowing that natural foods contain more than one nutrient, and giving examples, is examinable.

## Subtopic: One Food, Many Nutrients

In the last lesson, we sorted foods into groups: carbohydrates for energy, proteins for growth and repair, fats and oils for stored energy and warmth, and vitamins and minerals for health. That made it sound as if each food holds only one nutrient. In fact, most natural foods hold several.

Take the egg. An egg is meant to feed a growing chick, so it contains everything the chick needs. It has protein in the egg white and the yolk, fat in the yolk, and vitamins and minerals such as vitamin A, vitamin D and iron.

Take the glass of milk. Milk is meant to feed a young calf. It contains protein, fat, a natural sugar, which is a carbohydrate, and the mineral calcium, as well as several vitamins. It also contains a lot of water.

Take the dried beans. Beans are seeds, packed with a food store for the young plant. They are rich in starch, which is a carbohydrate, and in protein. They also contain fibre, iron and other minerals.

Take the peanuts. Peanuts are seeds too. They contain a lot of oil, plenty of protein, and some carbohydrate, vitamins and minerals.

Take the orange. It is mostly water, with natural sugar, vitamin C and fibre. Even a fruit is a mix.

Mr Dlamini explains why this makes sense. Seeds, eggs and milk are all made by living things to feed their young, so nature packs them with a full mix of nutrients.

The questions for this section are with you now: why natural foods like eggs, milk, beans and peanuts contain many nutrients.

## Subtopic: Grouping by the Main Nutrient

If a food contains many nutrients, how do we decide which group it belongs in? We look at which nutrient it has the most of.

A mealie is mostly starch, so we call it a carbohydrate food, even though it has some protein and some oil. An avocado is mostly oil, so we put it with fats and oils, even though it also has vitamins and fibre. Fish and chicken are mostly protein. An orange is mostly water and sugar, but we value it most for its vitamin C.

Food labels help us find out what is in a packaged food. On the back of each packet, there is a table that shows how much of each nutrient there is in one hundred grams of the food. Mr Dlamini shows the class a packet of peanut butter. Out of every one hundred grams, about half is fat, about a quarter is protein, and the rest is mostly carbohydrate and fibre. So peanut butter is rich in both fat and protein.

A packet of maize meal shows that about three quarters of it is carbohydrate, with a little protein and very little fat. A tin of pilchards shows mostly protein, with some fat and calcium.

Reading labels lets us compare foods and choose wisely. It also shows that some foods belong in two groups, like beans, which are rich in both starch and protein.

The questions for this section are with you now: how we group foods and how to read a food label.

## Subtopic: Testing Foods for Nutrients

Detectives need evidence, so the class tests their foods.

The first test is the starch test. A drop of brown iodine solution goes onto each food. On the mealie and on crushed, soaked beans, it turns blue-black, showing starch. On the egg white, the avocado and the orange, it stays brown.

The second test is the fat test. Each group rubs a small piece of their food onto a square of brown paper, and then holds the paper up to the window. Where there is fat or oil, the paper turns see-through, and light shines through it. This is called a translucent spot. The peanut, the avocado and the egg yolk leave clear greasy spots. Water also makes a damp patch, so the groups wait for the paper to dry. A water patch disappears, but a fat spot stays.

The tests show that the beans have starch, that the peanuts and the avocado have fat, and that the egg yolk has fat too, which matches what the labels and the teacher said.

Mr Dlamini reminds the detectives to work safely. Nobody tastes the test foods, spills of iodine are wiped up at once, and everyone washes their hands at the end of the lesson.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking each food has only one nutrient; most natural foods have several. Two: thinking milk is only a drink of water; it contains protein, fat, sugar, calcium and vitamins. Three: thinking a water mark on brown paper means fat; a water patch dries away, but a fat spot stays. Four: thinking beans are only a protein food; they are rich in starch as well as protein.

The questions for this section are with you now: the starch test and the brown paper test for fat.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the Food Detective Day in Mthatha — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Many Nutrients in One

Most natural foods have more than one nutrient.

An egg has protein, fat, vitamins and minerals.

Milk has protein, fat, sugar and calcium.

Keep the egg and the glass of milk in mind and try the questions for this part: many nutrients in one.

## Subtopic: The Biggest Nutrient

We group a food by the nutrient it has the most of.

A mealie is mostly starch. An avocado is mostly oil.

Food labels show what is in the food.

Hold the peanut butter label in mind and try this part's questions: the biggest nutrient.

## Subtopic: Spots and Colours

Iodine turns blue-black on starch. Fat leaves a see-through spot on brown paper.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Most natural foods contain more than one group of nutrients. Eggs, milk, beans and peanuts, which nature makes to feed young living things, contain a full mix. We place a food in the group of the nutrient it has the most of. Food labels show how much of each nutrient a food contains. Iodine solution tests for starch, and brown paper tests for fat.

Hold the greasy spot on the brown paper in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: spots and colours.
