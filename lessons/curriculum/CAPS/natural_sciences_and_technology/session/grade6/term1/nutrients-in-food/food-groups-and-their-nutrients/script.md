# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the main nutrients in food and what each one does for our bodies. The anchor is a primary school in Mamelodi, east of Pretoria, where the school kitchen cooks a free lunch every day for more than a thousand learners as part of the National School Nutrition Programme. The Grade 6 class spends a morning helping the cooks. Mrs Mahlangu, the head cook, has a weekly menu stuck to the wall: pap with pilchards and cabbage on Monday, rice with soya mince on Tuesday, samp and beans on Wednesday, pap with chicken and butternut on Thursday, and rice with beans and spinach on Friday, with fruit or milk on some days. She explains that the menu is planned so that the children get everything their bodies need. The big idea is that food contains nutrients, which are the substances our bodies need to work, grow and stay healthy, and that the main nutrient groups are carbohydrates, proteins, fats and oils, and vitamins and minerals. The nutrient groups, what each one does and examples of foods that contain them are examinable.

## Subtopic: Carbohydrates for Energy

The first nutrient group is carbohydrates. Carbohydrates are the body's main source of energy. We need energy to move, run, play, think, keep our hearts beating and keep our bodies warm.

There are two main kinds of carbohydrate in food: starch and sugar. Starchy foods include maize meal, samp, rice, bread, potatoes, sweet potatoes, pasta and porridge. These foods come from the food stores of plants, which we learnt about in the photosynthesis lessons. Sugary foods include fruit, honey, jam, sweets and cooldrinks, and table sugar made from sugar cane.

Starchy foods give energy slowly, over a few hours, so they keep you going through the school day. Sugary foods give a quick burst of energy that does not last long, and too much sugar is bad for your teeth.

Different people need different amounts of energy. A learner who plays soccer every afternoon, or a farm worker who works in the fields all day, needs more energy food than someone who sits still for most of the day.

Mrs Mahlangu points to the huge pots of pap and rice. Starchy foods are the biggest part of the school lunch, because growing, active children need a lot of energy. A learner who skips breakfast and lunch often feels tired, finds it hard to concentrate and may get a headache.

The questions for this section are with you now: carbohydrates, starch, sugar and energy.

## Subtopic: Proteins for Growth and Repair

The second nutrient group is proteins. Proteins are body-building foods. Our bodies use them to grow, and to repair damaged parts, such as when a cut on your knee heals or a broken bone mends. Muscles, skin, hair and nails are all built mostly from protein.

Foods rich in protein come from animals and from plants. Animal proteins include meat, chicken, fish such as pilchards, eggs, milk, maas and cheese. Mopane worms, eaten in Limpopo, are also very rich in protein. Plant proteins include beans, lentils, peas, peanuts and soya, which is used to make soya mince.

Proteins are made of smaller building blocks, a bit like a wall is made of bricks. When you eat protein, your body breaks it down into these bricks, and then uses them to build its own proteins for new muscle, skin and blood. The body cannot store much protein, so we need some every day.

Children need a lot of protein, because they are growing fast. Between the ages of eleven and fourteen, many children grow several centimetres taller in one year. Pregnant women and people getting better after an illness or an injury also need extra protein.

Mrs Mahlangu explains why there are beans or soya on the menu so often. Meat and fish cost a lot of money, but beans and soya are cheaper and still give the children good protein. When samp and beans are cooked together, as in umngqusho, the meal gives both energy and protein.

The questions for this section are with you now: proteins, growth and repair, and foods that give protein.

## Subtopic: Fats, Oils, Vitamins and Minerals

The third group is fats and oils. Fats are solid at room temperature, like butter, lard and the fat on meat. Oils are liquid, like sunflower oil and olive oil. Fats and oils give a lot of energy, more than the same mass of carbohydrate, and the body stores extra energy as fat under the skin. This layer of fat also acts as insulation, helping to keep the body warm, and fat around organs such as the kidneys helps to protect them from bumps. Foods rich in fats and oils include cooking oil, margarine, butter, peanuts, avocados, full-cream milk and the skin of chicken. We need some fat, but only a small amount.

The fourth group is vitamins and minerals. We need only very small amounts of them, but without them we become ill. They help the body use other nutrients, and they protect us from disease. Vitamin A, found in carrots, butternut, pumpkin, spinach and liver, helps us see in dim light and fight infection. Vitamin C, found in oranges, guavas, tomatoes and cabbage, keeps gums and skin healthy and helps wounds heal. Vitamin D helps us build strong bones, and our skin makes it in sunlight. Calcium is a mineral found in milk, maas, cheese and pilchards eaten with their soft bones, and it builds strong bones and teeth. Iron is a mineral found in red meat, liver, beans and dark green leaves like spinach and morogo, and it helps the blood carry oxygen.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking carbohydrates are only sweets and sugar; starchy foods like pap and bread are carbohydrates too. Two: thinking only meat gives protein; beans, peanuts, soya and eggs do too. Three: thinking all fat is bad; we need a small amount for energy, warmth and protection. Four: thinking vitamins give us energy; they keep us healthy, but carbohydrates and fats give energy.

The questions for this section are with you now: fats and oils, vitamins and minerals.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the school kitchen in Mamelodi — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Energy Foods

Carbohydrates give us energy.

Starch: pap, rice, bread, potatoes. Sugar: fruit, honey, sweets.

Keep the big pots of pap in mind and try the questions for this part: energy foods.

## Subtopic: Body-Building Foods

Proteins help us grow and repair our bodies.

Meat, fish, eggs, milk, beans, peanuts and soya give protein.

Hold the samp and beans in mind and try this part's questions: body-building foods.

## Subtopic: Store and Protect

Fats and oils store energy and keep us warm.

Vitamins and minerals keep us healthy.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Nutrients are the substances in food that our bodies need. Carbohydrates, like starch and sugar, give energy. Proteins help us grow and repair the body. Fats and oils store energy, insulate the body and protect organs. Vitamins and minerals, needed in tiny amounts, keep us healthy and protect us from disease, like vitamin C for healthy gums and calcium for strong bones and teeth.

Hold Mrs Mahlangu's weekly menu in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: store and protect.
