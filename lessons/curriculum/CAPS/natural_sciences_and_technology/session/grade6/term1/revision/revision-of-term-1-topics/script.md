# Part 1 — Expert

This session revises the Term 1 topics of Life and Living: photosynthesis; nutrients in food; nutrition; and ecosystems and food webs. The anchor is a revision day at a primary school in George in the Western Cape, which has a large vegetable garden behind the classrooms. The Grade 6 class must plan, pick and cook a healthy lunch for themselves, using as much as possible from the garden. They pick spinach, carrots, tomatoes, onions and green beans. The school kitchen gives them maize meal, dried beans, eggs, milk and a little oil. After lunch, the peels and leftovers go onto the compost heap in the corner of the garden, where earthworms, beetles and mushrooms are busy. Their teacher, Mr Adams, asks them to follow the energy in their lunch from the Sun to their plate, and then to the compost heap. The big idea of the term is that green plants capture energy from the Sun and make food, that we need a balanced diet of nutrients from that food, and that all living things in an ecosystem are linked through food webs. The whole of Term 1 is examinable.

## Subtopic: Photosynthesis and Starch

The journey starts in the vegetable beds. The spinach leaves are wide, flat and green. Mr Adams asks the class how the spinach made its food.

Green plants make their own food by photosynthesis, mainly in their leaves. They take in water from the soil through their roots, and carbon dioxide from the air through tiny holes in their leaves called stomata. Green chlorophyll in the leaves traps the energy of sunlight. The plant uses that energy to put water and carbon dioxide together to make glucose, a sugar. Oxygen is given off. In words: carbon dioxide plus water, with sunlight and chlorophyll, gives glucose plus oxygen.

The plant uses some glucose for energy and growth, and changes the rest into starch, which it stores. Starch is stored in leaves, stems such as potatoes, roots such as sweet potatoes and cassava, flowers such as cauliflower, fruits such as bananas, and seeds such as maize and beans. Brown iodine solution turns blue-black when it touches starch, so a drop on the maize meal turns blue-black.

The oxygen that plants give off is breathed in by animals and people, who use it to release energy from food, and breathe out carbon dioxide. Plants take in that carbon dioxide to make more food. So plants and animals depend on each other for these gases.

The questions for this section are with you now: photosynthesis, starch and the swapping of gases.

## Subtopic: Nutrients and a Balanced Diet

In the kitchen, the class sorts the ingredients into nutrient groups.

Carbohydrates give energy: the maize meal for pap is starch. Proteins help us grow and repair the body: the beans, eggs and milk. Fats and oils store energy, insulate the body and protect organs: a little oil for the pan. Vitamins and minerals keep us healthy: the spinach gives iron and vitamin A, the tomatoes and carrots give vitamins, and the milk gives calcium.

Most natural foods contain more than one nutrient. The eggs have protein, fat, vitamins and minerals. The beans have starch, protein and fibre. We put a food into the group of the nutrient it has the most of. A brown paper test shows fat: oil leaves a see-through spot that does not dry away.

Processed foods have been changed from their natural form. Many have added salt, sugar, preservatives, flavourings and colourings. We read the label, where the biggest ingredient comes first, and eat salty and sugary snacks only as small treats.

A diet is the selection of foods we eat every day. A balanced diet has all four nutrient groups in the right amounts, plus water and fibre. The class builds a healthy plate: half spinach, carrots and beans, a quarter pap and a quarter egg and beans, with a glass of water.

Some diseases are related to diet. A lack of protein causes kwashiorkor, and a lack of food causes marasmus. A lack of vitamin C causes scurvy, a lack of vitamin D or calcium causes rickets, a lack of iron causes anaemia, and a lack of vitamin A causes night blindness. Too much sugar, salt and fat can lead to tooth decay, obesity, type 2 diabetes and high blood pressure.

The questions for this section are with you now: nutrients, a balanced diet and diseases related to diet.

## Subtopic: Ecosystems and Food Webs

After lunch, the class carries the peels to the compost heap and looks around the garden as an ecosystem.

An ecosystem is an area where living and non-living things interact and depend on each other. The garden has living things, like vegetables, weeds, birds, bees, snails and earthworms, and non-living things, like sunlight, air, water and soil. Other ecosystems in South Africa include rivers, the sea, wetlands, grasslands, forests and deserts. A change to one part, like a drought or pollution, affects the whole ecosystem.

In the garden food web, the vegetables are producers. Snails and caterpillars are herbivores. Birds that eat the snails and caterpillars are carnivores, and people, who eat both plants and animals, are omnivores. Many food chains join to make a food web, with arrows from the food to the eater. In the compost heap, decomposers, mainly fungi and bacteria, break down the dead plant material, helped by earthworms. They return nutrients to the soil, and next season's vegetables will use them.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking plants get their food from the soil; they make it in their leaves by photosynthesis. Two: thinking vitamins give us energy; carbohydrates and fats do, while vitamins keep us healthy. Three: thinking a balanced diet means equal amounts of every food; about half the plate should be vegetables and fruit. Four: thinking food chain arrows point from the eater to the food; they point from the food to the eater.

So the energy in the lunch came from the Sun, was trapped by the plants, passed to the learners, and the leftovers will feed the soil for the next crop.

The questions for this section are with you now: ecosystems, food webs and decomposers.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the school garden in George — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Plants Make Food

Plants use sunlight, water and carbon dioxide to make glucose.

They store extra food as starch and give off oxygen.

Keep the spinach leaves in mind and try the questions for this part: plants make food.

## Subtopic: Healthy Eating

Carbohydrates, proteins, fats and oils, and vitamins and minerals.

A balanced plate, with water and fibre, keeps us healthy.

Hold the healthy plate in mind and try this part's questions: healthy eating.

## Subtopic: Living Together

Producers, consumers and decomposers are linked in food webs.

Pocket summary of the term. Green plants make glucose by photosynthesis in their leaves, using sunlight, carbon dioxide and water, and give off oxygen. They store extra food as starch. Food contains nutrients: carbohydrates for energy, proteins for growth and repair, fats and oils for stored energy and warmth, and vitamins and minerals for health. A balanced diet, with water and fibre, helps prevent diseases. In an ecosystem, living and non-living things depend on each other. Food webs link producers and consumers, and decomposers return nutrients to the soil.

Hold the whole journey from the Sun to the compost heap in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: living together.
