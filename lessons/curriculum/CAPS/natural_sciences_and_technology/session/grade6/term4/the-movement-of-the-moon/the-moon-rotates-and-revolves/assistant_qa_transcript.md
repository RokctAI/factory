### subtopic_1 — The Moon Revolves Around the Earth
**Assistant:** Can you see the Moon during the day?

**Tutor:** Yes, very often, and it surprises many people. The Moon is not only a night-time object. It rises and sets just like the Sun, but at a different time each day, because it is moving along its orbit around the Earth. For about half of each month, the Moon is above the horizon for part of the daytime. For example, about a week after new moon, when it looks like a half moon, it rises around midday and can be seen in the afternoon sky, looking pale and white against the blue. About a week after full moon, the half moon can be seen in the morning sky after sunrise. We see it in the day because it is big and close, and the sunlight it reflects is bright enough to shine through the blue sky. Stars and most planets are too faint to see in daytime. The only time the Moon is truly hard to find is around new moon, when it is close to the Sun in the sky.

### subtopic_2 — The Moon Rotates
**Assistant:** Does the Moon affect the sea?

**Tutor:** Yes, it does, through gravity. Just as the Earth's gravity pulls on the Moon and keeps it in orbit, the Moon's gravity pulls on the Earth. The solid ground hardly moves, but the water in the oceans can move. The Moon's pull makes the sea bulge slightly, and as the Earth rotates, coasts pass through these bulges. That causes the tides: the sea rises up the beach and falls back again, usually twice a day. If you visit a beach in Durban or Gqeberha, you can see high tide and low tide, and you can watch rock pools appear at low tide. The Sun's gravity also affects the tides. When the Sun, Earth and Moon line up, at new moon and full moon, their pulls add together, and the tides are higher and lower than usual. These are called spring tides, although they have nothing to do with the season of spring. Fishermen and surfers often check the Moon's phase to plan their day.

### subtopic_3 — Together Around the Sun
**Assistant:** If the Moon goes around the Earth every month, why is there not an eclipse every month?

**Tutor:** This is a clever question. An eclipse of the Moon happens when the Earth gets exactly between the Sun and the Moon, so the Earth's shadow falls on the Moon. An eclipse of the Sun happens when the Moon gets exactly between the Sun and the Earth, so the Moon's shadow falls on part of the Earth. You might expect this every month, at every full moon and new moon. But the Moon's orbit is tilted slightly compared to the Earth's path around the Sun. So most months, at full moon, the Moon passes a little above or below the Earth's shadow, and at new moon, the Moon's shadow passes a little above or below the Earth. Only when everything lines up exactly, a few times a year, do we get an eclipse. This also shows that the normal phases of the Moon are not caused by the Earth's shadow. The shadow only touches the Moon during an eclipse.
