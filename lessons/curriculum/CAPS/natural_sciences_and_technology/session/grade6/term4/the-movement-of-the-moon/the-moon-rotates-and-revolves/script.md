# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the movements of the Moon. The anchor is a Grade 6 class in Ladysmith, a town on the Klip River in KwaZulu-Natal, with the Drakensberg on the horizon. A month ago, their teacher, Mrs Pillay, gave each learner a Moon diary. Every clear evening, they had to go outside, find the Moon, draw its shape, and write down the date, the time and where it was in the sky. Today, the class lays all their diaries on the desks. They notice three things. The Moon's shape changed every night, from a thin curve to a full circle and back again. After about a month, the shapes started repeating. And in every single drawing, the dark patches on the Moon were in exactly the same places. The big idea is that the Moon revolves around the Earth in about a month, that it rotates on its axis once in about twenty-eight days, so the same side always faces us, and that the Earth and Moon revolve around the Sun together. The movements of the Moon are examinable.

## Subtopic: The Moon Revolves Around the Earth

The Moon travels around the Earth in a path called an orbit, just as the Earth travels around the Sun. This movement is called revolution. The Earth's gravity keeps the Moon in its orbit.

The Moon takes about a month to revolve once around the Earth. In fact, the word month comes from the word moon. People have used the Moon as a calendar for thousands of years.

As the Moon moves around the Earth, it is always half lit by the Sun. But we see different amounts of the lit half from Earth, depending on where the Moon is in its orbit. These changing shapes are called the phases of the Moon. When the Moon is between the Earth and the Sun, its lit side faces away from us and we can hardly see it: this is a new moon. Over the next two weeks, we see more and more of the lit half: a thin crescent, then a half moon, then a full moon, when the Moon is on the far side of the Earth from the Sun, and we see the whole lit half. Over the next two weeks, the lit part shrinks again. The whole cycle, from one new moon to the next, takes about twenty-nine and a half days. In South Africa, a growing crescent is seen in the western sky soon after sunset, with its bright edge facing down towards the place where the Sun has just set. The Moon itself never changes shape. It is always a round ball; only the part of the lit half that we can see changes.

The learners also notice that the Moon rose later each evening. Because the Moon is moving along its orbit, the Earth has to turn a little further each day to catch up with it, so the Moon rises about fifty minutes later each day.

The questions for this section are with you now: the Moon's revolution and its phases.

## Subtopic: The Moon Rotates

Why are the dark patches always in the same place? Mrs Pillay's chair test shows why. She puts a chair in the middle of the room. The chair is the Earth. A learner, Sipho, is the Moon. He must walk in a circle around the chair, always keeping his face towards it.

When Sipho has gone all the way around, the class notices something surprising. While he walked around the chair, Sipho also turned his body all the way around once: he faced the window, then the board, then the door, then the back wall. So, by going around the chair once while always facing it, he also rotated once.

The Moon does exactly the same. It rotates on its axis once in about twenty-eight days, which is about the same time it takes to revolve once around the Earth. Because the rotation and the revolution take the same time, the same side of the Moon always faces the Earth. That is why the patterns of dark plains look the same in every drawing, and why we never see the far side of the Moon from Earth.

This also means a day on the Moon is very long. From sunrise to sunset on the Moon takes about two Earth weeks, and the night lasts about two Earth weeks too.

The questions for this section are with you now: the Moon's rotation and why we always see the same side.

## Subtopic: Together Around the Sun

Now Mrs Pillay switches on a lamp in the centre of the classroom to be the Sun. She gives Thandeka a globe to be the Earth and asks her to walk slowly around the lamp. Sipho, as the Moon, must keep walking around Thandeka while she moves.

This is what really happens. The Moon revolves around the Earth, and at the same time the Earth and the Moon travel together around the Sun. The Earth takes 365¼ days to go around the Sun once, and in that time the Moon goes around the Earth about twelve or thirteen times. That is why there are twelve months in a year. While all this is happening, the Earth is also spinning on its axis every twenty-four hours, so the whole system is turning and travelling at once, like a carefully planned dance.

So the Moon has three movements: it rotates on its axis, it revolves around the Earth, and it travels with the Earth around the Sun.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking the Moon does not rotate because we always see the same side; it rotates once each time it goes around the Earth. Two: thinking the phases are caused by the Earth's shadow; they are caused by how much of the sunlit half we can see. Three: thinking the Moon changes its real shape; it is always a round ball. Four: thinking the Moon goes around the Sun on its own, separate from the Earth; it travels around the Sun together with the Earth.

The questions for this section are with you now: how the Earth and Moon move around the Sun together.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the Moon diaries in Ladysmith — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Around the Earth

The Moon goes around the Earth in about a month.

Its shape seems to change. These are phases.

Keep the Moon diaries in mind and try the questions for this part: around the Earth.

## Subtopic: Same Face

The Moon spins once in about 28 days, so we always see the same side.

Hold Sipho walking around the chair in mind and try this part's questions: same face.

## Subtopic: Together Around the Sun

The Earth and Moon go around the Sun together.

Pocket summary of the lesson. The Moon revolves around the Earth in about a month, held by the Earth's gravity. As it moves, we see different amounts of its sunlit half, so its shape seems to change in phases, from new moon to full moon and back, in about twenty-nine and a half days. The Moon rotates on its axis once in about twenty-eight days, the same time as its revolution, so the same side always faces the Earth. The Earth and Moon revolve around the Sun together once a year.

Hold the lamp, the globe and the walking Moon in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: together around the Sun.
