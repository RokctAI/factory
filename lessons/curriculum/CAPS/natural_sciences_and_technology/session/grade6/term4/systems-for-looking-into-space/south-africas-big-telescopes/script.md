# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers South Africa's big telescopes. The anchor is a Grade 6 class in Carnarvon, a small Karoo town in the Northern Cape. Most of the time, Carnarvon is a quiet farming town of sheep, windmills and wide, flat plains. But not far from the town stands one of the most powerful scientific instruments on Earth. Their teacher, Mr Jacobs, has organised a trip to see it, and before they get on the bus, he collects every cell phone and switches it off. The learners are puzzled. When the bus stops, they see why the place is special: sixty-four giant white dishes, each one taller than a house, spread across the veld and all pointing at exactly the same spot in the sky. The next day, on the way home, the bus climbs a hill near Sutherland, where a row of silver domes stands on top, and one is far bigger than the rest. The big idea is that South Africa has built and uses some of the largest telescopes in the world, because our dark, dry and quiet skies are among the best on Earth for looking into space. South Africa's telescopes are examinable.

## Subtopic: SALT, Seeing Faint Light

The big dome at Sutherland holds SALT, the Southern African Large Telescope. It is the largest single light telescope in the southern half of the world. It was opened in 2005.

SALT is a reflecting telescope. Its main mirror is about eleven metres across, as wide as a classroom. A mirror that big could not be made in one piece, so it is made of ninety-one six-sided mirrors, fitted together like the cells of a honeycomb. Computers keep all the pieces lined up perfectly, so they work together as one giant mirror.

Because the mirror is so big, SALT can collect light from extremely faint and distant objects, such as galaxies billions of light years away, exploding stars, and black holes swallowing gas. It does not have an eyepiece. Its light goes to cameras and other instruments, and computers record the information. South African scientists built and run SALT, together with partners from other countries. The same hill has several smaller telescopes too, run by the South African Astronomical Observatory, and visitors can tour SALT during the day. At night, the domes open, and the telescopes get to work, keeping track of faint objects as the Earth turns.

Why Sutherland? The hills are about one thousand eight hundred metres high, so there is less air above to blur the light. The Karoo air is very dry, with few clouds. And Sutherland is far from big cities, so the night sky is very dark. It is also known as one of the coldest towns in South Africa, so astronomers working there wear thick jackets.

The questions for this section are with you now: SALT and why it was built at Sutherland.

## Subtopic: MeerKAT, Listening with Dishes

The sixty-four dishes near Carnarvon make up MeerKAT, a radio telescope. Each dish is about thirteen and a half metres across. MeerKAT was opened in 2018, and it was designed and built mostly by South African engineers and scientists. Its name means more KAT, because it grew from an earlier, smaller telescope called KAT-7, which had seven dishes.

A radio telescope collects radio waves from space, not light. The dishes are spread over many kilometres, but a computer combines all their signals as if they were one gigantic dish. This lets MeerKAT make very sharp radio pictures of space. It has taken some of the clearest pictures ever made of the centre of our galaxy, the Milky Way, and found giant bubbles of radio waves there that no one had seen before.

Now the learners understand the cell phones. Radio telescopes are so sensitive that a single cell phone signal from nearby could drown out the faint radio waves from distant galaxies. So the area around MeerKAT is protected by law as a radio quiet zone, where cell phone towers, radio transmitters and even microwave ovens are controlled. The Karoo was chosen because very few people live there, so there is very little radio noise.

South Africa also has an older radio telescope at Hartebeesthoek, northwest of Johannesburg, with a dish twenty-six metres across.

The questions for this section are with you now: MeerKAT, radio telescopes and radio quiet zones.

## Subtopic: The SKA and Why South Africa

MeerKAT is now becoming part of an even bigger project: the Square Kilometre Array, or SKA. Many countries are working together to build it. In the Karoo, around one hundred and thirty new dishes are being added to MeerKAT's sixty-four, spread over a much larger area. A second part of the SKA, using many thousands of small antennas, is being built in Australia. When it is finished, the SKA will be the largest radio telescope in the world.

South Africa has several advantages for astronomy. From the southern half of the world, we have the best view of the centre of the Milky Way and of two nearby galaxies called the Magellanic Clouds, which cannot be seen from most northern countries. The Karoo has dark, dry, clear skies and very little radio noise.

The telescopes also bring benefits to people. They create jobs, train engineers and technicians, and give bursaries to young South Africans to study science, maths and engineering. Mr Jacobs points out that some of the scientists who work there grew up in small towns just like Carnarvon.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking MeerKAT takes photographs with light; it collects radio waves. Two: thinking SALT's mirror is one piece of glass; it is made of ninety-one pieces. Three: thinking cell phones do not matter near a radio telescope; their signals can drown out radio waves from space. Four: thinking big telescopes are built near cities so people can reach them easily; they need dark, dry, quiet places.
