### subtopic_1 — What a Telescope Does
**Assistant:** Why do stars twinkle, but planets usually do not?

**Tutor:** Stars twinkle because of the air above us. The air is always moving, with warm and cool pockets that bend light in slightly different directions, like the wobbly view you get when you look across a hot road or over a braai fire. Stars are so far away that each one is just a tiny point of light, a single thin beam reaching your eye. When the moving air bends that thin beam this way and that, the star seems to flicker and even change colour. Planets are much closer to us, so they are not single points but tiny discs, even though they look like dots to our eyes. Light comes from many points across the disc, and the wobbles from each point mostly cancel each other out, so a planet shines with a steadier light. That is one way to tell a planet from a star: if a bright dot is not twinkling much, it may well be a planet. Telescopes in space are above the air, so for them, stars do not twinkle at all.

### subtopic_2 — Lenses and Mirrors
**Assistant:** Can a telescope see the flag the astronauts left on the Moon?

**Tutor:** No, not even the biggest telescopes on Earth can. This surprises many people. The flags and the bottom parts of the landers are only a few metres across, and the Moon is about three hundred and eighty-four thousand kilometres away. From that distance, something a few metres wide is far too small for any telescope on Earth to show, even though telescopes can see galaxies much further away, because galaxies are enormous. The Hubble Space Telescope cannot see them either. However, spacecraft that orbit close to the Moon have taken photographs of the landing sites from just above the surface. In those pictures, you can see the landers, the tracks of the Moon buggies, and even the paths of footprints the astronauts made while walking around. So we know the equipment is still there, but we need a camera close to the Moon, not a telescope on Earth, to see it.

### subtopic_3 — Seeing the Invisible
**Assistant:** What is a light year?

**Tutor:** A light year sounds like a measure of time, but it is actually a distance. It is how far light travels in one year. Light is the fastest thing there is: it travels about three hundred thousand kilometres every second. In one second, light could go around the Earth more than seven times. Light from the Sun takes about eight minutes to reach us. In a whole year, light travels about nine and a half million million kilometres, a number too big to imagine. Astronomers use light years because distances to the stars are so huge that kilometres become silly. The nearest star to us after the Sun, called Proxima Centauri, is about four light years away. It is part of the Alpha Centauri group, the bright pointer star near the Southern Cross that you can see from South Africa. This also means that when you look at a star, you are seeing light that left it years ago, so you are looking back in time.
