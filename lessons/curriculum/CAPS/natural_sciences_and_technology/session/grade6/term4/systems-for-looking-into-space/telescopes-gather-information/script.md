# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers telescopes, the systems we use to look into space and gather information about it. The anchor is a Grade 6 class in Soweto, in Johannesburg. On a clear winter evening, the class and their parents stay late at school for a stargazing evening. Ms Zulu, a member of a local astronomy club, sets up a telescope on a tripod on the sports field. The school switches off the field lights. With their eyes alone, the learners can see the Moon, a few bright stars, and one bright, steady dot that Ms Zulu says is the planet Jupiter. Then, one by one, they look through the telescope. The Moon is suddenly covered in sharp craters. Jupiter is no longer a dot, but a small striped ball with four tiny bright moons lined up beside it. A learner called Lesedi asks how a tube with some glass inside can do that. The big idea is that a telescope is a system that collects light or other signals from space and makes faint, distant objects visible and clearer, so that we can gather information about them. Telescopes and the information they gather are examinable.

## Subtopic: What a Telescope Does

Ms Zulu explains that our eyes are not very good at seeing faint things. Light enters the eye through the pupil, the small black opening in the middle. In the dark, it opens wider, but it is still only a few millimetres across, so it can only catch a little light from a distant star.

A telescope does two main jobs. First, it collects light. Its big lens or mirror is much wider than your pupil, so it catches much more light, like a wide bucket catches more rain than a cup. This makes faint objects bright enough to see. Second, it magnifies, making distant objects look bigger, so that we can see details such as craters, rings and moons.

A telescope is a system. Its input is light from space. Its process is collecting and focusing the light with lenses or mirrors. Its output is a clear, bright image, which can be seen with an eye or recorded by a camera and a computer.

Binoculars are two small telescopes side by side, one for each eye.

Ms Zulu gives one rule that is never broken: never look at the Sun through a telescope or binoculars. They gather so much light that they can blind you instantly. Astronomers use special filters to study the Sun safely.

## Subtopic: Lenses and Mirrors

There are two main kinds of telescopes that collect light.

A refracting telescope uses lenses. A lens is a curved piece of clear glass that bends light. A big lens at the front collects light and brings it together, and a small lens near your eye, called the eyepiece, magnifies the image. About four hundred years ago, Galileo Galilei pointed a simple refracting telescope at the sky. He was the first to see mountains and craters on the Moon, four moons going around Jupiter, the phases of Venus, and that the hazy band of the Milky Way is made of countless stars. Those four moons are the same ones the learners see from Soweto.

A reflecting telescope uses a curved mirror instead. The big mirror at the bottom of the tube collects light and reflects it to a point, where an eyepiece or a camera picks it up. Ms Zulu's telescope is a reflector. Most large modern telescopes are reflectors, because a big mirror can be supported from behind, while a big lens is very heavy and can only be held at its edges, so it sags.

The bigger the lens or mirror, the more light a telescope collects, and the fainter the objects it can show. Ms Zulu's mirror is about twenty centimetres across, and it gathers hundreds of times more light than a learner's eye in the dark. Professional telescopes have mirrors many metres across. Today, most big telescopes do not even have an eyepiece. A sensitive camera takes the picture, and computers store and study it.

The questions for this section are with you now: refracting and reflecting telescopes.

## Subtopic: Seeing the Invisible

Some telescopes collect signals that our eyes cannot see. Stars, galaxies and clouds of gas also send out radio waves, the same kind of waves that carry radio and cell phone signals. A radio telescope uses a large dish, shaped like a giant satellite dish, to collect these radio waves. A computer turns them into pictures. Radio telescopes can work in the day and through clouds.

The air around the Earth blurs starlight, which is why stars twinkle. Clouds, dust and city lights also get in the way. That is why big telescopes are built on high mountains or in dry places far from cities. Some are even sent into space. The Hubble Space Telescope has circled the Earth since 1990, above the air, taking very sharp pictures. The James Webb Space Telescope, launched in 2021, has a huge golden mirror made of eighteen pieces, and it can see some of the earliest galaxies ever formed.

Telescopes gather a great deal of information. They show us what planets, moons and galaxies look like. They help scientists work out what stars are made of, how far away they are, and how hot they are. They have found thousands of planets going around other stars, and they watch for asteroids that might come close to the Earth.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking a telescope only makes things bigger; its main job is to collect more light. Two: thinking you can look at the Sun through a telescope if it is quick; you can be blinded instantly. Three: thinking all telescopes use light; radio telescopes collect radio waves. Four: thinking telescopes work best in cities; they need dark, clear, dry skies.

The questions for this section are with you now: radio telescopes, space telescopes and the information telescopes gather.
