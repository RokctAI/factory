### subtopic_1 — SALT, Seeing Faint Light
**Assistant:** How can sixty-four separate dishes work like one telescope?

**Tutor:** This is one of the cleverest parts of MeerKAT. Each dish catches radio waves from the same place in the sky. Because the dishes are spread out over many kilometres, the waves arrive at each dish at very slightly different times, by tiny fractions of a second. Every dish has an extremely accurate clock, and the signals from all sixty-four are sent along cables to a building full of powerful computers. The computers line up the signals using those precise times and combine them. The result is as if there were one gigantic dish as wide as the distance between the furthest dishes. The wider the telescope, the more detail it can see, so spreading the dishes out lets MeerKAT see fine details that a single dish never could. The computers need to work through a huge amount of information every second. In fact, the SKA will produce so much information that it needs some of the most powerful computers in the world to handle it.

### subtopic_2 — MeerKAT, Listening with Dishes
**Assistant:** What have South African telescopes discovered?

**Tutor:** They have helped make many discoveries. MeerKAT made one of the clearest radio pictures ever taken of the centre of the Milky Way, where a giant black hole sits, and it revealed enormous bubbles of radio waves, hundreds of light years tall, stretching above and below the centre of our galaxy. It has also found strange threads of radio waves near the galaxy's centre, and many galaxies full of hydrogen gas that were too faint to see before. MeerKAT is very good at studying pulsars, which are tiny, heavy dead stars that spin very fast, flashing radio waves like a lighthouse. SALT has studied exploding stars, black holes and very distant galaxies, and has helped scientists work out what faraway objects are made of. Much of this work was done by South African scientists and students, working with teams from around the world, and some of these scientists started out as learners in schools across the country.

### subtopic_3 — The SKA and Why South Africa
**Assistant:** How can I become an astronomer or engineer one day?

**Tutor:** There are many paths, and you are already on the first step by being curious about space. At school, focus on mathematics and physical sciences in the senior grades, because astronomers and engineers use them every day. Reading, writing and computer skills also matter, because scientists write reports and use computers to study huge amounts of information. Outside school, you can join an astronomy club, visit a planetarium, such as the ones in Johannesburg and Cape Town, or visit the visitor centre at Sutherland. On clear nights, learn to find the Southern Cross, the planets and the Moon's craters with a pair of binoculars. After school, you could study at a university or a college, and there are bursaries from programmes linked to the big telescopes, which help young South Africans to study science and engineering. You do not need to become an astronomer to work with telescopes, either. They also need engineers, technicians, computer programmers and electricians.
