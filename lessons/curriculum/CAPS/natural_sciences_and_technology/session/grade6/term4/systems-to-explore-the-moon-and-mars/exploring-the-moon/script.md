# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers how people have explored the surface of the Moon using a Moon rover. The anchor is a Grade 6 class in Knysna, a town on a lagoon on the Garden Route in the Western Cape. A learner called Liam brings his grandfather, Mr Fortuin, to class. In 1969, Mr Fortuin was a boy of ten. South Africa did not have television until 1976, so on the night of the first Moon landing he sat with his family around a radio, listening to crackling voices from almost four hundred thousand kilometres away. He still remembers the words: that's one small step for man, one giant leap for mankind. After he leaves, their teacher, Ms Daniels, gives the class a design challenge. Each group must design a car that astronauts could drive on the Moon, and explain how it would solve the problems of driving there. The big idea is that people have explored the Moon's surface on foot and in a Moon rover called the Lunar Roving Vehicle, a system designed to solve the special problems of the Moon, and that robot rovers continue to explore it. Exploring the Moon is examinable.

## Subtopic: People on the Moon

On 20 July 1969, the American spacecraft Apollo 11 landed on the Moon. Two astronauts, Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin, climbed down the ladder of their landing craft and became the first people to walk on another world. A third astronaut, Michael Collins, stayed in orbit around the Moon in the main spacecraft, waiting to take them home.

Between 1969 and 1972, there were six successful Moon landings, and twelve astronauts walked on the Moon. They collected more than three hundred and eighty kilograms of Moon rocks and dust and brought them back to Earth for scientists to study. They set up experiments, took thousands of photographs, and left footprints that are still there today, because the Moon has no wind or rain to wipe them away.

On the first three landings, the astronauts could only walk. Walking in a bulky spacesuit in low gravity is hard work, so they bounced along in kangaroo hops and could not go far from their landing craft. To explore more of the Moon, they needed a vehicle.

The questions for this section are with you now: the first Moon landings and what the astronauts did.

## Subtopic: The Moon Rover

The Lunar Roving Vehicle, often called the Moon buggy, was used on the last three Apollo missions, in 1971 and 1972. It was an open, two-seater car, a little like a light frame on four wheels, with no roof or doors. It carried two astronauts, their tools, cameras and the rocks they collected. It is a good example of a system designed to solve problems.

Problem one: the Moon has no air. A petrol engine needs oxygen from the air to burn its fuel, so it would not work. Solution: the rover ran on batteries, with an electric motor in each wheel.

Problem two: the Moon is covered in fine, powdery dust, rocks and craters, and it is extremely hot in the sun and extremely cold in the shade. Ordinary rubber tyres filled with air could burst or crack. Solution: the wheels were made of a woven mesh of thin steel wire, with metal strips for grip, so they could not go flat. Wide mudguards kept the dust from spraying over the astronauts.

Problem three: everything had to fit inside the small landing craft. Solution: the rover folded up, like a folding chair, and was strapped to the side of the lander. The astronauts pulled on ropes to unfold it.

Problem four: there is no magnetic north on the Moon, so a compass would not work. Solution: the rover had its own navigation system that kept track of its direction and distance from the lander.

The rover had a top speed of about thirteen kilometres an hour, and on the last mission, the astronauts drove it more than thirty-five kilometres in total. For safety, they never drove further than they could walk back if it broke down. All three rovers were left on the Moon, where they still stand today. Like every system, the rover had an input, a process and an output. The input was electrical energy from the batteries. The process was the motors turning the wheels, controlled by a single handle between the seats, which the driver pushed forward to go, pulled back to stop, and tilted left or right to steer. The output was movement across the Moon.

The questions for this section are with you now: the Moon rover and how its design solved problems.

## Subtopic: Robot Rovers

Since the Apollo missions ended in 1972, no person has walked on the Moon. But rovers have gone back without people. A robot rover is controlled by people on Earth, using radio signals, and carries cameras and instruments to study the rocks and soil.

The first robot rover on the Moon was sent by the Soviet Union in 1970. In 2019, a Chinese rover became the first ever to drive on the far side of the Moon. In 2023, an Indian rover explored near the Moon's south pole, where scientists hope to find ice that could one day give future visitors water.

Robot rovers are cheaper and safer than sending people, because they do not need air, food, water or a way home. But people can think quickly, notice surprises and decide what to do on the spot, so space agencies are planning to send astronauts back to the Moon.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking the Moon rover had a petrol engine like a car on Earth; there is no air for fuel to burn, so it used batteries. Two: thinking the rover had rubber tyres filled with air; it had wire-mesh wheels. Three: thinking the astronauts brought the rovers home; they were left on the Moon. Four: thinking only people can explore the Moon; robot rovers explore it too.

The questions for this section are with you now: robot rovers on the Moon.
