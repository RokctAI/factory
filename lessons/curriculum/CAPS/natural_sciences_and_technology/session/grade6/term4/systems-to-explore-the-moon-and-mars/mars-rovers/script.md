# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the robotic rovers that have explored the surface of Mars. The anchor is a Grade 6 class in Springbok, the main town of Namaqualand in the Northern Cape. Their teacher, Mr Cloete, takes them on a field trip into the dry hills outside town. The ground is covered in reddish-brown dust and broken rocks, there are no trees, and the wind blows grit into their eyes. Mr Cloete holds up a photograph taken by a rover on Mars. The learners gasp: it looks almost exactly like the hills around them. But Mr Cloete tells them that Mars is far colder, its air is too thin to breathe, and no person has ever been there. Everything we have seen on its surface has been seen through the cameras of robots. The big idea is that robotic rovers, designed as systems to survive the hard conditions on Mars and controlled from Earth, have explored the surface of Mars and sent back information about its rocks, its air and its past. Mars rovers are examinable.

## Subtopic: A Hard Place to Explore

Mars is the fourth planet from the Sun, about half the size of Earth. Its surface is red because the dust contains iron that has rusted. Its air is very thin and made mostly of carbon dioxide. On average it is about sixty degrees below freezing, and dust storms can sometimes cover the whole planet for weeks.

Mars is also very far away. Even when it is closest, it is more than fifty million kilometres from Earth, about one hundred and fifty times further than the Moon. A spacecraft takes about seven months to get there.

This distance causes a big problem. Radio signals travel at the speed of light, but they still take between about three and twenty-two minutes to travel from Earth to Mars, depending on where the planets are. So people on Earth cannot drive a Mars rover like a remote-control car. If a rover was heading towards a cliff, by the time the picture reached Earth and the stop command reached Mars, it could already have fallen. So Mars rovers are robots. Scientists on Earth send a list of instructions each day, and the rover carries them out on its own, using its cameras and computer to avoid danger.

Landing is also hard. The air is too thin for parachutes alone to slow a spacecraft enough, so engineers have used giant airbags that bounced along the ground, and rocket-powered cranes that lowered rovers gently on cables.

The questions for this section are with you now: conditions on Mars and why rovers must be robots.

## Subtopic: Robot Explorers

Back at school, the class builds a simple model rover that uses a sensor to stop before it bumps into a wall. Mr Cloete explains that real Mars rovers are complex systems designed to solve the problems of Mars.

Wheels: most Mars rovers have six wheels, attached to bendy legs that let them climb over rocks without tipping over. The newest rovers have strong aluminium wheels. They drive very slowly and carefully, often less than two hundred metres an hour.

Eyes and brain: many cameras act as eyes, and a computer acts as a brain, so the rover can plan a safe path by itself.

Arm and tools: a robotic arm, like a human arm with an elbow and wrist, carries tools such as a drill, a brush and a microscope camera to study rocks up close. Some rovers can even fire a laser at a rock and study the glowing light to work out what it is made of.

Power: some rovers use solar panels, which change sunlight into electricity. But dust can cover the panels. The two newest American rovers, Curiosity and Perseverance, use a small nuclear power source that keeps working day and night, in any weather.

Talking to Earth: antennas send pictures and information home, often through spacecraft orbiting Mars.

Weather station: some rovers measure the temperature, wind and dust in the air every day, so we have weather reports from another planet.

Mr Cloete points out that every part of the rover is there to solve a problem. The engineers asked: what could go wrong on Mars? Then they designed a part to deal with it, and tested it in deserts on Earth before launch.

The questions for this section are with you now: how a Mars rover is designed as a system.

## Subtopic: What the Rovers Found

The first Mars rover, Sojourner, landed in 1997. It was about the size of a microwave oven. In 2004, two rovers called Spirit and Opportunity landed. They were planned to last for three months, but Opportunity kept going for about fourteen years and drove more than forty-five kilometres, until a huge dust storm covered its solar panels. Curiosity, the size of a small car, landed in 2012 and is still exploring. Perseverance landed in 2021. It carried a small helicopter, Ingenuity, which made the first powered flight on another planet. China also landed its own rover, Zhurong, in 2021.

The rovers found strong evidence that Mars was once warmer and wetter. They found rocks that can only form in water, smooth pebbles rolled by ancient rivers, and the remains of old lake beds. Curiosity found that one crater once held a lake where tiny living things could have survived. No life has been found, but Perseverance is collecting rock samples in sealed tubes that scientists hope to bring back to Earth one day to study.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking people have walked on Mars; only robots have been there. Two: thinking scientists drive Mars rovers with a joystick in real time; signals take minutes, so rovers carry out instructions on their own. Three: thinking rovers have found living things on Mars; they have found signs of ancient water, not life. Four: thinking Mars is hot because it is red; it is very cold.

The questions for this section are with you now: Mars rovers and their discoveries.

## Subtopic: Signs of Water

Rovers found that Mars once had rivers and lakes.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Mars is a cold, dusty planet with thin air, so far away that radio signals take minutes to arrive and the trip takes about seven months. Robotic rovers explore it. They follow instructions sent from Earth and use cameras and computers to avoid danger. They have six wheels on bendy legs, robotic arms with drills and tools, and solar panels or a nuclear power source. Sojourner, Spirit, Opportunity, Curiosity, Perseverance and Zhurong have explored Mars and found signs that it once had rivers and lakes, but no life has been found.
