### subtopic_1 — Spinning on an Axis
**Assistant:** If the Earth spins so fast, why do we not fly off?

**Tutor:** That is a very sensible worry. The answer is gravity. The Earth's gravity pulls everything towards its centre, including you, the air and the oceans, and it is far stronger than the push you would get from the spinning. The spinning does have a tiny effect. Because of it, you weigh very slightly less at the equator than at the poles, but the difference is so small that you would never notice it on a bathroom scale. Think of riding on a slowly turning merry-go-round while holding tightly to the bar: you feel a small pull outwards, but holding on keeps you safe. Gravity is like an incredibly strong grip that always holds on. Also, the Earth turns smoothly and steadily, without sudden starts and stops, and it has been doing so for billions of years. We only feel movement when speed changes suddenly, like when a taxi brakes hard. So we stay firmly on the ground, spinning along without feeling a thing.

### subtopic_2 — Day and Night
**Assistant:** Why do some places have very long days in summer and very short days in winter?

**Tutor:** This is because the Earth's axis is not straight up and down. It is tilted, leaning over a little to one side, and it stays leaning the same way as the Earth travels around the Sun. For part of the year, the southern half of the Earth leans towards the Sun. Then we have summer, and the days are longer than the nights, because our part of the Earth spends more of each rotation in the light. Half a year later, the southern half leans away from the Sun. Then we have winter, and the nights are longer. In South Africa, the difference is noticeable: in Kokstad, the longest summer day has about fourteen hours of daylight and the shortest winter day has only about ten. Near the poles, the effect is extreme. In midsummer, the Sun does not set at all for weeks, and in midwinter, it does not rise. But all over the world, the rotation still takes about twenty-four hours.

### subtopic_3 — The Sun's Path Across the Sky
**Assistant:** How did people tell the time before clocks?

**Tutor:** They used the Earth's rotation, often without knowing it. The simplest method was to watch the Sun and the shadows it makes. Long before clocks, people noticed that shadows are long in the morning, shortest at midday, and long again in the afternoon, and that they swing around in the same way every day. So they made sundials. A sundial has a pointer that casts a shadow onto a flat surface with marks for the hours. As the Earth turns, the shadow moves from mark to mark, showing the time. Ancient Egyptians used tall stone pillars as shadow clocks thousands of years ago. At night, people used the stars, which also seem to wheel across the sky as the Earth turns. In many communities in southern Africa, people also knew the time of day from the length of shadows, the position of the Sun, and the behaviour of animals, such as cockerels crowing before dawn. Your own shadow stick at school is a simple sundial.
