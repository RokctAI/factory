# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the revolution of the Earth and the planets around the Sun. The anchor is a Grade 6 class in Thohoyandou, a town in the Vhembe district of Limpopo, near the forests and tea estates of the Soutpansberg. While the class is filling in a birthday chart, a learner called Mpho says that her family has a puzzle: her mother was born on 29 February, and most years that date is not on the calendar at all. Her mother's real birthday only comes once every four years, and she jokes that she is really only nine years old. The class laughs, but their teacher, Mrs Nemakonde, says the answer is hidden in the way the Earth moves around the Sun. She takes the class outside to the netball court, puts a learner with a yellow cap in the centre circle as the Sun, and gives Mpho a blue ball to carry as the Earth. The big idea is that the planets travel, or revolve, around the Sun in paths called orbits, and that one revolution of the Earth takes 365¼ days, which is one year. Revolution and the length of a year are examinable.

## Subtopic: Travelling Around the Sun

Mpho walks in a big circle around the Sun in the centre of the court. Mrs Nemakonde explains that this movement, travelling around another object, is called revolution. The path that a planet follows is called its orbit. The Earth's orbit is almost a circle, very slightly stretched, like a slightly squashed hoop.

The Earth is about one hundred and fifty million kilometres from the Sun, so its orbit is enormous: one trip around it is more than nine hundred million kilometres long. To get all the way around in a year, the Earth races along at about thirty kilometres every second, more than a hundred times faster than a passenger jet.

Why does the Earth not fly off into space? Mrs Nemakonde ties a ball to a string and swings it around her head. The string keeps pulling the ball towards her hand, so it moves in a circle. If she lets go, the ball flies off in a straight line. For the Earth, there is no string. Instead, the Sun's gravity pulls on the Earth all the time and keeps it in its orbit.

All eight planets revolve around the Sun, in the same direction, and so do the asteroids, the dwarf planets and the comets.

The questions for this section are with you now: revolution, orbits and gravity.

## Subtopic: A Year of 365 and a Quarter Days

The time it takes the Earth to go once around the Sun is called a year. It takes 365¼ days, which is three hundred and sixty-five and a quarter days.

So, while it revolves once, the Earth also rotates about three hundred and sixty-five times on its axis. Mpho tries to do both at once on the court: she spins slowly on the spot while walking around the Sun, like a dancer turning around a room. Rotation gives us days, and revolution gives us years.

But a calendar cannot have a quarter of a day. So most years on our calendar have three hundred and sixty-five days, and the extra quarter day is saved up. After four years, the four quarters add up to one whole day: a quarter plus a quarter plus a quarter plus a quarter makes one. So every fourth year, an extra day is added to the end of February. That day is 29 February, and a year with three hundred and sixty-six days is called a leap year. 2024 was a leap year, and 2028 will be the next one. Without leap years, the calendar would slowly drift, and after many years, the summer holidays would fall in winter.

That is why the birthday of Mpho's mother only appears every four years. Mrs Nemakonde points out that she has still gone around the Sun once every year, so she is as old as all her years, not nine, and she can celebrate on 28 February or 1 March in other years.

Each birthday you celebrate means that you have travelled once more around the Sun.

The questions for this section are with you now: the length of a year and leap years.

## Subtopic: Other Planets' Years

Each planet has its own year: the time it takes to revolve once around the Sun. The closer a planet is to the Sun, the shorter its orbit and the faster it moves, so its year is shorter. The further away it is, the longer its year.

Mrs Nemakonde puts more learners on the court as planets. Mercury, close to the Sun, runs around a tiny circle and finishes quickly. Its year is only eighty-eight Earth days. Venus takes about two hundred and twenty-five days. Mars takes nearly two Earth years. Jupiter takes about twelve Earth years, Saturn nearly thirty, Uranus about eighty-four and Neptune about one hundred and sixty-five. If you lived on Neptune, you would never have a single birthday in your whole life.

Mrs Nemakonde also clears up a common mistake. Many people think summer happens when the Earth is closest to the Sun. In fact, the Earth is closest to the Sun in early January, when South Africa has summer, but countries in the north, like those in Europe, have winter at exactly the same time. So the distance from the Sun does not cause the seasons. The seasons are caused by the tilt of the Earth's axis.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: mixing up rotation and revolution; rotation is spinning, revolution is travelling around the Sun. Two: thinking a year is exactly three hundred and sixty-five days; it is 365¼ days, which is why we have leap years. Three: thinking all planets have the same year; further planets have longer years. Four: thinking the Earth is closest to the Sun in summer everywhere; distance does not cause the seasons.

The questions for this section are with you now: the years of other planets.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the netball court in Thohoyandou — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Around the Sun

The Earth travels around the Sun. This is revolution.

Keep Mpho walking around the centre circle in mind and try the questions for this part: around the Sun.

## Subtopic: One Year

One trip around the Sun takes 365¼ days. Every four years, we add 29 February.

Hold the leap-day birthday in mind and try this part's questions: one year.

## Subtopic: Longer Years Far Away

Further planets take longer to go around the Sun.

Pocket summary of the lesson. The planets revolve around the Sun in paths called orbits, and the Sun's gravity keeps them there. The Earth revolves once in 365¼ days, which is one year, while it also rotates about three hundred and sixty-five times. Our calendar has three hundred and sixty-five days, so every four years the extra quarter days make a leap year with 29 February and three hundred and sixty-six days. Each planet has its own year: Mercury's is eighty-eight days, and Neptune's is about one hundred and sixty-five Earth years.

Hold all the planets running on the court in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: longer years far away.
