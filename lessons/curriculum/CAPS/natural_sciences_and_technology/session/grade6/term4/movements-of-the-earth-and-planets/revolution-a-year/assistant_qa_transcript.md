### subtopic_1 — Travelling Around the Sun
**Assistant:** If the Earth is moving so fast around the Sun, why does the Sun not look like it is rushing past?

**Tutor:** Because the Sun is so far away, and the orbit is so enormous. Even though the Earth moves about thirty kilometres every second, it takes a whole year to go once around a circle more than nine hundred million kilometres long. Each day, the Earth moves only a tiny part of the way around, less than one three-hundred-and-sixtieth of the circle. So from one day to the next, the direction in which we see the Sun changes only a little. Think of a car far away on a highway. Even if it is speeding, it seems to crawl slowly across your view, because it is so distant. But the Earth's revolution does show up in the night sky. Over the year, as we travel around the Sun, we look out at different parts of space at night, so the stars we see change with the seasons. For example, in South Africa, Orion is easy to see on summer evenings, and the Scorpion is easy to see on winter evenings.

### subtopic_2 — A Year of 365 and a Quarter Days
**Assistant:** Do all years with a number that divides by four have a leap day?

**Tutor:** Almost all of them, but there is a small exception. The Earth's year is not exactly 365¼ days. It is a tiny bit shorter, by about eleven minutes. That does not sound like much, but adding a leap day every four years makes the calendar a little too long, and after about four hundred years it would be three days out. So the people who planned our calendar made a rule: years at the start of a new century, like 1900 and 2100, are not leap years, unless the number can be divided by four hundred, like 2000. That is why 2000 was a leap year, but 1900 was not, and 2100 will not be. For the years that you and your parents will live through, the simple rule works: every fourth year, like 2024 and 2028, is a leap year. It is amazing that such a small difference was noticed hundreds of years ago, by careful observers of the sky.

### subtopic_3 — Other Planets' Years
**Assistant:** Why do we have seasons, if it is not because of distance from the Sun?

**Tutor:** Seasons happen because the Earth's axis is tilted. Imagine the Earth leaning a little to one side, like a tired person, and always leaning the same way as it travels around the Sun. For part of the year, the southern half of the Earth, where South Africa is, leans towards the Sun. The Sun climbs high in our sky at midday, its rays hit the ground more directly, and the days are longer, so we get more heat. That is our summer, from about December to February. Half a year later, the Earth is on the other side of its orbit, and the southern half leans away from the Sun. The Sun stays lower in the sky, its rays are more spread out, and the days are shorter, so it is colder. That is our winter, from about June to August. At the same time, the northern half has the opposite season. So when we are having Christmas in the summer sun, people in Europe are having Christmas in winter.
