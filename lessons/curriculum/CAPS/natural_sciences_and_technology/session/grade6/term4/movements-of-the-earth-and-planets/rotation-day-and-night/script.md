# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the rotation of the Earth and the planets, and how it causes day and night. The anchor is a Grade 6 class in Kokstad, a town in the green hills of southern KwaZulu-Natal, near Mount Currie. One morning, a learner called Ayanda tells the class a puzzle. Last night, at about seven o'clock, as she was getting ready for bed, she video-called her cousin, who lives in New York in the United States. Her cousin was in bright sunshine, eating lunch. How can it be night in Kokstad and the middle of the day in New York at the same time? Their teacher, Mr Ngcobo, closes the curtains, switches off the lights, and brings out a globe on a stand and a strong torch. The big idea is that every planet spins, or rotates, on its axis, that the Earth rotates once in about twenty-four hours, and that this rotation causes day and night, and makes the Sun seem to move across our sky. Rotation, day and night are examinable.

## Subtopic: Spinning on an Axis

Mr Ngcobo points to the metal rod that runs through the globe, from the top to the bottom. On the real Earth, there is no rod. Instead, there is an imaginary line through the middle of the Earth, from the North Pole to the South Pole. This line is called the axis.

The Earth spins around its axis, like a top or a soccer ball spinning on a finger. This spinning movement is called rotation. The Earth rotates once in about twenty-four hours, which is one day.

The Earth spins from west to east. Seen from above the South Pole, it turns clockwise; seen from above the North Pole, it turns anticlockwise. Mr Ngcobo spins the globe slowly in that direction, so that Kokstad moves towards the east.

The Earth spins quite fast. A point on the surface in South Africa travels at more than a thousand kilometres an hour. We do not feel it because everything around us, including the ground, the air, the buildings and the oceans, moves along with us smoothly, just as you do not feel a smooth aeroplane flight when you are sitting still inside it.

Every planet rotates on its axis, but they spin at different speeds. Jupiter spins once in less than ten hours, so its day is very short. Mars takes a little more than twenty-four hours, almost the same as Earth. Venus spins so slowly that one turn takes longer than its whole year.

The questions for this section are with you now: the axis and the rotation of the Earth and the planets.

## Subtopic: Day and Night

Now Mr Ngcobo shines the torch at the globe. The torch is the Sun. The half of the globe facing the torch is bright, and the half facing away is dark. The Sun can only light up one half of the Earth at a time. On the lit half, it is day. On the dark half, it is night.

He sticks a small sticker on Kokstad and slowly turns the globe. The sticker moves out of the dark half, across the line into the light, which is sunrise. It crosses the middle of the lit half, which is midday. Then it moves across the line into the dark again, which is sunset, and through the dark half, which is night. One full turn gives one day and one night, in about twenty-four hours.

Then he sticks another sticker on New York. When the Kokstad sticker is just moving into the dark, the New York sticker is still in the middle of the light. That solves Ayanda's puzzle. Because the Earth is round and spinning, different places have day and night at different times. When it is evening in South Africa, it is around midday in New York, and it is the middle of the night in Australia. That is why countries set their clocks to different times.

Day and night are caused by the Earth spinning on its axis, not by the Sun moving around the Earth, and not by the Sun switching off at night.

The questions for this section are with you now: how rotation causes day and night in different places.

## Subtopic: The Sun's Path Across the Sky

From the ground, it looks as if the Sun moves across the sky. It rises in the east in the morning, climbs high in the sky at midday, and sets in the west in the evening. But the Sun is not really moving across our sky. It only seems to move because the Earth is turning. As Kokstad spins towards the east, the Sun comes into view on the eastern horizon, and later disappears behind the western horizon. It is like sitting in a taxi: the trees seem to move backwards, but it is really you who is moving.

The class makes a shadow stick by pushing a stick upright into the ground and marking the end of its shadow every hour. In the morning, the shadow is long and points west. At midday, it is shortest, and in South Africa it points south, because the midday Sun is to the north. In the afternoon, the shadow grows long again and points east. The shadows move because the Earth is rotating. Some people say the Sun rises exactly in the east and sets exactly in the west, but that is only true on two days of the year. In summer, it rises a little south of east, and in winter a little north of east. It is still always on the eastern side in the morning and the western side in the evening.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking the Sun moves around the Earth every day; the Earth spins. Two: thinking the Sun switches off at night; it is always shining, on the other side of the Earth. Three: thinking it is day everywhere at the same time; only the half facing the Sun has day. Four: thinking all planets have twenty-four-hour days; each planet spins at its own speed.

The questions for this section are with you now: the Sun's path and shadows.

The Sun rises in the east and sets in the west, because the Earth turns.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Every planet spins, or rotates, on its axis, an imaginary line through its North and South Poles. The Earth rotates from west to east once in about twenty-four hours. Only the half of the Earth facing the Sun has day; the other half has night. As the Earth turns, each place moves from night to day and back again, so different countries have day and night at different times. The Sun seems to rise in the east and set in the west, and shadows change during the day, because the Earth is rotating. Other planets rotate at different speeds.
