# Part 1 — Expert

This session revises the Term 4 topics of Planet Earth and Beyond and Systems and Control: the Solar System; the movements of the Earth and planets; the movement of the Moon; systems for looking into space; and systems to explore the Moon and Mars. The anchor is a Grade 6 class in Khayelitsha, in Cape Town, where Table Mountain can be seen across the Cape Flats. For their end-of-year Space Fair, the class turns the school hall into three stations. At the first, a yellow beach ball hangs from the ceiling, with eight painted balls of different sizes around it, and a big poster of the Moon. At the second, there is a globe, a lamp, a chair and a calendar with 29 February circled. At the third, there are a cardboard telescope, photographs of SALT and MeerKAT, a model Moon buggy, and a six-wheeled rover made from recycled bottles. Families and other classes visit, and the Grade 6 learners guide them. The big idea of the term is that the Sun is the star at the centre of our Solar System, that the Earth rotates to give day and night and revolves to give a year, that the Moon rotates and revolves as it travels with the Earth around the Sun, and that people use systems like telescopes and rovers to explore space. The whole of Term 4 is examinable.

## Subtopic: The Solar System and Our Moon

At the first station, Anele points to the beach ball. The Sun is a star, a huge ball of hot, glowing gas that gives out light and heat. It is at the centre of our Solar System, and its gravity keeps eight planets moving around it in orbits. In order from the Sun, they are Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune. The four inner planets are small and rocky. The four outer planets are huge gas giants, with rings and many moons. Between Mars and Jupiter lies the asteroid belt, a ring of millions of rocky lumps that also orbit the Sun.

Each planet has its own features. Mercury is the smallest and has no moons. Venus is the hottest, because its thick clouds trap heat, and it also has no moons. Earth has water, air, life and one Moon. Mars is red and cold, with two small moons. Jupiter is the biggest, with the Great Red Spot. Saturn has bright rings. Uranus is tipped on its side, and Neptune is the furthest and windiest.

Then Anele turns to the Moon poster. Moons do not give out their own light. The Moon shines because it reflects sunlight. It is a rocky ball with no air, water or weather. Its surface has craters, which are bowl-shaped holes made by rocks from space; mountains, which are high and rough; and plains, which are flat, dark areas of hardened lava.

The questions for this section are with you now: the Sun, the planets, the asteroid belt and our Moon.

## Subtopic: How the Earth and Moon Move

At the second station, Zola switches on the lamp and spins the globe. Every planet rotates, or spins, on its axis, an imaginary line through its North and South Poles. The Earth rotates from west to east once in about twenty-four hours. The half facing the Sun has day, and the half facing away has night. As the Earth turns, the Sun seems to rise in the east and set in the west.

Then Zola carries the globe around the lamp. Every planet also revolves, or travels around the Sun, in an orbit. The Earth takes 365¼ days to revolve once, which is one year. Our calendar has three hundred and sixty-five days, so every four years the four quarter days add up to one extra day, 29 February, in a leap year. Planets further from the Sun have longer years.

Next, Zola's friend Ayabonga walks around the chair, always facing it, to show the Moon. The Moon revolves around the Earth in about a month. It rotates once in about twenty-eight days, the same time, so the same side always faces the Earth. As it moves, we see different amounts of its sunlit half, which gives us the phases. And the Earth and the Moon revolve around the Sun together.

The questions for this section are with you now: rotation, revolution and the movements of the Moon.

## Subtopic: Exploring Space

At the third station, Kayla explains that telescopes are systems for looking into space. They collect much more light than our eyes, so faint objects become visible, and they magnify them. Refracting telescopes use lenses, and reflecting telescopes use curved mirrors. Radio telescopes use dishes to collect radio waves. South Africa has some of the largest telescopes in the world: SALT, near Sutherland, is the biggest single light telescope in the southern half of the world, with a mirror of ninety-one pieces, and MeerKAT, near Carnarvon, is a radio telescope of sixty-four dishes, which is becoming part of the SKA. They are built in the Karoo because it is dark, dry and radio quiet.

Then Kayla shows the model Moon buggy. Twelve astronauts walked on the Moon between 1969 and 1972. On the last three missions, they drove the Lunar Roving Vehicle, which used batteries because there is no air for petrol to burn, had wire-mesh wheels that could not go flat, and folded up to fit on the lander.

Finally, the bottle rover. No person has been to Mars. Robotic rovers, such as Sojourner, Opportunity, Curiosity and Perseverance, have explored it. Signals take minutes to travel, so the rovers carry out instructions on their own. They have six wheels, cameras, robotic arms and solar or nuclear power, and they found signs that Mars once had rivers and lakes.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking the Moon makes its own light; it reflects sunlight. Two: thinking the Sun moves around the Earth each day; the Earth rotates. Three: mixing up rotation and revolution; rotation is spinning, revolution is travelling around. Four: thinking people have driven on Mars; only robots have explored it.

The questions for this section are with you now: telescopes, the Moon rover and Mars rovers.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the Space Fair in Khayelitsha — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Sun, Planets and Moon

The Sun is a star with eight planets. The Moon reflects sunlight.

Keep the hanging beach ball in mind and try the questions for this part: Sun, planets and Moon.

## Subtopic: Spin and Travel

Spin once: one day. Travel around the Sun once: one year.

Hold the lamp and the globe in mind and try this part's questions: spin and travel.

## Subtopic: Looking and Exploring

Telescopes catch light. Rovers explore the Moon and Mars.

Pocket summary of the term. The Sun is a star at the centre of our Solar System, with eight planets and the asteroid belt in orbit around it: four rocky inner planets and four gas giants. Moons do not make their own light, and our Moon has craters, mountains and plains. The Earth rotates in about twenty-four hours, giving day and night, and revolves around the Sun in 365¼ days, giving a year. The Moon rotates and revolves in about a month, and travels around the Sun with the Earth. Telescopes, including SALT and MeerKAT, collect light and radio waves from space. The Moon buggy and Mars rovers are systems for exploring other worlds.

Hold all three Space Fair stations in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: looking and exploring.
