# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers our Moon: why it shines, and the features on its surface. The anchor is a Grade 6 class in Giyani, a town in Limpopo surrounded by mopane trees and baobabs. Their teacher, Mr Baloyi, invites the class and their families to the school field on the evening of a full moon. The Moon rises over the trees, huge and orange at first, then climbs higher and turns silvery white. It is so bright that the learners can see their own shadows on the grass. One learner, Tsakani, says the Moon must be a lamp in the sky. Mr Baloyi smiles and passes around a borrowed pair of binoculars. Through them, the learners see that the Moon is not smooth at all. It is covered in round holes, bright rough patches and smooth dark patches. The big idea is that moons do not give out their own light, but reflect light from the Sun, and that our Moon is a rocky, airless world with craters, mountains and plains on its surface. Moons, moonlight and the features of our Moon are examinable.

## Subtopic: Moons Reflect Sunlight

A moon is a natural object that moves around a planet. Our Moon moves around the Earth. It is a ball of rock, about a quarter as wide as the Earth, and about three hundred and eighty-four thousand kilometres away. About fifty Moons could fit inside the Earth.

To answer Tsakani, Mr Baloyi does a demonstration. He switches on his torch and asks a learner to hold up a white soccer ball in the dark. Where the torch shines on the ball, it lights up brightly. The side facing away stays dark. The ball does not make any light of its own. It only bounces back, or reflects, the torch's light.

The Moon works in the same way. The Sun is the torch, and the Moon is the ball. Moonlight is really sunlight that has bounced off the Moon's grey rocks and travelled to our eyes. Half of the Moon is always lit by the Sun, and the other half is dark. On the night of a full moon, we can see the whole sunlit half.

This is true for all moons. The planets also shine only by reflected sunlight. Only stars, like the Sun, give out their own light.

The Moon has no air, so there is no wind, no rain, no clouds and no sound. Its sky is black, even in the daytime. There is no liquid water and no life. Because there is no air to spread heat around, the Moon's surface is hotter than boiling water in the day and far colder than a freezer at night. Its gravity is about one sixth of Earth's, so a learner who weighs thirty-six kilograms on Earth would feel as light as a six-kilogram bag of maize meal does on Earth.

The questions for this section are with you now: moons, moonlight and conditions on the Moon.

## Subtopic: Craters

The round holes the learners see are craters. A crater is a bowl-shaped hole made when a rock from space, called a meteoroid, smashes into the surface. The crash is so powerful that it blasts out a hole much bigger than the rock itself, and throws rock and dust around the hole, building a raised rim.

The Moon has many thousands of craters. Some are smaller than a coin, and others are hundreds of kilometres across. Some big craters have bright streaks, called rays, spreading out from them, made of rock thrown out by the crash. Near the bottom of the full Moon, the learners can see one crater, called Tycho, with rays spreading across much of the Moon.

Mr Baloyi asks why the Moon has so many craters and the Earth so few. Rocks from space hit the Earth too, but most burn up in our air before they reach the ground. The few craters that do form on Earth are slowly worn away by wind, rain, rivers and plants. South Africa has one of the biggest and oldest, the Vredefort crater in the Free State, but it is so worn down that its full round shape can only be seen from the air or on a map. The Moon has no air to burn up the rocks and no weather to wear its craters away, so they stay for billions of years. For the same reason, the footprints left by astronauts could last for millions of years.

The questions for this section are with you now: craters and why they last on the Moon.

## Subtopic: Mountains and Plains

The Moon has two other kinds of features.

Mountains are high, rough areas of rock. Some form long ranges, and many stand around the rims of big craters. Some Moon mountains are several kilometres high, higher than any mountain in South Africa. The bright, rough parts of the Moon, called the highlands, are full of mountains and craters.

Plains are huge, flat, smooth areas. They are the dark patches the learners can see without binoculars. Long ago, people thought these were seas of water, so they named them seas, such as the Sea of Tranquillity, where the first astronauts landed. But there is no water in them. They are flat areas of dark rock that formed when hot, melted rock, called lava, flooded into very large basins and hardened, billions of years ago. Plains have fewer craters than the highlands.

People around the world see pictures in the dark plains. Some see a face, others see a rabbit.

The Moon also always shows the same face to the Earth, so the patterns the learners see in Giyani are the same ones their grandparents saw.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking the Moon makes its own light; it reflects sunlight. Two: thinking the dark patches are seas of water; they are plains of hardened lava. Three: thinking craters were made by volcanoes; most were made by rocks from space crashing into the Moon. Four: thinking the Moon has air and weather like Earth; it has no air, no wind and no rain.

The questions for this section are with you now: mountains, plains and the patterns on the Moon.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the full moon over Giyani — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Borrowed Light

The Moon has no light of its own. It reflects sunlight.

Keep the torch and the soccer ball in mind and try the questions for this part: borrowed light.

## Subtopic: Holes from Space

Craters are bowl-shaped holes made by rocks from space.

No air and no rain means they stay.

Hold the view through the binoculars in mind and try this part's questions: holes from space.

## Subtopic: High and Flat

Mountains are high and rough. Plains are flat and dark.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A moon is a natural object that moves around a planet. Moons do not give out their own light; they reflect light from the Sun, so moonlight is reflected sunlight. Our Moon is a rocky ball with no air, no water, no weather and no life. Its surface has craters, which are bowl-shaped holes made by rocks from space; mountains, which are high, rough areas; and plains, which are flat, dark areas of hardened lava. With no air and no weather, its craters last for billions of years.

Hold the silver Moon above the baobabs in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: high and flat.
