### subtopic_1 — Moons Reflect Sunlight
**Assistant:** Why does the Moon look bigger and orange when it is rising?

**Tutor:** That is something many people notice. The orange colour is real. When the Moon is low near the horizon, its light has to travel through much more of the Earth's air to reach us than when it is high in the sky. The air scatters away a lot of the blue light, and dust and smoke in the air add to this, so the light that gets through looks orange or red. It is the same reason sunsets are orange. As the Moon climbs higher, its light passes through less air, and it looks white again. The bigger size, though, is mostly a trick of our brains. If you measure the Moon with a coin held at arm's length when it is rising, and again later when it is high, it is almost exactly the same size. Scientists think the trick happens because we see the Moon near trees, hills and buildings on the horizon, and our brains compare it with them, so it seems huge.

### subtopic_2 — Craters
**Assistant:** Is there really a dark side of the Moon?

**Tutor:** Not really, although people often say so. The Moon always keeps the same face turned towards the Earth, so there is a side that we never see from here. People sometimes call it the dark side, but it is better called the far side, because it is not always dark. The Moon spins slowly, so every part of it gets daytime and night-time, just like the Earth. In fact, when we see a new moon and our side looks dark, the far side is fully lit by the Sun. Nobody saw the far side until spacecraft flew around the Moon and photographed it. The pictures showed that it is covered in even more craters, with far fewer dark plains. In recent years, a robot lander and rover from China have even landed on the far side and explored it. So the far side is a real place we cannot see from Earth, but it is not always dark.

### subtopic_3 — Mountains and Plains
**Assistant:** Do other planets have moons like ours?

**Tutor:** Many of them do, and some are amazing. Mercury and Venus have no moons, and Mars has two tiny, lumpy moons, each smaller than a large city. The gas giants have dozens of moons each, and they are very different from each other. Jupiter's moon Ganymede is the biggest moon in the Solar System, even bigger than the planet Mercury. Another of Jupiter's moons, Io, has hundreds of active volcanoes. Europa, also a moon of Jupiter, is covered in a thick crust of ice, and scientists think there is a deep ocean of salty water underneath it. Saturn's biggest moon, Titan, has thick air and lakes of a liquid that is not water. But all these moons have one thing in common with ours: none of them gives out its own light. They all shine only because sunlight bounces off them. That is why moons are much harder to see than stars.
