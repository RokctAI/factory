### subtopic_1 — The Sun at the Centre
**Assistant:** If the Sun is a star, why can we not see it at night?

**Tutor:** That is a great question. The Sun is always shining, day and night. We cannot see it at night because our side of the Earth has turned away from it. The Earth spins like a ball on a stick, and as it turns, we move from the side facing the Sun, where it is day, to the side facing away, where it is night. At night, the Sun is still shining on the other side of the world, for example on countries like Mexico and the United States. And then, at night, we can see the other stars, which are far-away suns. During the day they are still there, but the Sun's light fills our sky with brightness, so the faint light of the other stars is drowned out. It is a bit like trying to see a torch in a brightly lit room. That is why the night sky is so full of stars in places like Kuruman, far from bright city lights.

### subtopic_2 — Eight Planets in Order
**Assistant:** Why is Pluto no longer called a planet?

**Tutor:** When Pluto was discovered in 1930, it was called the ninth planet. But over the years, scientists found that it is very small, smaller even than our Moon. They also found many other icy objects in the same part of space, far beyond Neptune, and some of them are almost as big as Pluto. In 2006, a large meeting of astronomers agreed on what a planet must be. A planet must orbit the Sun, it must be big enough for its own gravity to pull it into a round shape, and it must have cleared its path around the Sun of other objects, by pulling them in or pushing them away. Pluto does the first two things, but not the third, because it shares its path with many other icy bodies. So Pluto was called a dwarf planet instead. Ceres, the biggest object in the asteroid belt, is also a dwarf planet. Pluto has not changed at all; we have simply learnt more about it.

### subtopic_3 — The Asteroid Belt and the Size of Space
**Assistant:** How did people find out that the Earth moves around the Sun?

**Tutor:** For a very long time, most people believed that the Earth stood still in the middle, and that the Sun, Moon and planets moved around it. After all, the Sun seems to cross our sky every day. About five hundred years ago, an astronomer called Nicolaus Copernicus suggested that the Sun is at the centre and that the Earth and planets move around it. Many people did not believe him at first. Then, about four hundred years ago, Galileo Galilei pointed one of the first telescopes at the sky. He saw four moons going around Jupiter, which showed that not everything moves around the Earth. He also saw that Venus has phases like our Moon, which fits best if Venus goes around the Sun. Later, other scientists worked out the paths of the planets and explained gravity. Today, spacecraft have travelled to every planet, and the photographs they send back show clearly how our Solar System is arranged, with the Sun at its centre.
