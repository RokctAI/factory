# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the Sun at the centre of our Solar System, the eight planets and the asteroid belt. The anchor is a Grade 6 class in Kuruman, a town in the Northern Cape known for its clear night skies and the Eye of Kuruman spring. Their teacher, Mr Mothibi, carries a big yellow beach ball onto the school field and puts it down at one end. This is the Sun, he says. Then he hands out tiny objects: a grain of sand, two peppercorns, a small bead, a big marble, a smaller marble and two glass beads. These are the planets, and the learners must walk away from the beach ball to put each one where it belongs. By the time the last learner stops, she is far beyond the end of the field. The big idea is that our Solar System is made up of the Sun, which is a star at the centre, and everything that moves around it, including eight planets and the asteroid belt, and that it is very, very big. The Sun, the planets and the asteroid belt are examinable.

## Subtopic: The Sun at the Centre

The Sun is a star. It is a huge, very hot ball of glowing gas that gives out its own light and heat. It looks bigger and brighter than the other stars only because it is much closer to us. The other stars we see at night are also suns, but they are so far away that they look like tiny points of light.

The Sun is enormous. About one hundred and nine Earths could fit side by side across it, and more than a million Earths could fit inside it. That is why Mr Mothibi chose a beach ball, and a tiny peppercorn for the Earth.

The Sun is about one hundred and fifty million kilometres from Earth. Its light takes about eight minutes to reach us, even though light is the fastest thing there is. A car driving non-stop at one hundred kilometres an hour would take more than one hundred and seventy years to get there. On the field, the peppercorn for Earth sits dozens of big steps away from the beach ball.

The Sun is the centre of our Solar System. Its pull, called gravity, keeps all the planets moving around it in paths called orbits. Without the Sun's light and heat, there would be no life on Earth: plants need its light to make food, and it keeps our planet warm.

Never look straight at the Sun, not even with sunglasses, and never through binoculars or a telescope. Its light can damage your eyes forever.

The questions for this section are with you now: the Sun as a star at the centre of the Solar System.

## Subtopic: Eight Planets in Order

A planet is a large, round object that moves in an orbit around a star. Unlike the Sun, planets do not give out their own light. We see them because they reflect sunlight.

Our Solar System has eight planets. In order from the Sun, they are Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune. The class learns a sentence to remember the order: My Very Easy Method Just Speeds Up Naming. The first letter of each word is the first letter of a planet.

The first four planets, Mercury, Venus, Earth and Mars, are called the inner planets. They are fairly small and are made of rock and metal, so they are called rocky planets. The last four, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune, are the outer planets. They are huge and made mostly of gas, so they are called gas giants. Jupiter is the biggest planet of all.

Pluto was once called the ninth planet, but in 2006 scientists decided to call it a dwarf planet, because it is very small and shares its part of space with many other icy objects.

All the planets move around the Sun in the same direction. Planets closer to the Sun move faster and have shorter paths, so they take less time to go around once. Mercury, the closest, goes around in less than three Earth months, while Neptune, the furthest, takes about one hundred and sixty-five Earth years.

The questions for this section are with you now: the eight planets, their order, and rocky and gas planets.

## Subtopic: The Asteroid Belt and the Size of Space

Between Mars and Jupiter there is a wide ring of space filled with millions of rocky and metal lumps called asteroids. This is the asteroid belt. Some asteroids are as small as pebbles, and others are hundreds of kilometres across. The biggest one, Ceres, is so large that it is also called a dwarf planet. The asteroids also orbit the Sun. Even though there are millions of them, they are very far apart, and spacecraft have flown safely through the belt.

On the field, the asteroid belt sits between the small bead for Mars and the big marble for Jupiter, and it is where the gaps between the planets suddenly get much bigger. The inner planets are close together near the beach ball, but the outer planets are spread far apart. Neptune is about thirty times further from the Sun than Earth is.

The Solar System also contains moons, which orbit planets, and comets, which are balls of ice and dust that sometimes grow a glowing tail when they come close to the Sun.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking the Sun is not a star; it is a star, just close to us. Two: thinking planets make their own light; they reflect sunlight. Three: thinking the Sun moves around the Earth; the Earth and the other planets move around the Sun. Four: thinking the asteroid belt is so crowded that rocks bump into each other all the time; the asteroids are very far apart.

The questions for this section are with you now: the asteroid belt and the size of the Solar System.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the model on the Kuruman school field — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Our Star

The Sun is a star. It gives light and heat.

It is in the middle of the Solar System.

Keep the beach ball in mind and try the questions for this part: our star.

## Subtopic: Eight Planets

Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune.

Four small rocky planets, then four gas giants.

Hold the walk across the field in mind and try this part's questions: eight planets.

## Subtopic: The Belt of Rocks

Asteroids orbit the Sun between Mars and Jupiter.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Our Solar System is the Sun and everything that orbits it. The Sun is a star, a huge ball of hot, glowing gas that gives out light and heat, and its gravity keeps the planets in their orbits. There are eight planets: Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune. The four inner planets are rocky, and the four outer planets are gas giants. Planets do not make their own light. The asteroid belt, a ring of millions of rocky lumps, lies between Mars and Jupiter. The Solar System is enormous.

Hold the long walk to Neptune in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: the belt of rocks.
