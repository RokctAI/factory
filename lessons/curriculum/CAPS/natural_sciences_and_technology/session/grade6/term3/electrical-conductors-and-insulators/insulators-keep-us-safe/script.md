# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers electrical insulators: materials that do not let electricity flow through them. The anchor is a Grade 6 class in Ermelo in Mpumalanga, a farming and coal town with high-voltage power lines striding across the grasslands towards the power stations nearby. A power line technician, Ms Shabangu, visits the class. She arrives in her work clothes: thick rubber gloves up to her elbows, rubber-soled boots, a hard hat, and a tool belt with pliers and screwdrivers that have thick plastic handles. She puts a few things on the desk: a string of ridged glass discs from a pylon, a plug that has been cut in half to show the inside, and a piece of cable with its plastic coat partly stripped off. She asks the class what all these things have in common. The big idea is that insulators are materials that do not let electricity flow through them easily, that most non-metals, especially plastics, are insulators, and that insulators are used to keep electricity on its path and keep people safe. Insulators and their uses are examinable.

## Subtopic: Materials That Stop Electricity

In the last lesson, the class used a tester circuit with a gap. When they clipped in a plastic ruler, a rubber eraser, a piece of glass, wood, cloth or paper, the bulb did not light. These materials did not let electricity flow through them.

Ms Shabangu asks the class to test a few more things: a plastic straw, a rubber band, a china plate, a wooden clothes peg and a dry leaf. None of them lights the bulb. Then she gives them a paper clip coated in plastic. The bulb stays off, until they scrape a little plastic away from each end so that the clips touch the metal. Then it lights.

A material that does not allow electricity to flow through it easily is called an electrical insulator.

Most non-metals are insulators. Plastics are the most widely used, because they are cheap, light, waterproof and easy to shape. Rubber, glass, ceramic, dry wood, paper, cloth and leather are also insulators. So is dry air: that is why the gap in an open switch stops the electricity.

Ms Shabangu explains that in insulators, the tiny charged bits inside the material are held tightly in place and cannot move along, so electricity cannot flow through them. In metals, some charged bits are free to move, which is why metals conduct.

She adds a warning. Some insulators can let electricity through if they are wet or dirty, because water with dissolved substances conducts. Wet wood, a damp cloth or a dirty, wet plug can be dangerous. And if the push of electricity is strong enough, it can even break through air, as a lightning bolt does in a storm.

The questions for this section are with you now: what insulators are and examples of insulating materials.

## Subtopic: Insulators Keep Us Safe

Ms Shabangu points to the cut plug. Inside are three metal pins and copper wires, which are conductors, because electricity must flow through them. Everything around them is plastic: the cover of the plug, the coat of each wire, and the outer sheath of the cable. The plastic keeps the electricity on its path, inside the copper, and stops it from flowing into your hand.

Insulators are used everywhere electricity is used:

The plastic coat around every electrical wire and cable.

The plastic covers of plugs, sockets, light switches and appliances like kettles and hair dryers.

The plastic or rubber handles on electricians' screwdrivers and pliers.

Rubber gloves and boots, which protect electricians and technicians when they work.

The glass or ceramic insulators that hold the power lines on pylons. The power lines themselves are bare metal, without plastic, because they are high in the air, where nobody can touch them, and air is an insulator. The ridged discs stop the electricity from flowing down the steel pylon into the ground.

Insulators are hidden inside appliances too. In a toaster, the hot wires are wound on sheets of a heat-resistant insulating material, so that the electricity cannot reach the metal case.

A good design uses conductors where electricity must flow, and insulators where it must not. A kettle has a metal element inside to carry electricity and heat the water, and a plastic body and handle so that you can hold it safely.

The questions for this section are with you now: how insulators are used to keep us safe.

## Subtopic: When Insulation Fails

Ms Shabangu now shows the piece of cable with its plastic partly stripped off. The bare copper is exposed. If a live cable looked like this, anyone who touched it could get an electric shock.

Insulation can be damaged in many ways: an old cable cracks in the sun, a chair leg or a door pinches it, a dog chews it, or someone pulls a plug out by its cord. Damaged insulation is dangerous, and it can also cause sparks and fires.

So Ms Shabangu gives the class some rules:

Never use an appliance with a cracked plug or a frayed, damaged cord. Tell an adult so that it can be repaired by a qualified person.

Never cover damaged cords with sticky tape and keep using them.

Pull a plug out by holding the plug, not by pulling the cord.

Never touch plugs, switches or appliances with wet hands, or use them near water.

Never touch or climb on pylons, and stay far away from fallen power lines, even if they look dead.

Never steal or play with cables. People have died trying to cut live cables.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking power lines are covered in plastic; they are bare metal held up by glass or ceramic insulators. Two: thinking an insulator can never let electricity through; wet or dirty insulators, and very strong electricity, can. Three: thinking taped-up cables are safe; damaged cords must be repaired properly. Four: thinking metal handles are fine for electrical tools; tool handles must be insulated.

The questions for this section are with you now: damaged insulation and how to stay safe.
