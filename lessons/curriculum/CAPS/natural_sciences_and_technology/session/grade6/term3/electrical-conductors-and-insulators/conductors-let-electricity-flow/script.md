# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers electrical conductors: materials that let electricity flow through them. The anchor is a Grade 6 class in Phalaborwa in Limpopo, a town that grew up next to a huge copper mine, where a giant hole was dug into the earth to reach copper ore. Many of the learners' parents work at the mine or in businesses that serve it. Their teacher, Mr Mathebula, holds up a short piece of thick electrical cable, strips back the plastic, and shows the bright orange-brown copper strands inside. He says that copper from mines like this one ends up in the wiring of homes, cars and cell phones all over the world. Then he asks: why copper? Why not wood, or plastic, or glass? To find out, the class builds a tester circuit. The big idea is that some materials, called conductors, let electricity flow through them easily, that most metals are good conductors, and that copper is one of the best and most useful. Conductors are examinable.

## Subtopic: Testing Materials

Each group builds a circuit with a cell, a bulb and three wires. They leave a gap between two of the wires, with a crocodile clip on each end. If the two clips touch each other, the circuit is complete and the bulb lights. Now they can test any object by clipping it into the gap. If the bulb lights, the object let electricity flow through it, so it completed the circuit. If the bulb stays off, the object did not let electricity through.

They test a collection of objects and record the results in a table.

A steel nail: the bulb lights. A coin: the bulb lights. A piece of copper wire: the bulb lights. An aluminium foil strip: the bulb lights. A steel spoon: the bulb lights. A brass key: the bulb lights. A steel paper clip: the bulb lights.

A plastic ruler: no light. A wooden pencil, clipped to the painted wood: no light. A rubber eraser: no light. A piece of glass: no light. A piece of cloth: no light. A piece of paper: no light.

Mr Mathebula reminds them to test each object twice, and to check that the clips grip the object firmly, because a loose clip can make a conductor look as if it does not conduct. Good scientists repeat their tests.

The materials that made the bulb light are called electrical conductors. An electrical conductor is a material that allows electricity to flow through it easily.

The questions for this section are with you now: how to test whether a material is a conductor.

## Subtopic: Most Metals Conduct

The class looks at their table. Every object that lit the bulb was made of metal: steel, which is mostly iron, copper, aluminium and brass, which is a mixture of copper and zinc. None of the plastic, wooden, rubber, glass, cloth or paper objects did.

Most metals are good electrical conductors. This is one of the properties of metals, along with being shiny when polished, strong and good at conducting heat. Gold, silver, copper, aluminium, iron, tin and zinc all conduct electricity.

Some metals conduct better than others. Silver is the very best conductor of all, but it is far too expensive to use for ordinary wires. Copper is almost as good, and much cheaper. Gold is a good conductor that does not rust or tarnish, so very thin layers of gold are used on the tiny contacts inside cell phones and computers.

Mr Mathebula shows one surprise. He sharpens a pencil at both ends and clips the crocodile clips onto the two points of grey pencil lead. The bulb glows dimly! Pencil lead is made of graphite, a form of carbon, which is a non-metal that conducts electricity. Graphite is an unusual exception. Most non-metals do not conduct.

He also warns that water with dissolved substances in it, like tap water, sea water and sweat, can conduct electricity. That is why electricity and water are a dangerous mix.

The questions for this section are with you now: why metals conduct, and some exceptions.

## Subtopic: Why Copper Is Used for Wires

Now the class can answer Mr Mathebula's question. Copper is used for most electrical wires for several reasons.

It is an excellent conductor, almost as good as silver, so electricity flows through it easily with very little energy wasted as heat.

It is ductile, which means it can be pulled into long, thin wires without breaking.

It is flexible, so the wires can be bent around corners and inside walls without snapping.

It does not rust, so the wires last for many years.

It is much cheaper than silver or gold.

Look closely at the cable of a kettle or a cell phone charger, and you may see that the copper inside is made of many very thin strands twisted together, instead of one thick wire. This makes the cable even more flexible, so it can be bent thousands of times without breaking.

Copper is used in the wiring of houses, in the cables of chargers and appliances, in electric motors and in the circuits of cars and computers. For the long, high power lines that hang from pylons across the country, aluminium is often used instead, because it is much lighter than copper and cheaper, and still a good conductor.

Because copper is valuable, criminals sometimes steal copper cables from railway lines, streetlights and power lines to sell them. This cable theft causes power cuts and train delays, and costs the country a lot of money. It is also extremely dangerous, and people have been killed trying to cut live cables.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking all materials conduct electricity; only some do. Two: thinking only copper conducts; most metals conduct. Three: thinking all non-metals are insulators; graphite in pencil lead conducts. Four: thinking water is safe near electricity; water with dissolved substances can conduct.

The questions for this section are with you now: why copper is used for wires and aluminium for power lines.
