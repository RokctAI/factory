### subtopic_1 — Materials That Stop Electricity
**Assistant:** If air is an insulator, how does lightning get through it?

**Tutor:** Air is a good insulator, but every insulator has a limit. If the push of electricity becomes strong enough, it can force its way through even an insulator. In a thunderstorm, the rubbing of ice and water inside the clouds builds up a huge amount of electrical charge, with an enormous push, many millions of times stronger than mains electricity. When the push becomes too great, it breaks down the air in a narrow path, heating it to thousands of degrees, which is the bright flash we see, and the air expands with a bang, which is the thunder we hear. The same thing happens on a tiny scale when you see a small spark as a plug is pulled out, or feel a small shock after walking on a carpet. Very high-voltage power lines are spaced far apart and held on long strings of insulators for the same reason: the push is so strong that the electricity could jump across a short gap of air.

### subtopic_2 — Insulators Keep Us Safe
**Assistant:** Why are power lines not covered with plastic like house wires?

**Tutor:** There are a few good reasons. First, the power lines on tall pylons carry electricity with an enormous push, so a plastic coat would have to be extremely thick to stop it, making the wires very heavy and expensive. Second, bare wires lose heat more easily to the air, and these wires get warm when they carry a lot of electricity. Third, the wires hang high above the ground, far out of reach, and air is an insulator, so it does the job of keeping the electricity in the wires. The only places where the wires are attached to anything are at the pylons, and there they hang from long strings of glass or ceramic insulators. That is why it is extremely dangerous to fly kites near power lines, climb pylons, or touch a fallen line. In towns, some power cables are buried underground, and those are covered in thick insulation, because they are close to people and to wet soil.

### subtopic_3 — When Insulation Fails
**Assistant:** Why do electricians wear rubber boots?

**Tutor:** Rubber boots add one more layer of protection. When electricity flows through a person, it needs a complete pathway, usually going into the body through the hand that touches a live wire and out through the feet into the ground. The ground is a good conductor, especially when it is damp. Rubber is an insulator, so thick rubber soles make it very hard for electricity to flow from the feet into the ground. That breaks the pathway, a bit like an open switch, and makes a dangerous shock much less likely. Rubber gloves protect the hands in the same way. Technicians who work on power lines use special gloves and boots that are tested to make sure they have no holes or cracks, because even a tiny hole can let electricity through. Even with all this protection, they always switch off and test the power first if they can. The equipment is the last line of defence, not the first.
