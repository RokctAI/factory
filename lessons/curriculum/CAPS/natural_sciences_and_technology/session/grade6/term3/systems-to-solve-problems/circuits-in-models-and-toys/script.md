# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers how electric circuits are used in models and toys, and how to design and make a model that contains a circuit. The anchor is a Grade 6 class in Makhanda in the Eastern Cape, a town that hosts a big national science festival every year. On a visit to the festival, the learners see robots that follow lines on the floor, toy cars driven by solar panels, a model wind turbine lighting up a tiny house, and a steady-hand game that buzzes loudly whenever someone's hand shakes. Back at school, their Technology teacher, Mrs Botha, gives them a challenge: in groups, design and build a model or toy that uses an electric circuit, using only cells, wires, bulbs, buzzers, small motors and recycled materials. The big idea is that the circuits in toys and models are the same simple systems we have studied, with an energy source, conducting wires, a switch and a device, and that we use the design process to build them. Circuits in models and toys, and the design process, are examinable.

## Subtopic: Circuits in Toys

Mrs Botha empties a box of old toys onto the desk and asks the class to find the circuits.

A remote-control car has a battery pack, wires, a switch, and two small electric motors, one to drive the wheels and one to steer. The remote control sends signals that tell a control circuit which motor to run.

A doll that talks or sings has cells, a switch hidden in its tummy, which closes when it is squeezed, and a tiny speaker that changes electrical energy into sound.

A pair of children's shoes with flashing lights in the heels has a button cell, a tiny switch that closes when the heel hits the ground, and small LEDs.

A torch-shaped toy that plays music, a spinning top that lights up, and a toy drill with a motor all follow the same pattern.

In every toy, the energy source is usually a cell or battery, because toys must be portable and safe. Mains electricity is never used in toys, because it is far too dangerous. Some toys use rechargeable batteries or small solar panels.

So a toy is a system: input, energy from a cell; process, wires and a switch control the flow; output, light, sound or movement.

The questions for this section are with you now: the circuits inside toys.

## Subtopic: Designing a Model

Mrs Botha reminds the class of the design process that engineers and designers use. It has five steps.

Investigate. Find out about the problem and look at existing solutions. The groups look at the toys and at the festival models, and test which devices give light, sound and movement.

Design. Write a design brief, which says what you will make and for whom. Draw your ideas, including a circuit diagram using the correct symbols, and list the materials you need.

Make. Build the model safely, following your plan, and test the circuit as you go.

Evaluate. Test the finished model. Does it work? Does it meet the design brief? What could be improved?

Communicate. Present the model to the class, explaining how the circuit works.

Group 1 decides to build a model lighthouse, because their town is near the coast, where lighthouses warn ships of rocks. Their design brief: a model lighthouse with a light at the top that can be switched on and off. They draw a circuit diagram: a battery of two cells, a switch and a bulb in one loop. They use a cardboard tube from a paper towel roll, painted red and white, with a clear plastic cup at the top to protect the bulb. The wires run up inside the tube to the bulb, and the switch, made with a paper clip and two drawing pins, sits on the base.

The questions for this section are with you now: the steps of the design process.

## Subtopic: Testing and Improving

The groups build and test their models.

Group 2 builds a steady-hand game. A bare copper wire is bent into a wiggly shape and fixed at both ends into a block of wood. A small loop of wire on a handle goes around it. The bent wire, the loop, a cell and a buzzer are joined in a circuit with a gap between the loop and the bent wire. The player must move the loop from one end to the other without touching the wire. If the loop touches the wire, the gap closes, the circuit is complete, and the buzzer sounds. The loop and the bent wire act as a switch.

Group 3 builds a small cart. A motor is fixed to a cardboard base with bottle-top wheels, and a rubber band connects the motor's spindle to the back axle. The first time they test it, the motor spins but the cart does not move, because the rubber band is too loose. They evaluate, tighten the band, and test again, and the cart crawls across the floor.

When Group 1 tests the lighthouse, the bulb is too dim. They evaluate and find that a crocodile clip is loose. After fixing it, the light shines brightly. This is what evaluation is for: finding faults and improving the design.

Mrs Botha reminds everyone of the safety rules. Only use cells, never mains electricity. Never make a short circuit by joining the two terminals of a cell with a bare wire. Use scissors and craft knives carefully, with an adult's help. Remove the cells when the model is not being used.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking toys use a different kind of circuit; they use the same energy source, wires, switch and device. Two: thinking it is fine to use a plug from the wall for a model; only cells are safe. Three: skipping the circuit diagram; a diagram helps plan and fix the model. Four: thinking a model is finished as soon as it is built; it must be tested, evaluated and improved.

The questions for this section are with you now: testing, evaluating and staying safe.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Toys and models contain the same circuits we have studied: an energy source, usually cells, conducting wires, a switch and a device such as a bulb, LED, buzzer, speaker or motor. Mains electricity is never used. To build a model with a circuit, we use the design process: investigate, design with a design brief and a circuit diagram, make, evaluate and communicate. Testing and evaluating help us find faults and improve the model.
