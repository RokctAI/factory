# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers how electric circuits are used as systems to solve everyday problems. The anchor is a Grade 6 class in Durban on an evening walk with their teacher, Mr Govender, and some parents, through the streets near their school as the Sun sets. It is a hot, humid evening. As it gets dark, the street lights flicker on, one after another, without anyone touching a switch. At the corner, the traffic lights, which South Africans call robots, change from green to amber to red, and the cars stop so that people can cross. A car hoots at a house, and a tall electric gate slides open on its own. A shopkeeper locks up and presses buttons on a keypad, and a red light starts blinking above the door. Inside a café, ceiling fans turn slowly. Mr Govender asks the class to think of each of these as a problem that people solved with a circuit. The big idea is that electric circuits are systems with an input, a process and an output, designed to solve real problems, and that they are used for street lighting, traffic lights, alarms, electric gates, fans and heaters. Circuits as systems that solve problems are examinable.

## Subtopic: Lights for Safety

In Technology, we begin with a problem. Then we design a solution. Many solutions are systems that use electric circuits.

Street lights. The problem: streets are dark at night, which makes it dangerous to drive and walk, and easier for criminals. The solution: street lights. Each street light is part of a circuit, powered by mains electricity. Most have a small light sensor on top. The sensor acts like an automatic switch. When daylight fades, the sensor closes the circuit, and the light comes on. In the morning, when it gets light, the sensor opens the circuit, and the light goes off. This saves electricity and means nobody has to switch every light on and off. Input: electrical energy and a signal from the sensor. Process: the circuit is completed. Output: light.

Traffic lights, or robots. The problem: at busy crossings, cars from different directions would crash, and people could not cross safely. The solution: traffic lights. Each robot has three lights, red, amber and green, and a control box with a timer that switches each light on and off in the right order. Some robots also have sensors in the road that detect waiting cars, and buttons for people who want to cross. Output: coloured light signals that tell drivers and walkers when to stop and go. When the power goes off during load shedding, robots stop working, which shows how much we depend on them. Some now have their own batteries and solar panels for this reason.

The questions for this section are with you now: street lights and traffic lights as systems.

## Subtopic: Alarms and Electric Gates

Alarms. The problem: people want to know if someone breaks into their home, shop or car. The solution: an alarm system. Small switches and sensors are placed on doors and windows. When a door is opened, the switch changes the circuit, and the control box sets off a loud siren or buzzer and sometimes flashing lights. Some alarms use motion sensors that detect a person moving in a room. Many alarms also send a message to a security company. Input: a signal from a sensor or switch. Process: the control box checks the signal. Output: sound and light, a warning.

The class makes a simple door alarm in class later that week. They connect a cell, a buzzer and a switch made from two strips of foil. One strip is stuck to the door frame and one to the door, so that the circuit is complete only when the door opens. When someone opens the door, the buzzer sounds.

Electric gates. The problem: getting out of a car to open and close a heavy gate is slow and can be unsafe. The solution: an electric gate. A remote control sends a signal to a receiver in the gate motor's control box. The control box completes a circuit to the electric motor, which turns a toothed wheel that moves the gate along a track. Sensors stop the gate when it is fully open or closed, and some stop it if a person or car is in the way. Input: a signal from the remote. Process: the control box completes the motor circuit. Output: movement of the gate.

The questions for this section are with you now: alarms and electric gates as systems.

## Subtopic: Fans and Heaters

Fans. The problem: in hot, humid weather, like a Durban summer, people feel uncomfortable and cannot work or sleep well. The solution: an electric fan. When the fan is switched on, the circuit is complete, and electricity flows through an electric motor. The motor turns the blades, which push air along. Moving air helps sweat evaporate from our skin, which cools us. A fan's switch often has several speeds. Output: movement of air.

Heaters. The problem: in winter, especially on the Highveld and in the mountains, homes and classrooms can be very cold. The solution: an electric heater. Inside the heater, a special wire called a heating element gets very hot when electricity flows through it. It gives off heat, and sometimes a red glow. Many heaters have a thermostat, an automatic switch that turns the heater off when the room is warm enough and on again when it cools, which saves electricity. Output: heat. The same idea is used in kettles, stoves, irons and geysers.

Mr Govender sums up. Every one of these systems has an energy source, conducting wires, a switch or sensor that controls the circuit, and a device that gives a useful output: light, sound, movement or heat.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking street lights are switched on by a person each night; a light sensor does it automatically. Two: thinking a fan makes cold air; it moves air, which helps sweat evaporate and cool us. Three: thinking an electric gate opens by magic; a signal makes a control box complete the circuit to a motor. Four: thinking these systems are not circuits; they all need a complete circuit.

The questions for this section are with you now: fans and heaters as systems.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the evening walk in Durban — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Lights On

Street lights switch on by themselves when it gets dark.

Robots use red, amber and green lights to keep traffic safe.

Keep the street lights flickering on in mind and try the questions for this part: lights on.

## Subtopic: Warnings and Gates

An alarm sounds when a door switch or sensor is set off.

A gate motor opens the gate when the remote is pressed.

Hold the sliding gate in mind and try this part's questions: warnings and gates.

## Subtopic: Cool and Warm

A fan's motor moves air. A heater's element gives heat.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Electric circuits are systems that solve everyday problems. Street lights use a light sensor to switch on at dusk. Traffic lights use a timer to switch red, amber and green lights in order. Alarms use switches and sensors to set off a siren. Electric gates use a remote signal to start a motor. Fans use a motor to move air, and heaters use a hot element to give heat. Each has an input, a process and an output.

Hold the ceiling fan turning in the café in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: cool and warm.
