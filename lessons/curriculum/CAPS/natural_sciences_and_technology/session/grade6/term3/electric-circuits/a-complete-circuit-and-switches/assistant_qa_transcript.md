### subtopic_1 — A Complete Pathway
**Assistant:** Why does the electricity stop everywhere if there is only one small gap?

**Tutor:** Think of a circuit like a necklace of beads on a string that is pulled round and round by a wheel. The beads are like the tiny particles that carry electricity in the metal wire, and the cell is like the wheel that pushes them. The particles are already in every part of the wire, all the way round. When the cell pushes, all of them move round together, at the same time, like the beads on the necklace. If you cut the string anywhere, the beads on the other side of the cut cannot be pulled along, and the whole necklace stops moving. In the same way, a gap anywhere in the circuit means the particles cannot move round the loop, so they stop moving everywhere, and no energy reaches the bulb. That is why it does not matter whether the gap is near the cell or near the bulb. That is also why one switch, placed anywhere in a simple loop, can turn the whole circuit off.

### subtopic_2 — Finding the Fault
**Assistant:** Why is it dangerous to join a wire straight from one end of a cell to the other?

**Tutor:** This is called a short circuit. It is still a complete pathway, but it has no device in it to use the energy, only a short piece of wire. Wire lets electricity through very easily, so a very large amount of electricity rushes through it. The wire and the cell get hot, sometimes very hot, very quickly, and the cell goes flat. With a small torch cell, this can burn your fingers and damage the cell. With a car battery or a big rechargeable battery, a short circuit can cause sparks, melting wires and even fires. Small button cells and rechargeable batteries should never be carried loose in a pocket with keys or coins, because the metal can touch both ends and make a short circuit. In homes, a short circuit in mains wiring is very dangerous, which is why houses have circuit breakers that switch the power off if too much electricity flows. Always make sure a circuit has a device in it.

### subtopic_3 — Switches
**Assistant:** How does a two-way switch at the top and bottom of stairs work?

**Tutor:** This is a clever circuit used in many homes and schools. You want to switch the stair light on at the bottom, walk up, and switch it off at the top. With one ordinary switch, you could only do it from one place. A two-way switch has three metal contacts instead of two. Its lever always connects the middle contact to one of the other two. Two of these switches are joined by two wires between them, which give two possible pathways. If both switches connect to the same wire, there is a complete path and the light is on. If you flip either switch, it changes to the other wire, the path is broken, and the light goes off. Flip either one again, and the path is complete once more. So each switch can turn the light on or off, no matter how the other one is set. It still follows our rule: the light is on only when there is a complete, unbroken pathway.
