# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers why a circuit must be a complete, unbroken pathway, and how a switch is used to break or complete a circuit. The anchor is a Grade 6 class in Middelburg, a town in Mpumalanga. Their teacher, Ms Mahlangu, gives each pair one cell in a holder, a small bulb in a holder, three wires with crocodile clips, a block of wood, two drawing pins and a paper clip. The challenge is simple: make the bulb light up. Ms Mahlangu says nothing more, and watches. At first, there is a lot of frustration. Some pairs connect only one wire. Some connect both wires to the same end of the cell. One pair gets it right in a few seconds, and a cheer goes up. Then everyone wants to know why their own circuit did not work. The big idea is that electricity can only flow when there is a complete, unbroken pathway from one terminal of the energy source, through the device, and back to the other terminal, and that a switch opens or closes a gap in that pathway. Complete circuits and switches are examinable.

## Subtopic: A Complete Pathway

The pair that succeeded explains what they did. They clipped one wire from the positive terminal of the cell to one side of the bulb holder. They clipped a second wire from the other side of the bulb holder back to the negative terminal of the cell. The bulb glowed.

They made a complete circuit. A complete circuit is a closed, unbroken pathway that goes all the way round, from one terminal of the energy source, through the wires and the device, and back to the other terminal. The word circuit comes from the same idea as circle: it must go all the way round.

Electricity can only flow in a complete circuit. If there is a gap anywhere in the pathway, the electricity stops everywhere at once, not just at the gap. A circuit with a gap is called an open circuit or a broken circuit, and the device will not work.

The pairs whose bulbs did not light look again at their circuits. With only one wire, there was no pathway back to the cell. With both wires on the same terminal, the path did not go from one terminal to the other. When they fix their connections, all the bulbs light up.

The questions for this section are with you now: complete and open circuits.

## Subtopic: Finding the Fault

Even a circuit that looks complete may not work. Ms Mahlangu secretly makes a fault in four circuits, and the class must find it. This is called fault-finding, and electricians do it every day.

Circuit one: a crocodile clip has slipped onto the plastic covering of the wire instead of the bare metal. Plastic does not let electricity through, so there is a gap. Fix: clip onto the bare metal end.

Circuit two: the bulb is not screwed in properly, so its metal base does not touch the metal contact in the holder. Fix: screw it in firmly.

Circuit three: the bulb's thin filament is broken, so the bulb has blown. You can sometimes see the broken filament inside the glass. Fix: replace the bulb.

Circuit four: the cell is flat. All the connections are good, but the cell has no more stored energy to push the electricity round. Fix: replace the cell.

A good way to find a fault is to check one component at a time, changing only one thing, and testing after each change. Swap in a bulb that you know works. Swap in a cell that you know is good. Follow the path with your finger, checking each connection.

The questions for this section are with you now: why circuits fail and how to find the fault.

## Subtopic: Switches

Taking a clip off every time you want the bulb to go off is a nuisance. A switch does the job neatly. A switch is a component that breaks or completes a circuit, so that we can turn a device off and on.

The pairs build their own switch. They push two drawing pins into the block of wood, a few centimetres apart, with a paper clip hooked under one of them. They connect a wire to each drawing pin, so that the switch is part of the circuit. When the paper clip is swung across to touch the second drawing pin, metal touches metal, the circuit is complete, and the bulb lights. This switch is closed, or on. When the paper clip is swung away, there is a gap in the circuit, and the bulb goes out. This switch is open, or off.

Notice that the words can be confusing: a closed switch means the circuit is complete and on, and an open switch means there is a gap and the circuit is off.

Real switches work the same way inside. A light switch on the wall has metal contacts that touch or separate when you press it. A doorbell has a push switch that completes the circuit only while you press it. A torch often has a slide switch.

It does not matter where in a simple loop the switch is placed. Before or after the bulb, opening the switch makes a gap that stops the electricity everywhere in the loop.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking one wire from the cell to the bulb is enough; the pathway must return to the other terminal. Two: thinking electricity only stops at the gap; it stops in the whole loop. Three: thinking an open switch means the light is on; open means a gap, so it is off. Four: thinking a switch makes electricity; it only opens or closes the pathway.

The questions for this section are with you now: how switches break and complete a circuit.

## Subtopic: On and Off

A switch closes the gap to turn on, and opens it to turn off.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Electricity can only flow in a complete circuit: a closed, unbroken pathway from one terminal of the energy source, through the device, and back to the other terminal. A gap anywhere makes an open circuit, and the device stops working. Faults include loose connections, a clip on the plastic, a blown bulb and a flat cell. A switch breaks or completes the circuit: closed means on, open means off.
