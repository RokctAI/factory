# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the symbols used in circuit diagrams, and how to draw and read a simple circuit diagram. The anchor is a Grade 6 class in Phuthaditjhaba, in the foothills of the Maluti mountains in the Free State. A new community hall is being built next to their school, and an electrician, Mrs Moloi, is putting in the wiring. She visits the class and unrolls her plans on the teacher's desk. The learners expect pictures of lights and plugs, but instead they see neat straight lines, little circles with crosses in them, short and long lines side by side, and small breaks with lines tilted up. Mrs Moloi explains that this is a circuit diagram, a kind of map of the circuit, drawn with symbols that electricians, engineers and scientists use all over the world. The big idea is that every component in a circuit has a simple symbol, and that a circuit diagram uses these symbols to show clearly how a circuit is connected. The symbols and how to draw circuit diagrams are examinable.

## Subtopic: Why We Use Symbols

Mrs Moloi asks two learners to draw the same circuit on the board: one cell, two wires, one bulb and one switch. The first learner draws a realistic picture, with a shiny cell, curly wires and a bulb with a glowing filament. It takes five minutes and still is not clear where the wires join. The second learner uses symbols. It takes less than a minute, and everyone can see exactly how the parts are connected.

A circuit diagram is a drawing that uses symbols to show the components of a circuit and how they are connected. A symbol is a simple sign that stands for something.

Symbols have big advantages. They are quick and easy to draw. They are clear, because they leave out details that do not matter, like the colour of the wires or the shape of the cell holder. And they are the same everywhere: an electrician in Phuthaditjhaba, in Cairo or in Tokyo can read the same diagram, even if they do not speak the same language. This is why plans for houses, cars, cell phones and power stations all use circuit symbols.

The questions for this section are with you now: what a circuit diagram is and why symbols are useful.

## Subtopic: The Symbols

Mrs Moloi draws each symbol on the board and the class copies them into their books. Since we are listening, picture each one carefully.

A cell is drawn as two short lines, side by side, with a small gap between them, crossing the wire. One line is long and thin, and the other is short and thick. The long line is the positive terminal, and the short line is the negative terminal.

A battery is drawn as two or more cell symbols joined together in a row, always long line next to short line, because the cells are joined positive to negative.

A wire, or conducting lead, is drawn as a straight line.

A bulb, also called a lamp, is drawn as a small circle with a cross inside it, like the letter X in a circle.

An open switch is drawn as a gap in the wire, with a short line hinged at one side and tilted up, like a gate that is open. A closed switch is drawn with that short line lying flat across the gap, like a gate that is shut, so the line is complete.

A motor is drawn as a circle with a capital letter M inside it.

A buzzer is often drawn as a shape like a half-circle, or dome, sitting on a flat line.

The questions for this section are with you now: the symbols for a cell, battery, wire, bulb, switch, motor and buzzer.

## Subtopic: Drawing and Reading Diagrams

Mrs Moloi gives the class some rules for drawing a good circuit diagram.

Use a ruler and a sharp pencil.

Draw wires as straight lines, with square corners, so the diagram usually looks like a rectangle.

Make sure there are no gaps in the lines, except where there is an open switch, because a gap in a diagram means a gap in the circuit.

Draw each symbol in the position where that component is in the loop.

Then the class practises. First, they draw a diagram of the circuit on their desk: a battery of two cells on the left side of the rectangle, a closed switch on the top, and a bulb on the right side, with wire along the bottom. Next, they do the opposite. Mrs Moloi draws a diagram on the board with one cell, an open switch, a bulb and a motor in one loop, and the pairs must build the real circuit from it. Most pairs find it easy, because the diagram tells them exactly what to connect to what.

Finally, Mrs Moloi asks: in her board diagram, will the bulb and motor work? The learners look at the switch symbol. The gate is open, so there is a gap. The answer is no, not until the switch is closed. Reading a diagram lets you predict what a circuit will do before you build it.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking the short thick line of a cell is positive; the long thin line is positive. Two: drawing wires as wiggly lines with gaps; they should be straight lines with no gaps. Three: thinking an open switch symbol means on; the open gate means off. Four: drawing pictures instead of symbols; symbols are quicker, clearer and the same everywhere.

The questions for this section are with you now: drawing, reading and using circuit diagrams.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the electrician's plans in Phuthaditjhaba — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: A Map of the Circuit

A circuit diagram is a map of a circuit drawn with symbols.

Symbols are quick, clear and the same all over the world.

## Subtopic: Signs for the Parts

Cell: a long line and a short line. Bulb: a circle with a cross.

Switch: a gate, open or closed. Motor: a circle with an M.

Hold the symbols on the board in mind and try this part's questions: signs for the parts.

## Subtopic: Straight Lines, No Gaps

Use a ruler, straight lines and square corners.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A circuit diagram uses symbols to show the components of a circuit and how they are connected. A cell is a long thin line and a short thick line, with the long line positive. A battery is two or more cells joined. A wire is a straight line. A bulb is a circle with a cross. An open switch looks like an open gate, and a closed switch like a shut gate. A motor is a circle with an M. Draw diagrams with a ruler, straight lines, square corners and no gaps, except at an open switch.

Hold the neat rectangle of the diagram in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: straight lines, no gaps.
