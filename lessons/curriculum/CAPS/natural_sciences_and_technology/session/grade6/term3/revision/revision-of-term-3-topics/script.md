# Part 1 — Expert

This session revises the Term 3 topics of Energy and Change and Systems and Control: electric circuits; electrical conductors and insulators; systems to solve problems; and mains electricity. The anchor is a Grade 6 class in Potchefstroom, a university town on the Mooi River in North West. Their teacher, Mr Venter, has turned the classroom into an Energy Expo with three tables. On the first table there are cells, wires, bulbs, a buzzer, a small motor, a switch and a set of circuit symbol cards. On the second, there is a tester circuit beside a tray of objects to test, a cut-open electrical cord, and models made by the class: a burglar alarm, a model street light and a toy car with a motor. On the third, there is a lump of coal, a poster of a power station, a prepaid electricity meter, a box of light bulbs and a photograph of wind turbines and solar panels. A Grade 5 class visits, and the Grade 6 learners take turns to explain each table. The big idea of the term is that an electric circuit is a system that transfers energy from a source to a device, that conductors let electricity flow while insulators keep us safe, that circuits solve everyday problems, and that mains electricity is made in power stations, costs money and should be saved. The whole of Term 3 is examinable.

## Subtopic: Circuits and Symbols

At the first table, Kabelo explains that an electric circuit is a system. It has parts that work together to do a job: to transfer energy from an energy source to a device that uses it. A circuit has three main kinds of components. The energy source is a cell or a battery, which stores chemical energy. A battery is two or more cells joined together, positive terminal to negative terminal. The conducting wires carry electricity between the parts. The device, such as a bulb, a buzzer or a motor, changes electrical energy into another useful form: light from a bulb, sound from a buzzer and movement from a motor.

Kabelo connects a cell, two wires and a bulb, and the bulb lights. Then he unclips one wire, and the bulb goes out. Electricity can only flow through a complete, unbroken pathway, from one terminal of the cell, through the wires and the device, and back to the other terminal. Any gap breaks the circuit. A switch is a part that makes the gap on purpose. When the switch is closed, the circuit is complete and the bulb is on. When the switch is open, there is a gap, and the bulb is off.

Then Kabelo holds up the symbol cards. A circuit diagram uses symbols to show the parts of a circuit and how they are joined, because symbols are quick to draw, clear, and the same all over the world. A cell is a long thin line and a short thick line, and the long line is positive. A battery is two or more cell symbols in a row. A wire is a straight line. A bulb is a circle with a cross inside. A motor is a circle with the letter M. An open switch looks like an open gate, and a closed switch like a shut gate.

The questions for this section are with you now: circuits, components, switches and circuit symbols.

## Subtopic: Conductors, Insulators and Useful Systems

At the second table, Palesa uses the tester circuit, which has a gap with two crocodile clips. She places each object across the gap. A steel paper clip, an aluminium tin, a copper wire and a steel key make the bulb light, so they are conductors. A plastic ruler, a rubber band, a wooden pencil, a piece of cloth and a sheet of paper do not, so they are insulators. Most metals are good conductors, and copper is one of the best, which is why most electrical wires are made of copper. Most non-metals, such as plastic, rubber, wood, glass and cloth, are insulators.

Palesa holds up the cut-open cord. Inside there are copper wires to carry the electricity, and around each one there is a coat of plastic, an insulator, which stops the electricity from reaching our hands. Insulators keep us safe. She reminds the Grade 5s never to touch bare wires, never to put fingers or metal objects into plug sockets, never to touch switches with wet hands, and to stay far away from fallen power lines and substations.

Then Thato shows the class models. Electric circuits solve many everyday problems. Street lights make roads safer at night. Alarms warn us of burglars or fire. Electric gates open and close at the press of a button. Traffic lights control the flow of cars at busy crossings. Fans cool us in summer, and heaters warm us in winter. Each one is a system, with an energy source, conductors, a switch and a device. His burglar alarm uses a pressure switch under a doormat: stepping on the mat closes the switch and the buzzer sounds. The toy car uses a battery, a switch and a motor that turns the wheels.

The questions for this section are with you now: conductors, insulators, safety and circuits that solve problems.

## Subtopic: Mains Electricity

At the third table, Lerato holds up the lump of coal. Coal, oil and natural gas are fossil fuels. They formed over millions of years from the remains of plants and animals that died long ago and were buried, squashed and heated deep underground. Fossil fuels are non-renewable: once they are burned, they are gone, and new ones would take millions of years to form.

Using the poster, Lerato explains the power station. Coal is crushed and burned in a furnace, and the heat boils water into high-pressure steam. The steam spins a turbine, and the turbine turns a generator, where a magnet spins inside coils of copper wire and produces electricity. The energy changes from chemical energy in the coal, to heat energy in the steam, to movement energy in the turbine, to electrical energy from the generator. Power lines on tall pylons carry the electricity across the country to our homes.

Electricity costs money. The price pays for building and running power stations, mines, power lines and substations, for the fuel, and for the workers. Appliances that make heat, like geysers, heaters, kettles and stoves, use the most. We can save energy by switching off lights and appliances, using LED bulbs, boiling only the water we need, taking short showers and dressing warmly before using a heater.

Finally, Lerato points to the photograph. Electricity can also be made from renewable sources that do not run out: the wind turns wind turbines, sunlight shines on solar panels, and falling water spins turbines in hydro power stations. These cause much less pollution, but the wind does not always blow and the Sun does not shine at night.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking one wire from a cell is enough to light a bulb; a circuit must be a complete loop. Two: thinking all metals are insulators because they are hard; metals are conductors. Three: thinking the coal itself turns into electricity; it makes heat, the heat makes steam, and the steam turns the turbine and generator. Four: thinking renewable means free of any problems; renewable sources do not run out, but they do not work all the time.

The questions for this section are with you now: fossil fuels, power stations, the cost of electricity and renewable energy.

## Subtopic: Power to the Home

Coal makes steam, steam spins the turbine, and the turbine spins the generator.

Pocket summary of the term. An electric circuit is a system that transfers energy from a cell or battery, through conducting wires, to a device such as a bulb, buzzer or motor. It must be a complete, unbroken loop, and a switch opens or closes it. Circuit diagrams use symbols. Metals, especially copper, are conductors, and non-metals like plastic and rubber are insulators that keep us safe. Circuits solve everyday problems. Fossil fuels are non-renewable. In a power station, coal heats water to steam, which spins a turbine and a generator. Electricity costs money, so we save it, and renewable sources like wind, sunlight and moving water do not run out.
