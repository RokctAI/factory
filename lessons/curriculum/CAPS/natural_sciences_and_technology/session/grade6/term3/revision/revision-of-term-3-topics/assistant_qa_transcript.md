### subtopic_1 — Circuits and Symbols
**Assistant:** How do all the Term 3 topics connect to each other?

**Tutor:** They follow the journey of electrical energy. We started with the smallest system: a simple circuit with a cell, wires and a bulb. It taught us that electricity needs a complete loop and that a switch controls it. Then we asked what the wires should be made of, and found that metals like copper are conductors, while plastic and rubber are insulators that keep us safe. Next, we saw that circuits are useful systems that solve problems, like street lights, alarms and traffic lights. Finally, we zoomed out to the biggest system of all: mains electricity. Instead of a small cell, the energy source is a huge power station, where burning coal makes steam that spins a turbine and generator. Copper wires on pylons are the conductors, and the devices are the appliances in our homes. Because fossil fuels run out and electricity costs money, we learnt to save it and to use renewable sources. So the classroom circuit and the national grid follow the same rules: source, conductors and devices, in complete loops.

### subtopic_2 — Conductors, Insulators and Useful Systems
**Assistant:** Why does a bird sitting on a power line not get a shock?

**Tutor:** This is a great question, and it uses what we learnt about complete circuits. Electricity only flows through something if it is part of a complete path, from a place with high electrical push to a place with low push. When a bird sits on a single power line, both of its feet are on the same wire, so there is no difference in push between them. The electricity has an easy path straight along the thick copper or aluminium wire, so almost none goes through the bird. But if a large bird touches two wires at once with its wings, or touches a wire and the metal pylon, it becomes part of a complete circuit, and the electricity flows through its body. Sadly, this does happen, and it kills large birds like eagles and vultures in South Africa. That is why some power lines have special covers and bird perches. For people, the lesson is simple: never touch or go near power lines, and never fly kites near them, because your body can complete a circuit to the ground.

### subtopic_3 — Mains Electricity
**Assistant:** Is solar power better than coal power?

**Tutor:** Each has advantages and disadvantages, so the best answer is that we need a good mix. Solar panels use sunlight, which is renewable and free, and they do not burn anything, so they do not pollute the air while they work. South Africa has some of the sunniest places in the world, like the Northern Cape, so solar power works well here. Panels can also be put on the roofs of homes, schools and clinics, even far from power lines. But solar panels do not make electricity at night and make less on cloudy days, so the electricity must be stored in batteries, which are expensive, or another source must take over. Coal power stations can make large amounts of electricity day and night, in any weather, and South Africa has a lot of coal. But coal is non-renewable, mining it damages the land, and burning it pollutes the air and adds carbon dioxide, which is linked to climate change. That is why South Africa is building more solar and wind farms while still using coal.
