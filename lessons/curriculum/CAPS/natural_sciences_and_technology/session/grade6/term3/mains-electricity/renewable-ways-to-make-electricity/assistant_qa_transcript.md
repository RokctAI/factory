### subtopic_1 — Wind Power
**Assistant:** If renewable energy is so good, why doesn't South Africa use only wind and solar?

**Tutor:** There are a few big challenges. First, the wind and sunshine are not always there when people need electricity. The demand for electricity is often highest in the early evening, when people come home, cook and switch on lights, but that is exactly when the Sun is setting. On calm days, wind turbines stand still. To use only wind and solar, we would need ways to store huge amounts of energy, such as giant batteries or pumped storage dams, and these are expensive. Second, the national grid was built to carry electricity from coal power stations in Mpumalanga, but the best wind is near the coast and the best sun is in the Northern Cape, so new power lines are needed. Third, building enough renewable power takes time and money. So South Africa is slowly changing its mix, adding more wind, solar and storage each year, while the old coal power stations are gradually closed.

### subtopic_2 — Solar Power
**Assistant:** What is pumped storage, and how does it store electricity?

**Tutor:** Pumped storage is a clever way to store energy using two dams, one high on a mountain and one lower down, connected by tunnels. When there is spare electricity, for example late at night when few people are using it, or at midday when solar panels make a lot, the electricity is used to pump water from the lower dam up to the upper dam. The energy is now stored in the water, high up. When people need a lot of electricity, like in the early evening, the water is let down through the tunnels. It spins turbines and generators, just as in a hydro power station, and makes electricity again within minutes. South Africa has pumped storage schemes in the Drakensberg and at Ingula, on the border of KwaZulu-Natal and the Free State, and in the Western Cape. It is like a giant rechargeable battery made of water and mountains, and it helps the grid to keep up with changes in demand.

### subtopic_3 — Water Power and Other Sources
**Assistant:** How does a solar cell make electricity without any moving parts?

**Tutor:** A solar cell is a thin slice of a special material, usually silicon, which is made from very pure sand. The silicon is treated so that it has two layers that are slightly different from each other, and where they meet, there is a kind of invisible barrier. When light shines on the cell, the energy of the light knocks some of the tiny charged bits inside the silicon loose, so that they are free to move. The barrier between the two layers pushes these loose charged bits in one direction, towards metal contacts on the surface. If the contacts are connected in a circuit, the charged bits flow around it as an electric current. So the light itself provides the push, a bit like the chemicals in a cell do. There is nothing that spins and nothing that burns, which is why solar panels are quiet and can last twenty-five years or more. One solar cell gives only a small push, so many cells are joined together to make a panel.
