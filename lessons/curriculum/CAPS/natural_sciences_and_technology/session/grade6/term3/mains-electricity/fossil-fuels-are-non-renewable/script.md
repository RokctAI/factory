# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers fossil fuels: what they are, how they formed, and why they are called non-renewable. The anchor is a Grade 6 class in Lephalale in Limpopo, in the far north of South Africa. On the edge of the town is a huge open-cast coal mine, where giant machines dig coal from the ground, and next to it stand two enormous coal-fired power stations, Matimba and Medupi. Many families in the town work at the mine or the power stations. The teacher, Mr Mokoena, brings a large, shiny black lump of coal to class. When a learner called Karabo turns it over, she finds the faint print of a leaf, with all its veins, pressed into the coal. Mr Mokoena says that this leaf grew in a swamp about two hundred and fifty million years ago, before the dinosaurs. The big idea is that coal, oil and natural gas are fossil fuels, made from the remains of plants and animals that lived and died millions of years ago, and that they are non-renewable, because they took so long to form that once they are used up, they are gone. Fossil fuels are examinable.

## Subtopic: How Coal Formed

Long ago, much of southern Africa was dotted with huge swamps, full of ferns, trees and mosses. The plants grew, using sunlight to make food by photosynthesis. When they died, they fell into the swamp water.

Normally, dead plants rot away, because decomposers break them down. But in the swamps, the dead plants piled up under the water, where there was very little oxygen, so they did not rot completely. Over thousands of years, they formed thick layers of soft, brown, partly rotted plant material called peat.

Then the peat was buried under layers of mud and sand, carried in by rivers. Over millions of years, more and more layers piled on top. The weight of the layers pressed down on the peat, and the heat deep underground warmed it. Slowly, the water and gases were squeezed out, and the peat was changed into hard, black coal.

That is why we sometimes find fossils of leaves, stems and bark in coal, like Karabo's leaf. Coal is made of ancient plants, and the energy stored in it is energy that those plants trapped from the Sun millions of years ago.

South Africa has some of the largest coal deposits in the world, mainly in Mpumalanga, Limpopo and the northern Free State and KwaZulu-Natal. Coal is dug from mines, either deep underground or in huge open pits like the one at Lephalale.

The questions for this section are with you now: how coal formed from ancient plants.

## Subtopic: Oil and Natural Gas

Oil and natural gas are fossil fuels too, but they formed in the sea.

Millions of years ago, the oceans were full of tiny plants and animals, many too small to see, floating near the surface. When they died, they sank to the bottom of the sea and were buried in mud, where there was little oxygen, so they did not rot away completely.

Over millions of years, the mud was buried deeper and deeper and turned into rock. The heat and pressure deep underground slowly changed the remains of the tiny living things into crude oil, a thick, dark liquid, and natural gas. The oil and gas seeped upwards through tiny spaces in the rocks until they were trapped under layers of rock they could not pass through. Today, people drill deep wells, on land and in the sea, to reach them.

Crude oil is taken to refineries, where it is separated into many useful products: petrol and diesel for cars and trucks, paraffin for stoves and lamps, jet fuel for aeroplanes, oil for machines, and materials used to make plastics.

Natural gas is used for cooking, heating and making electricity. South Africa has very little oil of its own, and buys most of it from other countries. Some natural gas is piped to South Africa from Mozambique. South Africa also makes some petrol and diesel from coal, at a big factory in Secunda, in Mpumalanga.

The questions for this section are with you now: how oil and natural gas formed and how we use them.

## Subtopic: Non-Renewable Fuels

Mr Mokoena writes two words on the board: renewable and non-renewable.

A renewable resource is one that is replaced naturally, in a short time, so it will not run out. Sunlight, wind, flowing water and growing trees are renewable.

A non-renewable resource is one that cannot be replaced in a human lifetime, so once it is used up, it is gone for ever. Coal, oil and natural gas are non-renewable. They took millions of years to form, but we are burning them in just a few hundred years. In time, the deposits that are easy to reach will run out.

Fossil fuels have been very useful. They are full of stored energy, and they are easy to transport and store. In South Africa, most of our electricity is still made by burning coal.

But burning fossil fuels also causes problems. It releases smoke, ash and harmful gases that pollute the air and make people ill with lung problems. It releases large amounts of carbon dioxide, which traps heat in the air around the Earth and is causing climate change. Mining can damage land, rivers and underground water.

For these reasons, countries, including South Africa, are trying to use fossil fuels more carefully and to build more renewable ways of making electricity.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking fossil fuels are made from dinosaur bones; coal formed mostly from plants, and oil and gas from tiny sea creatures. Two: thinking coal forms in a few years; it took millions of years. Three: thinking fossil fuels are renewable because there is a lot of coal; once used, it cannot be replaced. Four: thinking burning coal is harmless; it pollutes the air and adds carbon dioxide.

The questions for this section are with you now: non-renewable fuels and the problems they cause.
