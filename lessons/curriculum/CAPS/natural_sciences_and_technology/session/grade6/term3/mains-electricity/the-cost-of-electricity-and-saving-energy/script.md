# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the cost of electricity and how we can save energy. The anchor is a Grade 6 class in Vryheid, a town in northern KwaZulu-Natal. Their teacher, Mrs Dlamini, sets an unusual homework task. For one week, each learner must read their family's electricity meter every evening, write down how many units are left or used, and make a list of every electrical appliance in their home. Many families have a prepaid meter, where they buy electricity units in advance with a code from a shop or a phone. One learner, Andile, reports that his family's units ran out on Thursday night, in the cold, and his mother was surprised at how quickly they had gone. Mrs Dlamini asks the class two questions: why does electricity cost so much, and where did Andile's family's units go? The big idea is that the cost of electricity includes building and running the whole system that makes and delivers it, and the amount each appliance uses, and that we can save energy and money by using electricity wisely. The cost of electricity and ways to save energy are examinable.

## Subtopic: Why Electricity Costs Money

Electricity seems to come from nowhere when we flip a switch, but a huge system is needed to make it and bring it to us. All of it costs money, and the cost is paid by the people and businesses that buy electricity.

Building the system. This is called infrastructure. A single large power station costs many billions of rand and takes years to build. Coal mines must be dug and run. Thousands of kilometres of power lines and pylons must be built across the country, along with substations, transformers, street cables, poles and meters.

Running and looking after the system. Power stations need fuel, like coal, every day. Thousands of workers must be paid, including engineers, technicians and electricians. Machines wear out and break, and they must be repaired and replaced. Cables that are stolen or damaged must be fixed.

The cost of the fuel and the damage it causes. Burning coal pollutes the air and water, and that harm also has a cost, for example in people's health.

All of this is part of the price of each unit of electricity. Electricity is measured in units called kilowatt-hours. When a family buys prepaid electricity, they are buying a certain number of these units. The prepaid meter counts the units down as they are used, and when the number reaches zero, the power goes off until more units are bought. Homes with a normal meter get a bill at the end of each month instead, and the bill shows how many units were used. Mrs Dlamini points out that the price of a unit has gone up many times over the years, because the cost of building new power stations, buying coal and repairing old machines keeps rising. That is one reason families feel the cost so strongly.

The questions for this section are with you now: infrastructure and the cost of making and delivering electricity.

## Subtopic: Which Appliances Use the Most

The class compares their appliance lists. Each appliance has a label, often underneath or on the back, showing its power in watts. The higher the number of watts, the more electricity it uses every second it is switched on.

Mrs Dlamini sorts the appliances into two groups.

Big users are appliances that make heat: the geyser, which heats bath and shower water; the stove and oven; the kettle; the electric heater; the iron; the hair dryer; and the toaster. A kettle or a heater uses far more electricity than a light or a television.

Small users are appliances that make light or movement, or run electronics: LED lights, cell phone chargers, the radio, the television and a fan.

How long an appliance is used also matters. A small user left on all day and all night can add up, and a big user used for a short time may use less.

In many South African homes, the geyser uses the most electricity of all, because it heats a large tank of water and keeps it hot day and night. Heaters in winter come next. That is why Andile's units ran out quickly during the cold week: the heater and the geyser were working hard. The class does a quick check. A kettle switched on for five minutes uses about the same electricity as a small LED bulb left on for a whole day. So the question to ask is always: how much power does it use, and for how long is it on?

The questions for this section are with you now: which appliances use the most electricity, and why.

## Subtopic: Ways to Save Energy

Saving electricity saves money for families. It also means that fewer power stations are needed, less coal is burned, there is less pollution, and there is less risk of load shedding. The class makes a list of ways to save.

Switch off lights and appliances when you leave a room, and switch off chargers and televisions at the wall, because many use a little electricity even on standby.

Use LED bulbs, which use much less electricity than old bulbs and last much longer.

Boil only as much water as you need in the kettle.

Take short showers instead of deep baths, to use less hot water.

Put a blanket around the geyser and set its temperature a little lower, or install a solar water heater, which uses the Sun to heat water.

Cook with lids on pots, and use a microwave or a pressure cooker for small amounts.

Keep the fridge door closed, and do not put hot food straight in.

In winter, put on warm clothes and close doors, curtains and windows before switching on a heater.

Dry washing on a line in the sun, instead of using a tumble dryer.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking electricity is free because it comes out of the wall; the whole system costs money. Two: thinking all appliances use the same amount; heat-making ones use the most. Three: thinking switching off at the wall makes no difference; standby uses a little all the time. Four: thinking saving electricity only helps your family; it also means less coal burned and less pollution.

The questions for this section are with you now: ways to save energy at home and at school.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the meter readings in Vryheid — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Paying for Power

Power stations, mines, power lines and workers all cost money.

We pay for electricity in units.

Keep the prepaid meter in mind and try the questions for this part: paying for power.

## Subtopic: Heat Costs the Most

Geysers, heaters, kettles, stoves and irons use the most electricity.

Lights and chargers use much less.

Hold Andile's cold Thursday night in mind and try this part's questions: heat costs the most.

## Subtopic: Save It

Switch off, use LEDs, boil less, shower quickly.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Electricity costs money because of the infrastructure needed to make and deliver it: power stations, mines, power lines, substations and meters, as well as fuel, workers and repairs. We pay for each unit we use. Appliances that make heat, like geysers, heaters, kettles and stoves, use the most electricity, and the longer an appliance runs, the more it uses. Saving electricity saves money, means less coal is burned and less pollution, and lowers the risk of load shedding.

Hold the list of ways to save in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: save it.
