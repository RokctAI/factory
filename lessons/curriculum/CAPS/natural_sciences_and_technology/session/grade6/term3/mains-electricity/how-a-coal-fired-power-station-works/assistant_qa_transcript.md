### subtopic_1 — From Coal to Steam
**Assistant:** Why does a generator make electricity when a magnet spins near copper wire?

**Tutor:** This is one of the most important discoveries in science, made about two hundred years ago. Scientists found that when a magnet moves near a loop of wire, or a wire moves near a magnet, electricity starts to flow in the wire. If the magnet stops moving, the electricity stops. Moving magnets push on the tiny charged bits inside the copper and make them drift along the wire, which is an electric current. The faster the magnet moves, and the stronger it is, and the more loops of wire there are, the more electricity is produced. That is why a generator has a powerful magnet spinning very fast inside thousands of turns of copper wire. A bicycle dynamo works the same way, on a small scale: a small magnet spins inside a coil when the wheel turns. Almost all the electricity in the world, whether from coal, wind or water, is made by spinning a generator. Only solar panels make electricity in a completely different way.

### subtopic_2 — Turbine and Generator
**Assistant:** What is load shedding and why does it happen?

**Tutor:** Load shedding is when the electricity supplier switches off the power to different areas for a few hours at a time, in turns, according to a schedule. It happens when the power stations cannot produce as much electricity as the whole country needs at that moment. Electricity cannot easily be stored in large amounts, so the amount produced must match the amount being used, every second. If people try to use more than the power stations can make, the whole national grid could become unstable and shut down completely, which could take days to fix. To prevent that, some areas are switched off for a short time, which reduces the load, or the demand, on the grid. In South Africa, load shedding happened often because many coal power stations were old and broke down, and new ones took a long time to build. Saving electricity, especially in the early evening when demand is highest, helps reduce the need for load shedding.

### subtopic_3 — From the Power Station to Your Home
**Assistant:** Why are cooling towers so big and shaped like that?

**Tutor:** Cooling towers have to get rid of a huge amount of heat. After the steam has spun the turbine, it must be turned back into water so that it can be pumped back to the boiler and used again. This happens in the condenser, where the steam passes over pipes filled with cooler water. That cooling water gets warm, so it is sent to the cooling tower. Inside the tower, the warm water is sprayed or trickled down, and air flows up through it. Some of the water evaporates, and evaporation carries heat away, just as sweat cools your skin. The warm, moist air rises out of the top, forming the white clouds you see. The tall, curved shape helps air flow up through the tower on its own, like a chimney, without needing big fans, and it is a very strong shape for such a huge, thin concrete structure. Some newer power stations, like Medupi and Kusile, use dry cooling systems with giant fans instead, to save South Africa's scarce water.
