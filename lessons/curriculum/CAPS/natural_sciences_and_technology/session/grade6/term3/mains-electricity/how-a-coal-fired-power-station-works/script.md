# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers how a coal-fired power station produces electricity. The anchor is a Grade 6 class from Ogies, a small town in Mpumalanga surrounded by coal mines and power stations. From the school field, the learners can see the giant, curved cooling towers of a power station on the horizon, with white clouds rising from them. Most of the electricity that reaches their homes, and homes all over South Africa, is made in power stations like this one. An engineer, Mr Nkosi, visits the class with a poster of the power station and two simple models. He says that the whole power station, as big as a small town, is really a very large machine for doing three things: making steam, using the steam to spin a turbine, and using the turbine to spin a generator. The big idea is that in a coal-fired power station, burning coal heats water into steam, the steam turns a turbine, and the turbine turns a generator that produces electricity, which is then carried along power lines to our homes. How a coal-fired power station works is examinable.

## Subtopic: From Coal to Steam

Mr Nkosi follows a lump of coal on its journey.

The coal is dug from a mine close to the power station, and carried on long conveyor belts into the power station. There it is crushed and ground into a very fine powder, as fine as flour. This powder burns quickly and completely.

The coal powder is blown into a huge furnace inside the boiler, where it burns at a very high temperature. Burning releases the chemical energy stored in the coal as heat energy.

All around the furnace are walls made of steel pipes filled with water. The great heat boils the water in the pipes and turns it into steam. The steam is heated even more and kept at very high pressure, so that it can push very hard.

Mr Nkosi shows the first model. On the teacher's desk, he boils water in a kettle. He holds a paper pinwheel, a little windmill on a stick, carefully in the jet of steam coming out of the spout, keeping his hand well away. The steam pushes on the paper blades, and the pinwheel spins fast. He warns the class never to do this themselves, because steam can cause terrible burns.

The questions for this section are with you now: how coal is burned to make steam.

## Subtopic: Turbine and Generator

In the power station, the high-pressure steam is piped to a turbine. A turbine is like a giant fan or pinwheel, with many rows of curved metal blades on a long shaft. The steam rushes through the turbine, pushing on the blades and making the shaft spin very fast, about three thousand times a minute. Heat energy in the steam has been changed into movement energy.

The turbine shaft is joined to a generator. A generator is a machine that changes movement energy into electrical energy. Inside it, a huge, powerful magnet spins inside coils of copper wire. When a magnet moves near a coil of wire, it makes electricity flow in the wire. The faster and stronger the spinning, the more electricity is produced.

Mr Nkosi shows the second model: a bicycle dynamo, which is a tiny generator. A learner turns the bicycle wheel by hand, and the dynamo's little wheel spins against the tyre. The bulb connected to it lights up. When she stops turning, the light goes out. Movement in, electricity out.

So the energy changes in a power station are: chemical energy in the coal, then heat energy in the steam, then movement energy in the turbine, then electrical energy from the generator.

After the steam has passed through the turbine, it is cooled in a condenser and turned back into water, which is pumped back to the boiler to be used again. The cooling towers help to cool the water that is used in the condenser. The white clouds rising from cooling towers are mostly water vapour, not smoke. The smoke and gases from burning coal go up the tall chimneys.

The questions for this section are with you now: turbines, generators and the energy changes in a power station.

## Subtopic: From the Power Station to Your Home

The electricity leaves the generator and goes to a transformer next to the power station. The transformer raises the voltage, which is the push of the electricity, to a very high level. This makes it possible to send the electricity hundreds of kilometres with less energy wasted along the way.

The electricity then travels along thick cables called transmission lines, carried high above the ground on tall steel pylons, across the whole country. The power stations, lines and pylons are joined together into one large network, called the national grid.

Near towns, the electricity reaches substations, where more transformers lower the voltage step by step. Smaller power lines on poles, or cables under the ground, carry it along the streets. A small transformer on a pole or in a green box lowers it again to the voltage used in homes, which is about two hundred and thirty volts. It then passes through the electricity meter into the house wiring, to the plugs and lights.

The electricity travels in a giant circuit: from the power station to our homes and back.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking the coal makes electricity directly; it makes heat, which makes steam. Two: thinking the white clouds from cooling towers are smoke; they are mostly water vapour. Three: thinking a turbine and a generator are the same thing; the turbine spins, and the generator changes that movement into electricity. Four: thinking electricity goes straight from the power station into the house; transformers change its voltage along the way.

The questions for this section are with you now: transformers, power lines and the national grid.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the power station near Ogies — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Burn and Boil

Coal is burned in a furnace.

The heat boils water into steam.

Keep the steam from the kettle in mind and try the questions for this part: burn and boil.

## Subtopic: Spin and Make Electricity

Steam spins the turbine. The turbine spins the generator.

The generator makes electricity.

Hold the spinning pinwheel and the bicycle dynamo in mind and try this part's questions: spin and make electricity.

## Subtopic: Along the Lines

Power lines and transformers carry the electricity to our homes.

Pocket summary of the lesson. In a coal-fired power station, coal is crushed into powder and burned in a furnace. The heat boils water into high-pressure steam. The steam spins a turbine, and the turbine turns a generator, where a magnet spins inside coils of copper wire to produce electricity. The energy changes from chemical, to heat, to movement, to electrical. Transformers raise the voltage, transmission lines on pylons carry the electricity across the country, and more transformers lower it before it reaches our homes.

Hold the tall cooling towers on the horizon in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: along the lines.
