**Bianca:** Revision brings out the same struggles every year, so let's meet them directly. First: free-body diagrams — learners either forget a force or invent one. What is the discipline that gets the arrows right?

**Tutor:** Interrogate the object with one question per possible toucher: what is physically in contact with it, plus the one force that acts without contact. Gravity first, always — weight, straight down, no exceptions. Then walk the contacts: a surface underneath gives a normal force perpendicular to itself; a rough surface adds friction along itself, opposing the motion or the tendency to move; a rope gives tension along the rope, pulling only, never pushing; a person's push enters at its actual angle. When the walk ends, STOP — that is the complete diagram. The two classic inventions are an arrow for "the motion", which is not a force at all, and a forward force to explain why a coasting object keeps moving, which Newton's first law says needs no explanation. And the classic omission is friction on a surface described as rough — underline the word rough the moment you read it. A diagram built by walking the contacts cannot smuggle and cannot forget, and the marks for the diagram itself are usually a third of the question.

**Bianca:** Next, the twins: gravitation and Coulomb look identical on the formula sheet, and learners mix their constants and contexts. How should the pair be revised?

**Tutor:** Revise them AS a pair, because the examiner tests the seam between them. The shared skeleton earns the calculation marks: multiply the two sources — masses in one law, charges in the other — divide by the distance squared, and apply the constant that matches, G at 6,67 times 10 to the minus 11 for masses, k at 9 times 10 to the 9 for charges. Then own the three differences, because they are the favourite theory marks. Sign: gravity only attracts; the electric force attracts or repels, so a Coulomb answer needs its direction argued from the charge signs. Strength: gravity is absurdly weak — it takes the whole Earth to hold down a feather, while a party balloon's static charge lifts your hair against that entire planet. Sources: mass is always positive; charge comes in two kinds and is quantised. In calculations the discipline is identical for both: convert prefixes to base units in a written line, square the distance before anything else, and finish with the ratio reflex as a check — if the separation had doubled, would your answer have quartered? A pair revised together is half the memorising and twice the understanding.

**Bianca:** The mixed resistor network: series chunk plus parallel pair. Learners solve half of it and stall. What is the drill that finishes every network?

**Tutor:** Collapse, solve, expand — and trust that the drill always terminates. Collapse: replace the parallel pair with its single equivalent, product over sum for two branches, and keep collapsing until one resistor faces the battery. Solve: one application of Ohm's law gives the total current — and that same current flows through every SERIES element of the original circuit, which is the fact that restarts stalled solutions. Expand: work back outward — potential difference across each series element from V equals I R; whatever emf remains sits across the parallel section, identical on every branch; branch currents from each branch's own resistance. The stall almost always happens at the junction, and its cure is remembering that the parallel section's potential difference is the leftover: the emf minus the series drops. Then run both audits, because they certify the whole solution: branch currents must sum to the total at the junction, and the power ledger must close — every watt from the battery accounted for in the resistors. In the 12-volt network from the lesson, 12 plus 9 plus 3 watts meets the battery's 24 exactly. When an audit fails, the error is upstream of that audit — which even tells you where to look.

**Bianca:** Last one: any advice for the final weeks of physics revision itself — what actually moves marks?

**Tutor:** Three shifts, in order of payoff. First, solve cold before checking — reading worked solutions feels productive and stores almost nothing, so take a past paper's mechanics and electricity sections, work them without notes, and mark against the memo; the gaps are your genuine revision list. Second, drill the trap museum by name, because November physics is familiar but the traps are always fresh: weight quoted in kilograms, the unsquared distance, the missed prefix conversion, the swapped parallel formula, the emf claimed from a steady flux. One page, five traps, one example each, read before every session. Third, make the audits a reflex rather than an afterthought: net-force direction against acceleration direction, the ratio reflex on every inverse-square answer, junction sums and the power ledger on every network. An answer that survives its own audit is sealed before the marker ever sees it, and the certainty it buys steadies every question after it. Cold solving, named traps, reflex audits — those three carry more marks than any quantity of rereading, and they fit the weeks you have left.
