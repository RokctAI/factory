**Bianca:** Revision brings back the same struggles every year, so let's take them head-on. First: free-body diagrams — learners either leave a force out or invent one that does not exist. What discipline gets the arrows right?

**Tutor:** Interrogate the object with one question per candidate: what is physically touching it, plus the single force that needs no touch. Gravity comes first, always — weight, straight down, no exceptions. Then walk the contacts one by one: a surface underneath contributes a normal force perpendicular to itself; a rough surface adds friction along itself, set against the motion or the tendency to move; a rope contributes tension along the rope, pulling only, never pushing; a hand's push enters at its true angle. When the walk around the object ends, STOP — the diagram is complete. The two classic inventions are an arrow for "the motion", which is not a force at all, and a forward force conjured to explain why a coasting object keeps moving — which Newton's first law says needs no explanation whatsoever. The classic omission is friction on a surface the question took the trouble to call rough — underline the word rough the moment you read it. A diagram built by walking the contacts can neither smuggle nor forget, and the diagram itself typically carries a healthy share of the question's marks.

**Bianca:** Next, the twins: gravitation and Coulomb sit side by side on the data sheet, and learners scramble their constants and their contexts. How should the pair be revised?

**Tutor:** Revise them AS a pair, because questions love to test the seam between them. The shared skeleton earns the calculation marks: multiply the two sources — masses in one law, charges in the other — divide by the distance squared, and attach the constant that matches: G at 6,67 times 10 to the minus 11 for masses, k at 9 times 10 to the 9 for charges. Then own the three differences, because they are the favourite theory marks. Sign: gravity only attracts, while the electric force attracts or repels — so a Coulomb answer must argue its direction from the charge signs. Strength: gravity is absurdly feeble — it takes the entire Earth to hold down a sheet of paper, while the static charge on a rubbed ruler lifts that paper against the whole planet. Sources: mass comes in one kind and is always positive; charge comes in two kinds. In the calculations the discipline is identical for both laws: convert prefixes to base units in a written line, square the distance before anything else, and end with the ratio reflex as a check — had the separation doubled, would your answer have quartered? One skeleton revised once, three differences owned: half the memorising, twice the understanding.

**Bianca:** The mixed resistor network — a series resistor feeding a parallel pair. Learners solve half of it and then freeze. What is the drill that finishes every network?

**Tutor:** Collapse, solve, expand — and trust that the drill always terminates. Collapse: replace the parallel pair with its single equivalent, product over sum for two branches, and keep folding until one resistor faces the battery. Solve: one application of Ohm's law delivers the total current — and that same current flows through every SERIES element of the original circuit, which is the fact that unfreezes most stalled solutions. Expand: work back outward — potential difference across each series element from V equals I R; whatever remains of the emf sits across the parallel section, identical on every branch; branch currents from each branch's own resistance. The freeze almost always happens at the junction, and its cure is remembering that the parallel section's potential difference is the LEFTOVER: the emf minus the series drops. In the lesson's network, 24 volts minus the 12 across the series resistor leaves 12 for both branches. Then run both audits, because together they certify the entire solution: the branch currents must sum to the total at the junction — 2 plus 4 giving the 6 that entered — and the power ledger must close, 72 plus 24 plus 48 watts meeting the battery's 144 exactly. When an audit fails, the error is upstream of that audit — the failure even tells you where to search.

**Bianca:** Last one: any advice for the final weeks of physics revision itself — what actually moves marks?

**Tutor:** Three shifts, in order of payoff. First, solve cold before checking: reading worked solutions feels productive and stores almost nothing, so take a fresh set of practice problems across mechanics and electricity, work them without notes, and only then compare against full solutions — the gaps you find are your genuine revision list, far more honest than any feeling of familiarity. Second, drill the trap museum by name, because end-of-year physics is familiar territory but the traps stay fresh: weight quoted in kilograms, the unsquared distance, the missed prefix conversion, the swapped parallel formula, the emf claimed from a steady flux. One page, five traps, one example each, read before every session. Third, promote the audits from afterthought to reflex: net-force direction checked against acceleration direction, the ratio reflex fired at every inverse-square answer, junction sums and the power ledger run on every network. An answer that survives its own audit is sealed before anyone else reads it, and the certainty it buys steadies every question that follows. Cold solving, named traps, reflex audits — those three carry more marks than any quantity of rereading, and they fit comfortably in the weeks you have left.
