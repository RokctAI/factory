# Part 1 — Expert

This session is the physics revision sweep for the final examination — the year's mechanics, fields and electricity reorganised into the four toolkits that earn the marks: the free-body method that solves every force problem, the two inverse-square laws that run gravity and electrostatics, the resistor network drill, and the electromagnetism chain from field to flux to induced emf. Nothing here is new; everything here is the exam-day version — compressed, ordered, and drilled through worked examples chosen to expose the classic traps.

## Subtopic: Vectors and Newton's Laws — the Free-Body Method

Every force problem in the paper surrenders to the same five-step method, so fix the method before the physics. Step one: draw the free-body diagram — the object as a dot, every force on it as a labelled arrow. Step two: choose a positive direction and declare it. Step three: resolve any slanted force into components — F x equals F cos theta and F y equals F sin theta; a 100-newton pull at 30 degrees above the horizontal becomes 86,6 newtons horizontal and 50 newtons vertical. Step four: write Newton's second law along each axis: F net equals m a. Step five: solve, and attach units and direction.

The three laws behind the method, stated for the definition marks. First law: an object continues at rest or at constant velocity unless a net force acts on it — that reluctance to change is inertia. Second law: when a net force acts, the object accelerates in the direction of the force, with F net equals m a. Third law: when object A exerts a force on object B, B exerts a force on A that is equal in magnitude and opposite in direction — and the pair act on DIFFERENT objects, which is why they never cancel.

Run the method. A 10-kilogram crate on a rough floor is pulled horizontally with 50 newtons while friction opposes with 10 newtons. Free body: applied force forward, friction backward, weight down, normal force up. Vertical forces balance. Horizontal: F net equals 50 minus 10, which is 40 newtons. Acceleration: a equals F net over m, which is 40 over 10, giving 4 metres per second squared, forward.

And the third-law reading of the same scene: the crate pulls back on the rope exactly as hard as the rope pulls the crate. Equal, opposite, different objects — the crate still accelerates, because only the forces ON the crate enter its free-body diagram.

This section's questions are with you now — the five steps in order, the three laws word for word, and the crate's 4 metres per second squared reproduced.

## Subtopic: The Inverse-Square Twins — Gravitation and Coulomb

Two of the year's laws are the same law wearing different constants, and revising them together halves the work. Newton's law of universal gravitation: every particle attracts every other with a force F equals G m one m two over r squared, with G at 6,67 times 10 to the minus 11. Coulomb's law: the force between two point charges is F equals k Q one Q two over r squared, with k at 9 times 10 to the 9.

The shared skeleton: both forces act along the line joining the centres, both multiply the two sources, and both fall off as the SQUARE of the distance. Double the separation and either force drops to a quarter; triple it, a ninth. That inverse-square reflex answers a whole family of ratio questions without a calculator.

The differences are the other half of the marks. Gravity only attracts; the electrostatic force attracts or repels, because charge comes in two signs and mass does not. And gravity is feeble — it needs a planet-sized mass to feel — while the electric force between everyday charges dominates completely.

Work one of each. Coulomb: charges of 2 microcoulombs and 3 microcoulombs, 0,1 metres apart. F equals 9 times 10 to the 9, times 2 times 10 to the minus 6, times 3 times 10 to the minus 6, all over 0,1 squared. The numerator: 9 times 10 to the 9 times 6 times 10 to the minus 12 is 5,4 times 10 to the minus 2. Divide by 0,01: 5,4 newtons. Both charges positive, so the force repels, along the line joining them — and by the third law each charge feels the same 5,4 newtons.

Gravitation earns its keep through weight: the familiar w equals m g is the universal law evaluated at the Earth's surface, where g equals G M over r squared comes out at 9,8 metres per second squared. Climb to one Earth-radius above the surface and r doubles, so g quarters — a 10-kilogram mass weighs 98 newtons on the surface and 24,5 newtons up there. Mass never changed; weight did.

Take this section's questions now — both laws stated, the ratio reflex fired, the 5,4 newtons reproduced, and mass held apart from weight.

## Subtopic: Circuits — Ohm's Law and the Network Drill

Ohm's law anchors the topic: V equals I R, at constant temperature. Series resistors add: R s equals R one plus R two. Parallel resistors combine by reciprocals: one over R p equals one over R one plus one over R two — and for exactly two resistors the shortcut is product over sum.

The exam's favourite is the mixed network, and it falls to a three-pass drill: collapse, solve, expand. Take the year's standard: a 12-volt battery feeding a 3-ohm resistor in series with a parallel pair of 4 ohms and 12 ohms.

Pass one, collapse. The pair: product over sum is 4 times 12 over 4 plus 12, which is 48 over 16, giving 3 ohms. The circuit is now 3 plus 3 in series: 6 ohms total.

Pass two, solve the collapsed loop. Total current: I equals V over R, which is 12 over 6, giving 2 amperes from the battery, and through the series 3-ohm resistor.

Pass three, expand back. Potential difference across the series resistor: V equals I R, 2 times 3, 6 volts. That leaves 12 minus 6, which is 6 volts across the parallel pair — the same 6 volts across each branch, because parallel branches share their potential difference. Branch currents: 6 over 4 is 1,5 amperes; 6 over 12 is 0,5 amperes. Junction audit: 1,5 plus 0,5 returns the 2 amperes that entered. Every number found, every rule used once.

Close with power, because it audits the whole circuit. P equals V I, or I squared R. The battery delivers 12 times 2, which is 24 watts. The components dissipate: the series resistor, I squared R, 4 times 3, 12 watts; the 4-ohm branch, 1,5 squared times 4, which is 9 watts; the 12-ohm branch, 0,5 squared times 12, which is 3 watts. Sum: 12 plus 9 plus 3 — 24 watts, matching the battery exactly. Energy in equals energy out, and that one-line audit certifies every current and potential difference before the marker looks.

This section's questions are ready — collapse, solve, expand on the full network, both audits run, and the power ledger closed.

## Subtopic: Electromagnetism — Field, Flux and Faraday

Electromagnetism is a chain of three ideas, and the chain order is the revision. Idea one: currents MAKE magnetic fields. Around a straight current-carrying conductor the field lines are circles centred on the wire, and the right-hand rule reads them: with the right thumb along the conventional current, the curled fingers give the circles' direction. A coil stacks those circles into the field of a bar magnet, north at one face.

Idea two: fields are counted as FLUX. Magnetic flux is the field passing through a surface: phi equals B A cos theta — field strength, times area, times the cosine of the angle between the field and the normal to the surface. Unit: the weber. Face the coil square-on to the field and theta is zero, cosine one, maximum flux: a field of 0,5 tesla through an area of 0,02 square metres gives phi equals 0,5 times 0,02, which is 0,01 webers. Turn the coil edge-on and the flux is zero — same field, same coil, nothing threading through.

Idea three: CHANGING flux makes emf. Faraday's law: the induced emf equals N delta phi over delta t — the number of turns, times the rate of change of flux. Not flux itself — its CHANGE. A coil sitting in the strongest steady field ever built induces nothing; wave a weak magnet near a coil and a current flows. Work it: a coil of 50 turns threads 0,04 webers, and the magnet is snatched away in 0,2 seconds. Emf equals 50 times 0,04 over 0,2, which is 50 times 0,2, giving 10 volts. Halve the snatch time and the emf doubles — speed of change is everything.

The minus sign in Faraday's law is Lenz's law, and it states the direction: the induced current flows so that its own magnetic field OPPOSES the change in flux that produced it. Push a north pole toward a coil and the coil's near face becomes a north to push back; pull it away and the face flips to south to cling. Energy conservation demands the opposition — an induced current that helped the change along would be a free energy machine.

The final questions of this part are with you now — the chain recited in order, the flux at three angles, the 10 volts reproduced, and Lenz's opposition argued from energy.

## Subtopic: Invisible Springs and the Thinning Spray

Start with what a force diagram really is: an honesty exercise. The object is alone on the page, and the only arrows allowed are forces that something ELSE exerts on IT — the Earth pulling down, the floor pushing up, the rope pulling forward, the roughness dragging back. No arrow for "its motion", no arrow for what it does to other things. Most force errors are smuggled arrows, and the dot-alone-on-the-page picture is the smuggling check.

Newton's third law stops being spooky the moment you see forces as two-ended springs. A spring never pushes at one end only — compress it and BOTH ends push outward, stretch it and both ends pull inward. Every force in nature is such a spring between two objects: the Earth pulls the falling apple and the apple pulls the Earth, with exactly equal force. The apple moves more because it is lighter, not because it is pulled harder — same spring, different masses on the ends.

Now the inverse-square picture. Stand a spray can in the middle of a room and spray in all directions: close to the can the paint lands thick; at double the distance the same paint spreads over four times the area, so the coat is a quarter as thick; at triple, a ninth. Gravity and the electric force spread their influence exactly like that spray — the SAME totals through every sphere, thinning with the square of the radius. That is all "inverse square" means, and it is why doubling a distance never halves these forces; it quarters them.

The field idea rides the same picture: a field map is the paint-thickness chart of the room — at every point, how hard the spray would hit whatever stands there. Gravitational field strength says newtons per kilogram placed there; electric field strength says newtons per coulomb. The map exists before the visitor arrives.

Your questions for this section are ready — smuggled arrows caught, both spring ends named, and the spray thinned at two distances.

## Subtopic: The Delivery Network

A circuit is a delivery network, and every quantity has a job in it. The battery is the depot, and its emf is the energy loaded onto each coulomb of cargo as it leaves. Current is the traffic — coulombs passing per second. Resistors are the delivery stops, where the cargo's energy is dropped off as heat and light. Potential difference across a stop is the energy each coulomb unloads there. The trucks are never used up; the cargo is — that is why charge circulates forever while the battery runs down.

Series is one delivery route through every stop in turn. The same traffic passes every stop — same current. Each stop takes its share of the cargo, and the shares add up to the full load — potential differences add to the emf. More stops on one route means slower traffic overall — resistances add.

Parallel is a fork into side streets that rejoin. Each side street connects depot-side to return-side directly, so every branch is offered the FULL load per coulomb — same potential difference on each branch. The traffic divides at the fork, more trucks down the easier street, and recombines after — currents add at the junction. And a second street always eases the total flow, however narrow it is — which is why parallel resistance is always below the smallest branch.

Power is simply the delivery rate: energy dropped per second, P equals V I. And the network audit is common sense made rigorous: every joule per second leaving the depot is dropped at some stop — 24 watts out of the battery, 12 plus 9 plus 3 dropped at the three resistors. If the ledger refuses to close, a number upstream is wrong, and the audit has found it for you.

Take this section's questions now — every quantity matched to its job, both route patterns argued, and the delivery ledger closed.

## Subtopic: The Trap Museum and the Last Check

Five exhibits, each labelled with the mark it destroys, each with its antidote.

Exhibit one: mass confused with weight. Mass is the amount of matter, in kilograms, the same everywhere; weight is the gravitational force on it, w equals m g, in NEWTONS, and it changes with location. Antidote: the unit polices the answer — any weight quoted in kilograms is already wrong.

Exhibit two: the unsquared distance. In both inverse-square laws, r is squared; leaving 0,1 unsquared inflates the Coulomb answer tenfold. Antidote: square the distance before anything else touches the calculator, and run the sanity ratio — double distance, quarter force — on the final number.

Exhibit three: the prefix slip. Microcoulombs are 10 to the minus 6, nanocoulombs 10 to the minus 9, and one missed prefix moves the answer three powers of ten. Antidote: convert every quantity to base units in a written line BEFORE substituting.

Exhibit four: series and parallel formulas swapped — reciprocals used on a series chain, or a plain sum on a parallel pair. Antidote: the junction question — no junction, plain sum; a junction, reciprocals — and the parallel result must come out SMALLER than the smallest branch, which catches the swap instantly.

Exhibit five: flux confused with change of flux. A coil in a steady field induces nothing, however strong the field; Faraday pays only for delta phi over delta t. Antidote: before writing the formula, name what is changing — the field, the area, or the angle. If nothing changes, the emf is zero and the question was a definition check.

And the last habit, the one that converts near-marks into full marks: every toolkit in this lesson closes its own books. Free-body answers: check the direction of the net force matches the acceleration. Inverse-square answers: check the ratio reflex. Networks: junction currents sum, potential differences return the emf, and the power ledger closes to the battery's output. An answer that survives its own audit is not a hope; it is a certainty, sealed before the marker arrives.

The final questions of the lesson are with you now — five exhibits dodged, and every answer sealed by its own audit.
