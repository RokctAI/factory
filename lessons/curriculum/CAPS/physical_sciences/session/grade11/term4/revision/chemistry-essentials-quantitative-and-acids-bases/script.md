# Part 1 — Expert

This session is the chemistry revision sweep for the final examination — the year's quantitative chemistry and acids and bases reorganised into the four toolkits that earn the marks: the mole conversions, the gas laws, the stoichiometry chain with its limiting reagents, and the acid-base machinery from definition to titration. Nothing here is new; everything here is the exam-day version — compressed, ordered, and drilled through worked examples chosen to expose the classic traps.

## Subtopic: The Mole Toolkit — Mass, Solution and Gas

Every quantitative question in the paper runs through one hub: the mole. Three formulas connect the hub to the three things a laboratory can actually measure.

Mass: n equals m over M — moles equal mass over molar mass, with M assembled from the periodic table. Sodium hydroxide, N a O H: 23 plus 16 plus 1, giving 40 grams per mole. So 8 grams of sodium hydroxide is 8 over 40, which is 0,2 moles.

Solution: c equals n over V — concentration in moles per cubic decimetre. Dissolve that same 0,2 moles to make 500 cubic centimetres of solution. Convert the volume FIRST: 500 cubic centimetres over 1000 is 0,5 cubic decimetres. Then c equals 0,2 over 0,5, which is 0,4 moles per cubic decimetre.

Gas at STP: n equals V over 22,4 — because at standard temperature and pressure, one mole of ANY gas occupies 22,4 cubic decimetres, regardless of which gas it is. So 4,48 cubic decimetres of any gas at STP is 4,48 over 22,4, which is 0,2 moles.

Notice what just happened: 8 grams on a balance, half a litre in a flask and 4,48 cubic decimetres in a syringe all became the same number — 0,2 moles. That is the hub working. The discipline for every mixed question in the paper: convert INTO moles first, do the chemistry in moles, convert OUT last. Grams never talk directly to cubic decimetres; they both talk to the mole.

This section's questions are with you now — all three conversions run in both directions, the volume converted before the concentration, and the hub discipline stated.

## Subtopic: Gases — the Ideal Model and Its Equation

The ideal gas model earns its marks in two currencies: the assumptions, and the equation. The kinetic theory pictures a gas as a swarm of particles in constant random motion, whose own volume is negligible next to the container, with no forces between them except during collisions, and collisions that are perfectly elastic. Temperature, in this picture, IS the average kinetic energy of the particles — which is why temperature must be measured in KELVIN, the scale whose zero is zero motion. Convert always: kelvin equals degrees Celsius plus 273. Twenty-seven degrees Celsius is 300 kelvin, and no gas law tolerates Celsius.

Boyle's law handles the fixed-temperature squeeze: p one V one equals p two V two. A gas at 100 kilopascals filling 2 cubic decimetres, squeezed at constant temperature to 1 cubic decimetre, rises to 200 kilopascals — halve the space, double the pressure, because the same swarm now hits the walls twice as often. The general equation chains all three variables for a fixed sample: p one V one over T one equals p two V two over T two.

The full ideal gas equation prices any single state: p V equals n R T, with R at 8,31 — and its units are strict: pressure in pascals, volume in CUBIC METRES, temperature in kelvin. Work it: 0,5 moles of gas at 300 kelvin in a 0,01 cubic metre container. Pressure equals n R T over V: 0,5 times 8,31 times 300 is 1246,5; divide by 0,01, giving 124 650 pascals — about 124,7 kilopascals.

And the fine print the examiners love: REAL gases obey this beautifully at low pressure and high temperature, and deviate at high pressure and low temperature — exactly the conditions where the particles are crowded close enough for their own volume to matter and slow enough for the forces between them to bite. The ideal gas is a model, and knowing where the model bends is a mark.

Take this section's questions now — the assumptions recited, Celsius banished, both gas calculations reproduced, and the deviation conditions named.

## Subtopic: Stoichiometry — Ratios, Limiting Reagents and Yield

Stoichiometry is a four-step bridge, crossed in the same order every time. Step one: balance the equation — its coefficients are the only legal exchange rates. Step two: convert what you are GIVEN into moles. Step three: cross the bridge — apply the mole ratio from the coefficients. Step four: convert the answer OUT into whatever the question wants.

Cross it once. Hydrogen burns: 2 H 2 plus O 2 gives 2 H 2 O. Given 3 moles of hydrogen with oxygen to spare, how many grams of water form? Ratio: two hydrogens to two waters — one to one. So 3 moles of water. Out: water's molar mass is 18, so 3 times 18, giving 54 grams.

Now the limiting reagent, the year's most feared idea, and it is only a comparison. Suppose the same 3 moles of hydrogen meet exactly 2 moles of oxygen. Ask of one reactant: how much partner does it demand? Three moles of hydrogen, at two-to-one, demand 1,5 moles of oxygen. Available: 2 moles — more than demanded, so oxygen is in EXCESS and hydrogen is the LIMITING reagent. The limiting reagent decides everything downstream: 3 moles of hydrogen still make 3 moles of water, 54 grams, and the excess half mole of oxygen simply remains, unreacted. Every limiting question is that same demand-versus-supply comparison, and ALL later steps use the limiting reagent's moles.

Two percentages finish the toolkit. Percentage yield: actual over theoretical, times 100. If the reaction above delivers only 40,5 grams of water instead of the theoretical 54, the yield is 40,5 over 54, which is 75 percent — real reactions leak product to spills, side reactions and incomplete mixing. Percentage purity runs the same logic on the input side: a 25-gram sample of limestone that is 80 percent pure calcium carbonate contains 20 grams of the real thing — and it is THOSE 20 grams, 0,2 moles, that enter the bridge, never the 25.

This section's questions are ready — the bridge crossed in order, the demand-versus-supply comparison run, and both percentages computed.

## Subtopic: Acids and Bases — Definitions, Pairs and the Titration

The Lowry-Brønsted definitions run this whole topic: an ACID is a proton donor; a BASE is a proton acceptor. One proton — one hydrogen ion — changes hands per event, and tracking it answers every conceptual question.

Watch one event. Hydrochloric acid in water: H C l donates its proton to a water molecule, leaving C l minus and making H 3 O plus, the hydronium ion. Every acid-base event creates two CONJUGATE PAIRS — two species that differ by exactly one proton. H C l and C l minus are one pair; H 3 O plus and H 2 O are the other. The rule for naming them: the acid's leftover is its conjugate base; the base's product is its conjugate acid. And water plays both sides — donating to ammonia, accepting from hydrochloric acid — which makes it an AMPHOLYTE, able to act as acid or base depending on the partner.

Keep two axes apart, because conflating them is the topic's classic error. Strong versus weak is about IONISATION: a strong acid like hydrochloric ionises completely; a weak acid like ethanoic ionises only partially. Concentrated versus dilute is about AMOUNT per volume. A dilute strong acid and a concentrated weak acid are both perfectly possible — the axes are independent.

The reactions, as a family: acid plus metal hydroxide gives salt plus water; acid plus metal oxide gives salt plus water; acid plus metal carbonate gives salt plus water plus carbon dioxide — the fizz that identifies a carbonate on sight.

And the topic's flagship calculation, the titration. The question: 20 cubic centimetres of 0,1 mole per cubic decimetre sodium hydroxide exactly neutralises 25 cubic centimetres of hydrochloric acid — find the acid's concentration. Moles of base: n equals c V, which is 0,1 times 0,020, giving 0,002 moles. The equation H C l plus N a O H gives N a C l plus H 2 O runs one to one, so moles of acid: 0,002. Concentration: 0,002 over 0,025, which is 0,08 moles per cubic decimetre. If the acid had been sulphuric, the ratio would be one acid to TWO base, and skipping that ratio step is the single most harvested titration mark in the paper.

The final questions of this part are with you now — donor and acceptor tracked, pairs named, the two axes separated, and the titration crossed via its ratio.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same revision with the laboratory coat off — a currency exchange, a pancake recipe, and a rugby pass that explains every acid-base question.

## Subtopic: The Mole as the Only Currency Exchange

Picture the paper's quantities as different currencies. Grams are one country's money; cubic decimetres of solution another's; gas volumes a third's. None of them exchange directly — every conversion goes through one central currency: the mole.

The exchange rates are printed on things you are given. The periodic table prices mass: 40 grams buys one mole of sodium hydroxide. The concentration on a flask prices solutions: at 0,4 moles per cubic decimetre, each cubic decimetre holds 0,4 moles. And STP prices gases with the flattest rate in science: 22,4 cubic decimetres per mole, for ANY gas whatsoever — the mole is the great equaliser, counting particles without caring what they are.

The exchange discipline is the whole method: change INTO moles at the first counter, do all business in moles, change OUT at the last counter. A question that gives grams of zinc and asks for cubic decimetres of hydrogen is not hard — it is two exchanges with a balanced equation standing between them, quoting the mole-to-mole rate.

The one queue people skip: volume units. The concentration counter deals only in cubic decimetres, and questions pay in cubic centimetres deliberately. Divide by 1000 at the door — 500 cubic centimetres is 0,5 cubic decimetres — and only then step to the counter. Skipping that queue mis-prices everything that follows by a factor of a thousand.

Your questions for this section are ready — every rate matched to its counter, one full exchange run through the hub, and the volume queue joined first.

## Subtopic: Pancakes and the Empty Shelf

Stoichiometry's dreaded limiting reagent is a kitchen problem you have already solved. The recipe: one cup of flour and two eggs make eight pancakes. Your shelf: three cups of flour and four eggs. How many pancakes?

Instinct says the flour is plenty — three batches' worth. But run the demand check: three cups of flour demand six eggs, and the shelf holds four. The eggs run out first: the EGGS are the limiting ingredient, the flour is in excess, and the kitchen makes exactly two batches — sixteen pancakes — with a cup of flour left on the shelf, useless without partners.

That demand-versus-supply check is the entire limiting-reagent method. In the chemistry version, the balanced equation is the recipe, the coefficients are the cups and eggs, and the moles on your shelf are what the question gives. Pick either reactant, compute how much partner it demands, compare with what is available, and the one that runs short is the limiting reagent. From that moment the limiting reagent does ALL the talking: product amounts, leftover amounts, everything downstream scales from it — just as the pancake count followed the eggs, not the generous flour.

Percentage yield is the kitchen's last honesty: the recipe promised sixteen pancakes, but one tore and one burned, and fourteen reached the plate. Fourteen over sixteen, times 100 — 87,5 percent. Real kitchens and real reactions both leak, and the yield simply measures the leak. Percentage purity is honesty about the shelf instead: if a quarter of the flour bag is actually bran, only the true flour enters the recipe arithmetic.

Take this section's questions now — the demand check run on the shelf, the limiting ingredient named, and both honesty percentages computed.

## Subtopic: The Proton Pass and the Trap List

An acid-base reaction is a rugby pass: the proton is the ball, the acid is the player passing it, the base is the player receiving it. One ball, one pass, two players — that is the entire Lowry-Brønsted picture.

The conjugate pairs are just the players before and after the pass. The passer, ball gone, is the conjugate base — same player, one ball lighter. The receiver, ball in hand, is the conjugate acid — same player, one ball heavier. Two players, four states, differing by exactly one ball: that is why conjugate pairs always differ by exactly one proton. And water is the utility player who can pass or receive depending on the opposition — the ampholyte.

Strong versus weak is passing COMMITMENT: a strong acid releases the ball completely, every molecule, every time; a weak acid mostly keeps hold, releasing only a fraction. Concentrated versus dilute is squad SIZE: how many players per cubic decimetre of field. A small squad of full commitment passers — dilute but strong — plays completely differently from a huge squad that rarely releases — concentrated but weak. Two independent axes, and the examiner's favourite trick is asking about one while tempting you with the other.

Now the trap list, five exhibits with antidotes. One: Celsius fed into a gas law — convert to kelvin first, always, plus 273. Two: cubic centimetres fed into c equals n over V — divide by 1000 at the door. Three: the skipped mole ratio — sulphuric acid demands two hydroxides, and one-to-one assumptions bleed marks. Four: calculating from the EXCESS reagent — the limiting reagent does all the talking. Five: strong confused with concentrated — commitment is not squad size.

And the final audit: every answer gets a sense check. Moles should be modest numbers, not millions; a concentration should survive comparison with what was dissolved; a yield above 100 percent means the theoretical and actual swapped places. A moment of disbelief at your own answer, before the marker gets the chance.

The final questions of the lesson are with you now — the pass tracked, both axes kept apart, five traps dodged, and every answer disbelieved for a moment first.
