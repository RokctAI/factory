**Bianca:** The revision questions that come up every single year — let's take them one by one. First: mole calculations have three formulas, and learners freeze over which one a question wants. What is the selection method?

**Tutor:** Let the measuring instrument in the question choose the formula, because each formula is tied to one instrument. The question mentions a balance or gives grams: n equals m over M, with the molar mass assembled from the periodic table. It mentions a flask, a burette or a solution: c equals n over V, and the volume goes to cubic decimetres before anything else happens. It mentions a gas at STP: n equals V over 22,4. That is the entire decision — no judgement, just instrument-spotting. The deeper habit is remembering that these are not three separate topics but three doors into the same room: the mole. A question giving grams of magnesium and wanting cubic decimetres of hydrogen is two doors used in sequence — in through the mass door, across the balanced equation's ratio, out through the gas door. Learners who see three formulas struggle; learners who see one hub with three doors finish with time to spare. And the sense check at the end: school-scale quantities give modest mole numbers, tenths and units, so a result in the thousands means a unit slipped somewhere upstream.

**Bianca:** Next, the limiting reagent: it is the most avoided question in the paper. Why does it scare people, and what is the reliable method?

**Tutor:** It scares people because instinct says the smaller pile runs out first, and stoichiometry says otherwise — the RATIO decides, not the size. The reliable method is one comparison, always the same: pick either reactant, use the balanced equation's coefficients to compute how much partner it demands, and compare demand with supply. Three moles of hydrogen in the two-to-one hydrogen-oxygen reaction demand 1,5 moles of oxygen; if two moles are on the shelf, oxygen is in excess and hydrogen limits — even though there is less oxygen than hydrogen. That is the pancake logic: four eggs limit the baking even when three generous cups of flour stand beside them, because the recipe demands two eggs per cup. Then the rule that earns the follow-through marks: once identified, the limiting reagent does all the talking — every product amount and every leftover calculation scales from ITS moles, and touching the excess reagent's moles afterwards is the error the memo is built to catch. Run the demand check in writing, one line, label the limiting reagent explicitly, and the rest of the question is ordinary bridge-crossing.

**Bianca:** The acids and bases theory: conjugate pairs, ampholytes, strong against concentrated — the vocabulary blurs under pressure. How should it be organised?

**Tutor:** Organise it around the single event the definitions describe: one proton changing hands. The acid donates it, the base accepts it — Lowry-Brønsted in one sentence. Conjugate pairs follow mechanically: after the pass, the acid minus its proton is a conjugate base, the base plus its proton is a conjugate acid, so every pair differs by exactly one proton — write the two formulas and check the difference is one hydrogen and one unit of charge, nothing else. The ampholyte is just the species that can play either role depending on the partner, and water is the exam's example: donor to ammonia, acceptor from hydrochloric acid. Then keep the two independent axes on separate shelves, because the examiner deliberately crosses them: strong versus weak is completeness of ionisation — a property of the substance's nature; concentrated versus dilute is amount per volume — a property of how the solution was made. A dilute solution of a strong acid and a concentrated solution of a weak acid are both everyday objects, and being able to say WHY they differ — every molecule ionised in the first, a small fraction in the second — is precisely the two-mark explanation the paper asks for.

**Bianca:** Last one: any advice for structuring these final weeks of chemistry revision — what genuinely moves marks?

**Tutor:** Three shifts, in order of payoff. First, drill the conversion reflexes often rather than in one long push: a short set of mixed mole conversions — mass, solution, gas, in both directions — done regularly installs the hub discipline deeper than any single marathon, because the skill sits inside more than half of the paper's calculation marks. Second, rehearse the two set-piece calculations until their shape is automatic: the limiting-reagent demand check with its one-line comparison, and the titration chain — moles of the known from c times V, across the ratio, out to the unknown concentration — including the diprotic version with sulphuric acid, since the skipped ratio is the single most harvested mark in the topic. Third, keep a personal trap list in your own handwriting and read it before every practice set: kelvin not Celsius, cubic decimetres at the door, the ratio step, the excess reagent silenced, strong kept apart from concentrated. Then close every answer with a moment of disbelief — a yield over 100 percent or a concentration wildly above what was dissolved is an error announcing itself while you can still fix it. Regular reflexes, automatic set pieces, a read-and-checked trap list: those three carry more marks than any amount of rereading notes.
