Hey, it's Bianca — redox has a reputation, and I know why: oxidation numbers, agents that seem named backwards, half-reactions, a data-sheet table... it looks like a topic built entirely out of traps. Here is the secret: it is a bookkeeping exercise, and bookkeeping is just careful, boring steps. Boring steps are learnable.

Start with the salt-preparation piece, because it is the friendly part: read the salt's name like a full name. First name is the metal — that came from the base. Surname is the acid's family — nitrate from nitric, sulphate from sulphuric, chloride from hydrochloric. Base plus acid gives salt plus water, and if the base was a carbonate, add the fizz of carbon dioxide. Decoded, not memorised.

Now the electron bookkeeping, in three moves. Move one: assign oxidation numbers by the short rule list — elements zero, ions their charge, hydrogen plus one, oxygen minus two — and let the sum-to-zero rule solve the unknown atom. Don't panic about the exceptions; the sum rule CATCHES them for you. If oxygen at minus two breaks the total, it is a peroxide, and the arithmetic tells you so.

Move two: compare before and after. Number went UP: electrons lost, oxidised. Number went DOWN: electrons gained, reduced. OIL RIG. And the agents: say the rhythm five times tonight — oxidising agent gets reduced, reducing agent gets oxidised. The agent is named for what it does to the other one, like a pickpocket named by the victim's loss.

Move three: half-reactions. Copy both from the table. Reverse the one that is donating. Multiply until the electron counts match — lowest common multiple, like adding fractions. Add, cancel the electrons, then audit: atoms balanced, charges balanced, zero loose electrons. That audit finds your mistakes before the marker does.

Full names for salts, three moves for redox, one rhythm for the agents. Every question in this topic is one of those, wearing different chemicals. You've got this!
