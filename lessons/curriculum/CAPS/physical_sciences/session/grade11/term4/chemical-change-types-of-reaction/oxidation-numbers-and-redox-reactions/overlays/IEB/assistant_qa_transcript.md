**Bianca:** The questions that come up again and again on this topic — starting with the one that feels like a trick: the oxidising agent is the species that ends up REDUCED? Every learner reads that as a misprint. What is the reliable way to keep it straight?

**Tutor:** Remember whose story the name tells: an agent is titled for what it does to its PARTNER, never for what happens to itself. An oxidising agent is defined as the substance that causes oxidation in the other species — and the only way to force another species to lose electrons is to take those electrons, which means gaining them, which means being reduced. In the copper-and-silver demonstration, the silver ions strip electrons off the copper wire; that act oxidises the copper, so the silver ion earns the title oxidising agent — and in earning it, each silver ion is reduced from plus one to silver metal at zero. Copper, symmetrically, donates the electrons that reduce the silver: reducing agent, itself oxidised. When pressure scrambles the logic, fall back on the rhythm — the oxidising agent is reduced; the reducing agent is oxidised — because it never fails and takes two seconds. And a working habit for identification questions: find the oxidation-number changes FIRST, who went up and who went down, and only then attach the agent names. Numbers to names is mechanical; guessing names directly is where the inversions breed.

***

**Bianca:** Next, the numbers themselves. Learners want to know if oxidation numbers are real — is the manganese in potassium permanganate genuinely carrying a seven plus charge?

**Tutor:** No, and seeing why not is what makes the tool trustworthy. Permanganate is held together by shared electron pairs; no atom in it carries seven full units of charge. The oxidation number comes from a deliberate accounting fiction: we ask what each atom's charge WOULD be if every shared pair were awarded outright to the more electronegative partner. Oxygen outranks manganese, so the bookkeeping hands all the shared electrons to the oxygens, and manganese is notionally stripped to plus seven. The fiction earns its keep through consistency: apply it the same way before and after a reaction, and any CHANGE in the numbers faithfully reports the real direction of electron drift, even though each individual number exaggerates. Think of it like crediting a shared bank account entirely to one account holder — unfair as a snapshot, but if the credited amount changes between two audits, money genuinely moved. There is one situation where the fiction becomes literal truth: ionic compounds, where electrons really are transferred outright, so sodium's oxidation number of plus one and its actual ionic charge coincide. Everywhere else, treat the numbers as reliable movement detectors, not photographs.

***

**Bianca:** Here is the panic point: learners memorise "oxygen is minus two", then a peroxide like Na2O2 turns up in a test and the whole system seems to collapse. How should the exceptions be managed?

**Tutor:** Stop protecting the exceptions with memory and let the sum rule police them, because it detects every one automatically. The rules run in a hierarchy — free elements at zero, simple ions at their charge, then fluorine, then hydrogen, then oxygen — closed by the law that everything must total zero in a neutral compound or the ion's charge in an ion. Now watch sodium peroxide confess. Two sodiums at plus one contribute plus two. If each oxygen took its usual minus two, the pair would contribute minus four and the compound would total minus two — impossible for a neutral formula. The books refuse to close, so the oxygens must sit at minus one each: plus two, minus two, zero. You never needed to recall the peroxide exception; arithmetic forced it into view. Hydrides work identically: in sodium hydride, sodium's plus one leaves hydrogen no option but minus one. So the examinable habit is: assign the confident atoms in hierarchy order, leave the stranger for last, and solve it with the sum rule — and if a "usual" value ever breaks the total, an exception is announcing itself. The sulphate ion shows the same solver working on ions: minus eight from the oxygens against a total of minus two forces sulphur to plus six, a number nobody guesses from habit.

***

**Bianca:** Last one: in the half-reaction method, why must the electron counts be matched before adding? A learner combined the magnesium and iron half-reactions directly and the charge balance failed.

**Tutor:** Because electrons cannot be left in stock — every electron the oxidation releases must be consumed by the reduction, exactly and immediately. Magnesium's line releases TWO electrons per atom; iron three plus collects THREE per ion. Add the lines unmodified and two electrons arrive where three are wanted: the shortfall appears as precisely the charge imbalance your learner found. The repair is the lowest common multiple, six. Run magnesium's line three times — three atoms releasing six electrons — and iron's line twice — two ions collecting six. Supply now equals demand, the six electrons cancel completely in the addition, and the result, three magnesium plus two iron three-plus gives three magnesium two-plus plus two iron, carries no electrons at all. Then always close with the audit, because it catches every slip of this family: count atoms — three magnesium, two iron, both sides — and total the charge — plus six on the left from the iron ions, plus six on the right from the magnesium ions. Atoms balanced, charge balanced, no loose electrons: only then is the equation finished. And do not forget the step that precedes all of this: the table prints every half-reaction as a reduction, so the actual donor's line must be REVERSED before use — the forgotten reversal and the unmatched electrons account for nearly all half-reaction errors between them.
