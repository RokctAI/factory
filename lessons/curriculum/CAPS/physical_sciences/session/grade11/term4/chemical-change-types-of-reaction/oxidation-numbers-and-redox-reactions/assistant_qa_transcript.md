**Bianca:** The questions that come up a lot on this topic, and the first one is the naming inversion that catches everyone: the oxidising agent is the thing that gets REDUCED? That sounds exactly backwards. How is anyone supposed to keep it straight?

**Tutor:** Keep it straight by remembering who the name describes: the agent is named for what it does to its PARTNER, not for what happens to itself. An oxidising agent is a substance that causes oxidation in the other species — and the only way to force someone else to lose electrons is to take those electrons yourself, which means you gain them, which means you are reduced. Copper ions in the zinc demonstration pull zinc's electrons away; that act oxidises the zinc, so the copper ion earns the title oxidising agent, and in earning it, it is reduced from plus two to copper metal at zero. Zinc, symmetrically, donates the electrons that reduce the copper — reducing agent, itself oxidised. If the logic ever deserts you in the exam, fall back on the rhythm: the oxidising agent is reduced; the reducing agent is oxidised. It never fails, and it is fast. And one practical tip for identification questions: find the oxidation-number changes FIRST — who went up, who went down — and only then assign the agent names. Working from the numbers to the names is mechanical; trying to intuit the names directly is where the inversions creep in.

***

**Bianca:** Next: oxidation numbers themselves. Learners ask whether the numbers are real charges — is the carbon in CO2 really carrying a four plus charge?

**Tutor:** No, and understanding why not makes the whole tool trustworthy. Carbon dioxide is a covalent molecule: the carbon and oxygen SHARE electron pairs, and no atom in it carries a full four-unit charge. The oxidation number comes from a deliberate accounting fiction: we ask what charge each atom WOULD have if every shared pair were awarded entirely to the more electronegative atom of the bond. Oxygen beats carbon in electronegativity, so the bookkeeping hands all the shared electrons to the oxygens, leaving carbon notionally stripped of four — hence plus four. The fiction is useful precisely because it is applied consistently: when a reaction happens and the oxidation numbers shift, that shift faithfully tracks the real direction of electron drift, even though the individual numbers exaggerate reality. It is like valuing a shared house entirely to whichever housemate earns more — unfair as a description, but if the valuation changes between two audits, money genuinely moved. So treat oxidation numbers as flags on a map: not photographs of the terrain, but reliable indicators of movement. In ionic compounds, where electrons really are transferred outright, the oxidation number and the actual charge coincide — sodium's plus one is both — which is the special case where the fiction becomes fact.

**Bianca:** Here's the peroxide panic: learners memorise "oxygen is minus two", then H2O2 appears in a test and everything collapses. How should the exceptions be handled?

**Tutor:** By demoting memory and promoting the sum rule, because the sum rule detects the exceptions on its own. The rules have a hierarchy: free elements at zero, ions at their charge, then fluorine, then hydrogen, then oxygen — and the closing law that everything must total zero for a neutral compound or the ion's charge for an ion. Now watch hydrogen peroxide expose itself. H2O2: hydrogen takes its usual plus one, giving plus two across the two hydrogens. If oxygen took its usual minus two, the two oxygens would supply minus four, and the compound would total minus two — impossible for a neutral molecule. The books refuse to close. So the oxygens must sit at minus one each: plus two, minus two, zero — closed. You did not need to remember the peroxide exception; the arithmetic forced it on you. Same with hydrides: in sodium hydride, sodium's plus one forces hydrogen to minus one. The examinable habit is therefore: assign the confident atoms first by the hierarchy, then let the sum rule solve for the last unknown, and if a "usual" value breaks the sum, the exception is announcing itself. HOCl is the elegant showcase — hydrogen plus one, oxygen minus two, and the sum rule leaves chlorine at PLUS one, a positive halogen, which no amount of memorising typical values would have predicted.

**Bianca:** Last one: in the half-reaction method, why must the electrons be multiplied to match before adding? A learner combined aluminium and copper half-reactions directly and the charges came out wrong.

**Tutor:** Because electrons are not a substance you can leave in stock — every electron released by the oxidation must be consumed by the reduction, immediately and exactly. Aluminium's half-reaction releases THREE electrons per atom; copper's collects TWO per ion. Add those lines as they stand and three electrons arrive where only two are wanted: one electron is left homeless in the equation, and its charge imbalance is exactly the error your learner saw. The fix is the lowest common multiple: six. Run the aluminium line twice — two aluminium atoms releasing six electrons — and the copper line three times — three copper ions collecting six. Now supply equals demand, the six electrons cancel completely in the addition, and the final equation, two aluminium plus three copper two-plus gives two aluminium three-plus plus three copper, carries no electrons at all. Then always run the closing audit, because it catches every slip of this kind: count atoms — two aluminium, three copper, both sides — and total the charge — plus six on the left from the copper ions, plus six on the right from the aluminium ions. Atoms balanced, charge balanced, no loose electrons: only then is a redox equation finished. And remember the step before all of this: the table gives every half-reaction as a reduction, so the species that is actually donating must have its line REVERSED first — forgetting the reversal is the other half of all half-reaction errors.
