# Part 1 — Expert

Term 4 closes the chemistry year with the second great family of reactions. Acid-base chemistry, completed here with the deliberate design of salts, moves PROTONS. Redox chemistry — the topic that carries Grade 12 electrochemistry, batteries and electrolysis — moves ELECTRONS. This session finishes the proton story, then builds the electron bookkeeping system called oxidation numbers, defines oxidation and reduction properly, and closes with the half-reaction method for balancing redox equations from the table of standard reduction potentials.

## Subtopic: Choosing an Acid and a Base to Prepare a Given Salt

Every salt has two parents, readable from its name: the positive ion comes from a base, the negative ion from an acid. Salt preparation questions run this backwards — name the salt, and you must select the parents and write the neutralisation equation.

The selection rules. The anion decides the acid: chloride demands hydrochloric acid, nitrate demands nitric, sulphate demands sulphuric, ethanoate demands ethanoic. The cation decides the base — a hydroxide, an oxide or a carbonate carrying the right metal: sodium hydroxide for sodium salts, potassium hydroxide for potassium, copper oxide for copper, calcium carbonate for calcium.

Run it forward through the three base types. Potassium nitrate: KOH plus HNO3 gives KNO3 plus H2O — potassium hydroxide plus nitric acid gives potassium nitrate plus water. Copper sulphate, the blue salt of the laboratory: CuO plus H2SO4 gives CuSO4 plus H2O — copper oxide plus sulphuric acid gives copper sulphate plus water. Calcium chloride from a carbonate: CaCO3 plus 2HCl gives CaCl2 plus H2O plus CO2 — calcium carbonate plus two hydrochloric acid gives calcium chloride, water and carbon dioxide, with the carbonate's signature fizz.

Balance with the ion charges in view. A diprotic acid neutralising a single-hydroxide base needs two of the base: 2NaOH plus H2SO4 gives Na2SO4 plus 2H2O. A single-charge anion meeting a double-charge cation doubles instead: CaCO3 plus 2HCl. Write the salt's formula first, from its charges, and the coefficients follow.

This section's questions are with you now: parents from the name, equations for all three base types, and charges driving the balancing.

## Subtopic: Oxidation Numbers and the Rules for Assigning Them

Redox reactions transfer electrons, but electrons are invisible in a formula. The OXIDATION NUMBER is the accounting device that makes them visible: the charge an atom WOULD carry if every bond's electrons were awarded entirely to the more electronegative atom. It is bookkeeping, not a real charge — but the books never lie about electron movement.

The rules, in order of authority. Rule one: any free element has oxidation number zero — the atoms in O2, H2, Na, all zero. Rule two: a simple ion's oxidation number is its charge — sodium ion plus one, chloride minus one. Rule three: fluorine in a compound is always minus one. Rule four: hydrogen in a compound is plus one, except bonded to a metal in a hydride, where it is minus one. Rule five: oxygen in a compound is minus two, except in peroxides, where it is minus one. Rule six, the closing rule: the oxidation numbers in a neutral compound sum to zero; in a polyatomic ion, to the ion's charge.

Now the syllabus's own practice set, worked aloud. Water, H2O: two hydrogens at plus one is plus two, so oxygen must be minus two — sum zero. Methane, CH4: four hydrogens at plus one is plus four, so carbon is MINUS four. Carbon dioxide, CO2: two oxygens at minus two is minus four, so carbon is PLUS four — the same element ranging from minus four to plus four, which is exactly why carbon chemistry is so rich. Hydrogen peroxide, H2O2: two hydrogens at plus one force the two oxygens to minus one each — the peroxide exception in action. And HOCl, hypochlorous acid, the pool sanitiser: hydrogen plus one, oxygen minus two, so chlorine must be PLUS one — chlorine positive, because it stands next to the more electronegative oxygen.

The discipline: assign by rule hierarchy, then let the sum rule solve for the stranger. The questions for this section are ready — the rules, the two exceptions, and the practice set.

## Subtopic: Redox as Electron Transfer — Agents and Their Names

The definitions, exact. OXIDATION is the LOSS of electrons; REDUCTION is the GAIN of electrons. The memory aid is OIL RIG — oxidation is loss, reduction is gain. In oxidation-number language: oxidation raises the oxidation number; reduction lowers it. A REDOX reaction is one in which oxidation numbers change — electrons actually move from one species to another.

Watch a strip of zinc in blue copper sulphate solution — the classic demonstration. The zinc darkens with a coat of copper, the blue fades, and the equation is: Zn plus Cu two plus gives Zn two plus, plus Cu — zinc metal plus copper ions give zinc ions plus copper metal. Audit the books: zinc went from zero to plus two — it LOST two electrons — oxidation. Copper went from plus two to zero — it GAINED two electrons — reduction. The two changes are simultaneous and inseparable: every electron lost is an electron gained, which is why the reaction is called red-ox and why one never happens without the other.

Now the naming that inverts in everyone's head. The OXIDISING AGENT is the species that CAUSES oxidation in the other — which means it takes the electrons, and is itself REDUCED. Here, the copper ion. The REDUCING AGENT causes reduction in the other by donating electrons, and is itself OXIDISED — here, the zinc. Say it as a rhythm: the oxidising agent is reduced; the reducing agent is oxidised. The agent is named for what it does to its PARTNER, like a referee who names the fouler, not the fouled.

Spot-check with oxidation numbers even when no charges are visible. Methane burning: CH4 plus 2O2 gives CO2 plus 2H2O. Carbon rises from minus four to plus four — oxidised. Oxygen falls from zero to minus two — reduced. Combustion is redox; so is rust; so is every battery you own.

Take this section's questions now: OIL RIG, the number audit, and the agents named without inversion.

## Subtopic: Balancing Redox Equations with Half-Reactions

A redox reaction is two transactions stapled together, and the half-reaction method staples them properly. Each HALF-REACTION shows one side of the electron trade: the oxidation half shows the donor releasing electrons; the reduction half shows the acceptor collecting them. The TABLE OF STANDARD REDUCTION POTENTIALS in your data sheet lists half-reactions, all written as reductions — ion plus electrons gives element. To use one as an oxidation, reverse it.

The method, four steps. Step one: write the two half-reactions from the table, reversing the one that must run as an oxidation. Step two: balance the ELECTRONS — multiply each half-reaction until both involve the same number, because electrons given must equal electrons taken; no electron survives loose. Step three: add the half-reactions and cancel the electrons. Step four: audit — atoms balanced, total charge balanced on both sides.

Work aluminium metal reducing copper ions. From the table: Al three plus, plus three electrons gives Al — but aluminium is the donor here, so reverse it: Al gives Al three plus, plus three electrons. Copper's half stays as a reduction: Cu two plus, plus two electrons gives Cu. Electrons: three against two — lowest common multiple six. Multiply the aluminium half by two: 2Al gives 2Al three plus, plus six electrons. Multiply the copper half by three: 3Cu two plus, plus six electrons gives 3Cu. Add and cancel the six electrons: 2Al plus 3Cu two plus gives 2Al three plus, plus 3Cu — two aluminium atoms plus three copper ions give two aluminium ions plus three copper atoms. Audit: aluminium two and two; copper three and three; charge, plus six on the left, plus six on the right. Balanced.

Name the roles from the finished equation: aluminium lost electrons — oxidised, the reducing agent; copper ions gained — reduced, the oxidising agent. The commonest errors: forgetting to reverse the oxidation half; adding the equations before the electron counts match; and leaving electrons visible in the final answer, where they must never appear.

The final questions of this part are with you now: table, reverse, match, add, audit.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same chemistry as money: names on accounts, and coins changing pockets.

## Subtopic: Assembling a Salt Like a Full Name

A salt's name is a full name: first name from one parent, surname from the other. The metal up front is the base's contribution; the family name at the back belongs to an acid. So when an exam says "describe how you would prepare potassium nitrate", it is a parentage question: potassium — so the base is potassium hydroxide; nitrate — so the acid is nitric. Mix, and the equation writes itself: base plus acid gives the salt plus water.

The base can arrive in three costumes, and the products barely change. A hydroxide, like potassium hydroxide: salt plus water. An oxide, like the black copper oxide powder that turns sulphuric acid into brilliant blue copper sulphate: salt plus water again. Or a carbonate, like the chalky calcium carbonate: salt plus water PLUS a fizz of carbon dioxide — the carbonate always tips a gas on the way out. Three costumes, one pattern, one optional fizz.

Balancing is charge arithmetic, not guesswork. Write the salt's formula first from its ion charges: calcium is two plus, chloride one minus, so calcium chloride must be CaCl2 — and the 2 in front of the HCl follows immediately, because two chlorides need two deliveries. Sulphuric acid carries two hydrogens, so it needs two hydroxides to soak them up: the 2 in front of NaOH is the acid's second hydrogen made visible.

First name, surname, three costumes, charges first. That is every salt-preparation question in the paper. Your questions for this section are with you now.

## Subtopic: Electron Bookkeeping — Every Atom Gets an Account

Chemistry's second family of reactions is not about protons at all — it is about ELECTRONS changing hands. Trouble is, formulas do not show electrons moving. So chemists run a bookkeeping system: every atom in every formula gets an account balance, called its oxidation number, and by comparing balances before and after a reaction, you can see exactly who paid and who collected.

The account rules are few. An element on its own — pure zinc, oxygen gas — stands at zero: no trades yet. A simple ion's balance is its charge. In compounds, the regulars have fixed habits: hydrogen sits at plus one, oxygen at minus two — with two famous exceptions, hydrogen at minus one next to a metal, oxygen at minus one in peroxides. And the golden closing rule: every neutral compound's balances sum to ZERO, and an ion's sum to its charge. That closing rule is the solver — fill in the atoms you know, and the unknown account reveals itself.

Watch it work. Water: two hydrogens at plus one, so oxygen must sit at minus two to zero the books. Methane, CH4: four hydrogens at plus one force carbon down to minus four. Burn that methane into CO2, and carbon is suddenly at PLUS four — the same atom swung eight units, which is bookkeeping's way of saying combustion moved serious electrons. Hydrogen peroxide: the hydrogens' plus two forces the two oxygens to minus one each — the exception, caught by the sum rule. Pool chlorine, HOCl: hydrogen plus one, oxygen minus two, so chlorine must sit at plus one to close the books.

No memorising of answers — just rules, then arithmetic. Balances up mean electrons lost; balances down mean electrons gained; and that is the next section's whole story. Your questions on the bookkeeping are ready now.

## Subtopic: Coins Change Pockets — and the Trade Must Balance

Now read a reaction like a transaction. Drop a zinc strip into blue copper solution: the strip grows a copper coat, the blue drains away. The books say: zinc went from zero to plus two — it PAID two electrons. Copper ions went from plus two to zero — they COLLECTED two. Oxidation is paying — losing electrons. Reduction is collecting — gaining them. OIL RIG: oxidation is loss, reduction is gain. And no payment ever vanishes: every electron paid is collected by someone, which is why oxidation and reduction only ever happen together, and the double event is called redox.

The naming trick that trips everyone, fixed with one image: agents are named for what they do to the OTHER party. The copper ion, by taking zinc's electrons, drove zinc's oxidation — so copper is the OXIDISING agent, and it got reduced. Zinc, by donating its electrons, drove copper's reduction — the REDUCING agent, itself oxidised. The pickpocket is named by the victim's loss. Say the rhythm until it is boring: oxidising agent gets reduced, reducing agent gets oxidised.

Balancing a redox equation is making the money add up. Your data sheet's table lists every trader's collecting habit — half-reactions, all written as collections. Pick the two traders. The one who is actually PAYING gets his line reversed. Then match the amounts: aluminium pays in threes, copper collects in twos — so run two aluminiums and three coppers, six electrons each way, a clean trade. Add the lines, cancel the six coins that crossed, and the final equation shows no loose electrons at all: two aluminium plus three copper ions gives two aluminium ions plus three copper. Atoms balance, charges balance, books closed.

That closing audit is the whole discipline of redox, and next year's batteries are nothing but this trade run through a wire so the passing coins can light a torch on the way. The last questions of the lesson are yours now — balances first, payer and collector named, and never leave a coin lying in the equation.
