# Part 1 — Expert

Paper 2 opens its chemistry with a deceptively simple question: why do atoms bond at all, and what shapes do the resulting molecules take? This session builds the covalent bond from energy first principles, drills the Lewis diagrams the syllabus names, derives molecular shapes from electron-pair repulsion, and ends with electronegativity — the number that decides whether a bond shares fairly, shares unfairly, or gives up sharing altogether. Every later chemistry topic, from intermolecular forces to organic reactions, stands on this one.

## Subtopic: Why Atoms Bond, and the Lewis Diagram Toolkit

A covalent bond is the sharing of at least one pair of electrons by two atoms. Atoms bond because bonding LOWERS their energy. Picture two hydrogen atoms approaching from far apart. At large distance they barely interact. As they close in, each nucleus begins to attract the other atom's electron, and the potential energy drops. At a separation of 74 picometres the energy reaches its minimum — the sweet spot where attraction and internuclear repulsion balance — and that separation is the BOND LENGTH of the hydrogen molecule. Push closer and the two positive nuclei repel each other fiercely, and the energy climbs steeply. The depth of the energy valley is the BOND ENERGY: the energy released on forming the bond, and the same energy needed to break it. Two helium atoms never find such a valley — each helium already holds a filled shell of two electrons, gains no lowering of energy by sharing, and so no bond forms. That contrast, hydrogen bonds while helium does not, is the syllabus's chosen illustration that bonding is about energy, not about sociability.

Lewis diagrams draw the valence electrons — the outermost electrons, the only ones involved in bonding — as dots or crosses around each atom's symbol. The rules: count each atom's valence electrons from its group number; pair up shared electrons between the symbols to form bonds; distribute the rest as LONE PAIRS, aiming for eight electrons around each atom, two for hydrogen. Water: oxygen brings six valence electrons, each hydrogen one; two shared pairs bond the hydrogens, and two lone pairs remain on the oxygen. Ammonia: nitrogen's five valence electrons give three bonds to hydrogen and one lone pair. Methane: carbon's four electrons make four bonds, no lone pairs.

Multiple bonds arise when one shared pair is not enough. Nitrogen, with five valence electrons each, shares THREE pairs — a triple bond — leaving one lone pair per atom. Oxygen shares two pairs, a double bond. Hydrogen cyanide, H C N, bonds hydrogen to carbon with a single pair and carbon to nitrogen with a triple bond. And in the DATIVE covalent bond, one atom supplies BOTH electrons of the shared pair: the hydronium ion forms when a water molecule's lone pair captures a bare hydrogen ion, and the ammonium ion forms the same way from ammonia. Once formed, a dative bond is indistinguishable from any other covalent bond.

Attempt this section's questions now — count valence electrons from the group number, and account for every electron in the diagram.

## Subtopic: Molecular Shape from Electron-Pair Repulsion

Molecules are three-dimensional, and their shapes are predicted by one idea: valence shell electron pair repulsion. Electron pairs around a central atom all carry negative charge, so they repel one another and settle into the arrangement that places them as far apart as possible. Count the electron pairs around the central atom, spread them out, and the shape follows.

Two pairs around the centre spread to opposite sides: a LINEAR molecule, 180 degrees, like carbon dioxide, whose two double bonds count as one repulsion group each. Three bonding pairs spread into a flat triangle: TRIGONAL PLANAR, 120 degrees, like boron trifluoride. Four bonding pairs spread into the corners of a tetrahedron: TETRAHEDRAL, 109,5 degrees, like methane — and note that the flat cross you draw on paper is a lie of convenience; methane is a three-legged stool with a fourth leg pointing up.

Lone pairs occupy positions too, but invisibly — the SHAPE is named after the atoms only. Ammonia holds four pairs in the tetrahedral arrangement, but one is a lone pair, so the visible shape is a TRIGONAL PYRAMID: nitrogen at the apex, three hydrogens below. Water holds four pairs of which two are lone pairs, leaving the visible shape ANGULAR, or bent. And because lone pairs sit closer to their atom, they repel harder than bonding pairs, squeezing the bond angles down: from methane's 109,5 degrees to about 107 in ammonia and about 104,5 in water. The sequence methane, ammonia, water — same four pairs, shrinking angles — is the examiner's favourite comparison.

The method, always: draw the Lewis diagram first, count bonding groups and lone pairs on the central atom, spread all the pairs, then name the shape by the atoms alone. A multiple bond counts as a single group.

This section's questions are with you now — Lewis first, count second, name last.

## Subtopic: Electronegativity and the Polarity of Bonds

Electronegativity is a measure of an atom's tendency to attract the shared electron pair in a chemical bond. It is a unitless number on the Pauling scale, and the values are on your data sheet: fluorine the champion at 4,0, oxygen at 3,5, nitrogen and chlorine at 3,0, carbon at 2,5, hydrogen at 2,1, sodium down at 0,9. Across a period electronegativity rises; down a group it falls.

The DIFFERENCE in electronegativity between two bonded atoms — delta E N — decides how the pair is shared. Difference of zero: the pair sits exactly midway, and the bond is NON-POLAR covalent. Hydrogen with hydrogen, or any atom with its twin, shares perfectly. Small differences: the pair spends more time near the more electronegative atom, which becomes slightly negative — delta minus — while its partner becomes slightly positive — delta plus. That is a POLAR covalent bond, a bond with two charged ends, called a dipole. Hydrogen chloride: chlorine 3,0 minus hydrogen 2,1 gives 0,9, so chlorine carries the delta minus. Water's oxygen-hydrogen bonds: 3,5 minus 2,1 gives 1,4, strongly polar. And when the difference grows very large — around 2 and beyond — the tug becomes a takeover: the electron pair transfers completely, forming ions, and the bond is IONIC. Sodium chloride: 3,0 minus 0,9 gives 2,1, and sodium hands its electron over entirely.

The picture to carry: bonding is a continuous slide from perfect sharing through unfair sharing to outright transfer, and delta E N is the position marker on that slide. There is no cliff between covalent and ionic, only a gradient.

The examinable skills: rank given bonds by polarity using data-sheet values, assign delta plus and delta minus to the correct ends, and classify a bond as non-polar covalent, polar covalent or ionic from its difference.

Work through this section's questions now, subtracting the smaller electronegativity from the larger before classifying.

## Subtopic: Polar Molecules, Bond Energy and Bond Length

A polar BOND does not guarantee a polar MOLECULE — shape casts the deciding vote, and this distinction is a guaranteed exam question.

Carbon dioxide contains two polar carbon-oxygen bonds, each oxygen pulling delta minus. But the molecule is LINEAR: the two bond dipoles point in exactly opposite directions, equal in strength, and they cancel like a perfectly balanced tug-of-war. Net result: a NON-POLAR molecule built from polar bonds. Water also has two polar bonds — but water is ANGULAR. Its two dipoles point partly in the same direction, like two people pulling a rope at an angle, and they add instead of cancelling. The oxygen side of the molecule is genuinely negative, the hydrogen side genuinely positive: water is a POLAR molecule. The same logic runs through the shapes: symmetric molecules — linear, trigonal planar, tetrahedral with identical outer atoms, like methane — cancel their dipoles and come out non-polar; asymmetric molecules — angular water, pyramidal ammonia, or a tetrahedron with mixed corners like chloromethane — keep a net dipole and are polar.

Why it matters: molecular polarity controls the intermolecular forces of the next topic, and through them boiling points, solubility and the whole behaviour of liquids. The sentence to memorise: a molecule is polar only if it contains polar bonds AND its shape does not cancel them.

Finally, bond strength. BOND LENGTH is the distance between the nuclei of two bonded atoms at the energy minimum; BOND ENERGY is the energy needed to break the bond. The two run opposite: shorter bonds are stronger bonds. Two trends govern them. More shared pairs pull atoms closer: carbon-carbon single, double and triple bonds get progressively shorter and stronger — the triple in nitrogen gas is why the atmosphere's nitrogen is so famously unreactive. And bigger atoms bond at longer distances: their valence shells sit further from the nucleus, so down a group the bonds to hydrogen grow longer and weaker.

The final questions of this part are in front of you now — polar bonds plus uncancelled shape equals polar molecule; more bonds and smaller atoms mean shorter, stronger bonds.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same chemistry told with balloons, tug-of-war ropes and an unfair playground.

## Subtopic: Why Atoms Hold Hands

Why does hydrogen gas come as H two — pairs of atoms holding hands — while helium drifts alone forever? The answer is comfort. Atoms bond when bonding makes them more comfortable, and comfort in physics means LOWER ENERGY.

Picture two hydrogen atoms drifting toward each other, each carrying one lonely electron in a shell built for two. As they approach, something appealing happens: each nucleus starts to enjoy the other atom's electron as well as its own. Sharing both electrons between them, both atoms get to feel like their shell is full. Energy drops, like a ball rolling into a valley, and at the bottom of the valley they settle — close enough to share, not so close that the two positive nuclei start shoving each other apart. That settling distance is the bond length, and the depth of the valley is the bond energy: the deeper the valley, the harder it is to pull the pair apart again.

Now try the same trick with two helium atoms. Each already owns a full shell of two electrons. Sharing offers them nothing — no energy drop, no valley, no reason to stay. They bounce past each other and drift on. That is the whole secret of bonding: it happens when it pays, and it pays in lowered energy, not in friendship.

Chemists keep track of the sharing with little dot diagrams — each atom's outer electrons drawn as dots, shared pairs sitting between the atoms like held hands, unshared pairs resting on their owner like folded arms. Some atoms hold one hand, some hold two or three at once: nitrogen grips nitrogen with a triple handshake, which is why nitrogen pairs are almost impossible to separate.

Your questions on this section are with you now — bonding is a ball rolling into an energy valley, nothing more mysterious.

## Subtopic: Balloons Tied at the Centre

Tie four balloons together at their necks and let go: they spring into a perfect three-legged stool with one balloon up — a tetrahedron. Not because balloons know geometry, but because each balloon shoves the others as far away as it can. Electron pairs around an atom do exactly the same, for the same reason: they all carry negative charge, they all repel, and they settle where the shoving balances.

That single idea gives every shape in the syllabus. Two balloons: they point opposite ways — a straight, LINEAR molecule, like carbon dioxide. Three: a flat triangle. Four: the tetrahedron, which is methane's true shape — the flat cross you draw in your book is just the shadow of the real three-dimensional stool, with angles of 109,5 degrees.

Now the twist that decides exam questions: some balloons are invisible. A lone pair — two electrons that belong to the central atom but bond nothing — still shoves like a balloon, but when you look at the molecule you only see ATOMS. Ammonia has four balloons, one invisible: what you see is a pyramid with nitrogen on top. Water has four balloons, two invisible: what you see is just a bent line of three atoms — angular. And because lone pairs hug their atom closer, they shove a little harder than bonding pairs, squeezing the visible angles: 109,5 in methane, about 107 in ammonia, about 104,5 in water. Same four balloons every time; the angle shrinks as more of them go invisible.

So the routine is: draw the dots, count ALL the pairs on the central atom, arrange them like balloons, then name only what is visible.

The questions for this section are coming to you now — balloons shove, lone pairs shove harder, name what you can see.

## Subtopic: Fair and Unfair Tug-of-War

Every covalent bond is a tug-of-war over a shared pair of electrons, and the number that measures pulling strength is electronegativity — every atom's score is printed on your data sheet, from sodium's feeble 0,9 up to fluorine's mighty 4,0.

Identical twins pull equally. Hydrogen against hydrogen, the rope's flag sits exactly in the middle, and the bond is NON-POLAR — a fair game. Mismatched players make an unfair game. Chlorine at 3,0 against hydrogen at 2,1: chlorine wins most of the time, the electrons camp at chlorine's end, and that end of the bond turns slightly negative while the hydrogen end turns slightly positive. Chemists write those slight charges as delta minus and delta plus. The bond has become a tiny magnet-like thing with two ends — a dipole — and the bigger the score gap, the stronger the unfairness. And when the gap becomes a mismatch of an adult against a toddler — sodium's 0,9 against chlorine's 3,0, a gap of 2,1 — the tug-of-war stops being a game at all: chlorine simply takes the electron, both become ions, and the bond is IONIC. Sharing, unfair sharing, taking: one smooth slide, position marked by the score difference.

But here is the final twist: a molecule full of unfair bonds can still be fair OVERALL — if its shape cancels the pulls. Carbon dioxide is a straight line with an oxygen pulling on each end: two strong pulls, exactly opposite, perfectly cancelled — the molecule as a whole is non-polar. Water's bent shape ruins the cancellation: both hydrogens sit on the same side, both pulls lean the same way, and the molecule ends up with a genuinely negative oxygen corner and a positive hydrogen side. That bent shape is why water is polar — and, as the next lessons will show, why water dissolves salt, beads on glass and boils at a temperature high enough for tea.

The last questions of the lesson are yours now — check the score gap for the bond, then check the shape for the molecule.
