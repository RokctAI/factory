# Part 1 — Expert

Why is water liquid at room temperature while carbon dioxide, a heavier molecule, is a gas? Why does nail polish remover evaporate in seconds while cooking oil sits all day? The answers never lie inside the molecules — they lie BETWEEN them. This session separates the forces between molecules from the bonds within them, classifies the whole family of intermolecular forces, and then puts them to work explaining boiling points, melting points, vapour pressure and solubility — the four properties Paper 2 asks about relentlessly.

## Subtopic: Between Molecules, Not Within Them

Two completely different kinds of attraction operate in every sample of a molecular substance, and confusing them is the topic's cardinal sin.

INTRAMOLECULAR forces are the chemical bonds INSIDE each molecule — the covalent bonds holding water's two hydrogens to its oxygen. They are strong, and breaking them is a chemical reaction that destroys the substance. INTERMOLECULAR forces act BETWEEN separate molecules — the attractions holding one water molecule to its neighbours. They are far weaker, typically tens of times weaker than covalent bonds, and overcoming them changes only the physical state, never the substance.

Boil a pot of water and watch what the energy does. The steam rising from the pot is still H two O — every covalent bond intact, every molecule whole. What the heat overcame was purely the grip between neighbouring molecules, prying them apart into the gas. Boiling, melting and evaporation are all fights against INTERmolecular forces only. If the covalent bonds had broken instead, the pot would be releasing hydrogen and oxygen gas — a different event altogether.

This gives the topic its master principle: the strength of a substance's intermolecular forces sets its physical behaviour. Strong grip between molecules means high melting point, high boiling point, slow evaporation. Weak grip means the opposite. The whole session is applications of that sentence.

One habit before moving on: in every answer, name the force you mean. The phrase "the bonds break when water boils" is a mark-losing ambiguity; "the intermolecular forces between water molecules are overcome" is the sentence the memo wants.

## Subtopic: The Family of Intermolecular Forces

The forces between molecules form a family, named van der Waals forces, with the members distinguished by what kind of molecules are attracting.

LONDON FORCES — also called induced dipole or dispersion forces — operate between ALL molecules, polar or not, and are the ONLY option for non-polar ones. The electrons in any molecule are in constant motion, and at any instant they may bunch slightly to one side, creating a fleeting, temporary dipole. That flicker induces a matching dipole in the neighbouring molecule, and the two attract for that instant. Individually feeble, these attractions strengthen as molecules get LARGER: more electrons make bigger, more polarisable clouds. That is why the halogens march through the states — fluorine and chlorine are gases, bromine a liquid, iodine a solid — with nothing but growing London forces to explain it.

DIPOLE-DIPOLE FORCES operate between POLAR molecules — molecules with permanent delta plus and delta minus ends, as the previous lesson established. The positive end of one molecule attracts the negative end of the next, a stronger and more permanent grip than a London flicker. Hydrogen chloride molecules hold each other this way. A hybrid case, the DIPOLE-INDUCED DIPOLE force, arises when a polar molecule induces a temporary dipole in a non-polar neighbour — how oxygen manages to dissolve, slightly, in water.

HYDROGEN BONDING is the family's strongest member, and its conditions are strict: a hydrogen atom covalently bonded to nitrogen, oxygen or fluorine — the three small, fiercely electronegative atoms — is left so electron-poor that it is attracted powerfully to the lone pair of an N, O or F on a NEIGHBOURING molecule. Water, ammonia and hydrogen fluoride all hydrogen-bond. Despite the name, this is NOT a chemical bond — it is an intermolecular force, roughly a tenth the strength of a covalent bond, but several times stronger than ordinary dipole-dipole attraction.

Finally, ION-DIPOLE FORCES act between IONS and polar molecules — the force at work whenever a salt dissolves in water, and the bridge to the solubility story later.

The ranking to memorise, weakest to strongest: London, then dipole-dipole, then hydrogen bonding, then ion-dipole — with molecule size able to promote London forces up the ladder for very large molecules.

## Subtopic: Boiling Points, Vapour Pressure and the Water Anomaly

Every physical property in this topic obeys one logic: stronger intermolecular forces need more energy to overcome, and the property follows.

Boiling point first. To boil, molecules must break free of their neighbours entirely, so stronger forces mean higher boiling points. Two comparisons carry the exam. Within a family of similar molecules, SIZE decides: the halogens' boiling points climb from fluorine to iodine as growing electron clouds strengthen the London forces. Between different force types, the TYPE decides: compare water, boiling at 100 degrees Celsius, with hydrogen sulfide — the same angular shape, the same group of the periodic table, and a HEAVIER molecule — boiling at minus 60. By size alone, hydrogen sulfide should boil higher. It does not, because water hydrogen-bonds and hydrogen sulfide, whose sulfur is too weakly electronegative, cannot. That single comparison, water against hydrogen sulfide, is the syllabus's showcase that force TYPE beats molecular size.

Water's hydrogen bonding explains its other anomalies too: an unusually high specific heat that lets the oceans stabilise the climate, high surface tension that insects walk on, and the strange fact that ice floats — the hydrogen bonds lock the solid into an open, spacious lattice less dense than the liquid.

Vapour pressure runs the same logic in reverse. In any liquid, the fastest molecules at the surface escape into the air; the pressure of that escaped vapour above the liquid is the vapour pressure. WEAK intermolecular forces let molecules escape easily: high vapour pressure, fast evaporation, low boiling point. STRONG forces hold them back: low vapour pressure, slow evaporation, high boiling point. Nail polish remover — propanone — has only weak forces, so it streams off your hand in seconds, stealing heat as it goes; water, gripped by hydrogen bonds, lingers. Vapour pressure and boiling point are the same ranking read in opposite directions.

Melting point follows suit: stronger intermolecular forces hold the solid lattice together to higher temperatures.

## Subtopic: Dissolving, Ion-Dipole Forces and Like Dissolves Like

Drop a spoon of salt into water and the crystal vanishes. The mechanism is ion-dipole attraction, and it is examinable step by step.

Sodium chloride is a lattice of positive sodium ions and negative chloride ions. Water molecules are dipoles: negative oxygen corner, positive hydrogen side. At the crystal surface, water molecules crowd each ion with the attracting end inward — oxygens toward the sodium ions, hydrogens toward the chlorides. Enough water molecules pulling together overcome the lattice's grip, pluck the ion free, and surround it in a travelling escort called a hydration shell. Ion by ion, the crystal dissolves. The energy to break the lattice is paid by the ion-dipole attractions formed.

The general rule is LIKE DISSOLVES LIKE. Polar solvents dissolve polar and ionic substances: salt and sugar dissolve in water — sugar by hydrogen-bonding its many O H groups to the water. Non-polar solvents dissolve non-polar substances: candle wax dissolves in petrol, both held by nothing but London forces. The mismatches fail. Wax will not dissolve in water, because the water molecules grip each other with hydrogen bonds far stronger than anything they could form with the wax — the water clings to itself and excludes the intruder. Oil and water separate in the salad bottle for exactly this reason. And salt will not dissolve in oil, because the feeble London forces of the oil cannot pay the cost of dismantling an ionic lattice.

The exam skill: explain a given solubility observation by naming the forces within the solute, within the solvent, and between them, then stating whether the new solute-solvent attractions can compete with the old ones. Answers that just recite like dissolves like without the forces earn half marks at best.

## Subtopic: The Glue Between, Not the Glue Within

Think of a brick wall. Each brick is solid in itself, and separately there is mortar holding brick to brick. Molecules work the same way. INSIDE each molecule, strong chemical bonds weld the atoms together — that is the brick. BETWEEN the molecules, a much weaker stickiness holds neighbour to neighbour — that is the mortar.

Here is the insight that unlocks the whole topic: melting and boiling only ever attack the MORTAR. When water boils, the steam is still water — every molecule intact, hydrogens still welded to oxygens. The heat merely broke the grip between neighbours so molecules could fly apart. To break the bricks themselves you need a chemical reaction, a far more violent event, and then you would not have steam — you would have hydrogen and oxygen, different substances entirely.

So when you see a physical property — how easily something melts, boils or evaporates — you are reading a measurement of MORTAR strength, nothing else. Strong mortar: the substance clings to itself, needs serious heat to melt or boil, evaporates slowly. Weak mortar: it falls apart at a touch, boils cold, vanishes into the air.

You can feel mortar strength on your own skin. Rub sanitiser on your hands: it is cold and dry within seconds, because its molecules barely grip each other and escape effortlessly, stealing body heat as they leave. Now wet your hands with water: they stay wet far longer, because water clings to itself with the strongest mortar in the everyday world. Same warmth, same skin — different glue.

## Subtopic: Three Grades of Sticky

Not all mortar is equal. Molecules grip each other with three grades of stickiness, and which grade a substance gets depends on what its molecules are like.

Grade one: the flicker. Even a perfectly balanced, non-polar molecule has electrons sloshing around inside it, and for an instant the slosh can lean to one side — a momentary charged tip. That flicker nudges the neighbour's electrons into leaning too, and for a heartbeat the two attract. Flickers are the weakest grip, but every molecule has them, and bigger molecules — with more electrons sloshing — flicker harder. That is why small non-polar molecules are gases, while big ones, like candle wax, are solid: enough weak flickers add up to a real grip.

Grade two: the permanent lean. Polar molecules, remember, have a built-in positive end and negative end. Line them up and plus faces minus down the whole crowd — a steady, everywhere grip, firmer than any flicker.

Grade three: the special handshake. When hydrogen is bonded to one of the three fierce pullers — nitrogen, oxygen or fluorine — that hydrogen is left so exposed and so positive that it locks onto a neighbouring molecule's oxygen or nitrogen with surprising strength. This is the famous hydrogen bond. Do not let the name fool you: it is still mortar, not brick — about a tenth as strong as a real chemical bond — but it towers over the other grades of stickiness.

The special handshake is why water is a scandal of chemistry. Compare it with hydrogen sulfide — same bent shape, same chemical family, and heavier, so by rights it should cling harder and boil hotter. Hydrogen sulfide boils at minus 60 degrees; water at plus 100. The difference is entirely grade three grip: water shakes hands, hydrogen sulfide only flickers and leans. Without hydrogen bonds, every dam, river and ocean on Earth would be vapour.

## Subtopic: Why Salt Disappears and Oil Refuses

Stir salt into water and it vanishes. Stir oil into water and it stubbornly floats. Both mysteries dissolve — so to speak — once you watch the forces at work.

The salt crystal is a stack of charged ions, plus and minus, gripping each other hard. But each water molecule is a tiny two-ended magnet-like thing: negative at the oxygen corner, positive on the hydrogen side. At the crystal's surface, water molecules swarm every ion, turning their attracting ends inward — oxygen corners nuzzling the positive sodiums, hydrogen sides courting the negative chlorides. One water molecule could never win, but dozens together out-pull the crystal, pop the ion loose and carry it away wrapped in a watery escort. Ion by ion, the crystal is dismantled. The salt has not stopped existing — it is travelling first class, fully surrounded.

Now the oil. Oil molecules are non-polar: no charged ends, nothing for water's little magnets to grab. Worse, the water molecules are busy holding HANDS with each other — those powerful hydrogen bonds — and letting an oil molecule in between would mean breaking handshakes for nothing in return. So the water closes ranks and squeezes the oil out, drop by drop, until the oil sits in its own layer on top. Oil and water do not so much repel as fail to offer each other a deal worth taking.

That is the famous rule, like dissolves like, with its machinery showing. Polar dissolves polar and ionic: water takes salt and sugar. Non-polar dissolves non-polar: petrol strips candle wax and dry-cleaning fluid lifts greasy stains that water cannot touch. The mismatches — salt in oil, wax in water — fail because the new attractions on offer cannot pay for the old attractions that must be broken.

One rule ran this whole lesson: everything a substance does physically — melt, boil, evaporate, dissolve — is decided by the grip between its molecules. Read the grip, and you can predict the behaviour.
