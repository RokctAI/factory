**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! First, the wording issue that haunts every test: a learner wrote "the bonds between water molecules break when water boils" and was penalised. But is that not exactly what the lesson said happens?

**Tutor:** The physics was right and the vocabulary betrayed it — and in this topic vocabulary IS the assessment. The word "bond", unqualified, means a chemical bond: the strong covalent weld inside a molecule. Say "bonds break when water boils" and the marker must read it as hydrogen-to-oxygen bonds breaking, which would mean water decomposing into hydrogen and oxygen gas — false, and a serious chemistry error. What actually yields during boiling is the grip BETWEEN whole molecules. The safe sentence, worth its full marks, is: the intermolecular forces between water molecules are overcome, while the covalent bonds within each molecule remain intact. Notice also the verb — forces are overcome, rather than broken, which many memos prefer for the same reason. There is one honest complication: the strongest intermolecular force is unfortunately NAMED the hydrogen bond, so the phrase "hydrogen bonds between water molecules are overcome during boiling" is correct, because the words between molecules pin it as intermolecular. The discipline, then: never let the bare word bond appear in a physical-change answer without saying whether you mean intramolecular bonds within molecules or forces between them. One clause of precision protects the whole answer.

***

**Bianca:** Next: how can non-polar molecules attract each other at all? If the molecule has no charged ends, there is nothing to do the attracting — yet iodine is a solid. Learners feel the theory is cheating.

**Tutor:** The escape from the paradox is that "non-polar" describes the molecule ON AVERAGE, not at every instant. Electrons are not parked; they are in ceaseless motion around the molecule, and at any given moment the cloud can slosh slightly to one side. For that instant the molecule has a charged tip — a temporary dipole — even though over time the average is perfectly balanced. That instantaneous flicker does something contagious: it repels or attracts the electrons of the neighbouring molecule, distorting ITS cloud into a matching lean — an induced dipole — and the two momentary dipoles attract. The flickers are individually feeble and constantly re-forming, but they never stop, and they operate between every pair of molecules in the sample simultaneously. Scale is what turns feeble into formidable: more electrons make a bigger, floppier, more polarisable cloud that flickers harder. Hence the halogen parade — fluorine's small cloud gives a gas, chlorine likewise, bromine's larger cloud makes a liquid, and iodine's big soft cloud flickers strongly enough to hold a solid together at room temperature. Same force, same mechanism, growing with size. So no cheating: the attraction between non-polar molecules is real, it is just built from moments rather than permanence.

**Bianca:** This one is the comparison everyone fumbles: rank propane, ethanol-type molecules and water-type molecules by boiling point. When does size win and when does force type win?

**Tutor:** Use a two-step decision, in strict order. Step one: classify the force TYPE in each substance — London only for non-polar molecules, dipole-dipole for polar ones, hydrogen bonding when hydrogen is bonded directly to nitrogen, oxygen or fluorine. If the types DIFFER, type decides, almost regardless of size — a hydrogen-bonded substance outboils a merely polar one, which outboils a comparable non-polar one. That is the water versus hydrogen sulfide showcase: hydrogen sulfide is nearly twice the mass, yet boils 160 degrees lower, because sulfur is not electronegative enough to enable hydrogen bonding and water's handshake dominates everything size could offer. Step two: only when the types are the SAME does size take over — within the halogens, or down the alkanes, boiling points climb steadily with molecular mass as the London forces grow with the electron count. The mark-scheme phrasing to imitate: first name the forces in each substance; then state which forces are stronger and why — type, or size within a type; then conclude that more energy is needed to overcome the stronger forces, giving the higher boiling point. Learners who mention energy in the conclusion collect a mark that pure ranking answers miss. Type first, size second, energy sentence last — the fumbles disappear.

**Bianca:** Last one: sugar is not ionic, yet it dissolves brilliantly in water while candle wax does not dissolve at all. Salt at least has charges — what is sugar's secret?

**Tutor:** Sugar's secret is that it is practically dressed for the occasion: a sucrose molecule carries eight O H groups — oxygen-hydrogen pairs chemically identical in character to the ones on water itself. Each of those groups can form hydrogen bonds, so when sugar meets water there is no awkwardness: water molecules hydrogen-bond directly to the sugar's O H groups, and the new sugar-water handshakes are of the same currency and comparable strength as the water-water handshakes they replace. The energy books balance, the crystal is prised apart molecule by molecule, and the sugar disperses — no ions needed, and note that this is dissolving by hydrogen bonding rather than by ion-dipole forces, which is salt's mechanism. Candle wax, by contrast, arrives at the party with nothing to offer: a long non-polar hydrocarbon whose only currency is weak London flickers. For water to accommodate wax it would have to break its own strong hydrogen-bond network and accept flickers in exchange — a terrible trade — so the water clings to itself and excludes the wax entirely. That is the machinery inside like dissolves like: dissolving happens when the new solute-solvent attractions can pay for the old attractions that must be broken, in the same currency or better. Sugar can pay in water's own currency; wax cannot pay at all. And the exam habit follows: name the forces in the solute, in the solvent, and the new ones formed between them — then judge the trade.
