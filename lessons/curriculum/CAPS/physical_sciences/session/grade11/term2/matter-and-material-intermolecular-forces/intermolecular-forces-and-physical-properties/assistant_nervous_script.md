Hey, it's Bianca — intermolecular forces feels like a topic with too many names: London, dispersion, induced dipole, dipole-dipole, hydrogen bonding, ion-dipole, van der Waals covering half of them. Breathe out. The entire topic is one sentence, and every question is that sentence in a costume.

The sentence: the grip between molecules decides everything a substance does physically. Melting, boiling, evaporating, dissolving — all of it is just asking how strong the grip is. Nothing in this topic ever breaks the molecules themselves; that would be a chemical reaction, and it is the one thing you must never say. Bricks and mortar: bonds inside molecules are the bricks, forces between molecules are the mortar, and physical changes only touch the mortar.

Then there are only three grades of grip to classify, and classification is a two-question quiz. Question one: is hydrogen bonded straight onto nitrogen, oxygen or fluorine? Then the substance hydrogen-bonds — the strongest grade. Question two, if not: is the molecule polar? Then dipole-dipole — the middle grade. Otherwise it is London flickers only — the weakest grade, though big molecules flicker harder, which is why size matters within a family.

Ranking questions then run themselves: compare force TYPES first, and only if the types match, let SIZE decide. And end every comparison with the energy sentence — stronger forces need more energy to overcome, so the boiling point is higher. That sentence is a mark on its own.

For solubility, think trade deal. Dissolving happens when the new attractions between solute and solvent can pay for the old attractions being broken. Salt pays water with ion-dipole attraction; sugar pays in water's own hydrogen-bond currency; wax offers only flickers, and water refuses the trade. Name the forces on both sides and the deal, and full marks follow.

One sentence, three grades, one trade deal. That is the whole topic. You've got this!
