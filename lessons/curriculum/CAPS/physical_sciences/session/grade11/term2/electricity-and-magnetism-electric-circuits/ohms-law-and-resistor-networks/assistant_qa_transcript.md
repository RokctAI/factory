**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! The first is the parallel paradox: adding a resistor should add resistance, surely — more components, more opposition. Why does a parallel resistor make the total SMALLER?

**Tutor:** Because a parallel resistor is not added to the road — it IS a new road. Series intuition says more components, more opposition, and for series that intuition is perfectly correct: every added resistor stands in the single path and every charge must fight through it. But a parallel resistor connects the same two points by a second route. Nothing about the first branch changed — its resistance, its voltage, its current are all untouched — and yet the battery now also drives current through the new branch. Total current between the two points goes UP, and since resistance is voltage over total current, resistance must come DOWN. The check that protects you in the exam: a parallel combination is always smaller than its smallest branch. Four ohms in parallel with twelve gives three — below four. If your parallel answer lands above the smallest branch, you almost certainly forgot the final flip: the formula delivers one over R, and the reciprocal must be taken as the last step. And when the intuition wobbles, return to the taxi ranks: between two ranks, three roads move more taxis than one road, no matter how good that one road is. More roads, less overall opposition, every time.

**Bianca:** Next: in the mixed circuit, a learner divided the full 12 volts by the 3 ohm branch to get 4 amperes, and everything after that collapsed. What rule did they skip?

**Tutor:** They spent money that was no longer there. The 12 volts is the battery's budget for the ENTIRE loop, not the property of any one resistor. Before the current ever reaches the parallel pair, it must pass through the series 2 ohm resistor, and passing through costs volts: current times resistance, 3 amperes times 2 ohms, which is 6 volts spent. Only the remainder — 12 minus 6, so 6 volts — arrives at the parallel section, and it is that 6 volts, not 12, which sits across the 3 ohm branch, driving 2 amperes, not 4. The discipline that prevents the error is to walk the circuit in order and never use a voltage you have not verified: start at the battery, subtract each series drop as you meet it, and label the diagram with what remains. The deeper rule underneath is that voltages around the loop must sum to the battery's emf — 6 across the series resistor plus 6 across the pair equals 12. Run that audit at the end of every circuit question. If your labelled voltages do not add up to the battery, a resistor got skipped, and the audit tells you before the marker does.

**Bianca:** Here is the graph question: the paper shows current against voltage for a resistor and for a light bulb, and asks which is ohmic. Learners can quote the law but misread the curves. What is the reading skill?

**Tutor:** The skill is to connect the shape of the graph to the constancy of the ratio. Ohm's law promises that current is directly proportional to voltage AT CONSTANT TEMPERATURE, and direct proportion has exactly one picture: a straight line through the origin. So the resistor's plot — straight, through zero — is the ohmic one, and its constant gradient is one over its constant resistance. The bulb's curve bends, and the direction of the bend tells the story: as voltage grows, the current gains get smaller and the curve flattens. Why: the filament runs enormously hot, its resistance climbs with temperature, and each new volt faces a stiffer filament than the last. The bulb does not disprove Ohm's law — it violates the law's fine print, the constant-temperature condition, which is precisely why examiners adore it. Full marks usually need three sentences: name the straight line through the origin as ohmic; say the filament's temperature rises with current, increasing its resistance; conclude that the ratio of voltage to current does not stay constant, so the filament is non-ohmic. And watch the axes before any of that — swapping voltage-against-current for current-against-voltage flips which way the bend should point, so read the labels first, always.

**Bianca:** Last one: the bill question gave a 2 000 watt appliance running 45 minutes, and half the class got costs in the thousands of rand. What went wrong, and what is the clean recipe?

**Tutor:** Two unit slips, both classics, usually together. The kilowatt-hour recipe demands kilowatts and hours, and the question hands you watts and minutes on purpose. Learners who multiply 2 000 by 45 are computing watt-minutes — a unit no municipality has ever billed — and then multiplying by the tariff as if it were kilowatt-hours, inflating the answer by orders of magnitude. The clean recipe is three conversions and two multiplications. Convert power: divide watts by 1 000, so 2 000 watts is 2 kilowatts. Convert time: divide minutes by 60, so 45 minutes is 0,75 hours. Multiply for energy: 2 times 0,75 is 1,5 kilowatt-hours. Multiply by the tariff: at 2 rand 50 per kilowatt-hour, 1,5 times 2,50 is 3 rand 75. A kettle-sized answer for a kettle-sized job — and that reasonableness check is your final defence. Everyday appliances cost cents to single rands per use; a geyser costs tens of rands per day; if a toaster comes out at 4 000 rand, a conversion was skipped. One more definition to keep polished: the kilowatt-hour measures ENERGY, the amount used, while the kilowatt measures POWER, the rate of use. The bill charges for how much you used, not how fast you used it.
