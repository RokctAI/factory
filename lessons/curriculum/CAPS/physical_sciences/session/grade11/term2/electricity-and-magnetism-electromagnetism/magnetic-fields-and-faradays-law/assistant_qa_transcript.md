**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! The first is the angle problem: in the flux formula, learners never know whether theta is measured from the surface or from the normal, and the memo always seems to disagree with them.

**Tutor:** The definition settles it once and for all: theta is the angle between the magnetic field and the NORMAL — the imaginary line standing perpendicular to the surface, like a flagpole on a flat roof. Never the surface itself. The confusion arises because questions often say the field is parallel to the plane of the coil, or makes some angle with the plane, and the plane is not the normal. The conversion is one subtraction: an angle of 30 degrees with the PLANE is an angle of 60 degrees with the NORMAL, because plane and normal are always 90 degrees apart. Two sanity checks catch every slip. Field passing straight through the coil, like rain into an upright bucket: field parallel to the normal, theta zero, cosine one, maximum flux. Field skimming along the surface, bucket on its side: theta 90, cosine zero, zero flux. Before you press the calculator, ask which of those extremes your situation is closer to, and check that your cosine leans the same way. If the field is nearly straight through but your cosine is coming out near zero, you took the angle from the wrong reference line. Draw the normal on every diagram — a small dashed arrow off the surface — and measure to it every time.

**Bianca:** Next: a learner wrote that a stronger magnet inside a coil gives a bigger current. The magnet is stationary. Why is this the most tempting wrong answer in the topic?

**Tutor:** Because it confuses having flux with changing flux, and the entire law lives in that distinction. A stationary magnet inside a coil creates an enormous flux — but a constant one, and Faraday's law pays out on the RATE OF CHANGE of flux, not on its size. Delta phi over delta t for a constant flux is zero, so the emf is zero and the current is zero, no matter how mighty the magnet. It is the eleven-year mistake of history: after Oersted, physicists across Europe left powerful magnets resting beside circuits waiting for a current that never came, because presence does nothing and only change is rewarded. Where does magnet strength genuinely help? When the magnet MOVES. A stronger magnet moving at the same speed sweeps a larger flux change past the coil in the same time, so the rate of change is larger and the induced emf grows. Strength is an amplifier of change, never a substitute for it. In the exam, any question of the form what happens to the current when the magnet is held stationary has the answer zero, with the reason: the flux through the coil is constant, so the rate of change of flux is zero, and by Faraday's law the induced emf is zero.

**Bianca:** This one is about the minus sign: the formula on the sheet reads emf equals negative N delta phi over delta t. Learners either drop the minus and worry, or keep it and get negative voltages. What should they do with it?

**Tutor:** Understand what it says, then set it aside for magnitude work. The minus sign is the direction rule compressed into one symbol: it records that the induced emf drives a current whose own magnetic field OPPOSES the change in flux that created it. It is nature's refusal to give free energy — if the sign were positive, the induced current would reinforce the change, which would reinforce the current, and energy would multiply out of nothing. So the sign carries real physics, and stating that meaning in words is often worth a mark. But in a calculation asking for the emf or the current, work with magnitudes: compute delta phi as final flux minus initial flux in size, multiply by N, divide by delta t, and report a positive number of volts. Then, if the question asks for the direction of the induced current, answer it as a separate step in words: identify the change — flux increasing into the coil, say — and choose the current direction whose field fights that change, using the right hand to translate between current direction and pole. Numbers from the formula, directions from the opposition principle. Splitting the two jobs is exactly how the memo allocates the marks.

**Bianca:** Last one: the calculation went fine for one learner until the answer came out a hundred times too big. The coil was 10 centimetres by 15 centimetres. What happened?

**Tutor:** The area conversion — the quietest mark-killer in this topic. Ten centimetres by fifteen centimetres invites the multiplication 10 times 15, giving 150, and then a hopeful division by 100 to make it metric, giving 1,5. But area does not convert like length. Each dimension must become metres FIRST: 0,1 metres by 0,15 metres, giving 0,015 square metres. Converting after multiplying requires dividing by 100 TWICE — once for each dimension — because a square metre contains ten thousand square centimetres, not a hundred. The learner who divides once is left a factor of 100 too big — exactly the hundred-fold inflation you described. The protection is a habit: convert every length to metres at the moment you first write it down, before any multiplication happens, so the arithmetic never sees a centimetre. And run the reasonableness check at the end: school-problem fluxes come out as small decimals of a weber, and induced emfs as a handful of volts. An emf of 720 volts from a hand-held coil should trigger the alarm, and the factor of a hundred between it and 7,2 points straight back at the units.
