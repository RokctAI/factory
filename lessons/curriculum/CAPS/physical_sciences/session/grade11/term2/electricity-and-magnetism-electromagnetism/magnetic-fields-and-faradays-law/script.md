# Part 1 — Expert

Every power station feeding the national grid runs on the physics of this session: a current makes a magnetic field, and a changing magnetic field makes a current. Those two sentences, discovered by Oersted and Faraday, are the entire topic. This session builds the Right Hand Rule for wires, loops and solenoids, defines magnetic flux precisely, states Faraday's law in the wording the marker wants, and finishes with the calculation the paper always sets: the emf induced in a coil when the field through it changes.

## Subtopic: The Magnetic Field of a Current and the Right Hand Rule

Oersted's discovery, in one sentence: a current-carrying conductor produces a magnetic field around itself. A compass needle placed near a wire deflects the moment the current flows, and returns when it stops — electricity and magnetism are not separate subjects.

For a long STRAIGHT wire, the field lines are concentric circles wrapped around the wire, closer together near the wire where the field is stronger. The direction comes from the Right Hand Rule: grip the wire with your right hand so that the THUMB points along the conventional current, and your curled FINGERS show the direction of the circular field lines. Reverse the current, and the circles reverse.

For a single LOOP of wire, apply the same grip to each piece of the loop and the circles all pass through the centre the same way: the field goes straight through the middle of the loop, one face acting like a north pole and the other like a south pole.

For a SOLENOID — many loops wound side by side — those single-loop fields add. Inside the solenoid the field is strong and nearly uniform, running along the axis; outside, it loops around exactly like the field of a bar magnet. The alternative grip for a solenoid: curl the fingers of the right hand along the direction of the current in the windings, and the thumb points to the north pole end. An iron core strengthens the field, and this electromagnet is switchable — cranes lift scrap cars with it, and relays and electric bells click with it.

The syllabus adds a discussion point: overhead power lines carry large alternating currents and therefore surround themselves with fields. The practical concerns are land cleared for servitudes, effects on bird flight paths, and public worry over long-term exposure near lines — worth two sentences of balanced comment in an exam answer, noting that research has not established harm from ordinary exposure levels.

Attempt this section's questions now — grip with the RIGHT hand, thumb for current, fingers for field.

## Subtopic: Magnetic Flux — Counting Field Through a Surface

Faraday's law needs a precise measure of how much magnetic field passes through a loop. That measure is magnetic flux, symbol phi. The definition: the product of the magnetic field strength and the area of the surface, multiplied by the cosine of the angle between the field and the NORMAL to the surface. In symbols, phi equals B A cosine theta. B is the magnetic flux density in tesla; A is the area in square metres; and theta is measured between the field direction and the normal — the line perpendicular to the surface. The unit of flux is the weber, and one weber equals one tesla times one square metre.

The cosine deserves care, because it is where the marks are lost. When the field is PERPENDICULAR to the surface, passing straight through it, the field is PARALLEL to the normal, theta is zero, cosine is one, and the flux is a maximum: everything passes through. When the field lies IN the plane of the surface, skimming along it, the field is at ninety degrees to the normal, cosine is zero, and the flux is zero: nothing passes through. A window catches the most sunlight facing the sun, none edge-on.

Work it. A rectangular coil encloses an area of 0,05 square metres in a magnetic field of 0,4 tesla. With the field along the normal: phi equals 0,4 multiplied by 0,05 multiplied by cosine of zero, which is 0,02 weber. Now the coil is tilted so the normal makes 60 degrees with the field: phi equals 0,4 multiplied by 0,05 multiplied by cosine of 60 degrees, which is 0,4 times 0,05 times 0,5, giving 0,01 weber. Same field, same coil, half the flux — orientation alone changed it.

That sensitivity to angle is the whole working principle of generators: rotating a coil in a steady field changes the flux continuously, and changing flux, as the next section shows, is what makes electricity.

This section's questions are with you now — check every angle against the normal, not the surface.

## Subtopic: Faraday's Law and the Direction of the Induced Current

Faraday's discovery: push a bar magnet into a solenoid connected to a galvanometer and the needle kicks. Hold the magnet still — inside or outside, it does not matter — and the needle sits at zero. Pull the magnet out and the needle kicks the OTHER way. Motion, not presence, makes the current.

The law, examinable: the magnitude of the induced emf across a conductor is directly proportional to the rate of change of the magnetic flux through the conductor. Faster change, bigger emf. Push the magnet in quickly and the kick is larger than pushing slowly; use a stronger magnet, or a coil of more turns, and it is larger again.

The direction of the induced current obeys a beautifully stubborn principle: the induced current always flows in the direction that OPPOSES the change causing it. Push the north pole of a magnet toward the face of a solenoid, and the induced current circulates to make that face a NORTH pole, repelling the approaching magnet — opposing its arrival. Pull the magnet away, and the current reverses to make the face a SOUTH pole, attracting the departing magnet — opposing its escape. Nature charges for electricity: the induced effects fight you, and the work you do against that fight is where the electrical energy comes from. Combine this with the solenoid Right Hand Rule — thumb to the required pole, fingers give the current — and you can answer every direction question in the topic.

The energy logic matters conceptually: if the induced current helped the change instead of opposing it, the magnet would be sucked in faster, inducing more current, accelerating forever — energy from nothing. The opposition is energy conservation wearing electrical clothes.

Work through this section's questions now, reasoning every direction in two steps: what change is happening, and what pole would oppose it.

## Subtopic: Calculating the Induced EMF

The calculating form of Faraday's law: the induced emf equals the negative of the number of turns multiplied by the change in flux divided by the time taken. In symbols, emf equals negative N delta phi over delta t. N is the number of turns in the coil, delta phi is the final flux minus the initial flux through ONE turn, and delta t is the time over which the change happens. The negative sign is the opposition of the previous section written into the formula; for magnitude calculations, work with sizes and state directions in words.

Work the standard problem. A coil of 200 turns encloses an area of 0,03 square metres, with the plane of the coil perpendicular to a magnetic field — so the field is along the normal and cosine theta is one. The field grows from 0,2 tesla to 0,8 tesla in 0,5 seconds. Find the induced emf.

Change in flux through one turn first. Initial flux: 0,2 multiplied by 0,03, which is 0,006 weber. Final flux: 0,8 multiplied by 0,03, which is 0,024 weber. Delta phi: 0,024 minus 0,006, which is 0,018 weber.

Now the law. Formula: emf equals N delta phi over delta t, in magnitude. Substitution: 200 multiplied by 0,018, divided by 0,5. Answer: 7,2 volts.

If that coil is part of a closed circuit of total resistance 3,6 ohms, Ohm's law finishes the job: current equals emf divided by resistance, 7,2 divided by 3,6, which is 2 amperes.

The traps, in order of harvest. Forgetting N — one bare loop obeys the same law with N equal to one, but a 200-turn coil multiplies the emf 200 times. Using B instead of delta phi — the law wants the CHANGE in flux, so compute initial and final flux and subtract. Forgetting cosine theta when the field is not along the normal. And units: area in square metres, so a coil measured in centimetres must be converted before multiplying.

The final questions of this part are in front of you now — flux first, change second, law last.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same two discoveries as a story: the accident that linked electricity to magnetism, and the eleven-year hunt to reverse it.

## Subtopic: The Compass That Flinched

In 1820 a Danish lecturer named Oersted was demonstrating electric currents when he noticed something in the corner of his eye: every time he switched the current on, a compass lying nearby FLINCHED. Switch off — the needle relaxed. Switch on — it flinched again. A wire was moving a magnet without touching it.

What Oersted stumbled on is now rule one of this topic: every electric current wraps itself in a magnetic field. The field of a straight wire circles around it like water swirling around a drain, and your right hand is the map: point the thumb along the current, and your curling fingers show the way the field circles.

Now coil the wire, because coiling is how you make the field useful. One loop concentrates the circles through its middle. Hundreds of loops — a solenoid — stack their fields into something with a genuine north end and south end: a bar magnet made of electricity. Slide an iron core in and it strengthens dramatically. The magic is the switch: unlike any bar magnet, this one turns OFF. That is why scrapyard cranes use electromagnets to pick up a car — carry it across the yard, cut the current, drop it on the pile. No permanent magnet could ever let go.

You have heard this rule in action your whole life: the buzz of an electric gate motor, the clunk of a relay, the hum under a big overhead power line — currents wearing their magnetic clothing.

Your questions on this section are with you now — thumb for current, fingers for field, iron for muscle.

## Subtopic: How Much Field Fits Through the Window?

Before the second discovery makes sense, you need one small idea with a fancy name: flux.

Picture a magnetic field as rain falling steadily, and picture a wire loop as the rim of a bucket. Flux asks: how much rain goes through the opening? Three things decide it. How hard it is raining — that is the field strength. How wide the bucket's mouth is — that is the area. And how the mouth is TILTED — that is the angle. Hold the bucket upright, mouth facing the rain, and you catch the most. Tilt it, and you catch less. Turn it fully sideways, rim edge-on to the rain, and you catch nothing at all, even in a downpour.

That is the whole formula: field, times area, times a tilt factor that runs from one, facing fully, down to zero, edge-on. A coil of half a square metre... make it concrete: a coil enclosing 0,05 square metres in a 0,4 tesla field catches 0,02 units of flux facing the field squarely, 0,01 tilted to sixty degrees, and zero side-on.

Why care about a bucket count? Because the next section reveals the strangest fact in electricity: nature only pays you when the count CHANGES. A huge, steady, unchanging flux generates precisely nothing. Flux is the number whose changes make the world's electricity, so learn to count it first.

The questions for this section are coming to you now — rain, bucket, tilt.

## Subtopic: Change Makes Electricity

After Oersted, the world's physicists asked the obvious question: if electricity makes magnetism, can magnetism make electricity? People put strong magnets next to wires and waited for a current. For eleven years, nothing. The magnet sat, the wire sat, the meter read zero.

Faraday found the missing ingredient, and it was not strength — it was CHANGE. Push the magnet toward the coil, and current flows. STOP, and it dies instantly, no matter how close the magnet sits. Pull the magnet away — current again, opposite direction. A magnet resting inside a coil forever generates nothing; a magnet MOVING generates every time. The rule: only a changing flux through a coil pushes current around it, and the faster the change, the bigger the push. More turns on the coil multiply it — each loop catches the change, and two hundred loops push two hundred times harder.

One more personality quirk: the induced current is stubborn. It always flows the way that FIGHTS the change. Shove a north pole at a coil, and the coil's face turns north to shove back. Pull away, and it turns south to cling. You never get the electricity for free — you pay in the effort of pushing against that opposition, and the payment is exactly the electrical energy you get out.

Now look at the whole national grid with new eyes. A power station is just this lesson at scale: steam or falling water spins coils inside magnetic fields, the flux through each coil rises and falls hundreds of times a second — the bucket tilting to the rain and away again — and the changing flux drives current through the wires to your home. The bicycle dynamo that lights a lamp from a spinning wheel is the same machine you could hold in one hand. Charge your phone tonight and the energy arrived by Faraday's law.

The last questions of the lesson are yours now — no change, no current; fast change, big current; and the current always fights back.
