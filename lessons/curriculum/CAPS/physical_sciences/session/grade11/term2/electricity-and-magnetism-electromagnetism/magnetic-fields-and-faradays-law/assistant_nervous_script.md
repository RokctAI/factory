Hey, it's Bianca — electromagnetism feels like two topics stapled together: hand rules and field circles on one side, flux and Faraday calculations on the other. Let me shrink it, because the whole thing is two sentences and one formula.

Sentence one: a current makes a magnetic field. That is Oersted. Your right hand is the entire theory — thumb along the current, fingers curl the way the field circles. Coil the wire and you get a magnet; for a solenoid, curl your fingers along the current in the windings and your thumb points at the north end. Every direction question in the first half of the topic is one of those two grips. Practise them physically, with an actual pen as the wire, until your hand does it without you.

Sentence two: a CHANGING magnetic field makes a current. That is Faraday, and the loud word is changing. A magnet lying still in a coil, however strong, makes nothing. Whenever a question asks what happens when something is held stationary, the answer is zero current, because the flux is not changing. Movement, growth, collapse, rotation — those make electricity.

The formula is emf equals N times delta phi over delta t, and the safe path through it never varies. First compute flux, B times A times cosine theta, remembering that theta is measured from the NORMAL, the perpendicular flagpole on the coil, not from the surface. Compute it twice — before and after. Subtract to get delta phi. Multiply by the turns, divide by the time. Convert lengths to metres before any area is calculated, because centimetres squared is where answers go to die.

And the minus sign in the formula? It just says the induced current fights the change — push a north pole in and the coil turns its north face to you. State that in words when asked for direction, and calculate with plain magnitudes otherwise.

Two sentences, two grips, one formula walked in order. You've got this!
