# Part 1 — Expert

Grade 10 kept forces and displacements on a single line, where adding them meant nothing more than watching the plus and minus signs. Grade 11 lifts them onto the Cartesian plane, where a vector may point in any direction at all. This session builds the one procedure that answers every such question in the examination: resolve each vector into perpendicular components, add the components separately, then rebuild a single resultant using Pythagoras and a trigonometric ratio. The same method returns in Newton's laws on an inclined plane and in electrostatics in two dimensions, so it is worth owning completely.

## Subtopic: Resultants, Tail-to-Head and the Closed Diagram

Definitions first, because marks live in them. A VECTOR has magnitude and direction; a SCALAR has magnitude only. The RESULTANT is the single vector that has the same effect as two or more vectors acting together.

Build the picture in your head. A tow truck on the N1 outside Bloemfontein pulls a stalled bakkie with two cables: one exerts 60 newtons due east, the other 80 newtons due north. In the TAIL-TO-HEAD method you draw the first arrow, 60 newtons pointing right across the page. From the sharp tip of that arrow — its head — you start the second arrow, 80 newtons pointing straight up the page. The resultant is the arrow drawn from the tail of the first to the head of the last: from the bottom-left corner to the top-right corner of the rectangle those two arrows frame. That closing arrow is 100 newtons, pointing up and to the right — and you will confirm that number in the fourth subtopic.

The TAIL-TO-TAIL, or parallelogram, method draws the same two arrows from a common starting point, then completes the parallelogram. The diagonal from the shared tail to the opposite corner is the resultant, identical to the tail-to-head answer. Two constructions, one truth.

Now the construction examiners like most: the CLOSED vector diagram. If three or more vectors are drawn tail-to-head and the last arrowhead lands exactly on the very first tail, the diagram is closed, which means the resultant is zero and the object is in EQUILIBRIUM. Turn that around and it becomes a tool: the single force that would bring a non-zero set of vectors into equilibrium is the EQUILIBRANT — equal in magnitude to the resultant but opposite in direction.

Hold that vocabulary steady, because the questions for this section are with you now: vector against scalar, the two graphical constructions, and exactly what a closed diagram is telling you about the net force.

## Subtopic: Resolving a Vector into Perpendicular Components

Graphical methods are honest but slow, and a ruler cannot give you three significant figures. The component method replaces drawing with arithmetic.

To RESOLVE a vector is to replace it with two perpendicular vectors — one along the x-axis, one along the y-axis — that together have exactly the same effect. Write the two formulae and say them aloud. R sub x equals R cosine theta: the x-component equals the magnitude of the vector multiplied by the cosine of its angle. R sub y equals R sine theta: the y-component equals the magnitude multiplied by the sine of the same angle.

Everything now depends on where theta is measured from, and this is where most marks are lost. Those formulae assume theta is the angle between the vector and the x-axis; if a question gives the angle from the VERTICAL instead, cosine and sine swap places. So draw the little right-angled triangle every time: the vector is the hypotenuse, the component ADJACENT to the marked angle uses cosine, the component OPPOSITE it uses sine. Adjacent takes cosine, opposite takes sine — that sentence is worth more than memorising which letter goes where.

Work one. A crate at the Durban container terminal is dragged by a cable exerting 250 newtons at 30 degrees above the horizontal. Formula: F sub x equals F cosine theta. Substitution: F sub x equals 250 newtons times cosine 30 degrees, which is 250 times 0,866. Answer: 216,5 newtons, horizontally forward. Formula: F sub y equals F sine theta. Substitution: 250 newtons times sine 30 degrees, which is 250 times 0,5. Answer: 125 newtons, vertically upward. That single slanted pull is now two straight pulls: 216,5 newtons forward and 125 newtons up, and they may be handled entirely separately.

Signs carry the direction, so fix a convention and declare it: east and north positive, west and south negative. A 120 newton force due west is written as x equals negative 120 newtons, y equals zero. A dropped minus sign is the single most common error in this topic, and it changes the final direction by tens of degrees.

Take the questions on this section now, watching in particular for which axis the given angle is measured from and which components must carry a negative sign.

## Subtopic: Adding the Components of Four Forces

With every vector resolved, addition becomes bookkeeping. The rule: all x-components add to give R sub x, all y-components add to give R sub y. Sideways parts never mix with upward parts.

Take four forces acting at one point on a steel ring in a Gauteng workshop. Force one: 250 newtons at 30 degrees above the horizontal, pointing east and up. Force two: 180 newtons due north. Force three: 120 newtons due west. Force four: 90 newtons due south.

Resolve each, in a two-column table you should physically draw. Force one, from the previous subtopic: x equals positive 216,5 newtons, y equals positive 125 newtons. Force two points straight up the y-axis, so it has no sideways part at all: x equals 0, y equals positive 180 newtons. Force three points straight west: x equals negative 120 newtons, y equals 0. Force four points straight south: x equals 0, y equals negative 90 newtons.

Now sum the columns. R sub x equals 216,5 plus 0 minus 120 plus 0. Answer: R sub x equals 96,5 newtons, positive, therefore pointing east. R sub y equals 125 plus 180 plus 0 minus 90. Answer: R sub y equals 215 newtons, positive, therefore pointing north.

Read what has happened: four awkward arrows have collapsed into exactly two, at right angles to each other — 96,5 newtons east and 215 newtons north. Any number of vectors reduces this way, which is why the method is worth more than the graphical constructions.

Two disciplines protect the marks. Resolve EVERY vector, even the ones already lying along an axis — writing "x equals 0" for the northward force proves you did not forget it. And do not round the components; carry 216,5 forward, not 217, because rounding early drifts into the final angle.

The questions for this section are in front of you now: resolving each of the four forces correctly, and summing the two columns without losing a negative sign.

## Subtopic: Magnitude by Pythagoras, Direction by Trigonometry

Two perpendicular vectors combine into one by the theorem you have known since Grade 8. The magnitude of the resultant equals the square root of R sub x squared plus R sub y squared. The direction comes from the tangent ratio: the tangent of theta equals R sub y divided by R sub x, so theta equals the inverse tangent of that quotient.

Finish the workshop problem. Formula: R equals the square root of the quantity R sub x squared plus R sub y squared. Substitution: the square root of 96,5 squared plus 215 squared, which is the square root of 9 312,25 plus 46 225, which is the square root of 55 537,25. Answer: R equals 235,7 newtons. Formula: theta equals the inverse tangent of R sub y over R sub x. Substitution: the inverse tangent of 215 divided by 96,5, which is the inverse tangent of 2,228. Answer: theta equals 65,8 degrees. Full statement: the resultant force is 235,7 newtons at 65,8 degrees north of east. A magnitude with no direction is an incomplete answer to a vector question and will be marked as such.

Return to the tow truck for the clean case: the square root of 60 squared plus 80 squared is the square root of 10 000, giving 100 newtons, at the inverse tangent of 80 over 60, which is 53,1 degrees north of east.

Three traps, named plainly. One: the calculator in radian mode — an inverse tangent that returns 1,15 instead of 65,8 is a mode error, so check the display shows DEG before you begin. Two: the quadrant. Your calculator's inverse tangent only ever answers between negative 90 and positive 90 degrees, so if R sub x came out negative you must add 180 degrees, or better, state the angle relative to a named axis. Three: describe the direction the way the question does — "north of east", "above the horizontal", or as a bearing measured clockwise from north, where 65,8 degrees north of east becomes a bearing of 24,2 degrees.

That completes the method, and the final questions of this part are with you now: Pythagoras applied to the two component totals, the inverse tangent handled with its quadrant, and the direction stated in full.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same two-dimensional vectors from the streets of a township grid, where nobody has ever needed a formula to know that walking east and then north gets you somewhere diagonal.

## Subtopic: Walking to the Shop the Long Way

The roads near your house run in two directions only: some run east-west, some run north-south. You leave home, walk 60 metres east along one street, then turn and walk 80 metres north up the next. You have walked 140 metres of pavement. But how far are you from home?

Not 140 metres. If a bird flew straight from your gate to where you are standing, it would fly 100 metres. That straight bird-flight is the RESULTANT: the one single trip that would have put you in exactly the same place. Every vector question in this topic asks the same thing — forget the journey, tell me the one arrow that does the job.

There is a lovely way to see it without numbers. Draw your first walk as an arrow, then start your second arrow at the tip of the first — head to tail, like carriages of a train. Then draw one long arrow from where you started to where you finished. That last arrow is the answer, and it does not care what order you walked in: north first and east second lands you on the same corner.

And here is the special case worth loving: if your trips bring you right back to your own gate, that final arrow has no length at all. In physics that means the forces cancel and nothing moves — the object is balanced, or as your teacher will say, in equilibrium.

Small win banked already, because you have just understood a resultant without a single calculation. The questions on this bit are waiting for you now — think about trips, tips and tails, and the trip that brings you home.

## Subtopic: Splitting One Push into Two Honest Pushes

The trouble starts when a force refuses to run along a street. Someone drags a suitcase at the Park Station taxi rank with the handle tilted upward. That pull is not fully forward and not fully upward — it is doing two jobs at once.

So do what a shopkeeper does with mixed change: separate it into piles. One pile is the FORWARD part of the pull, the part that actually moves the suitcase along the ground. The other pile is the UPWARD part, the part that lifts a little weight off the wheels. Add those two honest pushes together and they do exactly what the tilted pull was doing. Nothing has been invented and nothing has been lost — it has only been sorted.

How big is each pile? That depends on the tilt. A pull that is nearly flat is almost all forward and hardly lifts at all; a pull that is nearly straight up barely moves the suitcase. So the tilt decides the split, and the two calculator buttons that turn a tilt into a fraction are cosine and sine.

Choosing between them is a picture, not a memory trick. Sketch the right-angled triangle with the tilted pull as the long slanted side. The side lying right next to your marked angle uses cosine; the side across from the angle, facing it, uses sine. Next to it, cosine. Across from it, sine.

Try the suitcase with real numbers: a 250 newton pull tilted 30 degrees up. The forward pile is 250 times cosine 30, which is 250 times 0,866, giving 216,5 newtons. The upward pile is 250 times sine 30, which is 250 times 0,5, giving 125 newtons. One awkward pull, two easy numbers. And one last thing: anything pointing west or south gets a minus sign in front of it, the way money owed gets a minus in front of it.

Your questions for this section are coming to you right now — practise the sorting, and be strict about which pile gets the minus.

## Subtopic: Two Piles, One Answer

Last move, and it is the easiest of the three. Once every force has been split into a sideways pile and an upward pile, you add each pile on its own. Sideways money with sideways money, upward money with upward money — you would never add rands to litres, and you never add an eastward push to a northward one.

Do the workshop ring. Four forces pull on it. Their sideways parts are 216,5 east, nothing, 120 west, nothing. Add them with the minus sign doing its job: 216,5 minus 120 leaves 96,5 newtons pulling east. Their upward parts are 125 north, 180 north, nothing, 90 south. Add: 125 plus 180 minus 90 leaves 215 newtons pulling north. Four forces have become two.

And two forces at right angles is the bird-flight problem from the very first section. Square them, add, take the square root: 96,5 squared is 9 312,25; 215 squared is 46 225; together that is 55 537,25; the square root of that is 235,7. So the ring is being pulled with 235,7 newtons. For the direction, divide the upward pile by the sideways pile — 215 divided by 96,5 gives 2,228 — and ask the calculator's inverse tangent button what angle has that tangent. It answers 65,8 degrees. The pull is 235,7 newtons, pointing 65,8 degrees round from east towards north.

Never hand in the number alone. A vector answer without a direction is like giving someone a taxi fare without telling them the destination — half the information, and the marker will pay you half the marks. Say the size, then say where it points.

Three moves, that is the whole topic: split every force into two piles, add each pile, then rebuild one arrow. Check your calculator is in degrees, keep your minus signs, and this becomes some of the most reliable marks in the paper. The last questions of the lesson are with you now — take them steadily, one pile at a time.
