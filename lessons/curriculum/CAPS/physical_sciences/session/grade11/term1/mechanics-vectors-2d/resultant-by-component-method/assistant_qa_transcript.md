**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! First one, and it comes up every year: why bother with components at all? If I draw the arrows carefully to scale and measure the answer with a ruler and protractor, surely I get the same result?

**Tutor:** You get an approximation, and in a written examination you get fewer marks. Three reasons. First, precision: a graphical answer is only as good as your pencil, your ruler and your protractor, and physics answers are expected to three significant figures. Measuring 235,7 newtons off a page is simply not possible; you would report 236 or 234 depending on the thickness of your line. Second, scale: graphical methods need a chosen scale, a stated scale, and enough page to draw it on. Give a learner four forces of a few hundred newtons each and the diagram runs off the paper. Third, and most importantly, the component method extends. When you meet a block on an incline, you will resolve gravity into a part along the slope and a part into the slope; when you meet three charges in a right-angled formation, you will resolve the electric forces the same way; when you meet projectile motion, you will resolve velocity. None of those problems can be solved with a protractor. So use graphical constructions when a question explicitly asks for a scale drawing, or to sanity-check that your answer points into a sensible quadrant. For everything else, resolve and add.

**Bianca:** Next: what actually goes wrong when someone uses sine where cosine belonged? Does it just make the number a bit off, or does it break the whole answer?

**Tutor:** It breaks it, and in a way that is hard to spot because the answer still looks plausible. Consider the 250 newton pull at 30 degrees above the horizontal. Correctly resolved, the horizontal part is 250 times cosine 30, which is 216,5 newtons, and the vertical part is 250 times sine 30, which is 125 newtons. Swap them and you claim the horizontal part is 125 newtons and the vertical is 216,5 — the pull is now mostly lifting rather than mostly dragging, which is physically the opposite situation. Carry that into a Newton's second law problem and the friction changes, the net force changes, and the acceleration comes out roughly half of what it should be. Nothing in the arithmetic warns you. The protection is a habit rather than a rule: draw the right-angled triangle every time and mark the angle the question gave you, not the angle you wish it had given you. The side lying against that angle is the cosine side; the side facing it is the sine side. Then apply a common-sense check — a force tilted only slightly above the ground should have a big horizontal part and a small vertical one. If your numbers say otherwise, you have swapped them.

**Bianca:** Someone asked about the calculator giving a strange angle. They had a resultant pointing up and to the LEFT, and the inverse tangent gave them a negative number. What went wrong?

**Tutor:** Nothing went wrong with the physics; the calculator simply cannot tell you what it was never told. The inverse tangent function only ever returns an angle between negative 90 degrees and positive 90 degrees, because the tangent ratio repeats every 180 degrees and the calculator has no way of knowing which of the two possibilities you meant. If your x-total came out negative — say 60 newtons to the west — and your y-total was positive 80 newtons north, the true resultant points up and to the left, roughly 127 degrees measured anticlockwise from east. Your calculator, fed a negative quotient, will answer negative 53,1 degrees, which points down and to the right: the exact opposite arrow. There are two safe fixes. The formal one is to add 180 degrees when the x-component is negative. The practical one, and the one I would use under examination pressure, is to feed the calculator only the sizes of the components, ignoring signs, and then use the signs to decide the quadrant yourself: 53,1 degrees, with x pointing west and y pointing north, means 53,1 degrees north of west. Say the direction in words tied to a named axis, and no quadrant can trap you.

**Bianca:** Last one, and it is a good one: what is the difference between the resultant and the equilibrant? People keep writing one when they mean the other.

**Tutor:** They are the same size and they point in exactly opposite directions, which is precisely why they get confused. The resultant is what the given forces ADD UP to — the single force that could replace all of them and leave the situation unchanged. The equilibrant is the force you would have to ADD to the situation to bring everything to rest — the one that cancels the resultant and makes the total zero. So if three forces on a ring have a resultant of 235,7 newtons at 65,8 degrees north of east, the equilibrant is 235,7 newtons at 65,8 degrees south of west, which is the same as a bearing rotated by 180 degrees. There is a neat way to see it in the tail-to-head construction: draw the given forces head to tail and they leave a gap between the last arrowhead and the first tail. The arrow that closes that gap, drawn from the last head back to the first tail, is the equilibrant, and the arrow drawn the other way, from the first tail to the last head, is the resultant. Same line, opposite arrowheads. Read the question wording carefully: "determine the resultant" and "determine the force required to keep the object in equilibrium" are asking for answers with the same number and opposite directions, and stating the wrong direction loses the mark even though your arithmetic was perfect.
