**Bianca:** Class questions before we pack up! First: if I pull the Earth up with the same 588 newtons it pulls me down with, why do I fall towards the Earth instead of the Earth falling up to me?

**Tutor:** Both of you fall — just not equally visibly. The two forces are identical in size, but a force is only half of the story; the other half is the mass it must move. Newton's second law says acceleration equals force over mass. Your 60 kilograms under 588 newtons accelerates at 9,8 metres per second squared — brisk, obvious, bruising. The Earth's 5,98 times 10 to the 24 kilograms under the same 588 newtons accelerates at about 10 to the minus 22 metres per second squared — a number so small that in the entire age of the universe it would not add up to anything you could measure with any instrument ever built. So the Earth genuinely does accelerate toward you every time you jump off a wall; the motion is real and the symmetry is perfect, but the shares are grotesquely unequal because the masses are. This is also the clean answer to a favourite theory question: the equal-and-opposite forces do not cancel, because they act on different bodies — one on you, one on the planet — and each body responds according to its own mass. Equal forces, wildly unequal consequences.

**Bianca:** Next one: in the ratio questions, someone quadrupled a mass and doubled the distance and got the same force back, and they are convinced they made a mistake. Can two changes really cancel perfectly?

**Tutor:** They can, and engineering that cancellation is exactly what the question setter did. Track each change through the formula separately, because they enter differently. Mass sits on top, first power: quadrupling one mass multiplies the force by four, full stop. Distance sits underneath, squared: doubling it divides the force by two squared, which is four. Four times, then divided by four — home to the original force. No mistake; a designed coincidence. The method to trust under pressure is the two-factor routine: write the mass factor, write the distance factor with its square, multiply, and never touch the calculator or the value of G, because both factors are pure ratios. Where learners actually go wrong is in only two places. They forget the square — halving a distance multiplies by four, not two — or they apply a mass change to BOTH masses when the question changed only one. Read the wording like a lawyer: "one mass is doubled" gives a factor of two; "each mass is doubled" gives two times two. Get those two habits right and ratio questions become the fastest marks in the whole topic.

**Bianca:** Here is one from the back row: the lesson said g gets weaker as you go up, and at the space station it is still almost nine. So why do the astronauts up there float while we, in slightly stronger gravity, stand?

**Tutor:** Because floating was never about the strength of gravity — it is about the absence of a floor's push. Start with what weight FEELS like: it is not the Earth's pull you sense, it is the surfaces resisting it — the chair on your legs, the ground on your soles. Remove every supporting surface and the feeling of weight disappears even while gravity operates at full strength. That is precisely the astronauts' condition. The station is not parked above gravity; it is falling toward the Earth at nearly nine metres per second squared, continuously, while also travelling sideways at almost eight kilometres per second. The combination means it keeps missing: the Earth's surface curves away exactly as fast as the station falls toward it, so the fall never lands. Everything inside — astronauts, floors, water bubbles — falls together, so no surface presses on any other. No press, no sensation of weight. You have felt the budget version: the lurch at the top of a trampoline bounce, or the stomach-lift when a car crests a bump — brief moments when nothing supports you and gravity is nonetheless fully switched on. Orbit is that moment made permanent by sideways speed.

**Bianca:** Last question: for weight on another planet, one classmate used w equals m g with the new planet's g, and another rebuilt everything from the big formula with G and the planet's mass and radius. Whose method is right?

**Tutor:** Both are right, and knowing why they agree is worth more than either calculation. The two formulas are the same physics at different altitudes of processing. The universal law, G M m over r squared, is the raw ingredient list: planet's mass, planet's radius, your mass. The formula g equals G M over r squared is that law with your mass already divided out — a pre-cooked, per-kilogram rate for a given planet. Multiply the rate by your kilograms, w equals m g, and you have reassembled exactly what the full formula computes. So choose by what the question hands you. Given the planet's g directly — like the Moon's 1,62 — use w equals m g and be done in one line: 60 times 1,62 gives 97,2 newtons. Given the planet's mass and radius instead, you must first cook the rate yourself: g equals G M over r squared, as we did for Venus to get 8,87, then multiply by 60 for about 532 newtons. Going the long way when g was given wastes minutes; trying the short way when only mass and radius are given is impossible. One caution for the long route: r is the distance from the planet's CENTRE, so for a surface weight use the radius, and for an altitude question add the height to the radius before squaring. Same law throughout — just served raw or pre-cooked.
