Hey, it's Bianca — universal gravitation looks scary because every number in it wears a power of ten, and one slip on the exponent key feels like it ruins everything. Breathe. Underneath the big numbers this is one formula and two proportionality rules, and that is genuinely the whole topic.

The formula first: force equals G times the two masses multiplied together, divided by the distance squared. G is GIVEN on the data sheet — 6,67 times 10 to the minus 11 — so there is nothing to memorise except the shape. Masses on top, distance squared on the bottom. Say that shape to yourself twice and you own it.

The two rules do the ratio questions without a calculator. Rule one: the force follows the masses directly — double a mass, double the force. Rule two: the force follows the distance INVERSELY SQUARED — double the distance, quarter the force. Every "what happens to F when..." question is just those two rules multiplied together. Take the factors one at a time, write each one down, multiply at the end. No formula sheet needed, no exponents, quick marks.

For the calculator questions, three small habits keep you safe. Use the exponent button for every power of ten instead of typing times ten hat — it prevents the classic order-of-operations disaster. Square the whole distance, bracket and all. And check the size of your answer against common sense: gravity between small objects should come out absurdly tiny, and a surface g should come out as a single-digit number of metres per second squared. If your classroom learners attract each other with 300 newtons, the exponent slipped, and now you know to look.

And keep the two words apart: mass in kilograms never changes; weight in newtons changes with the planet. Whenever a question moves you to the Moon or Mars, only g changes — recalculate weight as m times the new g and leave the mass alone.

One formula, two rules, three calculator habits. You've got this!
