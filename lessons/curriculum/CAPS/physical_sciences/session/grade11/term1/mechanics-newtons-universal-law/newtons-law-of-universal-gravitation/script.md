# Part 1 — Expert

One equation explains why an apple falls in an orchard, why the Moon stays in orbit, and why you would weigh less standing on Mars. This session states Newton's law of universal gravitation in the wording the marker wants, works the standard substitution problems, derives the gravitational acceleration of any planet from its mass and radius, and closes the long-running confusion between mass and weight. A calculator with an exponent key is essential throughout, because every number in this topic wears a power of ten.

## Subtopic: The Law of Universal Gravitation and Its Equation

The statement, exactly: every particle in the universe attracts every other particle with a force that is directly proportional to the product of their masses and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between their centres. In symbols, F equals G m one m two divided by d squared — the gravitational constant multiplied by the two masses, divided by the distance squared. The constant G is 6,67 times 10 to the minus 11 newton metre squared per kilogram squared, and it is on the data sheet, so never memorise it wrongly.

Three features of the law earn theory marks. First, the force is always attractive — gravity never pushes. Second, the two objects pull on each other with forces equal in magnitude and opposite in direction, a perfect Newton's third law pair, no matter how different the masses are. The Earth pulls you down with the same magnitude of force that you pull the Earth up. Third, the distance d is measured between the CENTRES of the objects, not between their surfaces, which matters enormously for planets.

Now the scale of the thing. Two learners, 60 kilograms and 80 kilograms, sit 1 metre apart in a Bloemfontein classroom. Formula: F equals G m one m two divided by d squared. Substitution: 6,67 times 10 to the minus 11, multiplied by 60, multiplied by 80, divided by 1 squared. Answer: about 3,2 times 10 to the minus 7 newtons. A third of a millionth of a newton — far too small to feel, which is why gravity between everyday objects goes unnoticed. Replace one learner with the Earth, mass 5,98 times 10 to the 24 kilograms, at a centre-to-centre distance of one Earth radius, 6,38 times 10 to the 6 metres, and the force on a 70 kilogram person works out to 686 newtons. That is exactly the weight you would get from m g, and it is no coincidence — weight IS this force.

## Subtopic: Inverse-Square Reasoning and Ratio Questions

Examiners love to change the masses or the distance and ask what happens to the force, without giving a single number. These ratio questions are quick marks once the structure is visible.

The force is proportional to each mass, so doubling one mass doubles the force, and doubling both masses multiplies the force by two times two, which is four. The force is inversely proportional to the SQUARE of the distance, so doubling the distance divides the force by two squared, which is four. Tripling the distance divides it by nine. Halving the distance multiplies it by four.

Work a combined one. The force between two objects is F. One mass is tripled and the distance is halved. The new force: tripling a mass multiplies by three; halving the distance multiplies by two squared, which is four; so the new force is three times four, which is twelve F. Write the reasoning in exactly that shape — factor from the masses, factor from the distance, multiply — and the marks follow.

With numbers: the gravitational force between two spheres is 3,6 times 10 to the minus 7 newtons. If the distance between their centres is doubled, the new force is 3,6 times 10 to the minus 7 divided by 4, which is 9 times 10 to the minus 8 newtons.

The trap is forgetting the square. A learner who halves the force when the distance doubles has treated gravity as inversely proportional to distance, and that error costs everything the question was worth. Say it while you work: distance changes are always squared.

This section's questions are with you now — no calculator needed for most of them, just the two proportionalities.

## Subtopic: Gravitational Acceleration from g Equals G M Over r Squared

Set the universal law equal to weight and something powerful drops out. The gravitational force on an object of mass m at the surface of a planet of mass M and radius r is G M m divided by r squared. But that force is the object's weight, m g. Set them equal: m g equals G M m divided by r squared. The small m cancels, leaving g equals G M divided by r squared — the gravitational acceleration multiplied out of the planet's mass and radius alone.

The cancellation is the physics. Because the object's own mass disappears, every object at the same place falls with the same acceleration. A brick and a feather dropped on the airless Moon land together. Heavy things are pulled harder, but they are also harder to accelerate, and the two effects cancel exactly.

Calculate it for Earth. Formula: g equals G M divided by r squared. Substitution: 6,67 times 10 to the minus 11, multiplied by 5,98 times 10 to the 24, divided by the square of 6,38 times 10 to the 6. Answer: 9,8 metres per second squared — the number you have used since Grade 10, now derived rather than given.

Now the Moon, mass 7,35 times 10 to the 22 kilograms, radius 1,74 times 10 to the 6 metres. Substitution: 6,67 times 10 to the minus 11, multiplied by 7,35 times 10 to the 22, divided by the square of 1,74 times 10 to the 6. Answer: 1,62 metres per second squared, about one sixth of Earth's value.

Height matters too. Because r is the distance from the planet's CENTRE, g falls as you climb. A satellite orbiting at one Earth radius above the surface sits at two Earth radii from the centre, so g there is 9,8 divided by two squared, which is 2,45 metres per second squared. Gravity at the International Space Station is still almost 9 metres per second squared — remember that for the weightlessness discussion coming next.

Work this section's questions now, and write the cancellation argument in full sentences at least once.

## Subtopic: Weight on Other Planets and True Weightlessness

Mass is the amount of matter in an object, measured in kilograms, identical everywhere in the universe. Weight is the gravitational force on that object, w equals m g, measured in newtons, and it changes whenever g changes. This distinction is asked every single year.

Take a 70 kilogram astronaut. On Earth, weight equals 70 multiplied by 9,8, which is 686 newtons. On the Moon, where g is 1,62 metres per second squared, weight equals 70 multiplied by 1,62, which is 113,4 newtons. On Mars, mass 6,42 times 10 to the 23 kilograms and radius 3,39 times 10 to the 6 metres, first find g: 6,67 times 10 to the minus 11, multiplied by 6,42 times 10 to the 23, divided by the square of 3,39 times 10 to the 6, which gives about 3,7 metres per second squared. Then weight equals 70 multiplied by 3,7, which is about 259 newtons. Through all three answers the mass never moved from 70 kilograms.

Now weightlessness, properly. Astronauts on the space station float, yet g there is nearly 9 metres per second squared, so gravity has certainly not switched off. The station and everyone in it are in continuous FREE FALL around the Earth — falling towards it and moving sideways fast enough to keep missing it. In free fall there is no floor pushing up, no supporting force at all, and the sensation of weight comes entirely from that supporting force. Weightlessness is therefore the condition of experiencing no support force while gravity still acts. The same thing happens for a moment when a lift cable snaps or a plane flies a parabolic arc.

The exam sentence to own: mass is constant everywhere; weight depends on the local gravitational acceleration; weightlessness is free fall, not the absence of gravity.

## Subtopic: Everything Pulls on Everything

Here is the strangest true sentence in the syllabus: you are pulling on your desk right now, and your desk is pulling back on you. You are pulling on the learner next to you, on Table Mountain, on the Moon. Every object with mass tugs on every other object with mass. That is the whole meaning of the word universal.

So why do you not feel your desk pulling you? Because gravity is fantastically weak unless at least one of the objects is enormous. Two learners a metre apart attract each other with about a third of a millionth of a newton — less than the weight of a grain of dust. You only ever notice gravity when a PLANET is one of the partners, because a planet brings 10 to the 24 kilograms to the relationship.

And here is the part that feels wrong but is exactly right: the Earth pulls you down with 686 newtons, and you pull the Earth UP with 686 newtons. Equal, opposite, same pair of forces. The Earth does not visibly move because 686 newtons shared over its colossal mass produces an acceleration too small to measure, while the same 686 newtons on your 70 kilograms moves you very convincingly.

Two things decide how hard the pull is: how much mass, and how far apart. More mass, more pull. More distance, much less pull — and the next section shows exactly how much less.

Your questions on this section are with you now — think of the pull as a quiet thread between every pair of objects in the room.

## Subtopic: Double the Distance, Quarter the Pull

Gravity fades with distance, but it fades in a very particular way, and the pattern is worth marks in every paper.

Picture a torch shining on a wall. Step twice as far back and the same light spreads over four times the area, so each patch of wall gets a quarter of the brightness. Gravity spreads out exactly like that light. Double the distance between two objects and the pull drops to a quarter. Triple the distance, and it drops to a ninth. The rule is: whatever you multiply the distance by, you divide the force by that number SQUARED.

The masses behave more simply. Double one mass, double the pull. Double both, and the pull is four times stronger. No squaring there — squaring belongs to distance alone.

Play the game the examiners play. The pull between two objects is some force F. Now triple one mass and double the distance. From the mass: times three. From the distance: divide by two squared, which is four. New force: three quarters of F. You never needed a calculator, just the two rules in the right order.

One warning, because this is where the marks leak. When the distance doubles, the force does NOT halve. It quarters. If you remember only one sentence from this section, make it that one: distance always gets squared.

## Subtopic: Same You, Different Weight

Step on a bathroom scale at home and it reads, say, 70 kilograms. Load the same scale into a spacecraft and step on it on the Moon, and it reads about 12 kilograms. Have you shrunk? Not by a gram.

Untangle two words. MASS is how much of you there is — the amount of matter. It is measured in kilograms and it is the same in Polokwane, on the Moon, and floating between galaxies. WEIGHT is how hard gravity pulls on that matter. It is a force, measured in newtons, and it depends entirely on where you are standing. On Earth, each kilogram gets pulled with about 9,8 newtons. On the Moon, each kilogram gets only 1,62 newtons, because the Moon is a much smaller ball of rock. Same you, weaker pull, lower weight.

Why does the Moon pull less? The pull of a planet comes from two things fighting each other: how much mass the planet has, and how far its surface is from its centre. The Moon has far less mass than the Earth, so even though it is also smaller, the mass shortage wins and the surface pull is about a sixth of ours. Mars lands in between, at about 3,7 newtons per kilogram — on Mars you would weigh roughly a third of your Earth weight and could slam-dunk without a run-up.

Last picture: the floating astronaut. People say there is no gravity in space, and it is simply false — gravity up at the space station is nearly as strong as here. The astronauts float because they are FALLING, endlessly, around the planet, and their floor is falling with them. Nothing is pushing up on their feet, and that missing push is the entire feeling of weightlessness. You have felt a flicker of it on a trampoline, in the instant after the bounce, when nothing touches your soles and your stomach lifts.
