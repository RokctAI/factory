**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! The first one comes up every single year: if the Earth and I pull on each other with exactly equal forces, why do I fall towards the Earth instead of the Earth falling towards me?

**Tutor:** Both of you fall — that is the honest answer — but by amounts so unequal that only one of them can ever be noticed. The forces are identical in magnitude, say 686 newtons for a 70 kilogram person, because the third law and the gravitation formula both demand it. But the CONSEQUENCE of a force is acceleration, and acceleration is force divided by mass. Divide 686 newtons by 70 kilograms and the person accelerates at 9,8 metres per second squared. Divide that same 686 newtons by the Earth's mass of 5,98 times 10 to the 24 kilograms and the Earth accelerates at about 10 to the minus 22 metres per second squared — a distance smaller than an atom's width over your entire lifetime of falling. So the pair of forces is perfectly symmetric; the pair of motions is not, purely because the masses differ by 23 powers of ten. Examiners love to ask this as a true-or-false: the statement "the Earth exerts a bigger force on you than you exert on the Earth" is always false, and the statement "the effects differ because the masses differ" is the sentence that earns the mark.

***

**Bianca:** Next one: a learner doubled the distance in a ratio question and halved the force, and lost the marks. They want to know why gravity cares about the SQUARE of the distance and not just the distance.

**Tutor:** Picture the pull of a mass spreading outward in every direction at once, like light leaving a bulb or spray leaving a garden sprinkler. At a distance r, all of that influence is spread over the surface of a sphere of radius r, and the area of a sphere grows with the SQUARE of its radius — double the radius and the sphere has four times the surface to cover. The same total influence painted over four times the area is a quarter as concentrated at every point. That is the geometric reason the law carries d squared in the denominator, and it is the same reason sound gets quiet and light gets dim with the square of distance. For the examination, the working protection is mechanical: write the formula, put the new distance in as two d, and square the whole bracket so the four appears on paper — two d, squared, gives four d squared, so the force divides by four. Learners who reason in their heads halve it; learners who substitute and square never do.

***

**Bianca:** This one is about the derivation: when we set m g equal to G M m over r squared, the small m cancels. What is that cancellation actually telling us about the world?

**Tutor:** It is telling you the single most famous fact in mechanics: everything falls at the same rate. Follow the logic slowly. The gravitational force on an object IS bigger when the object is more massive — a 10 kilogram brick is pulled with ten times the force of a 1 kilogram brick. But acceleration is force divided by mass, so that ten times bigger force must move ten times more matter, and the two factors of ten cancel perfectly. The acceleration that survives, g equals G M over r squared, contains only the PLANET's mass and radius — nothing about the falling object at all. That is why a hammer and a feather dropped together on the airless Moon strike the surface at the same instant, an experiment an astronaut actually performed. On Earth a feather loses only because air resistance interferes, not because gravity treats it differently. In an examination, if you are asked why all objects fall with the same acceleration, the expected answer is exactly this cancellation argument, written in words: greater mass means proportionally greater force but also proportionally greater inertia, so the acceleration is independent of the object's mass.

***

**Bianca:** Last one: on the formula sheet g equals G M over r squared has an r, but the force law has a d. Learners mix up what distance to use, especially for satellites. What is the rule?

**Tutor:** One rule, applied without exception: the distance in both formulae is measured from CENTRE to CENTRE, never from the surface. For an object standing on a planet, the centre-to-centre distance happens to equal the planet's radius, which is why the surface formula uses r — but that is a special case, not a different law. The moment a question involves height, add before you square. A satellite 300 kilometres above the Earth sits at 6,38 times 10 to the 6 metres plus 0,3 times 10 to the 6 metres from the centre, so 6,68 times 10 to the 6 metres is what enters the formula. And for a satellite at a height of one full Earth radius, the centre-to-centre distance is TWO Earth radii, so g there is 9,8 divided by two squared, which is 2,45 metres per second squared — a quarter, not a half. The classic lost mark is squaring only the radius and forgetting the height, or adding the height in kilometres to a radius in metres. Convert everything to metres, add first, square second, and that family of questions becomes routine.
