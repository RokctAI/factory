**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we wrap the books! First one: if the first law says moving things keep moving, why does my bicycle stop the moment I stop pedalling? It feels like the law is just wrong.

**Tutor:** It feels wrong because you have never ridden a bicycle in a friction-free world. Your bicycle stops because forces stop it — rolling resistance in the tyres, friction in the bearings, air pushing against your chest. The first law does not promise perpetual motion on Earth; it says an object holds its velocity UNLESS a net force acts, and on a real road a net backward force is always acting the moment you stop supplying energy. Strip the resistances away and the law shows itself: a puck on an air-hockey table glides almost forever, and a space probe coasting between planets travels for decades with its engines cold. Here is the mental flip that fixes the intuition permanently: pedalling is not what keeps the bicycle moving — pedalling is what CANCELS the friction trying to stop it. On a level road at steady speed, your pedalling force exactly balances the resistive forces, the net force is zero, and the first law is satisfied in plain sight. The ancients believed motion needed a constant push because they lived, like you, in a world soaked in friction. The first law is the discovery that the push was only ever paying friction's bill.

**Bianca:** Next: in the lift examples the scale reading kept changing — 678, 588, 498, even zero. A classmate wants to know which of those numbers is the person's real weight, and how a marker wants that written.

**Tutor:** The real weight never appeared on the scale at all except by coincidence. Weight is the gravitational force on the person, w equals m g, which is 60 times 9,8 — 588 newtons — in every single case, moving or not. The scale never measures weight; it measures the normal force it exerts on the feet, and the normal force is whatever value Newton's second law demands for the person's current acceleration. Stationary or constant velocity: acceleration zero, so the normal force must balance the weight exactly — 588, agreeing with the true weight by coincidence of arithmetic. Accelerating upward: the floor must push MORE than the weight to create a net upward force, so 678. Accelerating downward: less, so 498. Free fall: nothing at all. For full marks, write the free-body diagram with weight down and normal force up, state your positive direction, write N minus m g equals m a, and then answer the actual question asked — if it asks for weight, the answer is 588 newtons regardless of the lift's antics; if it asks for the scale reading or apparent weight, solve for N. And never write "the weight is 60 kilograms" — 60 kilograms is the mass, and confusing them is the most reliably punished error in the topic.

**Bianca:** Here is one that caused a full-on argument at break: if the wall pushes back on me exactly as hard as I push it, the forces are equal and opposite — so why don't they cancel and leave nothing happening at all?

**Tutor:** Because cancellation is only possible between forces acting on the SAME object, and third-law partners never do. Look carefully at where each force lands. Your push acts on the wall. The wall's push acts on you. One force per body. When you ask "does the wall move?", you audit only the forces on the wall: your push forward, and the enormous forces from the bricks and foundations holding it — your push loses, the wall stays. When you ask "do I move?", you audit only the forces on you: the wall's push backward on your hands, friction from the floor on your shoes. Stand on smooth tiles in socks and the wall's push wins — you slide backwards, which you can test tonight. Equal-and-opposite forces that DO act on one object certainly exist — the dictionary's weight and the desk's normal force — but those are balanced forces, not a third-law pair; they are different types of force, from different partners. Questions love exactly this disguise, so make the habit permanent: before calling two forces an action-reaction pair, name the two objects involved, and check the pattern "A on B" and "B on A". If both forces are "on B", the answer is no.

**Bianca:** Last one from the class: for the rocket, why do we subtract the weight from the thrust? Someone assumed 100 000 newtons of thrust just means 100 000 newtons of acceleration force.

**Tutor:** Because the rocket does not feel thrust alone — it feels every force on it, and gravity never takes a day off. The engines push up with 100 000 newtons, but the Earth simultaneously pulls the 8 000 kilogram rocket down with m g, which is 78 400 newtons. The rocket responds only to the net: 100 000 minus 78 400, which is 21 600 newtons, giving 21 600 over 8 000 — 2,7 metres per second squared. Think of the weight as an entry fee: the first 78 400 newtons of thrust buy nothing but a hover, and only what remains above the fee produces climb. That is why a rocket whose thrust is less than its weight never leaves the pad no matter how long the engines burn — the net force points downward and the pad simply keeps supplying a normal force. There is a lovely third-law story inside the engine too: the rocket accelerates by hurling exhaust gas downward, and it is the gas pushing back UP on the rocket that provides the thrust — no road, no air, nothing to push against is needed, which is exactly why rockets work in empty space. Two laws in one machine: the third law supplies the force, the second law says what that force, minus the weight, achieves.
