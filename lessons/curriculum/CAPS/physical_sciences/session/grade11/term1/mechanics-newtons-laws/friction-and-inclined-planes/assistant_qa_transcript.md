**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! First: why does the perpendicular part of the weight use cosine and the part along the slope use sine? Everyone memorises it and then swaps them under pressure.

**Tutor:** Then stop memorising it and derive it in five seconds, which is what strong candidates do. Start from the geometry. The ramp makes an angle theta with the horizontal. The line perpendicular to the ramp therefore makes the same angle theta with the vertical, because if you rotate a surface by an angle, the line perpendicular to it rotates by the identical angle. Now the weight points straight down, along the vertical. So the angle between the weight vector and the perpendicular-to-the-ramp direction is exactly theta. Build the right-angled triangle with the weight as the hypotenuse: the side lying along the perpendicular direction is ADJACENT to theta, and adjacent means cosine, giving mg cosine theta. The side lying along the slope is OPPOSITE theta, and opposite means sine, giving mg sine theta. There is also a sanity check that never fails. Imagine the ramp almost flat, theta near zero. Almost nothing should slide the object; almost all the weight should press into the surface. Sine of a small angle is near zero and cosine of a small angle is near one, which matches. Now imagine a nearly vertical cliff: nearly all of the weight should send the object down, and it should press on the surface hardly at all. Sine of ninety degrees is one, cosine is zero. The formulae behave correctly at both extremes, so you can always check yourself in a few seconds.

***

**Bianca:** Next: a learner insisted that a wider crate must have more friction, because more of it is touching the floor. It sounds so reasonable. Why is it wrong?

**Tutor:** It is reasonable, and it is wrong, and the reason is a lovely piece of physics. Friction depends on the coefficient and on the normal force, and nothing else in this syllabus. Turn a brick from its broad face onto its narrow end and two things change in opposite directions, cancelling exactly. The area of contact shrinks, yes, but the same weight is now concentrated on that smaller area, so the pressure at every point of contact rises in the same proportion. What actually generates friction is the microscopic bonding and interlocking between the two surfaces where they genuinely touch, and the true contact area — which is far smaller than the apparent area, because surfaces are rough at a microscopic level — depends on how hard they are pressed together, not on how much of the outline overlaps. Double the apparent area at half the pressure and you get the same true contact and the same friction. This is exactly why the syllabus formula contains no area term at all: friction equals mu times the normal force, and nothing more. The one place learners meet an apparent exception is racing tyres, where the rubber deforms and the physics goes beyond the simple model, so it is not something the examination will ask you to reconcile.

**Bianca:** This one caused an argument: a box sits still on a ramp. Is the friction on it equal to the coefficient times the normal force, or not?

**Tutor:** Almost certainly not, and the argument is worth having because it costs marks every year. When the box is stationary the friction acting is STATIC friction, and static friction is not a fixed quantity — it is whatever value is required to keep the box in equilibrium, up to a ceiling. That ceiling, and only that ceiling, is the coefficient of static friction times the normal force. So take a 20 kilogram crate on a 30 degree ramp with a normal force of 169,7 newtons. The weight component pulling it down the slope is 98 newtons. If the box is sitting still, the friction acting is exactly 98 newtons up the slope, because that is what balance requires. The maximum available might be 0,55 times 169,7, which is 93,3 newtons — and there is your answer, because 93,3 is less than 98, so the box cannot in fact stay still and it slides. But if the coefficient had been 0,8, the maximum would be 135,8 newtons, the box would stay, and the actual friction would still be 98 newtons, not 135,8. So the working order is always: calculate what friction is needed for equilibrium, calculate the maximum available, then compare. Only if the object is actually sliding do you use the kinetic formula and take that value as a fact.

**Bianca:** Last one: why does the mass cancel out in that tangent test? It feels wrong that a heavy crate and a light crate slip at the same angle.

**Tutor:** It feels wrong, and it is one of the most satisfying results in the topic once you see why. At the point of slipping, the force sending the object down the slope has just been matched by the maximum grip. The force down the slope is m g sine theta. The maximum grip is mu s times the normal force, and the normal force on a slope is m g cosine theta, so the grip is mu s m g cosine theta. Set them equal and the m and the g appear on both sides and cancel, leaving sine theta equals mu s cosine theta, which rearranges to tangent theta equals mu s. Mass has vanished. The physical reason is that mass does two opposing jobs at once: making an object heavier increases the pull down the slope, but it presses the object harder into the surface by exactly the same factor, so the grip grows in step. A 200 kilogram crate has ten times the sliding force of a 20 kilogram crate and ten times the grip, so the competition ends in the same result. That is why a rough-ish plank tilted slowly will drop a matchbox and a brick at essentially the same angle, and it is also why engineers can specify a safe road gradient for a given surface without knowing anything about the vehicles that will use it.
