**Bianca:** Class questions to close! First up: why is the tension not simply equal to the pulling force? If I pull the front trolley with 40 newtons, surely 40 newtons travels down the cord to the back one?

**Tutor:** That instinct treats the cord as a pipe that transports your force, and it is worth demolishing properly. The front trolley does not forward your pull; it CONSUMES some of it. Your 40 newtons acts on the 6 kilogram leader, which needs a share of that force to accelerate its own mass. Look at the frictionless numbers: the pair accelerates at 4 metres per second squared, and the leader alone needs 6 times 4, which is 24 newtons, to do that. Only what remains — 16 newtons — appears in the cord, and 16 is exactly what the 4 kilogram trailer needs for its own 4 times 4. The tension is not a relayed copy of your pull; it is precisely the force required to keep the trailer matching the leader's acceleration, no more, no less. Test the idea at the extremes: if the trailer were nearly massless, the cord would carry nearly nothing, because a feather needs no force to accelerate; if the trailer were far heavier than the leader, the tension would climb toward your full pull. The cord's reading is a negotiation between the two masses, and the two-step method is how you sit in on the negotiation.

***

**Bianca:** Next: when we wrote the system equation for the bench and the hanging mass, the block's 29,4 newton weight just vanished. A classmate wants to know who gave us permission to ignore a real force.

**Tutor:** Newton did, through the geometry. The system equation adds up forces ALONG the direction of motion, and the block's motion is horizontal, along the bench. Its weight acts straight down — perpendicular to that motion — and the bench's normal force acts straight up, also perpendicular. Those two are locked in their own private vertical balance: the block does not accelerate into the bench or leap off it, so the normal force equals the weight and the pair contributes exactly zero along the direction of travel. Vanished is the wrong word; CANCELLED is the right one, and it happened off-stage, in the perpendicular direction. But notice the weight still matters enormously — indirectly. The normal force it sets determines the friction, 0,2 times 29,4, which is the 5,88 newtons that does appear in the system equation. So the honest summary is: forces perpendicular to the motion never enter the along-the-motion sum, but they may govern the size of forces that do. Contrast the hanging mass: its weight is fully ALONG its direction of motion, so all 49 newtons drive the system. One weight drives, one weight only sets the friction, and knowing which is which is most of this topic.

***

**Bianca:** Here is the one that always starts a debate: the string tension came out at 22,05 newtons, but the hanging thing weighs 49. Half the class is convinced the string must carry the whole weight. Who is right?

**Tutor:** The falling mass settles the debate all by itself. Ask one question: is the hanging mass accelerating? Yes — downward, at 5,39 metres per second squared. Newton's second law then demands a net downward force on it, which means the downward weight must BEAT the upward tension. If the string carried the full 49 newtons, the forces on the mass would balance perfectly, and a balanced mass does not gain speed — it hangs there, or drifts at constant velocity. So a taut string below its hanging weight is not a paradox; it is the signature of acceleration. Run the two extremes to make it stick. Nail the block to the bench so nothing can move: acceleration zero, and the tension rises to the full 49 newtons — the string now carries the whole weight, as your classmates expect. Cut the string entirely: tension zero, and the mass free-falls at 9,8. Every real problem lives between those ends: the faster the system accelerates, the lighter the string's load. You can even read the block's side as a check: the tension 22,05 minus friction 5,88 leaves 16,17 newtons on 3 kilograms — 5,39 again. One acceleration, both stories agree, and the class can stop arguing.

***

**Bianca:** Last question: on the slope problem, someone put the block's whole 49 newton weight into the system equation instead of 24,5 and got nonsense. What is the rule for weights on inclines in these systems?

**Tutor:** The rule is: a weight only enters the system equation with the part of itself that lies ALONG the track the system moves on. The hanging mass's track is vertical, so its full weight, 78,4 newtons for the 8 kilogram dangler, drives the system. The block's track is the slope's surface, so only the component of its weight along that surface — m g sine theta, which is 5 times 9,8 times 0,5, giving 24,5 newtons — resists the motion. The other component, m g cosine theta, presses into the slope, where the normal force answers it, exactly as the bench answered the block's weight in the flat problem; on a frictionless slope that pressing component then has no further part to play. Put the full 49 in and you are claiming the entire weight opposes the dangler, as if the block hung vertically too — the geometry of the question has been thrown away. So the drill for any incline-pulley system: resolve the slope-body's weight first, feed only the sine component into the system step, and keep the cosine component for the normal force and any friction. Here that gives 78,4 minus 24,5 over 13 kilograms — 4,15 metres per second squared — and a tension of 45,2 newtons, comfortably below the dangler's 78,4, exactly as an accelerating system requires.
