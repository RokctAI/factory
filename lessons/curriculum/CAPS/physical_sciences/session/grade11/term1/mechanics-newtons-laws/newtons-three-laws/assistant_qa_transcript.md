**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! The first one is honest confusion: if Newton's third law says every force comes with an equal and opposite force, why is the net force on anything ever non-zero? Shouldn't everything always cancel?

**Tutor:** This is the single best question in the topic, and the answer is one word: bodies. A net force is always calculated for ONE chosen object, and only the forces acting ON that object may be added. The two members of an action-reaction pair never act on the same object — that is part of the definition — so they can never both appear in the same sum. Take a crate being dragged across a floor. The forces on the crate are the rope's pull forward, friction backward, weight down and normal force up. Those are what you add. The crate's pull back on the rope, the crate's push down on the floor, the crate's upward pull on the Earth — every one of those is real and equal and opposite, but every one of them acts on something that is not the crate, so none of them enters the crate's equation. The discipline that protects you in an examination is to say out loud, before you write anything, "I am doing the forces on the crate." Then any force whose name does not end with "on the crate" gets left out. Do that and the paradox disappears permanently.

**Bianca:** Next: someone got full marks on the calculation and lost marks anyway. They wrote "the weight of the box is 15 kilograms". What is actually wrong with that, beyond it being sloppy?

**Tutor:** It is not sloppy, it is a physics error, and examiners treat it as one. Mass and weight are different quantities with different units, different natures and different behaviour. Mass is a scalar measured in kilograms; it counts how much matter is present, it measures the object's inertia, and it is identical on Earth, on the Moon and in deep space. Weight is a vector measured in newtons; it is the gravitational force the Earth exerts on the object, calculated as w equals m g, and it changes with location — that same 15 kilogram box weighs 147 newtons here and about 24 newtons on the Moon, while its mass stays 15 kilograms in both places. So "the weight is 15 kilograms" states a force in the units of matter, which is like giving a distance in litres. The practical protection is a rule about which number goes where: whenever you calculate an acceleration you divide by MASS in kilograms, and whenever you put gravity into a force equation you use WEIGHT in newtons. Write w equals mg as a separate line before you start, and the two never get swapped.

**Bianca:** Here is one from the lift question. If your weight does not actually change in a lift, why does the scale change? Is the scale lying, or is something real happening?

**Tutor:** Nothing is lying — the scale simply never measured your weight in the first place. A bathroom scale measures the force it has to exert upward on your feet, which is the normal force, and it prints that number as if it were a weight because, when you stand still on solid ground, the two happen to be equal. Put the scale in an accelerating lift and they stop being equal. Standing still, the only two vertical forces on you are your weight of 686 newtons down and the normal force up, and since you are not accelerating they must balance at 686 newtons. Now the lift accelerates upward at 2 metres per second squared. You must accelerate upward too, which requires an upward net force of mass times acceleration, 70 times 2, which is 140 newtons. The only force that can supply it is the normal force, so the normal force climbs to 826 newtons and the scale prints 826. Accelerating downward, the normal force must fall short of your weight by that same 140 newtons, and the scale prints 546. In free fall the floor falls away as fast as you do and needs to push with nothing at all, so the scale prints zero. Through every case your mass is 70 kilograms and your weight is 686 newtons. What changed was the support, and physicists call the scale reading the apparent weight for exactly that reason.

**Bianca:** Last one: in a question about a car crashing into a truck, a learner argued the truck exerts a bigger force because it is heavier. What is the right answer, and how do you explain it to someone who refuses to believe it?

**Tutor:** The right answer is that the forces are exactly equal in magnitude and opposite in direction, no matter how different the masses are, because that is what the third law says without exception. The way to make it believable is to separate force from consequence. Both vehicles feel the same size of force during the collision — say 50 000 newtons each, in opposite directions. But the consequence of a force is acceleration, and acceleration is force divided by MASS. Give a 1 000 kilogram car 50 000 newtons and it experiences 50 metres per second squared, which is violent. Give a 20 000 kilogram truck the same 50 000 newtons and it experiences 2,5 metres per second squared, which the driver barely feels. Same force, wildly different accelerations, because the masses differ by a factor of twenty. So the intuition that "the truck hits harder" is really an observation about damage and acceleration, not about force, and once you separate those two ideas the third law stops feeling unfair. The examination phrasing to remember: the magnitudes of the forces are equal; the effects are not, because the masses are not.
