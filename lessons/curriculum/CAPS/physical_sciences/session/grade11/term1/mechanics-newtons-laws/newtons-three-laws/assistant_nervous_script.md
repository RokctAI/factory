Hey, it's Bianca — Newton's laws look enormous because they show up in half the paper, and because every question seems to come with a different picture: a crate, a lift, a rocket, a rope. Take a breath. There are three laws and one method, and the method is the same whether the picture is a crate or a rocket.

The three laws in your own words, first. One: things keep doing what they are doing unless something unbalanced acts on them. Two: leftover push divided by mass gives you the speeding-up. Three: every push comes with an equal push back on something else. That is the whole syllabus content. Everything else is arithmetic.

The method is four steps, and I want you to write all four every single time, even when the answer looks obvious, because markers give method marks. Step one: draw the dot and the arrows — the free-body diagram. Step two: write down which direction you are calling positive. Step three: write Fnet equals ma for that direction. Step four: substitute numbers with units and state the answer with a direction. Four lines. Even if you get the number wrong, those lines are worth marks.

Now the habit that saves the most marks in this topic, and it is embarrassingly small: never write kilograms where newtons belong. Mass is kilograms and it never changes. Weight is a force in newtons and it is mass times 9,8. A 70 kilogram learner does not weigh 70 kilograms — she weighs 686 newtons. Half the lost marks in lift and rocket questions come from that one slip, so say it out loud while you work: mass in kilograms, weight in newtons.

Two more quick guards. If the question says "at rest" or "at constant velocity", the net force is zero — write that down immediately and pair the forces off. And when you are asked for an action-reaction pair, check that your two forces sit on two different objects; if they are both on the same object, you have the wrong pair, no matter how equal and opposite they look.

Three laws, four steps, kilograms versus newtons. That is genuinely all of it, and this is the topic where careful writing beats clever thinking every time. You've got this!
