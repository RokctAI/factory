**Bianca:** Time for the class questions! The first one is about the wardrobe: if static friction always matches my push, how can anything ever be "on the point of slipping"? Matching sounds like it could go on forever.

**Tutor:** The matching is real but the budget is finite. Think of static friction as a guard with a fixed maximum strength. While your push stays below that maximum, the guard matches you exactly — 100 newtons against 100, 250 against 250 — and the object stays perfectly still with zero net force. "On the point of slipping" is the precise moment your push equals the guard's maximum, mu s times N. One newton more and the surfaces' microscopic interlocking gives way; the object breaks loose, and a weaker guard — kinetic friction, mu k times N — takes over. That handover is why the first shove is the hard one and why the wardrobe lurches: at the instant of breaking free, your push was sized to beat 294 newtons of maximum grip, but the resistance suddenly drops to about 206, leaving a large net force for a moment. The disciplined method in any question is: compute the ceiling first, compare the applied force with it, and only then decide which friction you are dealing with. If the object is stationary under a push below the ceiling, the friction equals the push — writing the ceiling value for a stationary object is the most common error in this entire topic.

***

**Bianca:** Next question: on the ramp, why is the normal force 120,4 newtons and not 147? A classmate says the ramp must hold up the whole toolbox, so surely it pushes with the whole weight.

**Tutor:** The ramp does not hold up the whole toolbox — that is exactly the point. On level ground the surface is the only thing opposing gravity, so it must supply the full 147 newtons. On a slope, gravity's pull has been split into two jobs: 84,3 newtons of it pulls the toolbox ALONG the ramp, and only 120,4 newtons presses INTO the ramp. The surface can only answer the force pressing into it — a surface pushes perpendicular to itself, and it has no opinion about forces running along it. So the normal force is m g cosine theta, the pressing share, and the along-the-slope share is someone else's problem: it becomes the driving force that friction and tension must deal with. Notice the toolbox is not in vertical equilibrium in the everyday sense — it is in equilibrium perpendicular to the ramp, which is the direction that matters once you rotate your axes. And check the limits to convince your classmate: at zero degrees, cosine is one and N equals the full weight, as on a floor; at ninety degrees, cosine is zero and N vanishes, because a vertical wall cannot support a box resting against it at all. The slope lives between those extremes, always below the full weight.

***

**Bianca:** This one comes up every time we do ramps: when the toolbox slides down, friction acts up the slope, but when the mechanic drags it up, friction acts down. How do we know which way friction points before we start?

**Tutor:** Read the motion, not the ramp. Kinetic friction has one rule with no exceptions: it points opposite to the velocity of the sliding object, along the surface. Object sliding down — friction up. Object dragged up — friction down. The size does not change when the direction flips: it is mu k times N in both cases, 30,1 newtons for our toolbox, because neither mu k nor the normal force cares which way the box travels. That is why the two answers in the worked example differ so much: sliding down, friction fights gravity and the net force is 84,3 minus 30,1; dragged up, friction JOINS gravity and the mechanic must supply 84,3 plus 30,1. Static friction needs one more sentence: for a stationary object, friction opposes the TENDENCY to move — the direction the object would slide if friction went on strike. A parked box on a ramp would slide down, so static friction points up. The reliable routine is: first decide which way the object moves or would move, then draw the friction arrow opposite to that, and only then write your equation. Choosing the friction direction by habit instead of by motion is trap number three from the lesson, and it converts a correct method into a wrong sign in one stroke.

***

**Bianca:** Last one, and it is the shortcut everyone loves: the tangent test with no mass in it. Someone wants to know why the mass disappears — and whether that means a loaded bakkie and an empty one lose grip on the same hill.

**Tutor:** The mass disappears because both sides of the contest grow in proportion to it. The downhill pull is m g sine theta; the grip ceiling is mu s times N, which is mu s times m g cosine theta. Set them equal at the point of slipping and m g appears on both sides — cancel it, divide by cosine, and you are left with tangent theta equals mu s. Doubling the mass doubles the attacker and the defender identically, so the outcome of the contest never changes. On the point of slipping, only two things matter: how steep, and how grippy. So yes — in this idealised model, a loaded vehicle and an empty one begin to slide at the same slope angle, and a 150 kilogram toolbox slides on our 35 degree ramp exactly as the 15 kilogram one does, because tangent 35 degrees, at 0,700, beats a mu s of 0,6 regardless of mass. What the mass DOES change is everything after the slide begins: the forces, the momentum, the energy involved in stopping it — which is why runaway heavy vehicles are so much more dangerous even though they let go at the same angle. Keep the test as your opening move on any "does it slip" question: one tangent, one comparison, no mass required — then bring the mass back for the accelerations that follow.
