# Part 1 — Expert

Three laws written more than three centuries ago carry the largest single section of the Grade 11 physics paper. This session states each of them in the wording a marker is looking for, builds the four-step method that turns any force question into an equation, and works the standard applications: a crate dragged across a floor, a passenger in a lift, and a rocket leaving the pad. Free-body diagrams from Grade 10 are the tool throughout, so keep the idea of a single dot with labelled arrows close at hand.

## Subtopic: Newton's First Law and the Meaning of Inertia

The statement, exactly: an object continues in a state of rest, or of uniform velocity in a straight line, unless it is acted upon by a net or unbalanced force. Learn the phrase "net force", not "force" — forces are almost always present; what matters is whether they cancel.

INERTIA is the tendency of an object to resist a change in its state of rest or uniform motion, and MASS is the quantity that measures inertia. A loaded bakkie is harder to get moving and harder to stop than an empty one, at identical engine and brake settings, because it carries more mass and therefore more inertia.

The syllabus asks specifically about seatbelts, so reason it out properly. A minibus taxi travels at 60 kilometres per hour along the R21; the passengers travel at 60 kilometres per hour too. The driver brakes hard. A backward net force acts on the vehicle through the tyres, and the vehicle slows. No such force acts on the passengers, so, obeying the first law, the passengers continue forward at their original velocity — into the seat in front, or through the windscreen. The seatbelt exists to supply that missing backward force to the body. The headrest does the same job in the opposite direction in a rear-end collision, where the seat pushes the torso forward and an unsupported head is left behind, which is exactly what whiplash is.

Two traps sit here. First, learners write that "a force is needed to keep an object moving". It is not; a force is needed to CHANGE motion. A hockey puck on frictionless ice needs nothing at all to keep going. Second, "uniform velocity" includes the direction, so an object moving in a circle at constant speed is NOT obeying the first law's zero-net-force condition, because its direction is changing.

Fix that vocabulary now, because this section's questions are with you: the statement in full, inertia defined and linked to mass, and the seatbelt argument reasoned rather than recited.

## Subtopic: Newton's Second Law and the Fnet = ma Method

The statement: when a resultant or net force acts on an object, the object accelerates in the direction of the net force; the acceleration is directly proportional to the net force and inversely proportional to the mass of the object. In symbols, F net equals m a — the net force equals the mass multiplied by the acceleration. The unit follows directly: one newton is the net force that gives a mass of one kilogram an acceleration of one metre per second squared, so one newton equals one kilogram metre per second squared.

The method never changes, and writing it out earns method marks even when the arithmetic slips. Step one: draw the free-body diagram — the object as a dot, every force as a labelled arrow from that dot. Step two: choose and state a positive direction. Step three: write F net equals m a for that direction, adding forces along the positive direction and subtracting those against it. Step four: substitute and solve, then state the answer with units and a direction.

Work it. A 20 kilogram crate is dragged across a rough concrete floor in a Pretoria warehouse by a horizontal force of 100 newtons; friction on the crate is 40 newtons. Free-body diagram: weight down, normal force up, applied force 100 newtons forward, friction 40 newtons backward. Take forward as positive. Formula: F net equals F applied minus f. Substitution: 100 newtons minus 40 newtons. Answer: F net equals 60 newtons forward. Formula: a equals F net divided by m. Substitution: 60 newtons divided by 20 kilograms. Answer: a equals 3 metres per second squared, in the direction of the applied force.

Change the question: what applied force would move the same crate at CONSTANT velocity? Constant velocity means zero acceleration, so F net equals m times zero, which is zero, so the applied force equals the friction: 40 newtons. Notice that the phrase "constant velocity" is not scenery — it is the instruction to set the net force to zero.

The classic error is putting weight into the horizontal equation. Vertical forces belong in the vertical equation. Handle the two directions separately and they never contaminate each other.

Work through this section's questions now, writing every solution as free-body diagram, positive direction, equation, substitution, answer.

## Subtopic: Vertical Applications — Lifts, Cables and Apparent Weight

Vertically, two forces usually appear: the weight pulling down, and a normal force or a tension pulling up. The weight is calculated as w equals m g — the mass multiplied by the gravitational acceleration, 9,8 metres per second squared downward.

A 70 kilogram learner stands on a bathroom scale in a lift in a Sandton office block. The scale reads the normal force it exerts upward. Free-body diagram: weight 70 times 9,8, which is 686 newtons, downward; normal force N upward. Take upward as positive.

Case one, lift at rest or moving at constant velocity: acceleration is zero, so N minus 686 equals zero, giving N equals 686 newtons. The scale reads the true weight.

Case two, lift accelerating upward at 2 metres per second squared. Formula: N minus m g equals m a. Substitution: N minus 686 equals 70 times 2. Answer: N equals 686 plus 140, which is 826 newtons. The learner feels heavier, and the scale agrees, but the true weight is unchanged.

Case three, lift accelerating downward at 2 metres per second squared. With upward still positive, the acceleration is negative: N minus 686 equals 70 times negative 2, so N equals 686 minus 140, which is 546 newtons. Lighter on the scale, same weight in reality.

Case four, the cable fails and the lift falls freely. The acceleration is 9,8 metres per second squared downward, so N equals 686 minus 686, which is zero newtons. That is apparent weightlessness — the sensation astronauts experience in orbit — and it is not the absence of gravity but the absence of a supporting force.

The same equation launches a rocket from a pad. A 5 000 kilogram rocket produces 70 000 newtons of thrust. Weight equals 5 000 times 9,8, which is 49 000 newtons. Formula: F net equals F thrust minus w. Substitution: 70 000 minus 49 000. Answer: 21 000 newtons upward, so a equals 21 000 divided by 5 000, which is 4,2 metres per second squared upward.

The exam trap in every one of these is mass against weight: mass is 70 kilograms, weight is 686 newtons, and writing "the weight is 70 kilograms" costs the mark every time.

The questions for this section are in front of you now: sign convention chosen and stated, apparent weight distinguished from true weight, and every value carrying its correct unit.

## Subtopic: Newton's Third Law and Action-Reaction Pairs

The statement: when object A exerts a force on object B, object B simultaneously exerts a force on object A that is equal in magnitude and opposite in direction.

Four properties define a genuine action-reaction pair, and the examiner tests all four. The forces are equal in magnitude. They are opposite in direction. They act on DIFFERENT objects — this is the property that matters most. And they are of the same type: two gravitational forces, or two contact forces, never one of each.

Read the swimmer at a Cape Town pool: her hands push the water backwards, and the water pushes her forwards with an equal force. The rocket above pushes exhaust gas downwards; the gas pushes the rocket upwards. A prop in a scrum pushes on the opposing pack, and the opposing pack pushes back just as hard. You walk by pushing backwards on the ground, and the ground pushes you forwards.

Now the misconception that decides marks. A textbook rests on a table. Its weight is 20 newtons down and the normal force is 20 newtons up. Equal, opposite — but NOT an action-reaction pair, because both act on the SAME object, the book, and they are different types of force. Sort them properly. The Earth pulls the book down with 20 newtons; the pair to that is the book pulling the EARTH up with 20 newtons. The table pushes the book up with 20 newtons; the pair to that is the book pushing the TABLE down with 20 newtons. Two pairs, four forces, each pair acting on two different bodies.

That also answers the question learners ask about horses and carts: if the cart pulls back on the horse as hard as the horse pulls the cart, why does anything move? Because those two forces act on different objects and therefore never appear in the same net-force equation. The cart accelerates because of the forces on the CART — the horse's pull forward against friction backward — and nothing the cart does to the horse belongs in that sum.

The final questions of this part are with you now: state the law precisely, identify true pairs, and explain in full sentences why weight and normal force on a resting object are not one of them.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same three laws from inside a minibus taxi on a Monday morning, where every one of them happens to you before you have even had breakfast.

## Subtopic: Why You Jerk Forward When the Taxi Brakes

You are sitting in a taxi doing a comfortable speed down the road. The driver spots a pothole and stamps on the brakes. Your body flies forward. Why?

Here is the honest answer: your body did not fly anywhere. Your body kept doing exactly what it was already doing — travelling forward at the taxi's old speed. It was the TAXI that changed, because the brakes and tyres gave it a backward shove. Nothing gave your body that shove, so your body carried on, and the seat in front came to meet you.

This stubbornness has a name: inertia. Everything has it, and the more mass a thing has, the more of it there is. Try pushing a shopping trolley loaded with 20 kilograms of mielie meal and then push an empty one — same hands, same push, and the loaded one barely wants to start or stop. That reluctance is inertia, and mass is simply how much of it you have.

So the seatbelt is not there to hold you in your seat while you sit still. It is there to be the thing that shoves you backwards when the taxi shoves itself backwards, so that your body and your taxi change speed together. The headrest is the same idea for a bump from behind: the seat pushes your body forward and, without the headrest, your head gets left behind for a moment. That is whiplash, and it is the first law hurting your neck.

One last thing to unlearn: you do NOT need a force to keep something moving. You need a force to CHANGE its motion. A soccer ball on smooth ground keeps rolling not because something is pushing it but because nothing much is stopping it.

Your questions on this section are with you now — think taxi, think seatbelt, and use the word inertia proudly.

## Subtopic: Harder Push, Heavier Load

Second law, and it is really just two things you already believe.

Believe one: push harder, and something speeds up faster. Believe two: load it heavier, and the same push speeds it up more slowly. That is the entire law. Speeding-up is what physicists call acceleration, and the law says acceleration goes up when the push goes up and goes down when the mass goes up.

But which push? Not the push from your hands — the LEFTOVER push, after everything fighting you has been subtracted. Picture the warehouse crate: you pull it with 100 newtons, and the rough floor drags back with 40 newtons of friction. The leftover is 100 minus 40, which is 60 newtons. That leftover is what physics calls the net force, and it is the only push that matters.

Now share it out over the mass. The crate is 20 kilograms, so each kilogram gets 60 divided by 20, which is 3. The crate speeds up by 3 metres per second every second — an acceleration of 3 metres per second squared. Written as a formula, that is F net equals m a: net force equals mass times acceleration.

And here is a beautiful shortcut hidden in the same idea. If something is standing still, or moving at a steady speed in a straight line, then it is not speeding up at all, so the leftover push must be ZERO. Everything cancels. That single sentence solves half the questions in this topic — the moment you read "constant velocity" or "at rest", write down that the forces balance and start pairing them off.

Two habits, and they are worth real marks. Keep sideways forces with sideways forces and up-down forces with up-down forces; never mix them. And pick a direction to call positive before you start, so the minus signs behave.

Questions on this section are coming to you now — hunt for the leftover push first, then share it over the mass.

## Subtopic: The Lift Floor and the Wall That Pushes Back

Two last pictures, and they finish the topic.

First, the lift. Stand on a bathroom scale in a lift at the mall. The scale is not measuring you; it is measuring how hard it has to push up on your feet. Standing still, it pushes with your full weight — 686 newtons for a 70 kilogram person. Now the lift jerks upward: to get you moving up, the floor has to push a bit EXTRA, and the scale jumps to 826 newtons. You feel heavy. When the lift starts going down, the floor eases off and the scale drops to 546 newtons. You feel light. And if the cable ever snapped, the floor would fall away as fast as you do, push with nothing at all, and the scale would read zero. That is the "weightless" feeling astronauts have — not gravity switched off, just nothing underneath pushing back. Through all of it, your actual weight never changed once.

Second, the wall. Push a wall as hard as you can and the wall pushes back on you exactly as hard. That is the third law, and it is true of everything: you push the ground backwards and the ground pushes you forwards, which is the only reason walking works. A swimmer shoves water back and the water shoves her forward.

The bit that confuses everyone: if the pushes are always equal, why does anything ever move? Because the two pushes land on two DIFFERENT things. The horse pulls the cart forward and the cart pulls the horse backward — but the cart only feels the forces landing on the cart, and one of those is the horse's pull. They never meet in the same sum, so they never cancel.

Same trap wearing another coat: a book on a table has weight down and the table's push up, equal and opposite — but they are both on the BOOK, so they are not a pair at all. The real partner of the book's weight is the book pulling the whole Earth upwards. Odd, true, and worth a mark.

Three laws: things keep doing what they are doing, leftover push divided by mass gives acceleration, and every push comes with an equal push back on someone else. The last questions of the lesson are yours now — take them one picture at a time.
