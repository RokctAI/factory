# Part 1 — Expert

Newton's second law applies to one body at a time. The examination, however, prefers two bodies joined by a string — crates in a warehouse, a trailer behind a bakkie, a bucket on a rope over a pulley — and asks for both the acceleration of the system and the tension in the string. This session builds the two-step method that answers those questions reliably, then runs it through the three standard arrangements: two bodies on a flat surface, a body on a table connected over a pulley to a hanging body, and an incline with a pulley.

## Subtopic: One String, One Acceleration — the Rules of the System

Three assumptions do the work, and each one earns you a physical fact. The string is INEXTENSIBLE, meaning it cannot stretch. Therefore the two bodies move together, and their accelerations have the SAME MAGNITUDE. If one crate moves 30 centimetres, so does the other; if one gains speed at 3 metres per second squared, so does the other. Note "same magnitude" rather than "same direction" — with a pulley, one body accelerates downward while the other accelerates horizontally.

The string is LIGHT, meaning its mass is negligible. Therefore the tension is the SAME at both ends. A massive rope would need a net force of its own to accelerate, and the tension would differ from end to end; a light string passes the pull through unchanged.

The pulley is FRICTIONLESS and light. Therefore it only changes the DIRECTION of the tension, not its size. The rope tension pulling the crate towards the table edge equals the rope tension pulling the bucket upward.

One more fact protects you from the commonest error: tension always PULLS. A string can pull an object towards the pulley or towards the other body, but it can never push. Draw the tension arrow on each body pointing along the string, away from that body.

Finally, name the forces properly. The tension is an INTERNAL force of the two-body system, because it acts between members of the system. The applied force, friction and the weights are EXTERNAL. That distinction is exactly what makes the next subtopic work.

Take the questions for this section now, checking that you can justify each assumption rather than merely quote it.

## Subtopic: The Two-Step Method — Whole System, Then One Body

Step one: treat both bodies as ONE object of combined mass. Because tension acts forward on one body and backward on the other with equal magnitude, the two tensions cancel in this combined equation and vanish. What remains is F net external equals total mass times acceleration. Solve for a. This is why the system step is always first: it is the only equation in which the unknown tension does not appear.

Step two: return to ONE body — choose the simpler one, usually the body with the fewest forces on it — and apply F net equals m a to that body alone, using the acceleration you have just found. Tension is now the only unknown, and it falls out in one line.

Start with the cleanest case. Two crates stand on a frictionless factory floor: crate A of 8 kilograms, joined by a light string to crate B of 2 kilograms behind it. A horizontal force of 50 newtons pulls crate A forward.

Step one, the system. Formula: F net equals total mass times a. Substitution: 50 newtons equals 10 kilograms times a. Answer: a equals 5 metres per second squared, forward.

Step two, crate B alone. The only horizontal force on B is the tension. Formula: T equals m B times a. Substitution: 2 kilograms times 5 metres per second squared. Answer: T equals 10 newtons.

Check with the other body, which is how you catch errors: on crate A, 50 minus T equals m A times a, so 50 minus 10 equals 40 newtons, and 8 kilograms times 5 metres per second squared is also 40 newtons. Consistent.

Two disciplines. Never put tension into the system equation — writing "50 plus T equals 10 a" is the classic wrong start. And draw a separate free-body diagram for each body before you write anything, because that is where the marks for method live.

The questions on this section are with you now: whole system first, single body second, and tension nowhere in the first equation.

## Subtopic: Two Crates on a Rough Floor

Add friction and the method does not change; only the bookkeeping grows.

The same crates — 8 kilograms in front, 2 kilograms behind, joined by a light string, pulled by 50 newtons — now sit on a rough floor with a coefficient of kinetic friction of 0,2. Both crates experience friction, and each crate's friction depends on ITS OWN normal force.

Step one, the system. Total weight: 10 kilograms times 9,8, which is 98 newtons, so the total normal force is 98 newtons. Total friction: formula f k equals mu k times N, substitution 0,2 times 98, answer 19,6 newtons backward. Formula: F net equals F applied minus f total. Substitution: 50 minus 19,6. Answer: 30,4 newtons. Then a equals 30,4 divided by 10, which is 3,04 metres per second squared forward.

Step two, crate B alone — the 2 kilogram crate, which has only two horizontal forces. Its own normal force: 2 times 9,8, which is 19,6 newtons. Its own friction: 0,2 times 19,6, which is 3,92 newtons backward. Formula: T minus f B equals m B times a. Substitution: T minus 3,92 equals 2 times 3,04, which is 6,08. Answer: T equals 10,0 newtons.

Verify on crate A: its friction is 0,2 times 78,4, which is 15,68 newtons, so 50 minus 10 minus 15,68 gives 24,32 newtons, and 8 kilograms times 3,04 is 24,32 newtons. The books balance.

The trap here is subtle and expensive: using the TOTAL friction inside a single-body equation. Crate B feels only its own 3,92 newtons of friction, never the 19,6 newtons of the pair. Every quantity in a single-body equation must belong to that body alone.

Work this section's questions now, keeping each body's friction attached to that body and nowhere else.

## Subtopic: Over the Pulley — Table Edge and Incline

The arrangement changes; the method survives.

A 4 kilogram block rests on a rough table with a coefficient of kinetic friction of 0,25. A light string runs from it, horizontally, over a frictionless pulley at the table edge, and down to a 6 kilogram mass hanging freely. Released, the hanging mass falls and the block slides.

Picture the free-body diagrams. On the block: weight 39,2 newtons down, normal force 39,2 newtons up, tension T horizontally towards the pulley, friction backwards away from the pulley. On the hanging mass: weight 58,8 newtons down, tension T upward.

Step one, the system, taking the direction of motion as positive. The driving external force is the hanging weight, 6 times 9,8, which is 58,8 newtons. The opposing external force is friction on the block: 0,25 times 39,2, which is 9,8 newtons. Note that the block's weight and normal force are perpendicular to its motion and cancel, so they do not appear. Formula: F net equals total mass times a. Substitution: 58,8 minus 9,8 equals 10 kilograms times a, so 49 equals 10 a. Answer: a equals 4,9 metres per second squared.

Step two, the hanging mass alone, taking downward as positive. Formula: m g minus T equals m a. Substitution: 58,8 minus T equals 6 times 4,9, which is 29,4. Answer: T equals 29,4 newtons.

Read that result, because it is examined constantly. The tension is 29,4 newtons while the hanging weight is 58,8 newtons. Tension is LESS than the hanging weight — it must be, or the mass would not accelerate downward at all. Tension equals the hanging weight only when the acceleration is zero.

Now the incline version. A 6 kilogram block sits on a frictionless plane inclined at 30 degrees; a string from it runs up the slope, over a pulley at the top, and down to a 9 kilogram mass hanging vertically.

Step one. Driving force: the hanging weight, 9 times 9,8, which is 88,2 newtons. Opposing force: the component of the block's weight down the slope, m g sine theta, which is 6 times 9,8 times 0,5, giving 29,4 newtons. Formula: F net equals total mass times a. Substitution: 88,2 minus 29,4 equals 15 a, so 58,8 equals 15 a. Answer: a equals 3,92 metres per second squared.

Step two, the hanging mass. Substitution: 88,2 minus T equals 9 times 3,92, which is 35,28. Answer: T equals 52,92 newtons.

Three traps to name. One: assuming the tension equals the hanging weight — almost never true. Two: putting the block's full weight into the system equation on an incline instead of only the m g sine theta component. Three: sign confusion, cured by defining "the direction the system actually moves" as positive for every body, so that the falling mass's downward motion and the block's along-the-surface motion are both counted as positive.

The final questions of this part are with you now: system equation, single-body equation, and a stated sign convention that follows the motion.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same connected bodies from a Saturday morning at the shops, where two trolleys tied together behave exactly like the crates in the examination.

## Subtopic: Two Trolleys Tied Together

Picture two shopping trolleys nose to tail, joined by a short rope. You push the front one; the back one follows.

Three things are true, and they are the whole topic. First, they move together. Whatever speed the front one picks up, the back one picks up too, because the rope cannot stretch. So they share ONE acceleration, and you only ever have to find it once.

Second, the rope pulls equally at both ends. The rope is light, so it does not need any force to get itself moving; it simply passes your pull straight through. Whatever the rope tugs the back trolley with, the back trolley tugs the rope with, and that same size appears at the other end.

Third — and this one saves you every time — a rope can only PULL. It cannot push. So when you draw the force on each trolley, the rope's arrow points along the rope, away from the trolley, towards the other one.

Now notice something clever about that rope tension. It pulls the back trolley forward and drags the front trolley backward, with the same size. So if you think of the two trolleys as one single big trolley, the rope's pulls point in opposite directions and cancel out completely. They are inside the system, so they never affect the system as a whole. Only your push, and the friction from the floor, matter.

That is not a trick. That is the reason the method works, and it is worth saying out loud once: the rope only matters when you look at one trolley at a time.

Small win banked. Questions on this section are with you now — three rules, and the reason the rope disappears when you look at the pair together.

## Subtopic: Weigh the Whole Train First

Here is the recipe, and it is only two moves.

Move one: pretend the two objects are one object. Add their masses. Add up only the outside forces — your push, friction, gravity — and leave the rope out entirely, because it cancels. Divide the leftover push by the total mass and you have the acceleration. Everything is now known.

Move two: go back to ONE object, and choose the lazy one — the one with the fewest forces on it. Write down the forces on that one alone, including the rope's pull, set them equal to that object's mass times the acceleration you already found, and the rope tension is the only thing left to solve for.

Do the crates. Eight kilograms in front, two kilograms behind, tied together, pulled with 50 newtons, on a floor with a grip factor of 0,2.

Move one: together they are 10 kilograms, and together they press down with 98 newtons, so friction drags back with 0,2 times 98, which is 19,6 newtons. Leftover push: 50 minus 19,6, which is 30,4 newtons. Acceleration: 30,4 divided by 10, which is 3,04 metres per second squared.

Move two: look at the small crate only. Its own weight presses with 19,6 newtons, so its own friction is 0,2 times 19,6, which is 3,92 newtons. The rope pulls it forward with T. So T minus 3,92 equals 2 times 3,04, which is 6,08 — meaning T is 10 newtons.

And here is the mistake to avoid like a pothole: when you look at the small crate, use the SMALL crate's friction, not the friction of both. Its own numbers only.

Your questions for this section are coming to you now — whole train first, one wagon second.

## Subtopic: The Bucket Down the Well

Last picture, and it is the one that appears most in papers: something on a table, a string over a wheel at the edge, and something hanging.

A 4 kilogram block on the table, a 6 kilogram weight dangling, and a bit of friction on the table. What is doing the driving? The hanging weight, pulling down with 6 times 9,8, which is 58,8 newtons. What is fighting back? Friction on the block, which is 0,25 times the block's press of 39,2 newtons, giving 9,8 newtons. Nothing else matters — the block's own weight pushes straight into the table and the table pushes straight back, and those two cancel.

Move one: leftover is 58,8 minus 9,8, which is 49 newtons, shared over 10 kilograms of stuff, giving 4,9 metres per second squared.

Move two: look at the dangling weight only. Gravity pulls it down with 58,8; the string holds it up with T; and it is accelerating downward at 4,9. So 58,8 minus T equals 6 times 4,9, which is 29,4, meaning T is 29,4 newtons.

Now stop and enjoy that answer, because it is the thing examiners love to test. The string is holding with 29,4 newtons, but the hanging thing weighs 58,8 newtons. The string is holding with LESS than the full weight. It has to be — if the string held with the full weight, nothing would be left over and the mass would just hang there. The string only ever equals the full weight when nothing is accelerating.

Same story on a ramp: a 6 kilogram block on a smooth 30 degree slope tied over a pulley to a 9 kilogram hanging mass. The hanging mass drives with 88,2 newtons; the slope holds back with only half the block's weight, 29,4 newtons, because on a slope gravity's sliding pile is the weight times the sine of the angle. Leftover 58,8 newtons over 15 kilograms gives 3,92 metres per second squared, and the string works out at 52,92 newtons.

Two moves, one shared acceleration, and a string that always pulls and never pushes. The last questions of the lesson are yours now — start with the whole train every single time.
