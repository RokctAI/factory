# Part 1 — Expert

Newton's second law is simple arithmetic once the forces are known. The difficulty in Grade 11 is finding them, and two forces cause almost all the trouble: the normal force, which is not always equal to the weight, and friction, which comes in two varieties governed by two different coefficients. This session pins both down, then rotates the whole problem onto an inclined plane, where gravity has to be resolved into a part along the slope and a part into the slope. Master this and the rest of mechanics becomes bookkeeping.

## Subtopic: The Normal Force and the Free-Body Diagram

The NORMAL FORCE, symbol N, is the force a surface exerts on an object in contact with it, directed PERPENDICULAR to that surface. "Normal" is the mathematician's word for perpendicular; it has nothing to do with being ordinary.

Picture the free-body diagram for a crate on a level floor: the crate becomes a single dot, weight is an arrow straight down, and the normal force is an arrow straight up. On a level surface with nothing else pushing or pulling vertically, the crate is not accelerating vertically, so those two balance and N equals m g. That is the special case, not the rule.

Change one thing and the rule shows itself. Suppose a rope now pulls on the same crate at 30 degrees above the horizontal. The pull has an upward component, which helps hold the crate up, so the floor no longer needs to push as hard. The vertical equation becomes N plus F sine theta minus m g equals zero, so N equals m g minus F sine theta. For a 50 kilogram crate pulled with 100 newtons at 30 degrees: N equals 50 times 9,8 minus 100 times 0,5, which is 490 minus 50, giving N equals 440 newtons. Push the rope DOWNWARD at 30 degrees instead and the sign flips: N equals 490 plus 50, giving 540 newtons. Same crate, three different normal forces.

This matters because friction is calculated from N. Get the normal force wrong and every friction value afterwards is wrong, which is why examiners like to bury a slanted force in an otherwise routine question.

Two drawing rules the markers apply. Every arrow starts on the dot, carries an arrowhead and a label. And nothing appears on a free-body diagram except forces — no velocity arrows, no surfaces, no ropes drawn as objects.

The questions for this section are with you now: identify the normal force, and be certain about when it equals the weight and when it does not.

## Subtopic: Static and Kinetic Friction

Friction is the force a surface exerts on an object, parallel to the surface, opposing the motion or the TENDENCY towards motion. Grade 11 distinguishes two kinds.

STATIC friction, f s, acts while the object is stationary. It is self-adjusting: push a crate with 50 newtons and it pushes back with exactly 50 newtons; push with 120 and it pushes back with 120 — until it reaches its limit. That limit is the maximum static friction, and the formula is f s maximum equals mu s times N: the coefficient of static friction multiplied by the normal force.

KINETIC friction, f k, acts once the object is sliding, and it does NOT adjust. The formula is f k equals mu k times N: the coefficient of kinetic friction multiplied by the normal force. For any given pair of surfaces mu s is greater than mu k, which is why a heavy wardrobe is hard to start moving and easier to keep moving.

The coefficients depend only on the nature of the two surfaces. They have no unit, since they are a force divided by a force. And they do not depend on the contact area — a brick on its side and the same brick on its end experience the same friction.

Work the standard question. A 50 kilogram crate stands on a level floor at a Cape Town depot; mu s is 0,4 and mu k is 0,3. Normal force: N equals m g equals 50 times 9,8, which is 490 newtons. Maximum static friction: formula f s max equals mu s N, substitution 0,4 times 490, answer 196 newtons.

Now apply 150 newtons horizontally. Since 150 is less than 196, the crate does not move; static friction adjusts to exactly 150 newtons and the net force is zero. Writing "friction equals 196 newtons" here is wrong, and it is the most common error in the topic.

Apply 200 newtons instead. Since 200 exceeds 196, the crate breaks free and slides, and friction switches to kinetic: f k equals 0,3 times 490, which is 147 newtons. Net force equals 200 minus 147, which is 53 newtons. Acceleration equals 53 divided by 50, which is 1,06 metres per second squared in the direction of the applied force.

Take this section's questions now, being careful to test whether the object actually moves before deciding which friction formula applies.

## Subtopic: Resolving Weight on an Inclined Plane

Tilt the surface and the geometry changes, but the method is the one from vectors in two dimensions. The trick is to rotate the axes: instead of horizontal and vertical, use axes PARALLEL to the slope and PERPENDICULAR to the slope. Do that and the normal force and friction both lie neatly along an axis, and only the weight needs resolving.

Build the picture. A ramp rises at angle theta to the horizontal. A crate sits on it. The free-body diagram has three or four arrows from a single dot: the weight straight DOWN, vertically, regardless of the slope; the normal force perpendicular to the SLOPE surface, which is tilted away from vertical by the angle theta; friction parallel to the slope, opposing the sliding; and any applied force or tension along the slope.

The weight now splits into two. The component parallel to the slope, pulling the crate down the ramp, is F g parallel equals m g sine theta — the weight multiplied by the sine of the incline angle. The component perpendicular to the slope, pressing the crate into the surface, is F g perpendicular equals m g cosine theta — the weight multiplied by the cosine of the incline angle.

That result is worth understanding rather than memorising. The angle between the weight vector and the line perpendicular to the slope is equal to the incline angle itself, by simple geometry of similar right-angled triangles. So the perpendicular component sits adjacent to theta and takes cosine; the parallel component sits opposite theta and takes sine.

Two consequences follow immediately. First, since the crate does not accelerate into or off the surface, the perpendicular direction balances: N equals m g cosine theta. The normal force on an incline is ALWAYS less than the weight, and the steeper the slope the smaller it gets. Second, friction is therefore mu times m g cosine theta, not mu times m g.

The sign convention to declare before you start: choose "up the slope" as positive, or "down the slope" as positive, and say which. Mixing the two mid-question is how a correct method produces a negative acceleration in the wrong direction.

The questions on this section are in front of you now: rotate the axes, resolve the weight correctly, and state the normal force on a slope.

## Subtopic: Worked Incline Problems — Sliding, Held and Pushed

A 20 kilogram crate rests on a loading ramp inclined at 30 degrees at a Durban depot. The coefficient of static friction is 0,55; the coefficient of kinetic friction is 0,25.

First, the components. Weight: w equals m g equals 20 times 9,8, which is 196 newtons. Parallel component: F g parallel equals 196 times sine 30 degrees, which is 196 times 0,5, giving 98 newtons down the slope. Perpendicular component: F g perpendicular equals 196 times cosine 30 degrees, which is 196 times 0,866, giving 169,7 newtons. Therefore N equals 169,7 newtons — noticeably less than the 196 newton weight.

Will it slide on its own? Maximum static friction equals mu s times N, which is 0,55 times 169,7, giving 93,3 newtons. The slope pulls it down with 98 newtons; friction can resist only 93,3 newtons. Since 98 exceeds 93,3, the crate slides.

How fast does it accelerate? Once sliding, kinetic friction acts UP the slope, opposing the downward slide: f k equals 0,25 times 169,7, which is 42,4 newtons. Taking down the slope as positive: F net equals 98 minus 42,4, which is 55,6 newtons. Acceleration equals 55,6 divided by 20, which is 2,78 metres per second squared down the incline.

Now a worker drags the crate back UP the ramp at constant velocity. Constant velocity means zero net force. The crate now moves upward, so kinetic friction reverses and acts DOWN the slope, joining the weight component. Formula: T equals F g parallel plus f k. Substitution: 98 plus 42,4. Answer: T equals 140,4 newtons up the slope. Notice that friction changed direction, not size — its direction is set by the motion, its magnitude by mu k and N.

Four traps to name. One: writing N equals m g on an incline, which inflates every friction value. Two: swapping sine and cosine, which is guaranteed if you do not sketch the triangle. Three: assuming friction always points up the slope; it opposes the motion, so it points down the slope whenever the object moves up. Four: the sign convention, where a carelessly mixed positive direction turns a sensible answer into nonsense.

There is one elegant shortcut worth carrying. An object on the point of slipping satisfies m g sine theta equals mu s m g cosine theta, and the masses cancel, leaving tangent theta equals mu s. So a crate slips when the tangent of the slope angle exceeds the coefficient of static friction — here, tangent 30 degrees is 0,577, which exceeds 0,55, confirming that it slides, regardless of mass.

The final questions of this part are with you now: components, normal force, the slipping test, and friction pointed the correct way for the motion described.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same slopes and grip from a bakkie parked on a hill in Cape Town, where every one of these forces is something you can feel in your hands.

## Subtopic: Grip, and Why It Runs Out

Push a heavy wardrobe across a room. At first nothing happens — you push, it stays. Push harder, still nothing. Push harder again and suddenly it lurches, and then it slides much more easily than it was to start.

That whole story is friction, and it is telling you two things. First, while the wardrobe was standing still, friction was matching you push for push. You gave 50 newtons, it gave 50 back. You gave 120, it gave 120. Friction that adjusts itself like this is called STATIC friction, and it is why nothing moves until it is good and ready.

Second, that matching cannot go on forever. Every pair of surfaces has a limit of grip, and once you exceed it the object breaks loose. After that a different, WEAKER friction takes over — the sliding kind, called kinetic friction — which is why the wardrobe suddenly feels easier once it is moving.

Both kinds depend on the same two things: how rough the surfaces are, and how hard they are pressed together. Roughness gets a number called the coefficient, written as the Greek letter mu. Pressed-together-ness is the normal force, the push the floor gives back. Multiply the two and you have friction.

Real numbers. A 50 kilogram crate on a floor presses down with 490 newtons, so the floor pushes back with 490 newtons. If the grip coefficient is 0,4, the crate will not budge until you exceed 0,4 times 490, which is 196 newtons. Push with 150 and nothing happens, and friction is 150 — not 196. Push with 200 and it goes, with sliding friction now 0,3 times 490, which is 147 newtons. Leftover push: 200 minus 147, which is 53 newtons, on 50 kilograms, giving a gentle 1,06 metres per second squared.

Questions on this section are with you now — before you pick a friction formula, always ask first whether the thing is actually moving.

## Subtopic: Gravity Split into Down-the-Slope and Into-the-Slope

Now park that crate on a ramp, and everything you know about level ground quietly changes.

Gravity still pulls straight down — it does not care about slopes. But the crate can only do two things: slide along the ramp, or press into it. So gravity's single downward pull must be shared between those two jobs, and that is exactly the splitting-into-piles trick from vectors.

One pile pulls the crate DOWN THE SLOPE. That is the pile that makes things run away from you on a hill. The other pile presses the crate INTO THE RAMP. That is the pile the ramp has to push back against.

And here is the thing worth writing on your hand: on a slope, the ramp does NOT push back with the full weight. Some of gravity went into the sliding pile instead. So the pressing pile is smaller than the weight, which means the ramp's push is smaller, which means the grip is smaller too. Steeper hill, less grip — which is precisely why a steep gravel road in a Karoo pass is where you lose traction.

How big is each pile? The steeper the ramp, the more goes into sliding and the less into pressing. Sliding pile equals weight times the sine of the slope angle. Pressing pile equals weight times the cosine of the slope angle. And as always, do not memorise which is which — sketch the triangle and check which side leans against the angle.

Do the depot crate: 20 kilograms, so 196 newtons of weight, on a 30 degree ramp. Sliding pile: 196 times sine 30, which is 98 newtons. Pressing pile: 196 times cosine 30, which is 169,7 newtons — so the ramp pushes back with 169,7 newtons, not 196.

Your questions for this part are coming to you now — two piles, and remember the ramp's push is always the smaller number.

## Subtopic: Will It Slide? The Quick Test

Last move, and it is a straight competition between two numbers.

In one corner, the sliding pile — gravity trying to send the crate down the hill. In the other, the grip — the coefficient multiplied by the ramp's push. Whichever is bigger wins.

For our crate: sliding pile is 98 newtons; grip is 0,55 times 169,7, which is 93,3 newtons. Gravity wins by a nose, so the crate slides. Once it is sliding, the weaker sliding-friction takes over: 0,25 times 169,7, which is 42,4 newtons, pushing back up the hill. Leftover: 98 minus 42,4, which is 55,6 newtons, shared over 20 kilograms, giving 2,78 metres per second squared down the ramp.

Now flip it. A worker pulls the crate UP the ramp at a steady speed. Steady speed means everything balances. But watch friction: the crate is now moving UP, so friction turns around and drags DOWN the hill, joining forces with gravity's sliding pile. The worker must beat both: 98 plus 42,4, which is 140,4 newtons. Friction changed direction, not size — it always opposes the way the thing is going.

And a lovely shortcut to keep. If you only want to know whether something slips, you do not need the mass at all. Compare the tangent of the slope angle with the grip coefficient: bigger tangent means it slides. Tangent of 30 degrees is 0,577, and our grip was 0,55, so it slides — and it would slide whether the crate weighed 20 kilograms or 200.

So: on a slope, split gravity into two piles, remember the ramp pushes back with the smaller one, point friction against the motion, and then it is just Newton's second law again. The last questions of the lesson are yours now — sketch the ramp, sketch the triangle, and back yourself.
