**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! First one: the two formulas both give E, and learners keep grabbing the wrong one. Is there a foolproof way to know when it is E equals F over q and when it is k Q over r squared?

**Tutor:** There is, and it comes from asking one question about the charge in front of you: is this charge FEELING the field or MAKING it? E equals F over q belongs to the feeler. It is the definition of field strength: some test charge sits at the point, a force on it is known or wanted, and the field is force divided by the charge doing the feeling. The give-away words are "experiences a force" or "the force on the charge placed at". E equals k Q over r squared belongs to the maker. It computes the field a source charge creates at a distance r, and the give-away words are "the field at a point a certain distance from the charge". The two connect in one line: the force law F equals k Q q over r squared divided by the test charge q gives k Q over r squared, so the second formula is just the first with Coulomb's law substituted in. If a question gives you a source charge and a distance, use the maker's formula. If it gives you a force and the charge that felt it, use the feeler's. And if it gives you a field and asks for a force, multiply: F equals E q. Sort every charge in the problem into maker or feeler before you write anything, and the formula picks itself.

***

**Bianca:** Next: someone calculated a field at a point where the question said nothing was placed, and it felt like cheating. How can empty space have a field strength?

**Tutor:** Because the field is a property of the space, not of the visitor — and that is the entire conceptual point of this topic. A source charge modifies the space around it everywhere, permanently, whether anyone ever measures it or not. The field at your empty point P is a statement of readiness: IF a charge of one coulomb were placed here, it WOULD feel this many newtons in this direction. No charge needs to accept the invitation for the statement to be true. Compare it to a price tag on a shelf: the chicken costs 60 rand per kilogram whether or not anyone is buying, and the number describes the shop, not the customer. That is also why the test charge cancels out of every formula — when we divide the force by q, or watch q vanish from k Q q over r squared, nature is telling us the leftover number belongs to the location alone. In the examination this appears as a definition question: field strength is the force per unit positive charge AT A POINT, and the phrase at a point is doing real work — it anchors the number to the place, not to any particular charge.

***

**Bianca:** Here is the one about direction: a learner drew the field of a negative charge pointing outward, arguing that the electrons push things away. Where does the reasoning break?

**Tutor:** It breaks at the definition of direction, which is a convention with no room for negotiation: the field direction at a point is the direction of the force on a POSITIVE test charge placed there. Always positive — that is the agreed probe, the standard candle of electrostatics. Bring the positive probe near a negative source and the probe is ATTRACTED, pulled inward, so the field of a negative charge points inward, toward the charge, everywhere around it. The learner's mistake was imagining the source's electrons doing the probing, but the source never probes itself — its job is to create; the probe's job is to feel, and the probe is positive by decree. Once that is fixed, two bonus rules come free. First, a real positive charge placed anywhere simply moves along the field arrows. Second, a real NEGATIVE charge — an electron, say — moves against the arrows, because the force on it is opposite to the force the positive probe would feel. Examiners test exactly this with an electron between charged plates: the field points from the positive plate to the negative plate, and the electron accelerates the other way, toward the positive plate. Draw the field by the convention, then reverse it for negative passengers.

***

**Bianca:** Last one: in the two-charge problem, learners want to add the two source charges and do one calculation with the combined charge. Why is that not allowed?

**Tutor:** Because the two sources sit at different places, and in an inverse-square law WHERE you are matters as much as how much charge you are. The field at your point depends on each source's own distance, squared. Merge a 2 microcoulomb charge at 0,2 metres with a 3 microcoulomb charge at 0,2 metres on the other side, and you would be pretending 5 microcoulombs sits somewhere — but where? Any single location you choose gives the wrong pair of distances, and the directions go wrong too: at a midpoint between unlike charges the two contributions COOPERATE, both pointing from the positive toward the negative, which no single merged charge can reproduce. The only lawful procedure is superposition: one k Q over r squared calculation per source, using that source's own distance; one direction per contribution, away from positive and toward negative; then a vector addition of the arrows. In our worked example that gave 4,5 times 10 to the 5 plus 6,75 times 10 to the 5, cooperating, for 1,125 times 10 to the 6 newtons per coulomb toward the negative charge. The pattern to internalise: charges add only when they sit at the SAME point — like the touching spheres — while fields add always, but as vectors, at the point you care about.
