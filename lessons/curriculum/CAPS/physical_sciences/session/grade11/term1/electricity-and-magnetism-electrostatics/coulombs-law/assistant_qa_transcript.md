**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! The first is the sign question, and it decides more marks than any other: if I substitute the minus sign of a negative charge into Coulomb's law, I get a negative force. Is that wrong, and why does everyone tell me not to do it?

**Tutor:** It is not wrong in advanced physics, but at this level it is dangerous, and here is why. Coulomb's law as we use it gives the MAGNITUDE of the force — a size, which cannot be negative. A negative answer is not a smaller force or a leftward force until you have defined what negative means, and inside a single formula substitution you have not. Learners who carry the sign through then do one of two bad things: they report a force of negative 5,4 newtons, which loses the mark for a meaningless sign, or they add a second force to it algebraically and get the wrong resultant because the sign was never a direction to begin with. The safe procedure has two separate stages. Stage one: substitute magnitudes only, and get the size — 5,4 newtons. Stage two: look at the two signs and reason in words — unlike charges, therefore attraction, therefore the force on this charge points toward the other one. Then, if several forces must be combined, choose a positive direction and give each force its sign based on its ARROW, not on the charges that caused it. Size from the formula, direction from the physics, signs only when adding arrows. Keep those stages separate and this topic cannot hurt you.

**Bianca:** Next: a learner calculated the force of A on B correctly, then used the same force for B on A but made it smaller because B has less charge. Where is the thinking going wrong?

**Tutor:** It collides with Newton's third law, which does not switch off for electrostatics. The force A exerts on B and the force B exerts on A are an action-reaction pair: exactly equal in magnitude, opposite in direction, no matter how unequal the charges are. The formula itself already says so — it contains the PRODUCT Q one times Q two, and that product is identical whichever charge you call first. A charge of 6 microcoulombs and a charge of 1 microcoulomb pull on each other with precisely the same size of force. What differs is the EFFECT: if the small charge sits on a light sphere, the same force accelerates it more. The intuition that the bigger charge pushes harder is confusing the source of the field with the strength of the interaction — the interaction belongs to the pair, not to either member. In the exam, if asked to compare the two forces, the answer is always: equal in magnitude, opposite in direction, because they form a Newton's third law pair.

**Bianca:** This one comes from homework: in the touching-spheres question, someone averaged 6 and 2 to get 4 microcoulombs each, but the memo says 2. What did they miss?

**Tutor:** The sign. The spheres carried positive 6 microcoulombs and NEGATIVE 2 microcoulombs, and charge conservation adds charges algebraically, signs included. Positive 6 plus negative 2 is positive 4 — that is the total charge that exists. When identical spheres touch, that total spreads evenly, so each sphere ends with half of positive 4, which is positive 2 microcoulombs. The learner who got 4 added the magnitudes, 6 plus 2 equals 8, halved it, and effectively created charge out of nothing — 8 units where only 4 exist. Physically, what happens at contact is that electrons flow from the negative sphere to the positive one until both spheres sit at the same potential; some of the positive and negative charge cancels, and only the net surplus remains to be shared. Two checks protect you. First, the two final charges must add back to the original total, signs included — 2 plus 2 gives 4, correct. Second, after touching, identical spheres always carry identical charges of the SAME sign, so the force between them afterwards must be repulsive. If your after-touching force comes out attractive, the sharing step went wrong.

**Bianca:** Last one: in the right-angle problem, why do we not just add the 0,6 newtons and the 0,45 newtons to get 1,05? It works when the charges are in a line.

**Tutor:** Because plain addition is a straight-line privilege, and the right angle revokes it. Forces are vectors: they carry direction, and only forces along the SAME line may be added or subtracted as ordinary numbers. In the linear case both arrows lie on one axis, so arithmetic works. In the corner case one arrow points south and the other east — pulling in directions that are ninety degrees apart — and their combined effect is the diagonal of the rectangle they define, not the sum of their lengths. Walk it: six steps south then four and a half steps east does not put you ten and a half steps from where you started; it puts you seven and a half steps away along the diagonal, which is exactly the square root of 0,6 squared plus 0,45 squared scaled up. That is why Pythagoras gives 0,75 newtons, and the direction comes from the tangent: 0,6 divided by 0,45 gives the angle south of east, 53,1 degrees. The full answer states both — 0,75 newtons at 53,1 degrees south of east — because a resultant without a direction is half an answer. If you add magnitudes and get 1,05, the examiner knows immediately that the vector nature of force never entered your solution, and the question was testing exactly that.
