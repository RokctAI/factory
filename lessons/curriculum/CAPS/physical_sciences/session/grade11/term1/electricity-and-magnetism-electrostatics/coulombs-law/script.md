# Part 1 — Expert

Grade 10 established that charges attract and repel; Grade 11 demands the exact number of newtons. This session states Coulomb's law in examinable wording, works the substitution carefully through microcoulombs and centimetres, then builds the two problem types the paper always carries: three charges in a straight line, and three charges arranged at a right angle. The vector skills from earlier this term do the heavy lifting, because electrostatic forces add exactly like any other forces.

## Subtopic: Charge, the Coulomb, and the Statement of the Law

Charge is measured in coulombs, symbol capital C, and it comes in two kinds, positive and negative. Like charges repel; unlike charges attract. Charge is quantised: the smallest free charge is that of the electron, 1,6 times 10 to the minus 19 coulombs, so every charge on every object is a whole-number multiple of that. A sphere carrying negative 3,2 times 10 to the minus 6 coulombs has gained 3,2 times 10 to the minus 6 divided by 1,6 times 10 to the minus 19, which is 2 times 10 to the 13 electrons.

Coulomb's law, exactly: the magnitude of the electrostatic force between two point charges is directly proportional to the product of the magnitudes of the charges and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them. In symbols, F equals k Q one Q two divided by r squared, where the constant k is 9 times 10 to the 9 newton metre squared per coulomb squared, given on the data sheet. Notice the family resemblance: this is gravitation's twin, with charges in place of masses — the same product on top, the same square of distance underneath.

Work the standard substitution. A charge of positive 2 microcoulombs and a charge of negative 3 microcoulombs are held 10 centimetres apart. Convert first, always: 2 microcoulombs is 2 times 10 to the minus 6 coulombs, 3 microcoulombs is 3 times 10 to the minus 6 coulombs, and 10 centimetres is 0,1 metres. Formula: F equals k Q one Q two divided by r squared. Substitution: 9 times 10 to the 9, multiplied by 2 times 10 to the minus 6, multiplied by 3 times 10 to the minus 6, divided by 0,1 squared. Answer: 5,4 newtons, and since the charges are unlike, the force is attractive.

The crucial discipline: substitute MAGNITUDES only. The signs of the charges never enter the formula; they only tell you whether the force attracts or repels, and you state that direction in words at the end.

Attempt this section's questions now — conversions first, magnitudes only, direction stated in words.

## Subtopic: Three Charges in a Straight Line

When more than one charge acts, each pair is handled separately and the forces are added as vectors. On a straight line that means simple addition and subtraction, but only after each direction has been reasoned out.

The method: isolate the charge the question asks about. Calculate the magnitude of the force from each neighbour, one Coulomb's law calculation per neighbour, ignoring all other charges while you do it. Draw each force as an arrow on the isolated charge — attraction points toward the other charge, repulsion points away. Then choose a positive direction and add.

Work it. Three point charges sit on a straight line. Charge A, positive 2 microcoulombs, is on the left. Charge B, positive 1 microcoulomb, is 0,3 metres to the right of A. Charge C, negative 3 microcoulombs, is 0,2 metres to the right of B. Find the net force on B.

Force of A on B: both positive, so repulsion, pushing B to the right, away from A. Magnitude: 9 times 10 to the 9, multiplied by 2 times 10 to the minus 6, multiplied by 1 times 10 to the minus 6, divided by 0,3 squared, which is 0,018 divided by 0,09, giving 0,2 newtons.

Force of C on B: unlike charges, so attraction, pulling B to the right, toward C. Magnitude: 9 times 10 to the 9, multiplied by 1 times 10 to the minus 6, multiplied by 3 times 10 to the minus 6, divided by 0,2 squared, which is 0,027 divided by 0,04, giving 0,675 newtons.

Both forces point right, so take right as positive and add: 0,2 plus 0,675 equals 0,875 newtons to the right. Had they pointed opposite ways, you would subtract — which is why the arrows must be drawn before any adding happens.

The classic error is using the distance from A to C somewhere, or forgetting that B feels each neighbour at ITS OWN separation. Each pair keeps its own r.

This section's questions are with you now: two calculations, two arrows, one sum.

## Subtopic: Charges at Right Angles — Coulomb's Law in Two Dimensions

The syllabus places three charges at the corners of a right angle, and the resultant comes from Pythagoras, exactly as in the vectors topic.

Work the standard arrangement. Charge Q one, positive 2 microcoulombs, sits at the corner. Charge Q two, positive 3 microcoulombs, sits 0,3 metres due north of it. Charge Q three, negative 4 microcoulombs, sits 0,4 metres due east of it. Find the net force on Q one.

Force from Q two: both positive, repulsion, so Q one is pushed directly SOUTH, away from Q two. Magnitude: 9 times 10 to the 9, multiplied by 2 times 10 to the minus 6, multiplied by 3 times 10 to the minus 6, divided by 0,3 squared, which is 0,054 divided by 0,09, giving 0,6 newtons.

Force from Q three: unlike charges, attraction, so Q one is pulled directly EAST, toward Q three. Magnitude: 9 times 10 to the 9, multiplied by 2 times 10 to the minus 6, multiplied by 4 times 10 to the minus 6, divided by 0,4 squared, which is 0,072 divided by 0,16, giving 0,45 newtons.

The two forces are perpendicular — one south, one east — so the resultant comes from Pythagoras. Magnitude: the square root of 0,6 squared plus 0,45 squared, which is the square root of 0,36 plus 0,2025, the square root of 0,5625, giving 0,75 newtons. Direction: the tangent of the angle south of east is 0,6 divided by 0,45, which is 1,33, so the angle is 53,1 degrees. The net force on Q one is 0,75 newtons at 53,1 degrees south of east.

Notice the pattern the examiners chose: 0,45 and 0,6 and 0,75 is the 3-4-5 triangle wearing a disguise. The numbers are often kind in this question — the method must simply be clean: one Coulomb calculation per neighbour, directions from attraction or repulsion, then Pythagoras and inverse tangent.

Work through this section's questions now, drawing the right-angle sketch before touching the calculator.

## Subtopic: Touching Spheres and the Exam Traps

Identical conducting spheres share charge equally when they touch. Add the two charges, signs included, and divide by two; each sphere carries that average afterwards.

Work it. Sphere P carries positive 6 microcoulombs and sphere T carries negative 2 microcoulombs. They are brought into contact and returned to their original positions, 0,1 metres apart. Total charge: positive 6 plus negative 2 equals positive 4 microcoulombs. Each sphere afterwards: positive 2 microcoulombs. The force after touching: 9 times 10 to the 9, multiplied by 2 times 10 to the minus 6, multiplied by 2 times 10 to the minus 6, divided by 0,1 squared, which is 0,036 divided by 0,01, giving 3,6 newtons, now REPULSIVE, since both spheres are positive. Before touching, the same separation gave 9 times 10 to the 9, multiplied by 6 times 10 to the minus 6, multiplied by 2 times 10 to the minus 6, divided by 0,01, which is 10,8 newtons attractive. Touching changed both the size and the nature of the force — a favourite full-question storyline.

Now the traps, collected. Trap one: units. Microcoulombs must become times 10 to the minus 6 coulombs, nanocoulombs times 10 to the minus 9, and centimetres or millimetres must become metres BEFORE squaring. Trap two: signs in the formula. Substituting negative charges produces a negative force that learners then misread; keep magnitudes in the formula and reason the direction separately. Trap three: the distance is between CENTRES of the spheres. Trap four: in ratio questions, the inverse square — halving the distance quadruples the force, and doubling both charges also quadruples it, exactly the gravitation logic. Trap five: after touching, spheres share charge only if they are stated to be IDENTICAL.

The final questions of this part are in front of you now — hunt the traps deliberately.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same law with the exponents resting, told through school jerseys, winter shocks and a tug-of-war.

## Subtopic: The Crackle in Your Jersey

Pull a jersey over your head on a dry Highveld winter morning and it crackles. Rub a balloon on your hair and it sticks to the wall. Walk across a carpet, touch a doorknob, get bitten. All of that is charge — and charge is nothing exotic. Everything is built of atoms; atoms carry positive protons and negative electrons in equal numbers, so most things are neutral. Rubbing scrapes electrons from one surface onto another. The jersey that GAINS electrons goes negative; the hair that LOSES them goes positive. Nothing is created — charge only moves house.

Once things are charged, one rule runs the show: opposites attract, likes repel. The balloon sticks to the wall by attraction; the strands of your rubbed hair lift away from each other by repulsion, each strand fleeing its equally-charged neighbours.

Two useful sizes to carry. The electron's charge is unimaginably small — 1,6 with eighteen zeros of smallness in front of it — which is why real objects hold charges of MICROcoulombs, millionths of a coulomb, made of trillions of electrons. And a full coulomb of charge sitting next to another full coulomb would produce a force of billions of newtons — a coulomb is a huge amount of charge, which is why nature deals in millionths.

Your questions on this section are with you now — remember, only the electrons ever move.

## Subtopic: Gravity's Louder Twin

You already know a law exactly like Coulomb's — you learned it two weeks ago. Gravitation: multiply the two masses, divide by the distance squared. Coulomb: multiply the two charges, divide by the distance squared. Same skeleton, different clothing. Everything you practised there transfers here.

Double one charge, double the force. Double both charges, four times the force. Double the DISTANCE, and the force drops to a quarter — distance gets squared, always, in both laws. If a ratio question worked in gravitation, the identical reasoning works here.

Two differences matter, though. First, gravity only ever pulls; charge can pull OR push, depending on the signs. Second, the electric constant is huge where gravity's was tiny — that is why a balloon rubbed for five seconds can hold a piece of paper against the gravitational pull of the ENTIRE EARTH. The electric force between everyday charged objects easily beats the gravity between everyday masses.

And keep the two names straight when calculating: charges go into Coulomb's law as plain positive numbers — millionths of a coulomb, converted properly — and the plus or minus signs stay outside, deciding only one thing: whether the force is a pull or a push. Numbers give the size; signs give the direction. Separate jobs.

## Subtopic: The Tug-of-War on the Middle Charge

The exam's favourite picture: three charges in a row, and you must find what the middle one feels. Treat it as a tug-of-war.

The middle charge feels two separate forces, one from each neighbour, and each neighbour acts as if the other did not exist. So ask two simple questions, one neighbour at a time. Is this neighbour pulling my charge toward it, or pushing it away? Opposite signs pull; same signs push. Draw a little arrow for the answer. Then do the second neighbour: another arrow.

Now read your two arrows. Same direction? The forces gang up — add them. Opposite directions? They fight — subtract, and the bigger one wins. That is the entire strategy, and it is decided BEFORE any calculator work. The calculation only fills in how strong each arrow is, using multiply-the-charges-divide-by-distance-squared once per neighbour, each neighbour at its own distance.

One more picture for the corner case. If one neighbour is north of your charge and the other is east, the two arrows are at right angles — neither helping nor fighting, just sideways to each other. Then the combined pull is the diagonal of the rectangle they make, found with Pythagoras, the same square-add-root move you used for vectors. The 3-4-5 triangle shows up here constantly: arrows of 0,45 and 0,6 newtons combine into 0,75.

Arrows first, always. A learner who calculates before drawing adds forces that should subtract, and the answer dies with the first line. Arrows, then sizes, then combine.

The last questions of the lesson are yours now — draw the tug-of-war before you touch a single number.
