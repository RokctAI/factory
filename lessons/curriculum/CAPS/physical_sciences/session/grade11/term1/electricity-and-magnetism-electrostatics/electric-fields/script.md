# Part 1 — Expert

Coulomb's law gives the force between two charges that are both present. The electric field answers a stranger question: what does a single charge do to the empty space around it? This session defines the field precisely, draws the standard field patterns the paper asks for, and works the two calculations of the topic — E equals F over q at a point where a force is measured, and E equals k Q over r squared at a point near a known charge. The lesson ends by superimposing fields from more than one charge, which is where the vector discipline earns its keep again.

## Subtopic: The Field Concept and the Rules of Field Lines

The definition, examinable: an electric field is a region of space in which an electric charge experiences a force. Every charge fills the space around itself with such a region, whether or not any other charge is present to feel it. The field is the middleman of electrostatics — charge A does not reach across and touch charge B; charge A creates a field everywhere, and charge B feels a force from the field at its own location.

The DIRECTION of the field at a point is defined as the direction of the force that a POSITIVE test charge would experience there. Learn that phrase, because every arrow you will ever draw follows from it: fields point away from positive charges, because a positive test charge is pushed away, and they point toward negative charges, because a positive test charge is pulled in.

Field lines make the invisible drawable, and they obey four rules worth theory marks. They start on positive charge and end on negative charge. They never cross — a point cannot have two directions of force. The arrow on the line shows the direction a positive test charge would move. And the closer together the lines, the stronger the field, so the pattern always crowds near the charge and spreads with distance.

The syllabus patterns to master: a single positive point charge, lines radiating straight outward like a sea urchin; a single negative point charge, the same picture with arrows reversed, pointing inward; two unlike charges, lines leaving the positive and curving around to enter the negative; two like positive charges, lines bending away from each other with a dead spot between them where the field is zero; and a charged sphere, whose OUTSIDE field looks exactly like a point charge sitting at the centre.

## Subtopic: Field Strength from Force — E Equals F Over q

The electric field strength at a point is defined as the force per unit positive charge placed at that point. In symbols, E equals F divided by q — the force on the test charge, divided by the size of the test charge. The unit is newtons per coulomb.

The definition makes the field a property of the LOCATION, not of the test charge. Double the test charge and the force on it doubles too, so the ratio F over q comes out unchanged. The field was there before the test charge arrived and remains after it leaves.

Work it forward. A small charge of positive 2 times 10 to the minus 6 coulombs placed at point P experiences a force of 0,008 newtons east. Formula: E equals F divided by q. Substitution: 0,008 divided by 2 times 10 to the minus 6. Answer: 4 000 newtons per coulomb, east — the direction of the force on the positive test charge is the direction of the field.

Work it backward. The same field of 4 000 newtons per coulomb now acts on an electron, charge 1,6 times 10 to the minus 19 coulombs. Formula: F equals E multiplied by q. Substitution: 4 000 multiplied by 1,6 times 10 to the minus 19. Answer: 6,4 times 10 to the minus 16 newtons — and because the electron is NEGATIVE, the force on it points WEST, against the field. That reversal for negative charges is a favourite one-mark sting.

The classic error is dividing by the source charge instead of the test charge. In E equals F over q, the q is always the charge SITTING IN the field and feeling the force, never the charge that created the field. The creator gets its own formula, and it is next.

This section's questions are with you now — watch which charge is doing the feeling and which is doing the creating.

## Subtopic: Field of a Point Charge — E Equals k Q Over r Squared

To find the field a known charge creates, put Coulomb's law and the definition together. The force on a test charge q at distance r from a source charge Q is k Q q over r squared. Divide by q, as the definition instructs, and the test charge cancels: E equals k Q divided by r squared — the field of the source alone, with the constant k equal to 9 times 10 to the 9 as before.

The cancellation repeats the lesson of gravitation: the field at a point depends only on the source charge and the distance, never on whatever visits the point. And the inverse square is back — double the distance from the source and the field drops to a quarter; triple it and the field drops to a ninth.

Work it. Find the electric field 0,3 metres from a point charge of positive 5 microcoulombs. Formula: E equals k Q divided by r squared. Substitution: 9 times 10 to the 9, multiplied by 5 times 10 to the minus 6, divided by 0,3 squared. That is 45 000 divided by 0,09. Answer: 5 times 10 to the 5 newtons per coulomb, directed away from the charge, because the source is positive.

Two disciplines carry the marks. Distances convert to metres before squaring, exactly as in Coulomb's law. And the answer needs a direction: away from a positive source, toward a negative one. A field without a direction is half an answer.

Note the division of labour between the two formulas, because choosing the wrong one is the common failure. E equals F over q is for a point where a FORCE on a test charge is known or wanted. E equals k Q over r squared is for a point at a known DISTANCE from a known SOURCE. Read the question, decide which two quantities you have, and the formula chooses itself.

Work through this section's questions now, stating the direction in every answer.

## Subtopic: Fields from More Than One Charge

Fields superimpose: the net field at a point is the vector sum of the fields created there by every charge present. The syllabus keeps the charges in a straight line, so the vector work is one-dimensional — but the directions must still be reasoned one source at a time, exactly like the tug-of-war of forces.

The method mirrors the force method. Mark the point of interest, which is usually empty space — no charge needs to sit there. For each source charge, calculate the field magnitude at the point with k Q over r squared, using that source's own distance. Draw the direction of each contribution: away from positive sources, toward negative sources. Then add the arrows.

Work it. Points A and B are 0,4 metres apart. A charge of positive 2 microcoulombs sits at A, and a charge of negative 3 microcoulombs sits at B. Find the net field at point P, exactly midway between them, 0,2 metres from each.

Contribution from A: 9 times 10 to the 9, multiplied by 2 times 10 to the minus 6, divided by 0,2 squared, which is 18 000 divided by 0,04, giving 4,5 times 10 to the 5 newtons per coulomb. Direction: away from A, since A is positive — so from A toward B.

Contribution from B: 9 times 10 to the 9, multiplied by 3 times 10 to the minus 6, divided by 0,2 squared, which is 27 000 divided by 0,04, giving 6,75 times 10 to the 5 newtons per coulomb. Direction: TOWARD B, since B is negative — which at point P is again the direction from A toward B.

Both arrows point the same way, so add: 4,5 plus 6,75 gives 11,25, so the net field is 1,125 times 10 to the 6 newtons per coulomb, directed from A toward B. Between unlike charges the contributions always cooperate like this; between like charges they fight, and somewhere between two like charges the field passes through zero.

The trap: the signs of the sources never enter the magnitudes — they set the arrow directions only, and the addition happens on the arrows. Substituting a negative Q and adding algebraically doubles the ways to go wrong.

The final questions of this part are in front of you now — one contribution per source, arrows before addition, direction in the answer.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same idea without the formalism, starting with a question that sounds like philosophy: how does a charge push something it never touches?

## Subtopic: The Invisible Cushion Around Every Charge

Hold two like charges near each other and something eerie happens: a push with nothing visible doing the pushing. No string, no contact, just refusal. The field is physics' answer to how that works.

Think of a charge as never travelling alone. Every charge carries an invisible cushion of influence, filling the space around it in every direction — thick and intense close up, thinning out with distance, but never quite ending. That cushion is the electric field. When a second charge wanders in, it does not interact with the far-away first charge directly; it feels the cushion RIGHT WHERE IT IS STANDING, and gets pushed or pulled accordingly.

To map a cushion, physicists use a simple probe: a small positive test charge, carried from point to point like a candle in a dark cave. Wherever the probe feels a shove, you draw an arrow showing which way the shove points. Connect the arrows and you get field lines — a wind map of the invisible.

Reading the map takes three facts. Arrows point AWAY from positive charges, because the positive probe is chased off. Arrows point TOWARD negative charges, because the probe is welcomed in. And where the lines bunch tightly, the field is strong; where they spread thin, it is weak. That is why every pattern looks crowded at the charge and sparse far away — the cushion fades with distance.

One picture to keep: a single positive charge looks like a sea urchin, spikes radiating outward. A negative charge is the same urchin breathing in.

Your questions on this section are with you now — read every arrow as the shove on a small positive probe.

## Subtopic: How Hard Is the Shove at This Spot?

A field map shows direction. The next question is strength: standing at one particular spot, how hard is the shove?

Here is the trouble: the shove depends on what you send in. A big test charge feels a big force, a small one feels a small force — at the very same spot. So quoting the force tells you about the probe, not the place. The fix is to quote the force PER COULOMB. Divide the force by the charge that felt it, and the probe drops out of the story. What remains — so many newtons for every coulomb — belongs to the spot itself. That number is the electric field strength.

It is exactly how you compare prices. A till slip saying 120 rand tells you nothing until you know how many kilograms of chicken it bought; rand PER kilogram lets you compare shops. Newtons per coulomb lets you compare places in a field. A spot rated 4 000 newtons per coulomb will deliver 0,008 newtons to a two-millionths-of-a-coulomb visitor, and a different force to a different visitor, but the RATE — 4 000 per coulomb — is fixed until the sources move.

And if you know the charge that built the field, there is a second route to the same number: the source's charge divided by the distance squared, scaled by the big constant. Same inverse square as always — walk twice as far from the source and the field drops to a quarter.

One warning before the questions: a negative visitor gets shoved BACKWARDS, against the arrows. The map is drawn for positive probes; electrons read it in reverse.

The questions for this section are coming to you now — think rand per kilogram, newtons per coulomb.

## Subtopic: When Two Charges Both Have a Say

Real questions rarely have one charge. Put two charges in the room and every point in space hears from both of them. What does a spot feel then? Both influences at once, added like arrows.

Stand at a point between a positive charge on the left and a negative charge on the right. The positive one blows you away from it — pushing you rightward. The negative one sucks you toward it — pulling you rightward too. Both votes say right, so the votes add, and the field between unlike charges is strong. That is why the field pattern between them is a thick bundle of lines flowing from the positive straight into the negative.

Now stand between TWO positive charges. The left one pushes you right; the right one pushes you left. The votes fight. Close to the left charge, its vote wins and the field points right; close to the right charge, that one wins and the field points left; and at one special spot in between the votes cancel exactly and the field is ZERO. Halfway, if the charges are equal — shifted toward the weaker charge if they are not. That dead spot is a favourite exam question, and you can now see it without any algebra.

The bookkeeping, spelled once: work out each charge's contribution at your spot separately, each using its own distance, draw the two arrows, then combine — add when they agree, subtract when they disagree. It is the tug-of-war from Coulomb's law again, except the rope is pulling on empty space, at a point where nothing needs to sit.

That is the electric field: every charge broadcasting into the space around it, every point in space adding up what it receives.

The last questions of the lesson are yours now — one arrow per charge, then let the arrows vote.
