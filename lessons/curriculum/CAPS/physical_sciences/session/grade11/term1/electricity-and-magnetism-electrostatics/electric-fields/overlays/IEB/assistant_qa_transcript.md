**Bianca:** Question time from the class! First one: if the field is invisible and only shows itself when a charge feels a force, how do we know it is really there? Maybe the two charges just push each other directly.

**Tutor:** For everything in this course, the two descriptions give identical numbers, so you might call it taste — but physics has good reasons for taking the field seriously as a real thing. The deepest one is travel time. If you wiggle a charge here, a charge across the room does not respond instantly; the news of the wiggle ripples outward at the speed of light, and the distant charge only reacts when the ripple arrives. Something must carry that news through the empty space in between, and that something is the field. Light itself is exactly such a ripple — an electromagnetic wave is the field's own wobble, travelling with energy on board. Direct push-at-a-distance cannot explain a delay, and it cannot explain how energy sits in transit between the charges. For your current work, though, hold the practical version: the field is the middleman. A charge dresses all of space with a number and a direction at every point; a visitor consults only the dressing at its own location. That picture is what lets you compute a field at an empty point — something "direct pushing" cannot even ask about — and empty-point questions are precisely what this topic examines.

**Bianca:** Next: the two formulas confuse everyone. E equals F over q has a small q, E equals k Q over r squared has a big Q. How do we know which charge is which in a wordy question?

**Tutor:** Assign the two roles before touching either formula: every field question has a CREATOR and a FEELER, and mixing them up is the topic's classic own-goal. The big Q is the creator — the source that builds the field around itself. The small q is the feeler — the visitor sitting in the field, experiencing the force. Now interrogate the question's data. Are you given, or asked for, a FORCE on some charge at a point? Then that charge is the feeler, and E equals F over q is your tool — and notice it never mentions the creator at all, because the field strength already encodes everything the creator did. Are you instead given a source charge and a distance from it? Then you are being asked what the creator built, and E equals k Q over r squared is the tool — and notice IT never mentions any visitor, because the visitor cancelled out of the derivation. The wording gives you clean signals: "experiences a force of" flags a feeler; "the field at a distance from a charge of" flags a creator. And the two tools chain together in the standard two-step question: build the field with k Q over r squared, then charge a visitor for standing in it with F equals E q. Creator first, feeler second — label them in the margin before you compute.

**Bianca:** A classmate is stuck on the dead spot. Between two positive charges the field is zero somewhere — but if one charge is stronger, why does the zero point move TOWARD the weaker charge? She expected the stronger one to win the middle.

**Tutor:** The stronger one DOES win the middle — that is exactly why the zero cannot live there. Think of the two charges as two loudspeakers facing each other, one loud, one soft, and you are hunting the spot where the two volumes match. Stand at the midpoint and the loud speaker drowns out the soft one: no balance, and the net field points away from the strong charge. To find equality, you must handicap the loud one — walk AWAY from it, letting the inverse square drain its voice — which is the same as walking TOWARD the soft one. The balance point settles where closeness to the weak charge exactly compensates for its weakness: near enough to the weakling that its small Q over a small r squared matches the giant's big Q over a big r squared. If the strong charge is four times the weak one, the distances must satisfy r strong squared equal to four r weak squared — the zero sits twice as far from the strong charge as from the weak one. And a warning for the twin question: between UNLIKE charges there is no dead spot at all — both contributions point the same way everywhere between them, so they can never cancel there; the zero for unlike charges hides outside the pair, beyond the weaker charge. Sketch the two arrows first and the geometry announces itself.

**Bianca:** Last one for today: why does an electron in a field get pushed against the arrows? It feels like the map is lying to half the particles in the universe.

**Tutor:** The map is not lying — it is honest about its own convention, printed in the definition: field direction is the force direction on a POSITIVE test charge. Every arrow is a promise made to positive visitors only. The physics underneath is symmetric: a field pointing north means positive charges are shoved north and negative charges are shoved south, with exactly the same magnitude of force for the same magnitude of charge, F equals E times q either way. We simply had to pick one sign as the reference to draw a single map instead of two, and history picked positive. So teach yourself the one-line translation: electrons read the map mirror-reversed. Field arrows pointing away from a positive plate mean an electron accelerates TOWARD that plate — which, once you think about it, is just attraction wearing map language. In calculations, the safe routine is the one from the lesson: compute the magnitude with positive numbers, then set the direction in words by asking whether the visitor's sign follows or opposes the arrows. Our worked example is the template — a 5 000 newton-per-coulomb field pointing north delivers 8 times 10 to the minus 16 newtons SOUTH to an electron. Write the reversal sentence explicitly; that single word "south" is where the mark lives, and it is the most commonly dropped word in the topic.
