**Bianca:** Time for the questions everyone brings to this topic. Number one, the sign that refuses to make sense: the beaker is warm, energy is obviously pouring out — and delta H is NEGATIVE? Explain it so it finally sticks.

**Tutor:** The confusion comes from reading someone else's bank statement. Delta H belongs to the chemicals — the system — never to the beaker, the bench or your hand. In an exothermic reaction, energy leaves the chemicals and arrives in the surroundings. Your hand is part of the surroundings, so your hand registers a gain; but the statement you are asked to read is the chemicals', and their account went DOWN. Formally, delta H equals the energy of the products minus the energy of the reactants, and exothermic products sit lower than their reactants, so the subtraction must come out negative. Build the habit of asking a single question before writing any sign: did the chemicals finish with more stored energy or with less? Warm beaker means the chemicals paid out — less — so negative. Cold beaker means the chemicals absorbed — more — so positive. Ask it every time for a week and the backwards feeling never returns.

***

**Bianca:** Question two: exothermic reactions are downhill — they end up releasing energy. So why doesn't the gas in the pipes just burn on its own? Why do downhill reactions wait?

**Tutor:** Because the first stretch of every reaction is uphill, no matter where it finishes. New bonds cannot pay out until the old bonds have been broken open, and that breaking bill is presented in advance. The advance charge is the activation energy — picture it as a ridge between two valleys, and note that the destination valley being far below you does not flatten the ridge in between. Propane and oxygen molecules collide endlessly in and around a stove, but at everyday temperatures essentially none of those collisions carries enough energy to cover the admission, so the mixture waits, inert, indefinitely. The igniter's spark changes the arithmetic for a few molecules: it lifts them over the ridge, their reaction refunds far more than the spark spent, and the refund lifts the next molecules over. From then on the flame pays its own admission fees, which is why one spark is enough. Keep the two quantities in separate pockets: delta H measures whether the journey profits; activation energy measures the cost of beginning it. A reaction can be hugely profitable and nearly impossible to start — every unlit stove is a demonstration.

**Bianca:** Question three is the graph-reading one. People measure delta H up to the peak, or take the activation energy from the wrong side. Give us the routine that never fails.

**Tutor:** Three levels, read in a fixed order, every single time. Step one: identify the two horizontal levels — reactants on the left, products on the right. Delta H is the vertical gap between those two levels and involves the summit not at all: products lower means negative, products higher means positive. Step two: the forward activation energy runs from the REACTANTS' level up to the summit — always measured from where the journey begins. Step three: for the reverse reaction, stand on the products' level and measure up to the same summit; the reverse delta H is the forward one with its sign flipped. Rehearse it on the lesson's numbers — reactants 80, summit 170, products 30. Forward climb: 90. Delta H: minus 50. Reverse climb: 140, reverse delta H plus 50. Now the built-in self-check: the two activation energies must differ by exactly the magnitude of delta H — 140 minus 90 is 50, so the readings agree. If yours don't, one reading is wrong and you have caught it before it cost anything. Final habit: write "activated complex" beside the summit, because that label is frequently a mark all on its own.

***

**Bianca:** Last one, the catalyst paradox: if a catalyst makes the reaction easier, surely it should squeeze out more energy too? Why does delta H refuse to budge?

**Tutor:** Because the energy released depends on where you start and where you end — never on the road taken between them. A catalyst opens an alternative route over a lower ridge; in the lesson's picture, a tunnel partway up the mountain. Cheaper admission means a much larger fraction of collisions succeeds each second, so the rate climbs steeply. But the tunnel does not lower the reactants' valley or raise the products' valley by a single metre, and delta H is nothing but the difference between those two floors. Think of driving between two towns: your change in altitude is identical whether you crawl over the pass or cruise through the tunnel — the route changes the effort, never the altitudes. So a catalysed exothermic reaction delivers exactly the same total energy, only sooner, and the catalyst walks away chemically unchanged, ready to work again. On the drawing, show one dashed curve with a lower summit nested inside the solid one, meeting it at both ends. And the sentence to write when asked: a catalyst provides an alternative pathway of lower activation energy, increasing the reaction rate, while delta H remains unchanged.
