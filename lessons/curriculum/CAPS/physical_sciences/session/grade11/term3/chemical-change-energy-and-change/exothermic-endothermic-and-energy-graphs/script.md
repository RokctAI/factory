# Part 1 — Expert

Every chemical reaction is an energy transaction. Bonds in the reactants must be broken, which costs energy, and new bonds in the products are formed, which pays energy out. This session builds the balance sheet of that transaction: the heat of reaction delta H and its sign, the division of all reactions into exothermic and endothermic, the activation energy hill that even downhill reactions must climb, and the potential energy graph that puts the entire story into one picture — the picture this topic's exam questions are drawn on.

## Subtopic: Bond Breaking, Bond Making and the Heat of Reaction

Two rules anchor everything. Breaking a chemical bond always ABSORBS energy — a bond is an attraction, and pulling attracted particles apart costs work. Forming a chemical bond always RELEASES energy — particles falling into an attraction give energy up, exactly as a dropped ball gives up height. No exceptions, in either direction.

A reaction does both at once: old bonds break in the reactants, new bonds form in the products. The heat of reaction, delta H, is the net result of the transaction — formally, the difference between the potential energy of the products and the potential energy of the reactants: delta H equals E products minus E reactants. It is measured in kilojoules, or kilojoules per mole of reaction as written.

If the energy released by forming the new bonds EXCEEDS the energy absorbed breaking the old ones, the surplus leaves the system — usually as heat — and the products end up at LOWER potential energy than the reactants. Delta H is then negative. If the breaking costs more than the forming repays, the reaction must import energy from the surroundings, the products sit at HIGHER potential energy, and delta H is positive.

Note the accounting direction, because it decides the sign. Delta H describes the SYSTEM, the chemicals themselves. When the chemicals lose energy, the surroundings gain it — the test tube warms — and delta H is negative even though the room got hotter. Learners who attach the sign to the surroundings instead of the system invert every answer in the topic. Anchor it now: delta H negative means energy left the chemicals.

The questions for this section are with you now: the two bond rules, the definition and unit of delta H, and the sign convention anchored to the system.

## Subtopic: Exothermic and Endothermic Reactions

An EXOTHERMIC reaction releases energy to the surroundings: delta H is negative, the products sit below the reactants in potential energy, and the surroundings warm up. The syllabus examples to know by name: combustion, such as methane burning — CH4 plus 2O2 gives CO2 plus 2H2O, methane plus oxygen gives carbon dioxide and water; respiration, the controlled combustion of glucose that powers your body; and the neutralisation of an acid by a base, which is why a titration flask grows warm at the endpoint.

An ENDOTHERMIC reaction absorbs energy from the surroundings: delta H is positive, the products sit above the reactants, and the surroundings COOL. The two examples every paper reaches for: photosynthesis — 6CO2 plus 6H2O gives C6H12O6 plus 6O2, six carbon dioxide plus six water gives one glucose plus six oxygen, with the energy imported as sunlight — and thermal decomposition, such as calcium carbonate splitting into calcium oxide and carbon dioxide only while heat is continuously supplied.

The everyday diagnostics run in both directions. Flask gets hot: the reaction exported energy, exothermic, delta H negative. Flask gets cold: the reaction imported energy, endothermic, delta H positive. An instant cold pack is an endothermic dissolution put to work; a hand warmer and a braai fire are exothermic chemistry sold in packets.

One subtlety earns top marks. Endothermic does not mean "will not happen without a flame". It means the products store more energy than the reactants did, so the energy must come from somewhere — sunlight for photosynthesis, sustained heating for decomposition, or even just the warmth of the water in a cold pack. Where the energy comes FROM is the surroundings; what delta H records is that the system took it in.

Take this section's questions now: definitions, the named examples on each side, the sign of delta H in each case, and the hot-flask cold-flask diagnostic.

## Subtopic: Activation Energy and the Activated Complex

Petrol and oxygen sit together peacefully in a fuel tank; methane flows through a stove unburnt until the igniter clicks. If combustion is exothermic — energetically downhill — why does it not simply happen?

Because between reactants and products stands a hill. Before any new bonds can form, the old bonds must be stretched and broken, and that initial investment must be paid FIRST, whatever refund is coming later. The ACTIVATION ENERGY, E A, is the minimum energy that colliding particles need in order to react — the height of the hill measured from the reactants' energy level to the summit.

At the summit sits the ACTIVATED COMPLEX: a temporary, unstable arrangement in which old bonds are partly broken and new bonds partly formed. It is not a substance you can bottle; it exists for the duration of a molecular collision and then falls apart — either forward into products or backward into the original reactants. Define it exactly that way: the unstable transition state of maximum potential energy, through which the reaction must pass.

This is why a match matters. The struck match delivers enough energy to lift a few molecules over the hill; their reaction releases more energy, which lifts their neighbours over, and the process feeds itself — combustion sustains its own activation once started. The match does not supply the reaction's energy; it supplies the ENTRY FEE, and the reaction's own surplus pays for everyone who follows. A reaction with a low activation energy proceeds readily, sometimes dangerously so; a high activation energy means reactants can coexist for years, like the petrol and air in the tank.

Keep the two energies strictly apart, because exam questions blur them deliberately: delta H is the difference between the start and end levels; E A is the climb from the start to the summit. A reaction can be steeply downhill overall and still have a formidable entry fee. The questions for this section are ready for you now.

## Subtopic: Potential Energy Graphs, Catalysed and Uncatalysed

The potential energy graph plots potential energy on the vertical axis against the course of the reaction on the horizontal. Reactants start at one level, the curve rises to a single peak, and descends to the products' level. Three measurements, always read the same way. The activation energy: from the reactants' level UP to the peak. The heat of reaction delta H: from the reactants' level to the products' level — down for exothermic, up for endothermic. And the activated complex: the arrangement at the peak itself.

Work a full example in numbers. Reactants sit at 50 kilojoules, the peak at 120, the products at 20. Activation energy: 120 minus 50 — 70 kilojoules. Delta H: 20 minus 50 — negative 30 kilojoules, exothermic. And the reverse reaction, products back to reactants, must climb from 20 to the same peak of 120: a reverse activation energy of 100 kilojoules, with delta H of positive 30 — every reaction's reverse flips the sign of delta H, and the two activation energies differ by exactly the size of delta H.

Now add a CATALYST. A catalyst provides an alternative reaction pathway with a lower activation energy, and is itself unchanged when the reaction ends. On the graph, one thing changes and one thing does not: the peak drops — say from 120 down to 90, cutting the climb from 70 kilojoules to 40 — but the start and end levels stay exactly where they were, so delta H is untouched. A catalyst makes the reaction FASTER; it cannot make the reaction release one joule more, and it lowers the reverse climb by the same amount. Drawing the catalysed curve as a lower dashed peak inside the original, with the same start and end, is a standard mark.

The standard traps, in closing. Reading delta H from the peak instead of between the levels. Claiming a catalyst lowers delta H. Forgetting that endothermic graphs also have a peak — the climb exists in both directions of every reaction. And swapping the labels of E A forward and E A reverse. The final questions of this part are with you now: read each value off the levels, in kilojoules, with its sign.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the whole topic again with nothing but a braai, a bicycle and a hill between two valleys.

## Subtopic: Paying to Break, Earning to Build

Think of every chemical bond as two magnets clicked together. Pulling them apart costs you effort — always. Letting two magnets snap together gives energy back — you hear it and feel it — always. Chemistry has no exceptions to either rule: breaking bonds costs energy, making bonds pays energy out.

A reaction is a rebuild: pull the old structures apart, snap new ones together. So every reaction has a bill and a refund, and the only question is which is bigger. Refund bigger than bill: the spare energy pours out, mostly as heat, and everything nearby warms up — that is an exothermic reaction. Your braai fire is one long refund: the new bonds in carbon dioxide and water pay out far more than it cost to break up the wood and oxygen, and the difference is what grills the meat.

Bill bigger than refund: the reaction cannot proceed unless energy keeps arriving from outside, and it drinks warmth from its surroundings — endothermic. An instant cold pack is exactly this: snap the inner bag, the dissolving starts, the bill exceeds the refund, and the pack pays the difference by stealing heat from your bruised ankle.

Chemists record the transaction with one number, delta H: what the chemicals ended with minus what they started with. Refund reaction: the chemicals end up poorer in stored energy, so delta H is NEGATIVE — negative for the chemicals, even though your hands feel the heat. Bill reaction: the chemicals end richer, delta H positive, and the room paid for it. Attach the sign to the chemicals, never to your fingers, and the whole topic behaves.

Your questions for this section are ready — magnets apart costs, magnets together pays, and delta H is the chemicals' bank statement.

## Subtopic: The Entry Fee — Why the Braai Needs a Match

If burning wood pays out so handsomely, why doesn't every tree burst into flame on a warm day? Why does the braai sit there, wood and air together, until somebody strikes a match?

Because every reaction, even the most profitable, charges an entry fee. Before the new bonds can snap together and pay out, the old bonds must first be broken open — and that cost comes FIRST. It is a business that needs upfront capital: guaranteed profit, but only after you have paid to open the doors. That upfront cost is the activation energy, and if the colliding molecules cannot cover it, nothing happens, no matter how large the eventual profit would be.

The match is the loan that opens the doors. It lifts a handful of molecules over the fee; their reaction pays out; that payout covers the entry fee for the neighbours; and from then on the fire finances itself. That is why you only need one match for a whole evening — and why the fire dies if the first bit of wood is wet: the payout gets spent boiling water instead of funding the next entry fees.

Right at the moment of paying the fee, the molecules pass through a strange halfway arrangement — old bonds half broken, new ones half formed, nothing you could put in a jar. Chemists call it the activated complex: the wobbling instant at the top of the deal, which tips forward into products or falls back to where it started.

High entry fee: petrol and air share a tank for years in peace. Low entry fee: some chemicals react at a touch, which is why laboratories store them so carefully. The fee, notice, says nothing about the profit — that is a separate number, and keeping the two separate is the heart of the next section. Your questions are with you now.

## Subtopic: Reading the Hill Between Two Valleys

Put the whole lesson into one picture: a hiking trail from one valley, over a ridge, into another valley. The left valley is where the reactants live; the right valley is the products; the ridge between them is the entry fee made visible. Chemists draw exactly this picture and call it a potential energy graph — height is stored energy, and the walk from left to right is the reaction happening.

Three readings, straight off the picture. How high is the ridge above the LEFT valley? That is the activation energy, the climb the reactants must make. How much lower — or higher — is the right valley than the left? That is delta H: destination below the start means the walk pays out overall, exothermic; destination above the start means the walk cost energy, endothermic. And the single highest point of the trail is where the activated complex lives, wobbling, deciding which way to fall.

Put numbers on the trail: start at 50, ridge at 120, finish at 20. Climb: 70. Overall drop: 30 down, so delta H is minus 30 — a profitable walk. Now walk it BACKWARDS, products to reactants: the same ridge from the right side is a climb of 100, and the walk ends 30 higher than it began. Same trail, both directions readable from one drawing — which is exactly what examiners ask.

Last idea, and it is the one the graph makes obvious: a catalyst. A catalyst is a tunnel partway through the ridge. The path over the top drops — the entry fee shrinks, so far more hikers make it across per hour and the reaction speeds up. But the tunnel moves neither valley a single metre: start and finish heights are untouched, so delta H does not change by one joule. Faster, not more profitable. On the drawing: same start, same end, a lower dashed peak.

The last questions of the lesson are yours now. Draw the two valleys, mark the ridge, and read every number off the heights — the picture does the remembering for you.
