**Bianca:** The questions that come up a lot on this topic, and the first is the sign of delta H. The flask gets HOT, energy is clearly being produced — so why on earth is delta H NEGATIVE? It feels backwards every single time.

**Tutor:** It feels backwards because you are standing in the wrong place when you read the sign. Delta H is the bank statement of the CHEMICALS, not of the room and not of your hand on the flask. In an exothermic reaction the chemicals pay energy out — heat leaves the system and enters the surroundings. The room gets richer, the flask feels hot, but the chemicals themselves end up POORER in stored energy than they started, and a bank statement that ends lower than it began is negative. Formally: delta H equals the energy of the products minus the energy of the reactants, and for an exothermic reaction the products sit lower, so the subtraction gives a negative number. The trick that makes it stick is to ask one question every time: did the chemicals end up with more stored energy or less? Flask hot means the chemicals paid out, so less, so negative. Flask cold means the chemicals absorbed, so more, so positive. Read the sign from the system's account, and the feeling of backwardness disappears within a week of practice.

***

**Bianca:** Next: if exothermic reactions are downhill and release energy, why do they not all just happen by themselves? Why is the petrol in a car's tank not on fire right now?

**Tutor:** Because downhill overall does not mean downhill from the very first step. Before a single new bond can form and pay out energy, the existing bonds in the reactants must be stretched and broken, and that cost is charged upfront. That upfront charge is the activation energy, and it stands between the reactants and their profit like a ridge between two valleys — the destination valley can be far below you, but you still have to climb the ridge to reach it. Petrol and oxygen molecules collide constantly in the tank, but at ordinary temperatures almost none of those collisions carries enough energy to cover the entry fee, so the pair coexist for years. A spark changes everything: it lifts a small population of molecules over the ridge, their reaction releases far more energy than the spark supplied, and that payout funds the climbs of their neighbours — which is why combustion is self-sustaining after one match. So hold the two ideas apart: delta H tells you whether the journey is profitable; activation energy tells you how hard it is to begin. A reaction can be enormously profitable and almost impossible to start, and the fuel tank is the proof you drive around with.

***

**Bianca:** Here's the one that costs graph marks: learners read delta H from the top of the peak, or measure the activation energy from the products' side. What is the fixed reading routine?

**Tutor:** Anchor everything to the three horizontal levels, and measure in a fixed order. First, find the reactants' level on the left and the products' level on the right — two flat lines. Delta H is the vertical gap BETWEEN those two levels, nothing to do with the peak at all: products below reactants gives a negative delta H, products above gives positive. Second, the activation energy of the forward reaction is measured from the REACTANTS' level up to the peak — always from where the journey starts. Third, if the question asks about the reverse reaction, stand in the products' valley and measure from there up to the same peak; that reverse activation energy differs from the forward one by exactly the size of delta H, and the reverse delta H is the forward one with its sign flipped. Take the worked numbers: reactants 50, peak 120, products 20. Forward climb 70, delta H minus 30, reverse climb 100, reverse delta H plus 30. If your two activation energies do not differ by your delta H, one of your readings is wrong — that is a free self-check. And one more habit: label the peak "activated complex", because that label is routinely a mark on its own.

***

**Bianca:** Last one: the catalyst. If a catalyst makes a reaction easier, why does it not also make the reaction release more energy? More speed should mean more heat, surely.

**Tutor:** More speed means the SAME heat delivered sooner, and the graph shows why. A catalyst provides an alternative pathway over a lower ridge — a tunnel partway through the mountain, in the lesson's picture. That lowers the entry fee, so a far larger fraction of collisions succeeds every second, and the reaction runs faster. But the tunnel does not move either valley: the reactants still start at the same stored energy and the products still finish at the same stored energy, and delta H is purely the difference between those two levels. The energy a reaction releases is fixed by where it starts and ends, not by the route taken between them — exactly as your change in altitude between two towns is the same whether you drive over the pass or through the tunnel. So a catalysed exothermic reaction releases precisely the same total energy, just at a greater rate, and the catalyst emerges chemically unchanged to do it all again. On the drawing, that is one dashed curve with a lower peak inside the original, meeting it at both ends. In words for the exam: a catalyst lowers the activation energy by providing an alternative pathway, increasing the rate, while delta H remains unchanged.
