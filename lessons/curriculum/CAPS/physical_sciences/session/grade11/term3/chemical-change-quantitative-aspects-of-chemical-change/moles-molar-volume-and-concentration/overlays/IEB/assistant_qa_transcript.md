**Bianca:** Let's work through the questions this chapter always produces. The first is the eternal one: the balance reads grams and the answer is wanted in grams — so why is every calculation forced through moles, a unit nobody can see or touch?

**Tutor:** Because balanced equations speak in particle counts, and grams are not counts. When an equation says two carbon monoxide molecules react with one oxygen molecule, that is a statement about numbers of particles — and masses betray those numbers whenever molar masses differ. Fifty-six grams of carbon monoxide and 32 grams of oxygen are in the perfect two-to-one particle ratio, though the masses suggest nothing of the kind. The mole is the translator standing between the two languages: grams are the balance's language, counts are the equation's language, and molar mass is the dictionary. So the detour is not ritual — it is the only honest path. Convert grams to moles on arrival, reason about ratios in moles, convert back to grams on departure. Skip the translator and your answer is wrong by precisely the ratio of the molar masses involved — an error so predictable that markers watch for it by name.

***

**Bianca:** Next: 22,4 cubic decimetres for every gas feels impossible. A sulphur dioxide molecule is a monster next to a helium atom — how can a mole of each need identical space?

**Tutor:** It would be impossible if molecules in a gas were packed against each other — but they are not remotely close. A gas is overwhelmingly empty: the molecules themselves fill something like a thousandth of the volume at ordinary conditions. What sets the volume is the spacing of the molecules and the pressure their impacts exert on the walls, and at a given temperature and pressure that spacing settles to the same value no matter the molecular mass. The heavy molecules travel slower but strike harder; the light ones travel faster but strike more softly; the two effects cancel with beautiful precision, leaving the COUNT as the only thing that matters. Hence Avogadro's law — equal volumes at equal conditions hold equal counts — and hence one fixed molar volume, 22,4 cubic decimetres at STP, for helium and sulphur dioxide alike. Push a gas toward liquefaction with brutal pressure or deep cold and the molecules do begin to crowd, bending the rule — but at the conditions your problems specify, 22,4 is solid ground.

***

**Bianca:** Now the thousand-times error. Someone computes a concentration and it comes out a thousand times off. Where does that factor keep sneaking in?

**Tutor:** From feeding cubic centimetres to a formula that eats cubic decimetres. Concentration is moles per cubic decimetre, so the V in c equals n over V must be in cubic decimetres — yet every piece of glassware on the bench is graduated in cubic centimetres. One cubic decimetre is 1 000 cubic centimetres, so 750 cubic centimetres is 0,75 cubic decimetres. Substitute 750 instead and your volume is inflated a thousandfold, dragging the concentration a thousandfold the other way — or the moles a thousandfold too big when the formula is rearranged. The immunisation is procedural: make the volume conversion the FIRST line of every solution calculation, written out as its own statement — V equals 750 cubic centimetres equals 0,75 cubic decimetres — before any formula appears. Four seconds of writing removes the most common error in the topic. Add a plausibility sense as backup: bench solutions typically sit between roughly 0,01 and 2 moles per cubic decimetre, so an answer of 200 should trip an alarm before anyone else sees it.

***

**Bianca:** Last one: empirical versus molecular formula. The calculations go fine, but people write the wrong one on the answer line. What keeps them apart?

**Tutor:** Carry one permanent example: ethane. Its molecular formula is C2H6 — two carbons and six hydrogens, the actual census of one molecule. Its empirical formula is CH3, because two and six simplify to one and three. Empirical is the simplified RATIO; molecular is the genuine COUNT. Now the deep point: percentage composition can never deliver more than the ratio, because percentages say nothing about a molecule's overall size — any compound built of CH3 units in any multiple shows exactly 80 percent carbon and 20 percent hydrogen. Upgrading ratio to count requires one additional fact, the molar mass: divide the compound's molar mass by the mass of the empirical unit and the answer is your whole-number multiplier. For ethane: the CH3 unit weighs 15, the compound weighs 30, so the multiplier is 2, and every subscript doubles. Exam craft: read the final instruction of the question. If it says empirical, stop at the ratio. If it says molecular, a molar mass must appear somewhere in the question — and if your solution never used it, you have answered the wrong question and should go back.
