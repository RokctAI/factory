**Bianca:** The questions that come up a lot on this topic, and the first is the limiting reagent trap itself: why can't I just pick the reactant with fewer moles? It runs out first, surely — it's smaller.

**Tutor:** Because reactions do not consume reactants at the same rate per mole — the coefficients set different consumption rates, and the smaller stockpile can easily outlast the bigger one. Look at the hydrogen and oxygen example: 2 moles of hydrogen against 1,25 moles of oxygen. Oxygen is the smaller number, but the equation eats TWO hydrogens for every ONE oxygen, so 1,25 moles of oxygen is enough to serve 2,5 moles of hydrogen — more hydrogen than we have. The small stockpile outlasts the big one because it is spent half as fast. The only reliable procedure is the ratio test: convert both to moles, pick one reactant, multiply its moles by the ratio to find how much partner it demands, and compare that demand with what is actually available. If the partner covers the demand, your chosen reactant is limiting; if it falls short, the partner is. It is the toastie kitchen exactly — seven slices of cheese outlasted ten slices of bread because the recipe eats bread double-speed. Fewer moles proves nothing; the ratio decides everything.

***

**Bianca:** Next: a learner found the limiting reagent correctly and then calculated the product from the OTHER reactant anyway, because it had nicer numbers. Why exactly is that wrong, and how big is the damage?

**Tutor:** It is wrong because the excess reactant never finishes reacting — some of it is still sitting in the flask when the reaction dies, so building a calculation on its full amount counts material that never became product. The damage is precisely the ratio of the excess: in our example, calculating water from the full 1,25 moles of oxygen would promise 2,5 moles of water, when only 2 moles can ever form, an overstatement of 25 percent, and every subsequent mark falls with it. Think of it as the kitchen promising toasties based on the cheese while the bread bin is already empty. The discipline to write into every solution: after the ratio test, draw a box around the limiting reagent's mole count and let nothing else feed the product calculation. The excess reactant answers exactly one question — how much is LEFT OVER — and that is calculated as supplied minus used, where the amount used comes, again, from the limiting reagent through the ratio. Two jobs, strictly separated: limiting reagent builds the products, excess reagent explains the leftovers.

**Bianca:** Here's one about purity that puzzles people: we want to know how much calcium carbonate is in the seashell, but we measure the carbon dioxide coming OFF it. Why measure the product instead of the thing we actually care about?

**Tutor:** Because the thing we care about is physically inseparable from its impurities by any simple means — you cannot pick grains of pure carbonate out of crushed shell with tweezers. But chemistry offers a beautiful sorting trick: the genuine carbonate reacts with acid and the impurities do not. So we let the acid interrogate the sample. Every mole of real calcium carbonate that meets the acid surrenders exactly one mole of carbon dioxide — the balanced equation guarantees the exchange rate — while the sand and protein in the shell stay silent. Measure the gas, and you have counted the carbonate without ever seeing it: 1,792 cubic decimetres at STP is 0,08 moles of gas, therefore 0,08 moles of carbonate, therefore 8 grams, therefore 80 percent of the 10 gram sample. Two conditions make the logic watertight, and exams probe both. The acid must be in EXCESS, so that every last scrap of carbonate reacts — if acid ran out first, unreacted carbonate would go uncounted and the purity would come out falsely low. And the measured product must come only from the reaction — which is why the gas is collected carefully rather than left to mix with the air.

**Bianca:** Last one: the airbag question feels like a story problem, and learners freeze on where to start. What is the fixed route through it?

**Tutor:** Strip the story away and it is the same three-leg journey as every other stoichiometry question; the airbag is just the costume. The fixed route: first, write the balanced equation — two sodium azide gives two sodium plus three nitrogen gas — because nothing can move without the ratio. Second, convert the given quantity to moles: 130 grams of azide at 65 grams per mole is 2 moles. Third, cross the equation with wanted over given — three over two — so 2 moles of azide gives 3 moles of nitrogen. Fourth, convert to what the question asks: at STP multiply by 22,4, giving 67,2 cubic decimetres. The only new muscle the airbag adds is running the journey in reverse, because design questions give the bag volume and ask for the pellet mass: volume divided by 22,4 gives moles of gas, ratio flipped to two over three gives moles of azide, times 65 gives grams. Practise the route once forwards and once backwards and no storyline can hide it from you — sodium azide today, an antacid tablet or a fire extinguisher tomorrow, the same four lines every time.
