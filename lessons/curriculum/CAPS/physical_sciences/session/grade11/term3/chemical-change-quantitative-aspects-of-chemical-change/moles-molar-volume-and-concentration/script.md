# Part 1 — Expert

Every mark in the Chemical Change section of the paper passes through one idea: the mole. This session rebuilds the mole at Grade 11 depth and adds the three conversion tools the rest of the year depends on — molar mass for solids, molar volume for gases, and concentration for solutions — before closing with percentage composition and the route from an empirical formula to a molecular formula. Four tools, one habit: every quantity becomes moles before anything else happens to it.

## Subtopic: The Mole, Avogadro's Number and Molar Mass

The mole is the SI unit for amount of substance. One mole of any substance contains 6,02 times 10 to the power 23 particles — Avogadro's number, written N A. The particles may be atoms, molecules or ions; the count is always the same. The definition to write in an exam: one mole is the amount of substance containing as many particles as there are atoms in 12 grams of carbon-12.

Three symbols carry the whole topic. The amount in moles is n. The number of particles is N, and n equals N divided by N A — the number of particles divided by Avogadro's number. The molar mass M is the mass of one mole of a substance in grams per mole, read straight off the periodic table, and n equals m divided by M — the mass divided by the molar mass.

Molar mass is assembled atom by atom, respecting every subscript. Water, H2O: two hydrogens at 1 plus one oxygen at 16 — 18 grams per mole. Carbon dioxide, CO2: carbon 12 plus two oxygens at 16 — 44 grams per mole. Sodium carbonate, Na2CO3: two sodiums at 23 is 46, plus carbon 12, plus three oxygens at 16 is 48 — 106 grams per mole. The commonest error in the whole topic is a forgotten subscript, so total the atoms out loud before you add.

Now the conversions run in both directions. How many moles is 8,8 grams of carbon dioxide? Formula: n equals m over M. Substitution: 8,8 grams divided by 44 grams per mole. Answer: 0,2 moles. How many molecules is that? Formula: N equals n times N A. Substitution: 0,2 times 6,02 times 10 to the 23. Answer: 1,204 times 10 to the 23 molecules. And backwards: 0,5 moles of water has a mass of 0,5 times 18, which is 9 grams.

The questions for this section are with you now. They test the definition of the mole, molar masses built with their subscripts intact, and the two conversions — mass to moles and moles to particles — in both directions.

## Subtopic: Molar Gas Volume and Avogadro's Law

Gases refuse to be weighed conveniently, so chemistry measures them by volume — and one clean fact makes that possible. At STP, standard temperature and pressure, which is 0 degrees Celsius and 101,3 kilopascals, one mole of ANY gas occupies 22,4 cubic decimetres. This is the molar gas volume, V M, and it gives the third conversion: n equals V divided by V M — the gas volume divided by 22,4 cubic decimetres per mole.

Why is the volume the same for every gas, when a mole of hydrogen weighs 2 grams and a mole of carbon dioxide weighs 44? Because in a gas the molecules are far apart, and the volume is almost entirely empty space between them. The size of the individual molecules barely matters; only their COUNT does. That is Avogadro's law: equal volumes of gases, at the same temperature and pressure, contain equal numbers of molecules.

Work the conversion. How many moles of hydrogen gas is 5,6 cubic decimetres at STP? Formula: n equals V over V M. Substitution: 5,6 divided by 22,4. Answer: 0,25 moles. And backwards: 2 moles of oxygen at STP occupies 2 times 22,4, which is 44,8 cubic decimetres.

Avogadro's law pays a second dividend: for gases at the same temperature and pressure, the coefficients of a balanced equation can be read directly as VOLUME ratios. Take N2 plus 3H2 gives 2NH3 — one nitrogen molecule reacts with three hydrogen molecules to form two ammonia molecules. Then 10 cubic decimetres of nitrogen needs exactly 30 cubic decimetres of hydrogen and delivers 20 cubic decimetres of ammonia, all measured at the same conditions. No molar masses, no grams — the volumes carry the ratio themselves. This shortcut works for gases only; the moment a solid or a solution enters the problem, you must go back through moles.

Take this section's questions now: the value and conditions of the molar volume, the statement of Avogadro's law, the volume-to-moles conversion, and the volume-ratio shortcut with its gas-only warning.

## Subtopic: Concentration — How Much Is Dissolved in How Much

Solutions need their own measure, because 1 gram of salt in a teaspoon of water and 1 gram in a swimming pool are chemically different situations. Concentration is the amount of dissolved substance per volume of solution: c equals n divided by V — the moles of solute divided by the volume of solution in cubic decimetres. The unit is moles per cubic decimetre, and a solution of 1 mole per cubic decimetre has one mole of solute in every cubic decimetre of solution.

Watch the units, because this is where the marks leak. Volume must be in cubic decimetres, and laboratory glassware speaks in cubic centimetres: 1 000 cubic centimetres is 1 cubic decimetre, so divide centimetre readings by 1 000. A volume of 500 cubic centimetres is 0,5 cubic decimetres; 250 cubic centimetres is 0,25 cubic decimetres.

Work it forwards. Dissolve 4 grams of sodium hydroxide, NaOH, in water and make the solution up to 500 cubic centimetres. Molar mass of NaOH: sodium 23 plus oxygen 16 plus hydrogen 1 — 40 grams per mole. Moles: 4 divided by 40 is 0,1 moles. Formula: c equals n over V. Substitution: 0,1 moles divided by 0,5 cubic decimetres. Answer: 0,2 moles per cubic decimetre.

And backwards, the form titration questions love: how many moles of acid are in 250 cubic centimetres of a 0,4 mole per cubic decimetre solution? Rearrange: n equals c times V. Substitution: 0,4 times 0,25. Answer: 0,1 moles. Notice the pattern of every mole calculation so far — identify which conversion applies, write the formula, substitute with units, state the answer.

The questions for this section are ready for you: the definition and unit of concentration, the cubic centimetre trap, and both directions of c equals n over V.

## Subtopic: Percentage Composition, Empirical and Molecular Formulae

Percentage composition asks what fraction of a compound's mass each element supplies. The recipe: the mass of that element in one mole, divided by the molar mass of the compound, times 100. Calcium carbonate, CaCO3, molar mass 100 grams per mole: calcium contributes 40 out of 100, which is 40 percent; carbon 12 out of 100, which is 12 percent; oxygen 48 out of 100, which is 48 percent. The three percentages must total 100 — a free check, so always take it.

The empirical formula is the simplest whole-number ratio of atoms, and it is recovered from percentages by running the logic backwards. A gaseous compound is 75 percent carbon and 25 percent hydrogen. Take 100 grams, so the percentages become masses. Moles of carbon: 75 divided by 12 is 6,25. Moles of hydrogen: 25 divided by 1 is 25. Divide both by the smallest: 6,25 over 6,25 is 1; 25 over 6,25 is 4. Ratio one to four — empirical formula CH4.

The molecular formula is the ACTUAL count of atoms in a molecule, and it is always a whole-number multiple of the empirical formula. To find the multiple you need one extra fact: the molar mass of the compound. A compound has empirical formula CH2O and molar mass 180 grams per mole. The empirical unit weighs 12 plus 2 plus 16, which is 30 grams per mole. The multiple: 180 divided by 30 is 6. Multiply every subscript by 6: the molecular formula is C6H12O6 — glucose. Check: six carbons at 12 is 72, twelve hydrogens is 12, six oxygens is 96; total 180. Confirmed.

Keep the two formulae distinct in your written answers: CH2O is the ratio, C6H12O6 is the molecule, and the examiner asks for each by name. The questions for this section are with you now — percentages that must total 100, the divide-by-smallest routine, and the multiple that turns empirical into molecular.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same four tools from the till point of a tuck shop, where nobody counts sweets one at a time.

## Subtopic: Counting by the Boxful

Nobody at a tuck shop counts out 500 loose sweets at the till point. They count boxes, because the supplier packs the same number into every box. Chemists have exactly one box size for everything, and it is enormous: 6,02 times 10 to the 23 particles. That box is called a mole. Atoms are so absurdly small that a count of that size is the first number big enough to be useful — one mole of water molecules is 18 grams, a couple of swallows from a bottle.

Here is the tuck-shop trick that unlocks the whole chapter: a box of marshmallows and a box of toothpicks hold the same COUNT but have different WEIGHTS. Same with chemistry's boxes. A mole of hydrogen atoms weighs 1 gram; a mole of carbon atoms weighs 12; a mole of carbon dioxide molecules weighs 44. That per-box weight is the molar mass, and the periodic table is simply the supplier's catalogue listing it for every element.

So when a question gives you grams and asks for moles, it is asking: how many full boxes is this? Divide the mass by the per-box weight. Given 8,8 grams of carbon dioxide at 44 grams per box: 8,8 divided by 44 is 0,2 boxes — 0,2 moles. Want the actual particle count? Multiply the boxes by the count per box: 0,2 times 6,02 times 10 to the 23. Boxes to items, items to boxes, grams to boxes — every question in this chapter is one of those hops.

Your questions for this section are ready. Think boxes: the count per box never changes, the weight per box depends on what is inside.

## Subtopic: Balloons Are Honest About the Count

Gases pull a beautiful stunt that solids never manage. Blow up two identical balloons at the same temperature — one with helium, one with air. Different gases, very different weights of gas inside. But the number of molecules in the two balloons? Identical. In a gas the molecules are tiny specks flying through mostly empty space, so the balloon's size depends on how MANY specks are bouncing around inside it, not on how fat each speck is.

That honesty gets a name and a number. Equal volumes of gas at the same temperature and pressure hold equal counts — that is Avogadro's law. And at standard conditions, one full mole-box of any gas takes up 22,4 cubic decimetres, about the space inside two big shopping bags. So gas questions skip the weighing entirely: 5,6 cubic decimetres of hydrogen at standard conditions is 5,6 divided by 22,4, which is a quarter of a mole. Volume divided by 22,4 — that is the whole trick.

Even better, balanced equations turn into balloon arithmetic. Nitrogen plus hydrogen making ammonia goes one to three to two — so 10 balloons of nitrogen swallow 30 balloons of hydrogen and give back 20 balloons of ammonia, no scale in sight. One warning taped to the trick: it works for gases only. The moment something solid or dissolved appears in the problem, back to boxes — back to moles.

The questions on this section are with you now. Balloons hold counts, 22,4 is the size of one mole-box of gas, and coefficients are balloon ratios.

## Subtopic: How Strong Is the Mix

Two jugs of juice on the counter. Each got one scoop of concentrate — but one jug is small and one is huge. Same amount of concentrate, completely different drink. The small jug tastes strong, the big one tastes like regret. What your tongue is measuring is concentration: how much stuff per how much liquid.

Chemistry writes the tongue's verdict as a fraction: concentration equals moles of dissolved stuff divided by cubic decimetres of solution. Dissolve 0,1 moles of sodium hydroxide and top up to half a cubic decimetre: 0,1 divided by 0,5 gives 0,2 moles per cubic decimetre. Double the water and the same 0,1 moles gives half the concentration — one scoop in a bigger jug.

Two habits keep this easy. First, the glassware in a lab is marked in cubic centimetres, but the formula eats cubic decimetres, so divide by 1 000 before anything else: 250 cubic centimetres is 0,25 cubic decimetres. Second, the formula runs backwards just as happily: a question that gives the concentration and the volume is quietly asking for the moles, and moles equal concentration times volume — 0,4 moles per cubic decimetre times 0,25 cubic decimetres is 0,1 moles.

And one last connection to close the lesson. The percentages on a food label — so much of this element, so much of that — are chemistry's ingredient list, and from those percentages alone you can rebuild the simplest recipe of a compound, its empirical formula. Divide each percentage by that element's per-box weight, simplify the ratio, done. The full molecule is that recipe multiplied up a whole number of times, and the compound's molar mass tells you the multiplier.

The last questions of the lesson are yours now. Scoops per jug for concentration, boxes for everything else, and every quantity visits moles before it goes anywhere.
