**Bianca:** The questions that come up a lot on this one, starting with the classic: why do we bother with moles at all? The balance gives grams, the question asks for grams — why must every calculation take a detour through this invisible unit?

**Tutor:** Because chemical equations count particles, and grams do not. When an equation says one nitrogen molecule reacts with three hydrogen molecules, that is a statement about counts, and counts and masses disagree the moment two substances have different molar masses — 28 grams of nitrogen and 6 grams of hydrogen are in the perfect one-to-three ratio, even though the masses look nothing like one to three. The mole is the translator between the two worlds. Grams are what the balance speaks; moles are what the equation speaks; molar mass is the dictionary between them. So the detour is not bureaucracy, it is the only honest route: convert your grams into moles at the start, do all reasoning about ratios in moles, and convert back to grams at the end. Learners who try to shortcut past the mole get answers that are wrong by exactly the ratio of the molar masses involved, which is why the error is so consistent and so heavily penalised.

***

**Bianca:** Next one: why is the molar volume 22,4 cubic decimetres for EVERY gas? A carbon dioxide molecule is far bigger and heavier than a helium atom, so surely a mole of carbon dioxide needs more room.

**Tutor:** It would, if the molecules were packed shoulder to shoulder — but in a gas they are nowhere near each other. A gas is overwhelmingly empty space; the molecules themselves occupy a tiny fraction of the volume, something like a thousandth of it at ordinary conditions. The volume a gas takes up is set by how far apart the molecules fly and how hard they hammer the container walls, and at a fixed temperature and pressure that spacing is the same regardless of what the molecules weigh. Heavy molecules move more slowly but hit harder; light molecules move faster but hit more gently; the effects balance so exactly that only the COUNT of molecules matters. That is why equal counts of any gas fill equal volumes, and why one mole of anything gaseous at STP is 22,4 cubic decimetres. The moment you compress a gas hard or cool it toward liquefaction the molecules do start crowding each other and the rule bends — but at exam conditions, 22,4 holds for helium and carbon dioxide alike.

***

**Bianca:** Here is the one that costs the most marks: a learner calculated a concentration and got an answer a thousand times too big. Where does that factor of a thousand keep coming from?

**Tutor:** From substituting cubic centimetres where the formula demands cubic decimetres. Concentration is moles per cubic decimetre, and the volume in c equals n over V must be in cubic decimetres — but every burette, pipette and flask in the laboratory is labelled in cubic centimetres. There are 1 000 cubic centimetres in one cubic decimetre, so a volume of 250 cubic centimetres is 0,25 cubic decimetres. Substitute 250 instead of 0,25 and your volume is a thousand times too large, which makes the concentration a thousand times too small — or, if the volume is in the numerator of a rearranged formula, a thousand times too big. The protection is mechanical: the very first line of any solution calculation converts the volume, before any formula is written. Write "V equals 250 cubic centimetres equals 0,25 cubic decimetres" as its own line. It takes four seconds, and it removes the single most common error in this entire topic. A quick sanity check helps too: school laboratory concentrations usually sit between about 0,01 and 2 moles per cubic decimetre, so an answer of 400 should feel wrong before the marker sees it.

***

**Bianca:** Last one: empirical formula versus molecular formula — learners can do both calculations but keep writing the wrong one down as the final answer. How do you keep them straight?

**Tutor:** Hold one example in your head permanently: glucose. Its molecular formula is C6H12O6 — six carbons, twelve hydrogens, six oxygens, the actual census of one molecule. Its empirical formula is CH2O, because six, twelve and six simplify to one, two and one. The empirical formula is the simplified RATIO; the molecular formula is the real COUNT. Percentage composition can only ever give you the ratio, because percentages carry no information about how big the molecule actually is — glucose and formaldehyde have identical percentage compositions, since formaldehyde IS CH2O. To promote a ratio to a real count you need one more fact, the molar mass: divide the compound's molar mass by the mass of the empirical unit, and that whole number is your multiplier. For glucose, 180 divided by 30 is 6, so multiply every subscript in CH2O by 6. In the exam, read the question's last line carefully: if it says empirical, stop at the ratio; if it says molecular, it must supply a molar mass somewhere — and if you have not used that molar mass, you have answered the wrong question.
