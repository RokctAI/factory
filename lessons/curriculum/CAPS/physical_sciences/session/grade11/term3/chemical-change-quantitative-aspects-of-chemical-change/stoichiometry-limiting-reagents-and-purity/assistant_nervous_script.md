Hey, it's Bianca — limiting reagents, percentage yield, percentage purity, exploding airbags... this feels like Grade 10 stoichiometry with three new monsters bolted on. Good news: they are not monsters, they are checkpoints, and each one is a single extra line in a method you already own.

The core journey has not changed since last year: grams to moles, ratio across the equation, moles back to whatever the question wants. Write those three legs every time, and the Grade 11 extras slot in around them.

Checkpoint one, limiting reagent. It only appears when the question gives you TWO amounts. The routine: both to moles, then the ratio test — take one reactant, work out how much partner it needs, and check whether that much partner exists. Whoever falls short is limiting, and every product calculation is built on it. Do not trust the smaller number; trust the test. Ten slices of bread lose to seven slices of cheese when the recipe eats bread two at a time.

Checkpoint two, percentage yield. Actual over theoretical, times one hundred — and the actual is always the measured, disappointing number from the question, while the theoretical is the ceiling YOU calculated. If your answer comes out above one hundred, do not celebrate; check whether you divided the wrong way around.

Checkpoint three, percentage purity. Mass of the pure substance over mass of the whole sample, times one hundred — and you find the pure mass by working BACKWARDS from a measured product through the equation. The impurities never react, so they never appear in the moles; that is the whole trick.

And the airbag question? That is just the journey wearing a seatbelt: balanced equation, grams to moles, two-to-three ratio, times 22,4 for the gas volume. Nothing in it you have not already done today.

Three legs, three checkpoints, one mass-conservation check at the end if you have time — in equals out, always. Take the questions one line at a time. You've got this!
