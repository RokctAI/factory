# Part 1 — Expert

Grade 10 built the mole-ratio journey: mass to moles, ratio across the equation, moles back to mass. Grade 11 promotes it with the three complications that examiners love, because industry lives with them daily: the reactant that runs out first, the product that falls short of the prediction, and the raw material that arrives less than pure. One thread ties the session together: the balanced equation promises in moles, and every complication is measured against that promise.

## Subtopic: The Mole-Ratio Method at Grade 11 Speed

The three-leg journey, compressed to exam speed on a reaction with real coefficients. Ammonia is made industrially from nitrogen and hydrogen: N2 plus 3H2 gives 2NH3 — one nitrogen molecule reacts with three hydrogen molecules to form two ammonia molecules. Question: what maximum mass of ammonia can 28 grams of nitrogen deliver, with hydrogen in plentiful supply?

Leg one, mass to moles. Molar mass of N2: two nitrogens at 14 — 28 grams per mole. So 28 grams is exactly 1 mole of nitrogen.

Leg two, ratio across. The coefficients say nitrogen to ammonia is one to two, so 1 mole of nitrogen promises 2 moles of ammonia. Write the ratio as a fraction — coefficients of wanted over given, two over one — and multiply. This is the leg where Grade 11 equations bite: ratios of two to three and three to two appear constantly, and writing the fraction out is the defence against inverting it.

Leg three, moles to mass. Molar mass of NH3: nitrogen 14 plus three hydrogens at 1 — 17 grams per mole. Mass equals n times M: 2 moles times 17 grams per mole — 34 grams of ammonia. That is the theoretical yield: the ceiling the atoms allow.

Every calculation in this session is this journey with one extra checkpoint bolted on. Speak the discipline aloud as you work: grams never cross the equation; only moles cross. The questions for this section are with you now — molar masses of diatomic elements, the wanted-over-given fraction, and the full journey to 34 grams.

## Subtopic: The Limiting Reagent — Who Runs Out First

Real reactions are seldom fed in perfect ratio. When two given quantities are supplied, one reactant runs out first and stops the reaction; that one is the LIMITING REAGENT, and every product calculation must be built on it. The other reactant is in excess, and some of it is left over when the reaction dies.

The method: convert BOTH given quantities to moles, then use the mole ratio to test whether the second reactant covers what the first demands. Hydrogen burns in oxygen: 2H2 plus O2 gives 2H2O — two hydrogen molecules react with one oxygen molecule to form two water molecules. Supplied: 4 grams of hydrogen and 40 grams of oxygen.

Both to moles. Hydrogen, molar mass 2: 4 divided by 2 is 2 moles. Oxygen, molar mass 32: 40 divided by 32 is 1,25 moles.

Now the test. The ratio demands one oxygen for every two hydrogens, so 2 moles of hydrogen needs 1 mole of oxygen. Available: 1,25 moles — more than enough. Oxygen is in excess, hydrogen is limiting. Had the oxygen been below 1 mole, the verdict would have reversed.

Build everything on the limiting reagent. Water formed: hydrogen to water is two to two, so 2 moles of hydrogen gives 2 moles of water — 2 times 18 is 36 grams. Oxygen left over: 1,25 supplied minus 1 used is 0,25 moles — 0,25 times 32 is 8 grams. And the mass audit closes the case: 4 grams of hydrogen plus 40 grams of oxygen in; 36 grams of water plus 8 grams of unused oxygen out; 44 equals 44. Conservation of mass is a free check, and markers reward it.

The trap: choosing the limiting reagent by comparing masses, or by grabbing the smaller mole count without consulting the ratio. There is MORE oxygen than needed here despite its smaller mole count being irrelevant — 1,25 moles of oxygen serves up to 2,5 moles of hydrogen. Only the ratio test decides. This section's questions are ready: both-to-moles, the ratio test, product from the limiting reagent, and the leftover excess.

## Subtopic: Percentage Yield and Percentage Purity

Two honesty checks, one for the product and one for the raw material.

Percentage yield compares what the laboratory collects with what the equation promised: actual yield divided by theoretical yield, times 100. The ammonia plant above promised 34 grams; suppose the plant collects 27,2 grams. Substitute: 27,2 divided by 34, times 100 — 80 percent. Yields fall short for physical reasons worth naming in words: reactions that do not run to completion, product lost in transfers and filtrations, side reactions consuming reactant, and reverse reactions clawing product back. A yield above 100 percent is never a triumph; it announces wet or contaminated product, or unreacted material weighed in with the goods.

Percentage purity faces the other direction: the raw material itself is part rubbish. Seashells are mostly calcium carbonate, but not entirely. The standard technique: react the sample with an excess of acid, measure a product precisely, and work BACKWARDS through the equation to find how much genuine calcium carbonate the sample contained.

Work it. A 10 gram sample of crushed seashell reacts with excess hydrochloric acid: CaCO3 plus 2HCl gives CaCl2 plus H2O plus CO2 — calcium carbonate plus hydrochloric acid gives calcium chloride, water and carbon dioxide gas. The gas is collected: 1,792 cubic decimetres at STP. Moles of carbon dioxide: 1,792 divided by 22,4 is 0,08 moles. The ratio of carbonate to gas is one to one, so the sample held 0,08 moles of calcium carbonate — 0,08 times 100 grams per mole is 8 grams. Percentage purity: mass of pure substance over mass of sample, times 100 — 8 over 10, times 100 — 80 percent pure. The impurities never entered the equation because they never reacted; the acid was in excess precisely so that every scrap of genuine carbonate would.

Take this section's questions now: the two formulae, the backwards journey from measured gas to hidden carbonate, and the correct diagnosis of a yield over 100.

## Subtopic: Airbags and Explosions — Stoichiometry at Speed

An explosion, to a chemist, is a reaction that converts a small volume of solid or liquid into a large volume of hot gas in milliseconds. The destructive push is the gas volume arriving faster than the surroundings can move aside. The same chemistry, tamed and aimed, saves lives every day: the car airbag.

Behind the steering wheel sits a pellet of sodium azide, NaN3. On impact, a sensor fires an electrical igniter and the azide decomposes: 2NaN3 gives 2Na plus 3N2 — two sodium azide units decompose into two sodium atoms and three molecules of nitrogen gas. Solid pellet in, harmless nitrogen gas out, and the bag is full before your head arrives.

The exam question is pure stoichiometry: what volume of nitrogen, at STP, does 130 grams of sodium azide deliver? Molar mass of NaN3: sodium 23 plus three nitrogens at 14, which is 42 — 65 grams per mole. Moles of azide: 130 divided by 65 is 2 moles. Ratio across: azide to nitrogen is two to three, so 2 moles of azide gives 3 moles of nitrogen. Volume: 3 times 22,4 — 67,2 cubic decimetres of gas, from a pellet you could hold in one palm. That thousandfold volume expansion, delivered in about 30 milliseconds, is exactly what an explosion is; the airbag simply catches it in a nylon bag.

Design notes the examiner may probe: the reaction must be fast, the product gas must be inert — nitrogen, not something flammable or toxic — and the pellet mass is calculated from the bag volume by exactly the sum you just did. Engineers run your three-leg journey in reverse: bag volume to moles of gas, ratio back across, moles to grams of pellet.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the balanced airbag equation, the 67,2 cubic decimetres, and the definition of an explosion as violent gas production.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the whole session again from a kitchen that sells toasted sandwiches, where every complication in this lesson happens before lunchtime.

## Subtopic: The Ingredient That Runs Out First

The sandwich recipe is fixed: two slices of bread plus one slice of cheese makes one toastie. Recipes are ratios, exactly like balanced equations — and the ratio is in SLICES, not in grams, the way an equation's ratio is in moles, never in grams.

This morning's stock: ten slices of bread, seven slices of cheese. How many toasties? The wrong move is to glance at the smaller number and blame the cheese. The right move is the ratio test: ten slices of bread demands five slices of cheese — do we have five? Yes, seven. So the cheese is fine; the BREAD runs out first. Five toasties, and then the kitchen stops with two slices of cheese still on the counter.

That is the limiting reagent: the ingredient that runs out first and shuts the kitchen. Everything you can promise a customer is built on IT, never on the leftover cheese. Chemistry's version is identical: convert both reactants to moles, ask whether the second covers what the first demands, crown the limiting reagent, and calculate every product from that one. And notice the trap the toastie exposes: cheese was the SMALLER number, yet bread ran out first, because the recipe eats bread twice as fast. Smallest number does not mean limiting — the ratio decides.

Your questions for this section are ready. Run the ratio test before you answer anything, and count the leftover cheese while you are there, because examiners ask for the excess too.

## Subtopic: What the Kitchen Promised and What Reached the Plate

The recipe promised five toasties. Watch the lunch rush: one slips off the grill, one burns and gets binned, and somebody's little brother steals a corner of cheese off the counter. Four toasties reach the plates. The kitchen's percentage yield: what actually arrived, divided by what the recipe promised, times 100 — four over five is 80 percent.

Chemistry's kitchen loses product the same ways. Some reactant never reacts — the middle of the lump stays raw. Some product sticks to the glassware — the toastie left on the grill. Side reactions pinch ingredients — the little brother. So real yields sit below 100 percent, and the formula is the honest scoreboard: actual over theoretical, times 100.

Now the diagnosis worth marks: a yield OVER 100 percent. Six toasties from a five-toastie recipe is not a miracle kitchen; it means something extra got weighed in — a plate counted with the food, a soggy toastie heavy with water. In the laboratory an over-100 yield means wet product, or unreacted powder mixed in with the goods. The atoms set a ceiling, and nothing honest goes above it.

And one more everyday swindle: the raw material itself. You bought a bag labelled cheese, but a quarter of the slices are the plastic dividers. Purity is the same scoreboard pointed at ingredients: the mass of real cheese over the mass of the whole bag, times 100. Chemists measure it cunningly — react the sample with plenty of acid, catch the gas, and work backwards to how much real substance must have been there, because the plastic never fizzes.

The questions for this section are with you now: promised versus delivered, why over-100 always means contamination, and the backwards trick that measures purity.

## Subtopic: The Pillow That Inflates Before You Blink

Take everything from this lesson and put it behind a steering wheel. Inside the wheel sits a small solid pellet — sodium azide, about 130 grams, the mass of a bar of soap. Its one talent: when zapped, it falls apart into sodium and a huge amount of nitrogen gas, the same harmless gas that makes up most of the air you are breathing.

The crash sensor fires, and the pellet runs the recipe: two units of sodium azide give two of sodium and THREE of nitrogen gas. Run the kitchen arithmetic: 130 grams at 65 grams per box is two boxes of azide; the recipe pays three boxes of gas for every two of azide, so three boxes of nitrogen; and each box of gas at standard conditions fills 22,4 cubic decimetres. Three times 22,4 — over 67 cubic decimetres of gas, a fully fattened pillow, from a soap-bar of solid, in about the time it takes a raindrop to cross your windscreen.

That violent unpacking of solid into gas is what an explosion IS. Dynamite does it to shatter rock; the airbag does the identical trick and simply catches the blast in a bag. The engineers chose sodium azide because the gas it throws is inert nitrogen — an airbag full of something flammable would be solving one crash with another — and they sized the pellet by running our sandwich arithmetic backwards: how big is the bag, how many boxes of gas is that, how many grams of pellet buys those boxes.

So the skill you practised with bread and cheese is the one that decides how hard a pillow punches back in a crash. The last questions of the lesson are yours now: recipe in boxes, ratio test first, honest scoreboard after — and every gram accounted for on both sides.
