Hey, it's Bianca — Arrhenius, Brønsted-Lowry, conjugate pairs, ampholytes... this topic sounds like a law exam. It is actually a playground game with a scoreboard, and once you see the game, the vocabulary stops biting.

The game: one tiny proton gets passed from molecule to molecule. Thrower is the acid. Catcher is the base. That single sentence IS the Brønsted-Lowry theory, and it answers half the questions in the paper. Given any equation, find who lost an H going left to right — acid — and who gained one — base. Watch the ball, not the fancy names.

Conjugate pairs? Spot-the-difference. Match each species on the left with the one on the right that differs by exactly ONE hydrogen. The one holding the extra hydrogen is the acid of that pair. Two loops per equation, drawn diagonally across the arrow, never side by side. And police the "exactly one": H2SO4 and SO4 two minus differ by two, so they are not a pair — there is a stepping stone in between.

Ampholytes sound exotic but the question is always the same: write TWO equations, one where the substance throws the proton, one where it catches. Water and hydrogen carbonate are the two you will be asked. Two equations, done.

The three acid reactions compress into one line: acid plus base gives salt plus water — and if the base was a carbonate, add a fizz of carbon dioxide. That is all three memorised at once. Salt's first name from the base, surname from the acid.

Indicators: make yourself a two-column colour table tonight — acid side, base side — four rows: litmus red-blue, methyl orange red-yellow, bromothymol blue yellow-blue with green at neutral, phenolphthalein colourless-pink. Eight colours plus one green. Five minutes to make, permanent marks thereafter.

Thrower, catcher, spot-the-difference, one master pattern, one colour table. The topic is smaller than its vocabulary. You've got this!
