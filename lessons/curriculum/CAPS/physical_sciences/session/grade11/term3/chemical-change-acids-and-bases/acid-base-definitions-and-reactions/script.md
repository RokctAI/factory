# Part 1 — Expert

The last session fixed the names and formulae of the common acids and bases. This one asks the deeper question — what MAKES a substance an acid or a base — and answers it twice, first with Arrhenius and then with the sharper Brønsted-Lowry definition that runs on protons. From there: conjugate pairs, the substances that can play both roles, the three reactions of acids that every paper demands, and the indicator colours that make the invisible chemistry visible.

## Subtopic: Two Definitions — Arrhenius and Brønsted-Lowry

The historical first attempt is Arrhenius. An Arrhenius acid produces hydrogen ions, H plus, when it dissolves in water; an Arrhenius base produces hydroxide ions, OH minus. The definition works for the obvious cases — HCl delivers hydrogen ions, NaOH delivers hydroxide — but it has two failures the examiner expects you to name. It confines acid-base chemistry to water, and it cannot explain ammonia: NH3 contains no hydroxide at all, yet its solution is plainly basic.

Brønsted and Lowry repaired both faults by watching what actually moves: the proton. A hydrogen ion IS a bare proton — a hydrogen atom stripped of its one electron — so the definitions become: an acid is a PROTON DONOR; a base is a PROTON ACCEPTOR. No water required, no hydroxide required; acid-base chemistry becomes the story of a single proton changing hands.

Watch both definitions at work on dissolution. Hydrochloric acid in water: HCl plus H2O gives H3O plus, plus Cl minus — the HCl molecule DONATES its proton to a water molecule, making a hydronium ion, H3O plus, and leaving a chloride ion behind. HCl acted as the acid; water, accepting the proton, acted as a base. Now ammonia in water: NH3 plus H2O gives NH4 plus, plus OH minus, and this reaction runs in both directions, so write it with the double arrow of equilibrium. Here ammonia ACCEPTS a proton from water, becoming ammonium — so ammonia is the base, and water, donating, is the acid. The ammonia mystery dissolves: a base needs no hydroxide of its own if it can pull a proton off water and leave hydroxide behind.

Notice what water just did across the two equations — accepted a proton in one, donated in the other. Hold that thought for the ampholyte section. The questions for this section are with you now: both definitions, both dissolution equations, and the named shortcomings of Arrhenius.

## Subtopic: Conjugate Acid-Base Pairs

Every Brønsted-Lowry transaction creates its own mirror image. When an acid donates its proton, what remains is a species that could, in the reverse reaction, accept that proton back — a base. The acid and that leftover form a CONJUGATE ACID-BASE PAIR: two species differing by exactly one proton, the acid being the one WITH the proton.

Take HCl plus H2O gives H3O plus, plus Cl minus, and extract the pairs. HCl donated a proton and became Cl minus: first pair, HCl the acid, chloride its conjugate base. Water accepted a proton and became H3O plus: second pair, H3O plus the acid, water its conjugate base. Every acid-base equation contains exactly two such pairs, and finding them is a standard question with a mechanical method: match each species on the left with the species on the right that differs from it by one H — and the partner with the extra H is the acid of that pair.

Run the method on ammonia's equation: NH3 plus H2O in equilibrium with NH4 plus, plus OH minus. Ammonia gained a proton to become ammonium: NH4 plus is the acid, NH3 its conjugate base. Water lost a proton to become hydroxide: H2O is the acid of the second pair, OH minus its conjugate base.

Two traps guard this section. First, a conjugate pair differs by ONE proton only — H2SO4 and SO4 two minus are NOT a conjugate pair, because they differ by two; the pairs are H2SO4 with HSO4 minus, and HSO4 minus with SO4 two minus. Second, the pairs are diagonal, never side-by-side: the two reactants are never a pair, because one is busy donating and the other accepting in the SAME direction. Write the pairs with connecting brackets under the equation once, and the geometry stays learnt.

Take this section's questions now: extract both pairs from any given equation, and police the one-proton rule.

## Subtopic: Ampholytes, and the Three Reactions of Acids

A substance that can act as an acid in one reaction and as a base in another is AMPHIPROTIC, or an AMPHOLYTE. Water is the great example, as the last two sections showed: donor opposite ammonia, acceptor opposite HCl. The other syllabus ampholyte is the hydrogen carbonate ion, HCO3 minus. As an acid: HCO3 minus plus H2O in equilibrium with CO3 two minus, plus H3O plus — it donates. As a base: HCO3 minus plus H2O in equilibrium with H2CO3, plus OH minus — it accepts. Same ion, both directions; which role it plays depends on what it meets. The hydrogen sulphate ion HSO4 minus behaves the same way. The exam skill: given an ampholyte, write BOTH equations, one donating, one accepting.

Now the three reactions of acids, the engine room of the topic. All three follow one master pattern — acid plus base gives salt plus water — with the carbonate version adding a bonus gas.

Reaction one, acid plus metal hydroxide: HCl plus NaOH gives NaCl plus H2O — hydrochloric acid plus sodium hydroxide gives sodium chloride plus water. Reaction two, acid plus metal oxide: 2HCl plus MgO gives MgCl2 plus H2O — two hydrochloric acid plus magnesium oxide gives magnesium chloride plus water; check it balances — two chlorines, two hydrogens, one magnesium, one oxygen on each side. Metal oxides are bases because the oxide ion happily accepts protons.

Reaction three, acid plus metal carbonate: salt plus water plus CARBON DIOXIDE. With sodium carbonate: 2HCl plus Na2CO3 gives 2NaCl plus H2O plus CO2 — two hydrochloric acid plus sodium carbonate gives two sodium chloride, water and carbon dioxide. With calcium carbonate and sulphuric acid: H2SO4 plus CaCO3 gives CaSO4 plus H2O plus CO2. The fizz of carbon dioxide is the recognisable signature — it is the school volcano, the antacid tablet, and the standard laboratory test question.

The questions for this section are ready: both ampholyte equations for HCO3 minus, and all three acid reactions, balanced, with products named.

## Subtopic: Indicators — Making the Chemistry Visible

An acid-base INDICATOR is a substance whose colour depends on whether its surroundings are acidic or basic. The syllabus requires four, with exact colours on each side.

Litmus: RED in acid, BLUE in base. The oldest and bluntest of the four — it announces which side of neutral you are on, and nothing more. Methyl orange: RED in acid, YELLOW in base, passing through orange near its changeover. Bromothymol blue: YELLOW in acid, BLUE in base, and GREEN in a neutral solution — the only one of the four with a distinct neutral colour, which makes it the examiner's choice for questions about pure water or perfectly neutralised mixtures. Phenolphthalein: COLOURLESS in acid, PINK in base — the dramatic one, invisible until the mixture turns basic, then suddenly and unmistakably pink.

Build the memory scaffolding deliberately. Litmus and methyl orange are both red in acid. The two "blue" indicators — litmus and bromothymol blue — are both blue in base. Phenolphthalein is the odd one out entirely, trading colour against colourless.

Why indicators matter beyond memory work: they are the visible evidence of an invisible event. Run a neutralisation — add acid drop by drop to a basic solution carrying phenolphthalein, and the pink holds, holds, holds, then vanishes at the moment the base is used up. Bromothymol blue tracks the same journey blue, then green precisely at neutral, then yellow into excess acid. Titration, the precision version of this experiment, is Grade 12's opening act; recognising the changeover point is this year's skill.

The final questions of this part are with you now: four indicators, eight colours, one green, and the changeover read correctly from a described experiment.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the whole topic again as one playground game: pass the ball, and watch who catches it.

## Subtopic: Acid-Base Is Pass-the-Ball

Strip away the Latin and the topic is one move: a tiny particle — a proton, written H plus — gets passed from one molecule to another. The molecule that GIVES the proton is the acid. The molecule that CATCHES it is the base. That is the entire Brønsted-Lowry theory: donor and catcher, every equation, no exceptions.

Watch one pass. Hydrochloric acid lands in water: HCl throws its proton, water catches, and the catch changes both players' names — water holding an extra proton is now H3O plus, the hydronium ion, and HCl minus its proton is just chloride, Cl minus. Thrower was the acid, catcher was the base. Simple.

Now the pass that fools everyone. Ammonia lands in water — and this time WATER throws. NH3 catches a proton from H2O, becoming NH4 plus, and the water left short a proton becomes hydroxide, OH minus. So ammonia is a base not because it carries hydroxide in its pocket — it carries none — but because it is a good CATCHER. It even manufactures hydroxide at the moment of the catch, from the water it robbed. The old Arrhenius definition, which demanded that bases arrive carrying hydroxide, could never explain that; the pass-the-ball definition explains it in one sentence.

Every acid question this term is asking one of two things: who threw, and who caught. Underline the species that lost an H going left to right — that was your acid. Underline the one that gained an H — your base. Your questions for this section are with you now: watch the ball, not the jerseys.

## Subtopic: Partners Across the Pass, and the Player Who Plays Both Sides

Freeze the game right after a pass, and look at the thrower. He is now standing empty-handed — which means he is perfectly placed to CATCH the ball back if the game reverses. The acid, having thrown, has become a potential catcher: a base. That thrower-and-his-empty-handed-self couple is what chemists call a conjugate acid-base pair: the same player photographed before and after the pass, one proton apart. Whoever holds the proton in the photo is the acid.

Every equation has TWO such couples, one for the thrower and one for the catcher. HCl and Cl minus: with the ball, without the ball. H3O plus and H2O: with, without. The finding trick is pure spot-the-difference: pair each left-side species with the right-side species that differs by exactly one H. Exactly one — a player two protons different, like H2SO4 against SO4 two minus, skipped a photo, and the marker will dock it.

Now meet the most interesting player on the field: the one who can throw OR catch, depending on the opponent. Water is the star example. Facing HCl, a champion thrower, water catches. Facing ammonia, an eager catcher, water throws. Same molecule, opposite roles, chosen by the company it keeps. Chemists call such a player amphiprotic, or an ampholyte, and the hydrogen carbonate ion from baking soda is the other one you must know — put it with an acid and it catches, put it with a base and it throws. The exam question "show that X is an ampholyte" just means: write two short equations, one where X throws, one where X catches.

Your questions for this section are ready. Before-and-after photos for the pairs, and both equations for the double-role player.

## Subtopic: Three Fizzes and Four Colour Changes

What happens when an acid finally meets a proper base head-on? A trade so complete it cancels both players. The acid's protons and the base's hydroxide merge into plain water; the leftover ions settle down as a salt. Sour meets slippery, and out comes salt water — that is neutralisation, and it is why a tablet of base settles an acid stomach.

Acids run this trade with three different partners, and the products follow one pattern. With a metal hydroxide like sodium hydroxide: salt plus water, quietly. With a metal oxide like magnesium oxide: salt plus water again — the oxide ion is a keen proton catcher. With a metal CARBONATE — baking soda, washing soda, the chalk in seashells — the same trade plus a bonus: the carbonate collapses and exhales carbon dioxide gas. That is the fizz. Vinegar on baking soda, acid on a seashell, the school volcano: all one reaction wearing costumes — salt, water, and a gasp of CO2.

But protons are invisible. How do you SEE any of this? You hire a spy: an indicator, a dye that changes colour with the chemistry around it. Litmus: red among acids, blue among bases — the old faithful. Methyl orange: red in acid, yellow in base. Bromothymol blue: yellow in acid, blue in base — and GREEN exactly at neutral, the only spy with a middle colour, so it can tell you the moment a neutralisation completes. And phenolphthalein, the drama queen: invisible in acid, shocking pink in base — add acid to a pink basic solution and the colour cuts out at the exact drop the base runs out.

Pass the ball, photograph the pairs, know the double-role players, expect the fizz from carbonates, and read your spies' colours. That is the whole topic. The last questions of the lesson are yours now — call each reaction by its pattern and the products write themselves.
