**Bianca:** The questions that come up a lot on this topic, and the first one is about the two definitions: if Brønsted-Lowry is better, why do we still learn Arrhenius at all? Can't we just use the good one?

**Tutor:** You learn both because the syllabus examines both, but there is a better reason: seeing WHY a definition failed teaches you what the improved one actually achieves. Arrhenius said acids release hydrogen ions in water and bases release hydroxide ions, and for HCl and NaOH that works perfectly. Then ammonia arrives — plainly basic, turns litmus blue, neutralises acids — with not a single hydroxide in its formula, and Arrhenius has no answer. Brønsted and Lowry shifted attention from what a substance CONTAINS to what it DOES: an acid donates a proton, a base accepts one. Under that definition ammonia's baseness is obvious — it accepts a proton from water, and the robbed water becomes the hydroxide that Arrhenius was looking for. The exam uses this history directly: a favourite question gives you ammonia and asks you to explain why the Arrhenius definition cannot classify it while Brønsted-Lowry can. Answer with the two definitions side by side and the ammonia equation as evidence, and the marks are yours.

***

**Bianca:** Next: conjugate pairs. Learners can recite the definition but still pair the wrong species in an actual equation. What is the foolproof extraction method?

**Tutor:** Treat it as spot-the-difference with one strict rule: a pair is two species, one from each side of the arrow, differing by exactly one H — and the member WITH the extra H is the acid of that pair. Work HCl plus H2O gives H3O plus and Cl minus. Take HCl from the left; scan the right for its one-proton-different partner — Cl minus. That is pair one, HCl the acid. Take H2O; its partner is H3O plus, one proton heavier. Pair two, with H3O plus as the acid. Notice the geometry the errors come from: learners pair the two species standing next to each other on the same side, but the two reactants can never be a pair — in the forward direction one is donating and the other accepting, so they are opponents, not partners. The pairs always connect diagonally across the arrow. And enforce the one-proton rule ruthlessly: H2SO4 and SO4 two minus differ by two protons, so they are related but NOT conjugate; the true pairs run H2SO4 with HSO4 minus, then HSO4 minus with SO4 two minus, one step at a time. Draw the two connecting loops under any equation and check each loop's difference is a single H — that habit is worth every conjugate-pair mark in the paper.

***

**Bianca:** Here's one that seems contradictory: how can water be an acid AND a base? Doesn't it have to be one or the other?

**Tutor:** It has to be one or the other IN A GIVEN REACTION, but it gets to choose fresh each time — and that flexibility is the definition of an ampholyte. Brønsted-Lowry roles are not permanent job titles; they are descriptions of what a substance does in one particular transaction. Water has both a proton it could donate and lone pairs of electrons with which it could catch one, so its role depends on its opponent. Facing a strong donor like HCl, water catches: it becomes H3O plus and acts as the base. Facing a keen catcher like NH3, water donates: it becomes OH minus and acts as the acid. Same molecule, opposite roles, chosen by the company it keeps. The hydrogen carbonate ion works the same way, and the exam phrasing to expect is "show, with the aid of equations, that HCO3 minus is amphiprotic". The answer is always two short equations: HCO3 minus donating — giving CO3 two minus and hydronium — and HCO3 minus accepting — giving H2CO3 and hydroxide. Two equations, two roles, one ion. If you write only one equation you have shown it is an acid or a base, not that it is both, and the question was the both.

**Bianca:** Last one: the three acid reactions all blur together — hydroxide, oxide, carbonate. How do you keep the products straight without memorising three separate lists?

**Tutor:** Compress them into one master pattern plus one exception flag. The master pattern: acid plus base gives salt plus water — always. The salt takes its metal from the base and its surname from the acid; the acid's protons and the base's oxide or hydroxide become the water. Metal hydroxides and metal oxides both follow the pattern with nothing extra: HCl plus NaOH gives sodium chloride and water; two HCl plus MgO gives magnesium chloride and water. The one exception flag is the carbonate: everything above still happens, but the carbonate ion, once it has accepted its protons, is unstable and collapses into water and carbon dioxide — so carbonates deliver the standard salt and water PLUS a fizz of CO2. One pattern, one flag. When you face a question, identify the partner type first: hydroxide or oxide means two products; carbonate means three, and the third is always carbon dioxide. Then balance, remembering diprotic sulphuric acid demands doubled partners. And keep the observable in your pocket for descriptive questions: the carbonate reaction is the only one of the three you can recognise by eye, because it is the only one that fizzes.
