**Bianca:** Let's hit the questions this topic always produces. First, the kelvin rule: Celsius served us perfectly all year — why is it suddenly banned from the gas equations?

**Tutor:** Because the gas equations make claims about RATIOS of temperature, and ratios are meaningless on a scale whose zero is parked in the wrong place. Suppose a rigid container of gas warms from 20 degrees Celsius to 40. Has the pressure doubled? Celsius whispers yes — forty is twice twenty. But molecules are indifferent to where humans pinned their zero; their kinetic energy is measured from the point where motion truly stops, minus 273 degrees Celsius. From that genuine floor the gas went from 293 kelvin to 313 — a rise of under seven percent, and the pressure rises under seven percent, nowhere near doubling. Celsius survives in topics that only ever subtract temperatures, because a DIFFERENCE of ten degrees is identical on both scales. But the instant temperature is multiplied or divided — and in p V over T it is divided — the scale must begin at absolute zero or every ratio is fantasy. Hence the unbreakable habit: add 273 before any temperature touches a gas equation, written as its own line at the top of the answer. That one line protects everything beneath it.

***

**Bianca:** Next, the two equations. General gas equation or ideal gas law — how do you know instantly which one a question wants?

**Tutor:** Count the states described, and the question sorts itself. If one parcel of gas appears in TWO situations — before and after squeezing, dawn cold and midday heat, coast and altitude — that is a two-state comparison, and it belongs to the general gas equation: p1 V1 over T1 equals p2 V2 over T2. Notice you never need the amount of gas; it cancels, because the gas is being compared with itself. But if the question paints ONE state and asks about AMOUNT — moles, mass, number of molecules — only the ideal gas law can answer, p V equals n R T, because n stands explicitly in it. That law is the bridge between this topic and the mole chapter: gauge, jug and thermometer on one side, a molecule count on the other. Two states, general equation; one state plus an amount, ideal gas law. And treat yourself to the consistency check once: feed one mole at STP into p V equals n R T — 101 325 pascals, 273 kelvin — and the volume returns as 0,0224 cubic metres, exactly the 22,4 cubic decimetres the mole chapter gave you. The two chapters shake hands, and having watched it happen once, you trust both.

***

**Bianca:** Now the unit disaster: someone ran p V equals n R T with kilopascals and cubic decimetres and landed a million times off. What's the protection?

**Tutor:** Understand that R dictates the currency. The value 8,31 joules per kelvin per mole is quoted in SI base units — and since a joule is a pascal times a cubic metre, the equation only balances with pressure in pascals, volume in cubic metres, temperature in kelvin. Feed it kilopascals and you are a thousand out one way; cubic decimetres, a thousand out the other. The truly treacherous case is doing both at once, because the two errors cancel and reward the wrong habit — which then detonates the first time only one unit is off. The protection is procedural: three conversion lines before the formula ever appears. Kilopascals times 1 000 into pascals. Cubic decimetres divided by 1 000 into cubic metres. Celsius plus 273 into kelvin. Then substitute. Then hold the answer up against the world: ordinary gas pressures live near 100 000 pascals — one atmosphere. Our worked example returned 109 069 pascals for one and a half moles in forty cubic decimetres at 350 kelvin: believable. An answer claiming your deodorant can holds a hundred gigapascals is announcing its own missing thousand — believe the announcement and go find it.

***

**Bianca:** Last one: the ideal gas disclaimer. We're told real gases aren't ideal, then we use the ideal equations anyway. Why does the disclaimer matter?

**Tutor:** Because the disclaimer is examinable physics, and because knowing where a model fails is half of owning it. The ideal model tells two deliberate lies: molecules occupy no space of their own, and they never attract one another between collisions. In everyday conditions both lies are almost true — molecules genuinely are minute against the emptiness between them, and genuinely too fast for their feeble attractions to catch — so the ideal equations track real gases to within about a percent, and we use them in good conscience. But each lie has its own breaking point, and you must be able to name both. Compress the gas ferociously and the molecules' own bodies stop being negligible beside the vanishing gaps — the high-pressure failure. Chill the gas and the sluggish molecules give the attractions time to seize hold — the low-temperature failure; push on and the gas condenses to liquid, resigning from gas behaviour altogether. Reverse both statements for the companion question: gases behave most ideally at low pressure and high temperature, and the best-behaved gases are the small, weakly-attracting pair, helium and hydrogen. Learn the four sentences as sentences — high pressure, molecular volume matters; low temperature, intermolecular forces matter — and that entire question type is banked.
