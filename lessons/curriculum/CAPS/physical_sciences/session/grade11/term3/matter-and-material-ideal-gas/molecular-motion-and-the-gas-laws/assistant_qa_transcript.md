**Bianca:** The questions that come up a lot on this topic, and the first is the kelvin business: why is Celsius suddenly forbidden? It worked fine all year in every other topic.

**Tutor:** Because the gas equations make PROPORTIONAL claims about temperature, and proportional claims only make sense when the scale starts at true zero. Say a gas at constant volume goes from 10 degrees Celsius to 20. Is its pressure doubling? Celsius tempts you to say yes — twenty is twice ten. But the molecules do not care where humans put the zero of their scale; their kinetic energy is measured from where motion actually stops, at minus 273 degrees Celsius. From that true floor, your gas went from 283 kelvin to 293 — a rise of about three and a half percent, and the pressure rises three and a half percent, not one hundred. Celsius is fine in topics that only ever take temperature DIFFERENCES, because a difference of 10 degrees is the same size on both scales. But the moment temperature is multiplied or divided — and in p V over T it is divided — the scale must start at absolute zero or the ratios are fiction. Hence the iron rule: add 273 before the temperature touches any gas equation. Write the conversion as its own line at the top of your answer and the mark scheme smiles on you twice, once for the conversion and once for everything it saved.

***

**Bianca:** Next: the difference between the general gas equation and the ideal gas law — learners are never sure which one a question wants.

**Tutor:** One test settles it instantly: count the STATES in the question. If the same parcel of gas appears in two situations — before and after compression, cold morning and hot afternoon, sea level and altitude — that is a two-state problem, and it belongs to the general gas equation, p1 V1 over T1 equals p2 V2 over T2. You never need to know how much gas there is, because the amount cancels; you are comparing the gas with itself. But if the question describes ONE state and asks about the AMOUNT — how many moles, what mass, how many molecules — then only the ideal gas law will do, p V equals n R T, because n appears in it explicitly. The ideal gas law is the bridge between this topic and the mole chapter: pressure gauge, thermometer and measuring jug on one side, a count of molecules on the other. Two states, general equation; one state plus an amount, ideal gas law. And a bonus consistency check: put one mole at STP into p V equals n R T — 101 325 pascals, 273 kelvin — and the volume comes out at 0,0224 cubic metres, which is exactly the 22,4 cubic decimetres you have been using all term. The two chapters agree, and seeing that agreement once makes both stick.

***

**Bianca:** Here's the unit trap: a learner used p V equals n R T with kilopascals and cubic decimetres and got an answer a million times out. What is the discipline?

**Tutor:** The discipline is that R sets the currency, and R equals 8,31 joules per kelvin per mole is quoted in the SI base units: pascals for pressure, cubic metres for volume, kelvin for temperature. A joule is a pascal times a cubic metre, so the equation only balances in those units. Use kilopascals and you are a factor of a thousand out; use cubic decimetres and you are a thousand out the other way — and using both at once happens to cancel, which is the most dangerous outcome of all, because it teaches the wrong habit that fails the moment only one unit is off. The safe routine is three conversion lines before the formula: kilopascals times 1 000 into pascals; cubic decimetres divided by 1 000 into cubic metres; Celsius plus 273 into kelvin. Then substitute. Then sanity-check the answer against the world: ordinary gas pressures live around 100 000 pascals — 100 kilopascals, one atmosphere. Our worked example gave 99 720 pascals for two moles in fifty cubic decimetres at room temperature — believable. If your answer says a bicycle tyre holds forty gigapascals, the answer is announcing its own unit error; listen to it and go find the missing thousand.

***

**Bianca:** Last one: why do we bother stating that real gases are not ideal? The whole topic then uses the ideal equations anyway — it feels like a disclaimer nobody acts on.

**Tutor:** It is a disclaimer the examiner acts on, and the physics is worth having. The ideal model makes two deliberate lies: that molecules occupy no volume of their own, and that they exert no forces on each other between collisions. Under everyday conditions both lies are nearly true — molecules really are tiny compared to the space between them, and they really are moving too fast for their weak attractions to grip — so the ideal equations predict real behaviour to within a percent or so, and we use them with a clear conscience. But the exam wants you to know exactly WHEN and WHY the lies fail, and each lie fails under its own condition. Compress the gas hard, and the molecules' own volume stops being negligible next to the shrinking gaps — the high-pressure failure. Chill the gas, and the slow-moving molecules give the attractive forces time to grab — the low-temperature failure, and push it far enough and the gas condenses to liquid, abandoning gas behaviour altogether. Flip both statements for the standard question about when gases behave MOST ideally: low pressure and high temperature, and the closest-to-ideal gases are the small, weakly-attracting ones, helium and hydrogen. Four sentences, learnt as sentences — deviation at high pressure because molecular volume matters, deviation at low temperature because intermolecular forces matter — and that question is banked.
