# Part 1 — Expert

A gas is a crowd of molecules in ceaseless, random motion, and everything a gas does — the pressure in a tyre, the swelling of a hot balloon, the hiss of a deodorant can — follows from that crowd's behaviour. This session builds the kinetic model of gases, defines the ideal gas and its limits, establishes Boyle's law from the practical that CAPS prescribes, and closes with the general gas equation and the ideal gas law, p V equals n R T, the single equation that contains the whole topic.

## Subtopic: Molecular Motion — the Kinetic Model of a Gas

The kinetic model in five statements. A gas consists of particles in continuous, rapid, RANDOM motion. The particles collide with each other and with the container walls, and those collisions are elastic — no kinetic energy is lost in them. The particles are far apart, so a gas is mostly empty space. Forces between the particles are negligible except at the instant of collision. And the average kinetic energy of the particles is directly proportional to the absolute temperature.

Two macroscopic quantities fall straight out of the model. PRESSURE is the combined effect of billions of molecular collisions with the container walls — each impact delivers a tiny push, and the storm of impacts per second, spread over the wall, is the steady pressure a gauge reads. More frequent or harder collisions mean higher pressure. TEMPERATURE measures the average kinetic energy of the particles: heat a gas and its molecules move faster; cool it and they slow.

Note the word AVERAGE. At any instant the molecules of a gas have a whole DISTRIBUTION of speeds — some dawdling, some tearing along, most bunched near the middle — because random collisions constantly rob speed from one molecule and award it to another. Raising the temperature shifts the whole distribution toward higher speeds; it does not give every molecule the same speed. When this topic says "the average speed of the molecules increases", that spread is what the word average is respecting.

Temperature has one more requirement: the KELVIN scale. Since temperature measures kinetic energy, its zero must sit where molecular motion would cease — absolute zero, minus 273 degrees Celsius. Convert always: temperature in kelvin equals temperature in Celsius plus 273. Room temperature, 25 degrees Celsius, is 298 kelvin. Every gas equation in this topic eats kelvin and nothing else.

The questions for this section are with you now: the statements of the model, pressure as collisions, temperature as average kinetic energy, and the Celsius-to-kelvin conversion.

## Subtopic: Ideal Gases and Real Gases

The kinetic model as stated describes an IDEAL gas — a theoretical gas whose particles have zero volume of their own and exert zero forces on each other between collisions. No real gas is truly ideal, but most real gases come close under everyday conditions, which is why the model earns its keep.

When does a real gas deviate? Under exactly two conditions, and the reasons are the two idealisations failing. At HIGH PRESSURE, the molecules are squeezed close together, and the volume of the molecules themselves stops being negligible next to the shrinking space between them — the "zero volume" assumption breaks. At LOW TEMPERATURE, the molecules move slowly, and the weak attractive forces between them get time to act — molecules tug on each other, collisions soften, and the "no forces" assumption breaks. Push both conditions far enough and the gas condenses into a liquid, the ultimate failure of gas behaviour.

Flip the logic for the conditions of most ideal behaviour: LOW pressure and HIGH temperature — molecules far apart, moving too fast for attractions to matter. And among the gases themselves, the closest to ideal are the small, light ones with the feeblest intermolecular forces: helium and hydrogen. A big, easily-liquefied molecule like ammonia or carbon dioxide strays furthest.

The exam asks this section in words, so rehearse the sentences: real gases deviate from ideal behaviour at high pressure and low temperature; at high pressure the molecular volume becomes significant; at low temperature the intermolecular forces become significant; ideal behaviour is approached at low pressure and high temperature. Take this section's questions now.

## Subtopic: Boyle's Law from the Practical

The prescribed practical: a fixed amount of gas trapped in a sealed syringe or tube, temperature held constant, pressure read from a gauge as the volume is changed step by step. A typical results table: at 100 kilopascals, 2,0 cubic decimetres; at 125 kilopascals, 1,6; at 200 kilopascals, 1,0; at 400 kilopascals, 0,5.

Interrogate the table. Multiply each pressure by its volume: 100 times 2,0 is 200; 125 times 1,6 is 200; 200 times 1,0 is 200; 400 times 0,5 is 200. The product is CONSTANT. Halve the volume and the pressure doubles; quarter the volume and it quadruples. Pressure is INVERSELY proportional to volume.

That is Boyle's law: the pressure of a fixed amount of gas at constant temperature is inversely proportional to the volume. In symbols, p V equals k, a constant — and therefore p1 V1 equals p2 V2 between any two states. The kinetic model explains it in one breath: squeeze the same molecules into half the space and each molecule hits the walls twice as often, so the collision rate — the pressure — doubles.

The graphs, because CAPS asks for all three. Pressure against volume: a falling curve, a hyperbola, never touching either axis. Pressure against 1 OVER volume: a STRAIGHT line through the origin — the standard test of inverse proportionality, and the graph you draw to prove Boyle's law from data. And p V against p: a horizontal line, the constant product on display.

A calculation, exam style. A sample of gas at 100 kilopascals occupies 2 cubic decimetres; what is its pressure when squeezed to 0,5 cubic decimetres, temperature constant? Formula: p1 V1 equals p2 V2. Substitution: 100 times 2 equals p2 times 0,5. Answer: p2 equals 400 kilopascals. State the condition every time — constant temperature, fixed amount of gas — because the law holds only there.

The questions for this section are ready: the constant product in a table, the three graphs, the molecular explanation, and the two-state calculation.

## Subtopic: The General Gas Equation and the Ideal Gas Law

Boyle held temperature constant. Free it, and experiment delivers two more proportionalities: at constant pressure, volume is directly proportional to kelvin temperature; at constant volume, pressure is directly proportional to kelvin temperature. Hot molecules hit harder and more often — the model again. Combine all three relationships and one compact statement emerges, the GENERAL GAS EQUATION: p1 V1 over T1 equals p2 V2 over T2, for a fixed amount of gas, with temperature in kelvin, always.

Worked, in one pass. A gas occupies 3 cubic decimetres at 27 degrees Celsius and 100 kilopascals. What volume at 127 degrees Celsius and 150 kilopascals? Convert first: 27 plus 273 is 300 kelvin; 127 plus 273 is 400 kelvin. Formula: p1 V1 over T1 equals p2 V2 over T2. Substitution: 100 times 3 over 300 equals 150 times V2 over 400. Left side: 1. So 150 V2 equals 400, and V2 equals 2,67 cubic decimetres. Notice Celsius would have given nonsense — the conversion is not optional.

One step remains: pin down the constant itself. For one mole of gas, p V over T is the same number for ALL gases — the universal gas constant R, 8,31 joules per kelvin per mole. For n moles the constant is n R, and the result is the IDEAL GAS LAW: p V equals n R T — pressure times volume equals number of moles times R times kelvin temperature. This equation joins the mole work of this term to the gas laws: it counts molecules with a pressure gauge and a thermometer.

Units, strictly: with R in joules per kelvin per mole, pressure must be in PASCALS — not kilopascals — volume in CUBIC METRES, temperature in kelvin. One cubic metre is 1 000 cubic decimetres. Worked: what pressure does 2 moles of gas exert at 300 kelvin in a 50 cubic decimetre vessel? Convert: 50 cubic decimetres is 0,05 cubic metres. Formula: p equals n R T over V. Substitution: 2 times 8,31 times 300, divided by 0,05. Numerator: 4 986. Answer: 99 720 pascals, which is 99,7 kilopascals — almost exactly atmospheric pressure. A number that size is your sanity check: everyday gas pressures live near 100 kilopascals.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the general gas equation with kelvin discipline, and the ideal gas law with its pascals-and-cubic-metres discipline.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same physics from a taxi rank pumping tyres and shaking cans, where your hands already know every law in this lesson.

## Subtopic: A Room Full of Super Balls

Picture a room where someone has flung ten thousand bouncy balls in every direction at once, and nothing ever slows them down. They ricochet off the walls, off the floor, off each other, forever. That is a gas — except the balls are molecules, there are trillions of trillions of them, and the room might be a cooldrink bottle.

Now stand inside that room. The balls hammer you from all sides, thousands per second — not one big shove but an endless drumroll of tiny taps. That drumroll is PRESSURE. A pressure gauge is just counting the drumroll on the container wall. More balls, or faster balls, or a smaller room: louder drumroll, higher pressure.

What makes the balls faster? Heat. TEMPERATURE is nothing but the average liveliness of the balls — warm the room and the whole crowd speeds up; chill it and the crowd goes sluggish. Average matters here: at any moment some balls are creeping and some are rocketing, the way a taxi rank has both grannies and sprinting schoolkids, but heating shifts the whole crowd faster. And if you could chill the room until every ball stopped dead? That is absolute zero, minus 273 Celsius — the floor of all temperature. Scientists measure from that floor and call it the kelvin scale: your comfortable 25 degrees is 298 kelvin. Every gas formula demands kelvin, because only kelvin starts counting where motion starts.

One honest confession about the model: it pretends the balls take up no space and never grab at each other, which is nearly true when they are far apart and flying fast — but squash them together or chill them sluggish, and the pretence fails; squeeze hard enough and the crowd collapses into a liquid. Nearly-true has a name: the ideal gas. Your questions for this section are with you now.

## Subtopic: Squeeze the Bottle — Boyle in Your Hands

Grab an empty plastic cooldrink bottle, cap it tight, and squeeze. You feel it fight back — and the harder you squash it, the harder it fights. You have just run the Grade 11 prescribed practical with your fist.

Here is what your hand discovered. Same number of balls inside, same temperature — but you shrank the room. Same crowd, half the space: every ball reaches a wall twice as often, the drumroll doubles, the push-back doubles. Half the volume, double the pressure. A third of the volume, triple the pressure. Volume down, pressure up, in exact proportion — that is Boyle's law, and your bicycle pump repeats it every afternoon: push the plunger halfway and the second half of the stroke fights you twice as hard.

The arithmetic is one sentence: pressure times volume stays the same. So exam questions are a see-saw: 100 kilopascals at 2 cubic decimetres becomes what at half a cubic decimetre? The product must stay 200, so the pressure must rise to 400. Old pressure times old volume equals new pressure times new volume — one line, every time. Just check the fine print first: same amount of gas, same temperature, or the see-saw is broken.

And the graph they love to ask: plot pressure against volume and you get a slide — steep at first, flattening out, never touching the floor or the wall. Plot pressure against ONE OVER volume and the slide straightens into a ruler-line through the origin. A straight line through the origin is science's signature for "inversely proportional" — that graph is the proof.

Your questions for this section are ready. Squeeze the bottle in your head: room shrinks, drumroll doubles, product stays constant.

## Subtopic: One Equation for Every Balloon

Leave a capped bottle in a car on a January afternoon in Durban and it swells drum-tight. Nothing was added — the sun just made the balls inside faster, and faster balls drum harder. Pressure rises with temperature. Free the container to stretch, like a balloon, and heat swells the VOLUME instead. Warmth pushes on both dials.

So gas behaviour has three dials — pressure, volume, temperature — and they move together. Physics ties all three in one line: pressure times volume, divided by kelvin temperature, stays constant for a fixed amount of gas. Old state on the left, new state on the right, solve for the missing dial. The only tollgate: temperature must pass through in kelvin — add 273 to every Celsius reading before it touches the equation, because a gas at 20 degrees is not "twice as hot" as one at 10; measured from the true floor, 293 against 283, it barely differs.

Then, the master stroke. Ask HOW MUCH gas sets the constant, and the answer turns out breathtakingly tidy: for every mole of gas — every standard box of 6,02 times 10 to the 23 molecules — the constant is the same universal number, R, 8,31 in scientific units. The result is the ideal gas law: p V equals n R T. Four dials now — pressure, volume, moles, temperature — and knowing any three reveals the fourth. It means you can COUNT invisible molecules with nothing but a pressure gauge, a measuring jug and a thermometer: the drumroll on the walls betrays how many drummers there are.

That is the whole topic in one object: a crowd of tireless bouncy balls, a drumroll called pressure, a liveliness called temperature, a see-saw called Boyle, and one equation that reads the crowd's headcount off its noise. The last questions of the lesson are yours now — convert to kelvin first, then let the equation do the lifting.
