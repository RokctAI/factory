**Bianca:** Let's take the questions this lesson always raises. First, the protest I hear every year: ethanoic acid shows FOUR hydrogens in CH3COOH, while sulphuric acid shows two. Shouldn't vinegar be the terrifying one?

**Tutor:** The headcount deceives, because an acid is judged only on the hydrogens it will actually RELEASE — and ethanoic acid releases exactly one of its four. The three hydrogens inside the CH3 group are bonded straight onto carbon, and carbon-hydrogen bonds simply do not break in water; those three are permanent passengers in every reaction the molecule ever joins. The single acidic hydrogen is the one written last, bonded to oxygen — and that is why the formula is deliberately written CH3COOH rather than in some tidier order: the arrangement flags the active hydrogen for you. Hence ethanoic acid is monoprotic, while sulphuric acid, whose two hydrogens both sit on oxygens, is genuinely diprotic. There is a second, independent reason vinegar is tame: ethanoic acid is the syllabus's standard WEAK acid — even its one available hydrogen is surrendered reluctantly and incompletely in water. So count the offered hydrogens, not the printed ones, and judge fierceness by willingness to donate, never by the crowd in the formula.

***

**Bianca:** Next up, ammonia. NH3 has no hydroxide, no oxygen, no metal — people flatly refuse to call it a base. What's the case for the defence?

**Tutor:** The defence rests on behaviour, not on contents — which is exactly the direction the whole topic is moving. The first working definition of a base you met was a substance that supplies hydroxide ions in water, and NH3 appears to fail on inspection. But follow one ammonia molecule into solution: it plucks a hydrogen ion off a neighbouring water molecule, turning itself into ammonium, NH4 plus, and leaving that water short one proton — as a hydroxide ion, OH minus. The solution genuinely fills with hydroxide; ammonia built it from water instead of importing it. The deeper definition, formalised in the acid-base definitions lesson, says a base is anything that ACCEPTS a hydrogen ion — and ammonia's nitrogen, with its lone pair of electrons, is a superb catcher. Assessors return to ammonia again and again precisely because it separates formula-gazers from understanders: it is the standard demonstration that base-ness lives in proton-accepting behaviour, not in the letters O and H appearing side by side.

***

**Bianca:** Now the pair that swaps in everyone's head at the worst moment: Na2CO3 versus NaHCO3. What's the fix that survives exam nerves?

**Tutor:** Refuse to memorise it — derive it, because a five-second derivation cannot be scrambled by adrenaline. Both compounds are built on the carbonate ion, CO3 carrying two minus. Take carbonate whole, with its full double negative, and reaching zero requires two single-plus sodium ions: Na2CO3 — sodium carbonate, the washing soda. Alternatively, let the carbonate first catch one hydrogen ion, becoming hydrogen carbonate, HCO3 minus: that hydrogen has already paid off one of the two negative charges, so a single sodium finishes the job: NaHCO3 — sodium hydrogen carbonate, the baking soda. The rule then states itself: the hydrogen and the second sodium are substitutes for each other — the ion carries a hydrogen or it takes a second sodium, never both and never neither. The name backs you up: "hydrogen carbonate" declares its hydrogen aboard, so one sodium suffices. When pressure spikes, scribble the charge sum in the margin — two minus needs two plus — and the pair can never trade places again.

***

**Bianca:** Last one: the backwards question — "which acid and base prepare such-and-such a salt" — trips up people who know all nine formulae cold. What's the reliable routine?

**Tutor:** Treat the salt's name as two independent clues and answer them separately. Clue one, the first word: the metal, which can only descend from the base — sodium in the salt points to sodium hydroxide, potassium to potassium hydroxide. Clue two, the second word: the acid's family surname, and each surname has a unique parent — chloride from hydrochloric acid, nitrate from nitric, sulphate from sulphuric, ethanoate from ethanoic. Sodium sulphate therefore decodes as sodium hydroxide plus sulphuric acid; potassium ethanoate as potassium hydroxide plus ethanoic acid. Nothing is recalled beyond the four surnames — everything else is read off the name in front of you. The characteristic failure is trying to remember a matching reaction seen once in class; recall collapses on unfamiliar salts, decoding never does. And when the question also wants the equation: write acid plus base gives salt plus water, then balance — keeping in mind that sulphuric acid's two hydrogens demand two hydroxides, so preparing potassium sulphate takes a 2 before the KOH and a 2 before the water.
