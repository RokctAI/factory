**Bianca:** The questions that come up a lot on this one, starting with the complaint I hear most: ethanoic acid's formula, CH3COOH, has FOUR hydrogens. Why is it not a stronger acid than sulphuric with its two? More hydrogens should mean more acid, surely.

**Tutor:** Only the hydrogens an acid is willing to RELEASE count, and ethanoic acid releases exactly one of its four. The three hydrogens in the CH3 group are bonded directly to carbon, and carbon-hydrogen bonds do not break in water — those three are spectators, riding along in every reaction the acid ever has. The only acidic hydrogen is the one written at the very end, attached to an oxygen, and that is why chemists deliberately write the formula as CH3COOH instead of alphabetically: the structure of the name shows you which hydrogen is the active one. So ethanoic acid is monoprotic — one proton on offer — while sulphuric acid, H2SO4, is diprotic, with both of its hydrogens attached to oxygens and both available. And there is a second, separate reason ethanoic acid is the gentle one: it is a WEAK acid, meaning even its one available hydrogen is released reluctantly and incompletely in water. Count the offered hydrogens, not the printed ones, and judge strength by willingness, not by headcount.

***

**Bianca:** Next: ammonia. Learners look at NH3 and refuse to believe it is a base — no hydroxide, no metal, no oxygen. How does it qualify?

**Tutor:** It qualifies by what it DOES, not by what it contains — and that distinction is precisely where the syllabus is heading. The first definition of a base you met was a substance that gives hydroxide ions in water, and ammonia seems to fail that test on sight. But watch its behaviour when it dissolves: an ammonia molecule pulls a hydrogen ion off a water molecule, becoming ammonium, NH4 plus, and the mugged water molecule is left as a hydroxide ion, OH minus. So a solution of ammonia genuinely is rich in hydroxide — ammonia manufactured the hydroxide out of the water rather than carrying it in. The deeper definition, which the acids and bases lesson formalises, says a base is any substance that ACCEPTS a hydrogen ion, and ammonia does exactly that, using the lone pair of electrons on its nitrogen as the catching glove. Examiners love ammonia precisely because it punishes formula-gazing and rewards understanding: it is the standard example that bases are defined by proton-accepting behaviour, not by hydroxide in the formula.

**Bianca:** Here's the pair that swaps in everyone's head: sodium carbonate and sodium hydrogen carbonate. Two sodiums in one, one in the other — which is which, and how do you keep them straight under pressure?

**Tutor:** Do not memorise it — derive it, because the derivation takes five seconds and cannot be shuffled by nerves. Both compounds are built around the carbonate ion, CO3 with a two minus charge. Pure carbonate, with its full double negative, needs two single-positive sodium ions to reach zero: Na2CO3, sodium carbonate, the washing soda. But carbonate can also pick up one hydrogen ion first, becoming hydrogen carbonate, HCO3 — and that hydrogen has already neutralised one of the two negative charges, leaving only a single minus. A single minus needs only a single sodium: NaHCO3, sodium hydrogen carbonate, the baking soda. So the rule writes itself: the hydrogen and the second sodium are ALTERNATIVES — the ion either carries a hydrogen or needs a second sodium, never both, never neither. The name is honest too: "hydrogen carbonate" announces the hydrogen aboard, so the sodium count must be one. Under exam pressure, sketch the charge arithmetic in the margin — two minus needs two pluses — and the pair can never swap again.

**Bianca:** Last one: the backwards question — "which acid and base would you use to prepare such-and-such a salt" — keeps catching people who know all nine formulae perfectly. What is the routine?

**Tutor:** The routine is to read the salt's name as two separate clues and answer each clue on its own. The first word of the salt names the metal, and the metal can only have come from the base — so match it: sodium in the salt means sodium hydroxide, potassium means potassium hydroxide. The second word is the acid's family name, and each family has exactly one parent: chloride points to hydrochloric acid, nitrate to nitric acid, sulphate to sulphuric acid, ethanoate to ethanoic acid. So potassium sulphate reads apart as potassium hydroxide plus sulphuric acid, and sodium ethanoate as sodium hydroxide plus ethanoic acid — the answer is assembled from the name, with no memory involved beyond the four family surnames. Learners who go wrong here almost always try to recall a matching reaction they once saw instead of decoding the name in front of them, and recall fails on unfamiliar salts while decoding never does. Then, if the question wants the equation as well, write acid plus base gives salt plus water, and balance — remembering that sulphuric acid's two hydrogens need two hydroxides, so the sodium sulphate equation takes a 2 in front of the NaOH and a 2 in front of the water.
