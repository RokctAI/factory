Hey, it's Bianca — nine formulae to memorise sounds like a vocabulary test, and vocabulary tests before an exam make everyone's mind go blank. So let's not memorise. Let's build, because everything in this lesson can be rebuilt from two small facts even when your memory is hiding under the desk.

Fact one: the pieces. Positives — hydrogen, sodium, potassium, each one plus. Negatives — chloride, nitrate, hydroxide, hydrogen carbonate, each one minus; and the two heavyweights, sulphate and carbonate, each two minus.

Fact two: charges must cancel to zero. That is the whole construction kit. One plus meets one minus: HCl, HNO3, NaOH, KOH, NaHCO3 — five formulae without memorising anything. A two-minus piece needs TWO one-plus pieces: H2SO4, Na2CO3. That little subscript 2 that people forget under pressure? It is not decoration — it is the second positive that the double charge demanded. If you remember WHY it is there, you cannot leave it out.

Ammonia is the one oddball: NH3, a base with no hydroxide in sight. Give it its own sentence in your head — the base that steals hydrogen from water — and it stops being scary and starts being the question you hope for.

For the household names, walk your own house: vinegar and baking soda in the kitchen, washing soda in the laundry, caustic soda under the sink, ammonia in the window cleaner, pool acid and battery acid in the garage. You have met all nine. The exam is introductions, not strangers.

And when the salt questions come, read the salt like a compound word: first name from the base, surname from the acid. Potassium sulphate: potassium hydroxide, sulphuric acid. Decoded, not recalled.

Build from charges, walk the house, read the surnames. Three small skills, and every question in this lesson opens to one of them. You've got this!
