# Part 1 — Expert

Nine substances carry the whole acid-base chapter: four acids and five bases that CAPS names explicitly, whose names and formulae must be produced from memory, instantly and exactly. This session fixes all nine, shows how each formula is built from its ions so that memory has logic underneath it, and closes with the naming system that links every acid to the family of salts it produces — the connection the rest of the term is built on.

## Subtopic: The Four Acids You Must Know

Acid one: hydrochloric acid, HCl — one hydrogen bonded to one chlorine. In water it releases one hydrogen ion per molecule. It is the acid in your stomach, the laboratory's workhorse, and, diluted, the pool acid sold in hardware stores.

Acid two: nitric acid, HNO3 — one hydrogen, one nitrogen, three oxygens. Speak it as H N O three. One hydrogen ion per molecule again. Industrially it is the acid of fertiliser manufacture.

Acid three: sulphuric acid, H2SO4 — TWO hydrogens, one sulphur, four oxygens. Speak it as H two S O four. This is the acid in car batteries and the most-produced industrial chemical on Earth. Its two hydrogens matter: it can release two hydrogen ions per molecule, and chemists call such an acid DIPROTIC, against MONOPROTIC for the one-hydrogen acids.

Acid four: ethanoic acid, CH3COOH, also called acetic acid — the acid of vinegar. Speak it as C H three C O O H. Its formula looks alarming: four hydrogens in total, yet only ONE of them is acidic — the one written at the end, attached to the oxygen. The three in the CH3 group stay put. Ethanoic acid is monoprotic despite its crowd of hydrogens, and it is also the syllabus's standard example of a WEAK acid, one that releases its hydrogen reluctantly and incompletely — which is why you can put it on your chips.

The memory anchors: stomach and pool for HCl, fertiliser for HNO3, battery for H2SO4, vinegar for CH3COOH. The questions for this section are with you now — name to formula, formula to name, and monoprotic against diprotic.

## Subtopic: The Five Bases You Must Know

Base one: sodium hydroxide, NaOH — sodium, oxygen, hydrogen. Household name: caustic soda, the aggressive ingredient in drain cleaners. Base two is its twin, potassium hydroxide, KOH — swap the sodium for potassium and nothing else changes. Both are strong bases, dissolving in water to give hydroxide ions, OH minus.

Base three: ammonia, NH3 — one nitrogen, three hydrogens. Speak it as N H three. The odd one out, and examiners love it for that: it contains NO hydroxide, NO oxygen, NO metal — yet it is a base, because dissolved in water it accepts hydrogen ions. It is the sharp smell in many household cleaners.

Base four: sodium carbonate, Na2CO3 — two sodiums, one carbon, three oxygens. Speak it as N A two C O three. Household name: washing soda. Base five: sodium hydrogen carbonate, NaHCO3 — sodium, hydrogen, carbon, three oxygens. Speak it as N A H C O three. Household name: baking soda, or bicarbonate of soda — the fizz in your koeksister batter and the powder that settles a sour stomach.

Watch the pair at the end, because they are the classic confusion: sodium carbonate has TWO sodiums and no hydrogen; sodium hydrogen carbonate has ONE sodium and one hydrogen. The names say it exactly — "hydrogen carbonate" announces the hydrogen — and the formulae follow from charge balancing, which the next section makes automatic.

The full list, once more, spoken cleanly: NaOH, KOH, NH3, Na2CO3, NaHCO3. Five bases, four of them white solids, one a pungent gas dissolved in water. This section's questions are ready: names, formulae, household identities, and the carbonate pair kept straight.

## Subtopic: Building the Formulae from Their Ions

Memory fades under exam pressure; construction does not. Every one of these formulae can be BUILT from two facts: what ions are involved, and that a compound's charges must total zero.

The ions to hold: hydrogen H plus, sodium Na plus, potassium K plus. Then the negatives: chloride Cl minus, nitrate NO3 minus, hydroxide OH minus, all carrying single minus charges; and sulphate SO4 two minus and carbonate CO3 two minus, carrying double minus charges. Hydrogen carbonate, HCO3 minus, is carbonate that has already picked up one hydrogen, leaving a single minus.

Now build. Hydrochloric acid: H plus meets Cl minus — one each balances, HCl. Nitric acid: H plus meets NO3 minus — one each, HNO3. Sulphuric acid: H plus meets SO4 two minus — the double minus needs TWO hydrogens, H2SO4. The subscript 2 is not decoration; it is the charge balance made visible.

Sodium hydroxide: Na plus meets OH minus — NaOH. Sodium carbonate: Na plus meets CO3 two minus — two sodiums needed, Na2CO3. Sodium hydrogen carbonate: Na plus meets HCO3 minus — one each, NaHCO3. There is the carbonate pair explained rather than memorised: carbonate's double charge demands two sodiums; hydrogen carbonate's single charge takes one.

The classic wrong answers now diagnose themselves. NaCO3 fails because a single sodium leaves a minus one unbalanced. H2NO3 fails because nitrate only carries a single minus and needs a single hydrogen. HSO4 as a formula for the acid fails because the sulphate's two minus is only half-neutralised. Check every formula you ever write with one question — do the charges total zero? — and this family of errors disappears.

The questions for this section are in front of you: ions to formula, formula back to ions, and the charge audit applied to spot the fakes.

## Subtopic: Acid Plus Base — the Salt Takes the Acid's Surname

Put an acid and a base together and they NEUTRALISE each other: acid plus base gives a salt plus water. The salt is an ionic compound assembled at the meeting: its positive ion comes from the BASE, its negative ion comes from the ACID. This single sentence organises the entire rest of the term.

The naming follows the acid, like a surname. Hydrochloric acid fathers CHLORIDES. Nitric acid fathers NITRATES. Sulphuric acid fathers SULPHATES. Ethanoic acid fathers ETHANOATES. Run it forwards: sodium hydroxide plus hydrochloric acid — NaOH plus HCl gives NaCl plus H2O, sodium hydroxide plus hydrochloric acid gives sodium chloride plus water. The sodium came from the base, the chloride from the acid, and the leftover H and OH became the water.

Run it with the other partners. Potassium hydroxide plus nitric acid: KOH plus HNO3 gives KNO3 plus H2O — potassium nitrate plus water. Sodium hydroxide plus sulphuric acid: 2NaOH plus H2SO4 gives Na2SO4 plus 2H2O — two sodium hydroxide plus sulphuric acid gives sodium sulphate plus two water; the diprotic acid needs two hydroxides to strip both its hydrogens, and the balancing shows it.

Now run it BACKWARDS, because that is the exam's favourite direction: which acid and which base would prepare potassium sulphate? Read the salt apart: potassium from a potassium base — potassium hydroxide; sulphate from sulphuric acid. Which pair gives sodium ethanoate? Sodium hydroxide and ethanoic acid. The salt is a compound word, and each half names its parent.

This is deliberately a first look — the full machinery of neutralisation reactions, and of acids meeting oxides and carbonates, is the work of the coming lessons. What must be automatic TODAY is the reading of a salt's two names. The final questions of this part are with you now: forwards from parents to salt, and backwards from salt to parents.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same nine characters met where you actually know them: your kitchen, your bathroom and the garage.

## Subtopic: A Tour of the House in Nine Chemicals

Start at the stove. The vinegar on the shelf is ethanoic acid, CH3COOH — the acid mild enough to eat, because it is weak and lazy about releasing its hydrogen. Next to it, the baking soda: sodium hydrogen carbonate, NaHCO3, the gentle base that raises your vetkoek and calms a burning stomach. The two of them fizzing together in a school-project volcano is an acid meeting a base — you have been doing this chapter since primary school.

Move to the laundry: washing soda, sodium carbonate, Na2CO3 — baking soda's tougher sibling, two sodiums instead of one. Under the sink, the drain cleaner: sodium hydroxide, NaOH, caustic soda, the base strong enough to dissolve the hair clog and your skin with it, which is why the bottle shouts warnings. The window cleaner with the sharp smell: ammonia, NH3, the base that sneaks into the club with no hydroxide in its formula at all.

Now the garage. The pool acid in the white bottle: hydrochloric acid, HCl — the same acid your own stomach brews for digestion. The car battery: sulphuric acid, H2SO4, carrying two acidic hydrogens where the others carry one. And on the farm supply shelf, the fertiliser sacks trace back to nitric acid, HNO3.

Nine chemicals, and you own most of them already. The exam simply asks you to greet each one by BOTH names — the household name and the formula — without hesitation. The questions for this section are with you now: match the shelf to the symbol.

## Subtopic: Charges Snap Together Like Magnets

Stop memorising the formulae; build them. Every acid and base here is made of two pieces carrying opposite charges, and the pieces snap together until the total charge is zero — like magnets that refuse to rest until every pole is paired.

The positive pieces each carry one plus: hydrogen, sodium, potassium. Most of the negative pieces carry one minus: chloride, nitrate, hydroxide, hydrogen carbonate. Those pair one-to-one, click: HCl, HNO3, NaOH, KOH, NaHCO3. Done — five formulae for free.

Two negative pieces are double-strength, two minus each: sulphate and carbonate. A double magnet needs TWO single magnets to settle, so sulphuric acid must carry two hydrogens — H2SO4 — and sodium carbonate must carry two sodiums — Na2CO3. That is the entire mystery of the little subscript 2: it is the second magnet the double charge demanded.

And now you can smell a wrong formula from across the room. NaCO3? A double-minus carbonate held by a single sodium — one pole left unpaired, impossible. H2NO3? Two hydrogens crowded onto a single-minus nitrate — one magnet too many. Run the zero-check on anything you write: pluses and minuses must cancel exactly. It takes two seconds and catches the exact errors examiners bait their questions with.

Your questions for this section are ready. Build, don't recall: pieces, charges, snap to zero.

## Subtopic: Reading a Salt Like a Compound Word

Every salt's name is two words, and each word tells you where it came from — like a child carrying a first name from one parent and a surname from the other. First word: the metal, straight from the BASE. Second word: the acid's family name. Chlorides come from hydrochloric acid, nitrates from nitric, sulphates from sulphuric, ethanoates from ethanoic.

So read salts like sentences. Sodium chloride — table salt — announces its parents: a sodium base and hydrochloric acid. Potassium nitrate, the old gunpowder salt: potassium hydroxide and nitric acid. Sodium ethanoate: sodium hydroxide and vinegar's acid. The naming never lies, and it works in both directions.

The exam's favourite trick is the backwards direction, dressed as a task: "Which acid and which base would you choose to prepare potassium sulphate?" Do not reach for memory — read the name apart. Potassium: use the potassium base, KOH. Sulphate: the surname of sulphuric acid, H2SO4. Answer assembled from the name itself.

What happens to the rest of the molecules when acid and base meet? The acid's hydrogen and the base's hydroxide pair off quietly into water — H plus OH becoming H2O — which is why the products of a neutralisation are always the salt AND water. The fizz in the school volcano is a bonus product that only carbonate bases produce, and the full story of that fizz belongs to the next lessons.

For today, the skill is the reading. First name from the base, surname from the acid, water on the side. The last questions of the lesson are yours now — take each salt apart like a compound word and the answers assemble themselves.
