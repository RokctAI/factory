**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! The first one is the question everyone is too shy to ask out loud: if gravity pulls the ball down the entire time, what is holding it up while it rises?

**Tutor:** Nothing holds it up — and discovering that nothing is required is the whole insight. This is Newton's first law dressed up as a projectile problem. While your hand held the ball, your hand gave it velocity. The instant it leaves, the hand's force vanishes entirely; there is no stored "force of the throw" riding along with the ball, even though almost every human being privately assumes there is. The ball keeps rising simply because it HAS upward velocity and nothing has yet removed all of it. Gravity's job is the removing: 9,8 metres per second of upward speed deducted every second, which is why the ball slows, pauses for an instant, and then gains downward speed at exactly the same rate. The correct free-body diagram of a rising ball therefore shows one arrow only — the gravitational force, pointing down — and nothing else. Adding an upward "throw force" arrow to a ball in flight is among the most consistently penalised sketches in the subject. If you want one sentence to carry into your exams: motion needs no force to continue; motion needs a force to CHANGE, and gravity is the only changer on duty.

***

**Mandy:** Next: choosing the positive direction. When do we pick downward and when upward, and can the choice change the final answer? People lose everything by swapping signs halfway.

**Tutor:** The choice is entirely yours, the physical answer is identical either way, and the losses come only from changing conventions mid-solution — so the discipline is: choose once, write it down, obey it to the last line. As a practical habit, point the positive axis along the bulk of the motion. Dropped or thrown downward: take downward positive, and both the initial velocity and the acceleration come out positive — friendly arithmetic. Thrown upward: take upward positive, and the initial velocity is positive while the acceleration is negative 9,8 for the entire flight, including the descent. What you may never do is flip the acceleration's sign when the ball turns at the top — the acceleration never turned; only the velocity's sign changes as the motion reverses. That is also the deep reason a single equation can span the whole up-and-down journey: with one fixed convention, a landing point below the launch is simply a negative displacement, and the equation absorbs it without you slicing the flight into pieces. And when an answer emerges negative, do not panic and do not delete the sign — translate it. Negative velocity with upward positive means moving downward; negative displacement means finishing below the start. The sign is information the mathematics is handing you at no charge.

***

**Mandy:** Here is the graph puzzle: how can the position-time graph be a curve when the velocity-time graph is a straight line? Those two pictures look like they describe different journeys.

**Tutor:** They describe one journey, and each picture forces the other's shape — the bridge is the gradient. The gradient of the position-time graph at any instant IS the velocity at that instant. Now read the straight, sloping velocity line of a projectile: the velocity is different at every moment, changing by the same amount each second. If the velocity differs at every moment, the steepness of the position graph must differ at every moment — and a graph whose steepness changes continuously is, by definition, a curve. A straight position-time line would mean one constant gradient, meaning constant velocity, which the sloping velocity graph flatly denies. So the curved position graph and the straight velocity graph are two portraits of the same motion that can only exist together. Push the chain one link further and it closes: the gradient of the velocity-time graph is the acceleration, and since that line is straight, its gradient is a single constant — 9,8 downward — which is precisely the flat, boring acceleration graph. Curve, straight slope, flat line: position, velocity, acceleration, each graph the gradient-story of the one before. Checking your three sketches against that chain takes thirty seconds and finds your errors before anyone else can.

***

**Mandy:** Last one: bridge questions — a stone thrown up from a bridge that then falls past the bridge into the gorge. Can we still use time up equals time down and same speed up as down, or is that cheating?

**Tutor:** You may use the symmetry precisely as far as it holds, and knowing its boundary is what the question is really testing. Symmetry — equal times, equal speeds at equal heights — is a mirror hung at the top of the flight, and mirrors only pair points at the SAME height. For a stone thrown up at 29,4 metres per second from the bridge railing: from the hand to the peak and back down to hand height, the mirror is perfect — 3 seconds up, 3 seconds down, passing the railing again at 29,4 metres per second, now downward. All of that is free. But from railing height down into the gorge, the stone covers ground the upward journey never visited, so the mirror has nothing to say: the extra fall adds time, and the final speed exceeds the launch speed. The efficient method is one clean equation across the entire flight: take upward positive, set the total displacement to negative the height of the bridge — negative because the landing lies below the start — and solve displacement equals v i t plus a half a t squared for the total time, or use v f squared equals v i squared plus 2 a delta y for the landing speed. Symmetry then retires from method and becomes your auditor: the total time must exceed 6 seconds and the landing speed must exceed 29,4 metres per second, and if either comes out smaller, a sign slipped. Partial symmetry plus one honest equation — that is the entire recipe for every bridge and cliff variation you will meet.
