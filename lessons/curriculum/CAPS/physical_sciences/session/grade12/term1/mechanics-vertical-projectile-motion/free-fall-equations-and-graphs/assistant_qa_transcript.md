**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! The first is the one I hear every year: if gravity is pulling the ball down the whole time, what actually keeps it moving upward after it leaves my hand?

**Tutor:** Nothing keeps it moving upward — and realising that nothing is needed is the whole point. This is Newton's first law wearing a projectile costume. While the ball was in your hand, your hand gave it velocity. The moment it leaves, the hand's force is gone completely; there is no leftover "force of the throw" travelling with the ball, even though almost everyone privately believes there is. The ball continues upward simply because it HAS velocity and nothing has yet removed all of it. What gravity does is chip away at that velocity: 9,8 metres per second of upward speed removed every second, which is why the ball slows, stops for an instant, and then gains downward speed at exactly the same rate. So the correct free-body diagram of a rising ball shows one single force — the gravitational force, downward — and nothing else. Drawing an upward "throw force" arrow on a ball in flight is one of the most reliably punished errors in Paper 1. If you want a sentence to carry into the exam: motion does not need a force to continue; it needs a force to change, and gravity is the only changer present.

***

**Mandy:** Next: when do we choose downward as positive and when upward, and does the choice change the answer? Learners lose marks swapping signs halfway through.

**Tutor:** The choice is completely free, the final physical answer is identical either way, and the marks are lost only when the convention changes mid-solution — so the rule is: choose once, write it down, obey it to the last line. Practically, choose the direction in which most of the motion happens. For a ball dropped or thrown downward, take downward as positive: then the initial velocity and the acceleration are both positive and the arithmetic is friendly. For a ball thrown upward, take upward as positive: the initial velocity is positive and the acceleration is negative 9,8, every time, even while the ball falls. What you may never do is give the acceleration a new sign when the ball turns around at the top — the acceleration never turned around; only the velocity's sign changes as the motion reverses. That is the deep reason one equation can cover the whole up-and-down journey in a single line: with a fixed convention, a displacement below the starting point is simply negative, and the equation delivers it without you cutting the flight into pieces. When your calculation produces a negative answer, do not panic and do not delete the sign: translate it. Negative velocity with upward positive means the object is moving downward; negative displacement means it ended below where it began. The sign is information, not an error message.

**Mandy:** Here is the graph one: how can the position-time graph be curved when the velocity-time graph is straight? Learners feel those two pictures contradict each other.

**Tutor:** They not only fail to contradict each other — each one forces the other's shape. The bridge between the graphs is the gradient. The gradient of the position-time graph at any instant IS the velocity at that instant. Now read the straight velocity-time line of a falling ball: the velocity is changing steadily, growing every second. If the velocity is different at every moment, then the steepness of the position-time graph must be different at every moment — and a graph whose steepness changes continuously is a curve. A straight position-time line would mean one fixed gradient, which would mean constant velocity, which would flatly contradict the sloping velocity graph. So: straight sloping velocity-time line and curved position-time graph are two portraits of the same motion, and they must appear together. Push the chain one step further and it closes beautifully: the gradient of the velocity-time graph is the acceleration, and since that line is straight, the gradient is one constant number, 9,8 downward — which is why the acceleration-time graph is the world's most boring picture, a flat horizontal line. Curve, straight slope, flat line: position, velocity, acceleration. Each graph is the gradient-story of the one before it, and checking that your three sketches obey that chain is the fastest way to find your own mistakes before the marker does.

**Mandy:** Last one: in cliff questions — a ball thrown up from the edge, landing at the bottom — can we still use time up equals time down and same speed up as down, or is that cheating?

**Tutor:** You can use the symmetry only for the part of the journey that is actually symmetric, and knowing where that boundary lies is exactly what the cliff question tests. Symmetry — equal times, equal speeds at equal heights — is a mirror around the top of the flight, and the mirror only works between two points at the SAME height. So for a ball thrown up at 19,6 metres per second from the cliff edge: from the hand up to the top and back down to hand height, everything is symmetric — 2 seconds up, 2 seconds down, returning past the hand at 19,6 metres per second downward. That much is free. But from hand height down to the base of the cliff, the ball is covering new ground the upward trip never saw, so there is no mirror for it: the fall takes extra time and the landing speed exceeds the throwing speed. The efficient method is one clean equation over the whole flight: take upward as positive, let the total displacement be negative the height of the cliff — negative because the landing point is below the start — and solve displacement equals initial velocity multiplied by time plus a half a t squared for the total time, or use final velocity squared equals initial velocity squared plus 2 a delta y for the landing speed. Symmetry then becomes your checking tool rather than your method: your total time must exceed 4 seconds, and your landing speed must exceed 19,6 metres per second. If either comes out smaller, a sign slipped somewhere. Partial symmetry plus one honest equation — that is the whole recipe.
