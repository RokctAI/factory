# Part 1 — Expert

A stone dropped from a bridge, a ball thrown straight up, a bounce on a paved driveway — vertical projectile motion is the most predictable question in Paper 1, because only one force is involved and only three equations are needed. This session sets up the sign conventions, works the standard calculations for objects dropped and thrown, and then turns to the graphs of position, velocity and acceleration, which is where the topic's real marks live.

## Subtopic: Free Fall and the Equations of Motion

The definition first: a projectile is an object on which the only force acting is the gravitational force. No engines, no hands, and — by the syllabus's standing instruction — no air resistance. The moment an object leaves your hand, whether it is rising, falling or momentarily still, it is in free fall, and its acceleration is 9,8 metres per second squared, directed downward, at every instant.

Three equations of motion do all the work, and each must be said correctly. First: final velocity equals initial velocity plus acceleration multiplied by time. Second: displacement equals initial velocity multiplied by time, plus a half multiplied by the acceleration multiplied by the time squared. Third: final velocity squared equals initial velocity squared plus two multiplied by the acceleration multiplied by the displacement. Every symbol in them is a vector except time, so the first line of every solution is the sign convention: choose downward as positive, or upward as positive, state it, and never change it mid-question.

Work the simplest case. A brick is dropped from scaffolding 19,6 metres above the ground. Take downward as positive. Initial velocity: zero. Formula: displacement equals initial velocity multiplied by time plus a half a t squared. Substitution: 19,6 equals zero plus a half multiplied by 9,8 multiplied by t squared, so t squared equals 19,6 divided by 4,9, which is 4. Answer: the brick falls for 2 seconds. Its landing speed: final velocity equals zero plus 9,8 multiplied by 2, which is 19,6 metres per second downward.

Notice what "dropped" gave us for free: initial velocity zero. Question words carry data in this topic — "dropped" means zero initial velocity, "thrown down at 5" means initial velocity 5 in the downward direction, and "reaches maximum height" means final velocity zero. Underline those phrases before you touch the formulas.

This section's questions are with you now: sign convention stated, equation quoted, substitution shown, answer with unit and direction.

## Subtopic: Thrown Upward — Symmetry and the Top of the Flight

Throw a ball straight up at 19,6 metres per second. Take upward as positive, so the acceleration is negative 9,8 metres per second squared — gravity still pulls down while the ball rises.

At the very top of the flight, the velocity is zero, but the acceleration is still 9,8 metres per second squared downward. That sentence decides a mark nearly every year, so hold it firmly: the ball is momentarily at rest, yet still accelerating, because gravity never pauses.

Time to the top. Formula: final velocity equals initial velocity plus a t. Substitution: zero equals 19,6 plus negative 9,8 multiplied by t. Answer: t equals 2 seconds. Maximum height: final velocity squared equals initial velocity squared plus 2 a delta y, so zero equals 19,6 squared minus 2 multiplied by 9,8 multiplied by delta y, giving delta y equals 384,16 divided by 19,6, which is 19,6 metres.

Now the symmetry, which halves the work in every question. The upward journey and the downward journey are mirror images: the time up equals the time down, so the total flight back to the throwing height is 4 seconds; and the ball returns to your hand at 19,6 metres per second, the same speed it left with, now directed downward. At every height, the speed going up equals the speed coming down. Symmetry holds whenever the projectile lands at the same height it started — and breaks the moment it does not, as when a ball is thrown up from the edge of a cliff. In that case, trust the equations rather than the shortcut: a single equation with the full displacement handles the whole flight, provided the signs stay honest.

One warning on displacement against distance. A ball that rises 19,6 metres and returns to the hand has travelled a distance of 39,2 metres, but its displacement is zero. Read which one the question wants.

The questions for this section are in front of you now — state the top-of-flight facts precisely and let symmetry check your arithmetic.

## Subtopic: The Three Graphs — Dropped, Thrown Up and Bouncing

Every projectile question can arrive dressed as a graph, and three graphs tell the whole story: position against time, velocity against time, and acceleration against time.

Start with the easiest: acceleration against time is a horizontal line at 9,8 downward, for the entire flight, whatever the ball is doing. Rising, falling, at the top — the line never moves. Only a bounce, when the ball touches the ground and is no longer a projectile, interrupts it.

Velocity against time is a straight line with a constant gradient of 9,8. Take upward as positive and throw the ball up at 19,6: the line starts at positive 19,6, slopes steadily down, crosses zero at 2 seconds — the top of the flight — and reaches negative 19,6 at 4 seconds. The crossing point is the moment of maximum height, and the steady gradient is gravity itself.

Position against time is a parabola: steep at first, flattening to a horizontal tangent at the top, then steepening again on the way down. It is NOT a picture of the ball's path — the ball moves straight up and down — it is a record of height against clock time, and reading it as a trajectory is a classic error.

Now the bouncing ball, the syllabus's favourite. Drop a ball and let it bounce. On the velocity-time graph with downward positive: the line rises from zero to the impact velocity, then the bounce appears as an almost vertical jump to a negative value — the ball leaves the floor moving up — with a smaller magnitude than the arrival, because the bounce loses energy. Then another straight segment with the same gradient of 9,8, crossing zero at the top of the bounce, and so on: a sawtooth of parallel lines, each tooth shorter than the last. Between bounces the acceleration graph sits at 9,8 downward throughout; the brief contact with the floor is excluded.

Sketch questions mark the shape, the intercepts and the parallel gradients. Questions on this section are with you now — draw with a ruler where the physics is straight.

## Subtopic: Reading Numbers off the Graphs

The graphs are not decoration; they are instruments, and three read-out rules extract every number the examiner can ask for.

Rule one: the gradient of a position-time graph is the velocity. A horizontal tangent means the object is momentarily at rest — the top of the flight. The steeper the curve, the faster the motion.

Rule two: the gradient of a velocity-time graph is the acceleration. For any projectile segment, that gradient is 9,8 in the downward sense — which is precisely how a graph tells you the object is in free fall.

Rule three: the area between a velocity-time graph and the time axis is the displacement. Areas above the axis count positive; areas below count negative; and adding them with their signs gives displacement, while adding their magnitudes gives distance.

Apply rule three to the ball thrown up at 19,6 metres per second, upward positive. From zero to 2 seconds, the graph makes a triangle above the axis: area equals a half multiplied by 2 multiplied by 19,6, which is 19,6 — the maximum height, read straight off the graph without an equation of motion. From 2 to 4 seconds, an equal triangle sits below the axis: negative 19,6. Total displacement: zero, exactly as it should be for a ball back in the hand. Total distance: 39,2 metres.

Finally, describing motion from a graph, which is asked in words and marked in words. Say four things: the direction of motion, whether the speed is increasing or decreasing, the value of the acceleration, and any special instants — maximum height where the velocity is zero, or a bounce where the velocity reverses suddenly. For a segment moving from positive velocity toward zero: the ball moves upward, slowing down at 9,8 metres per second squared, reaching maximum height at the instant the velocity is zero.

The final expert questions are with you now: gradient, gradient, area — and full sentences for the descriptions.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same motion with no equations at all — just a ball, a stopwatch in your head, and gravity's one simple habit.

## Subtopic: Gravity's Tax — 9,8 Every Second

Gravity runs the simplest tax system on Earth. Throw a ball straight up, and every second of flight, gravity takes 9,8 metres per second off your upward speed. No exceptions, no holidays.

Throw a ball up at 19,6 metres per second. After one second it is doing 9,8. After two seconds, nothing — it hangs at the top for an instant. But gravity does not stop collecting: the next second the ball is doing 9,8 downward, and the second after that, 19,6 downward — exactly the speed you threw it with, arriving back in your hand. Two seconds up, two seconds down, same speeds passed in reverse order, like watching the trip up played backwards.

That mirror is your free gift in this topic. Time up equals time down. Speed at any height going up equals speed at the same height coming down. If you know one half of the story, you know the other half at no charge — as long as the ball lands at the same height it started from.

And the trap that catches thousands of matrics: at the very top, the ball's speed is zero, so learners write that the acceleration is zero too. It is not. The tax collector does not go home just because your account hits zero. The acceleration is 9,8 metres per second squared downward the whole time — rising, falling, and at the top. Speed can be zero while the CHANGE in speed is running at full rate.

Your first questions are with you now — count in seconds, take off 9,8 each time, and never let the tax collector rest.

## Subtopic: The Ball's Diary — Three Graphs, One Story

The three graphs of this topic are just three diaries the ball keeps at the same time — one recording where it is, one recording how fast it moves, one recording gravity.

The height diary is a rainbow-shaped curve: climbing steeply at first, levelling off at the top, dropping steeply again. Careful — that curve looks like the ball's path, but it is not one. The ball goes straight up and straight down; the curve is height plotted against time, the way a savings graph is money against time, not a road map.

The speed diary is a ruler-straight line sloping downward. It starts at the throwing speed, hits zero exactly at the top of the flight, and keeps going into negative numbers — negative simply meaning "now moving the other way". The line is straight because gravity's tax is steady: same 9,8 off, every second. Where that line crosses zero, the height diary is at its peak. The two diaries agree, always.

The gravity diary is the dullest page ever written: a flat line at 9,8 downward, from the moment the ball leaves the fingers until the moment it lands. Nothing to see — and that flatness IS the answer to any question asking what the acceleration was doing.

To pull numbers out: steepness of the height diary tells you speed. Steepness of the speed diary tells you acceleration. And the area trapped under the speed diary tells you how far the ball moved — triangle areas, half base times height, the same geometry you have done since Grade 8.

Questions on the diaries are coming to you now — match the peak of one graph to the zero of another.

## Subtopic: The Bouncing Ball Without Tears

The bouncing ball graph frightens everyone the first time, so build it slowly, bounce by bounce.

Drop a tennis ball from shoulder height and let downward be the positive direction. The speed diary starts at zero and climbs in a straight line — the ball falls faster and faster — until it smacks the driveway. In that instant the diary does something violent: the line jumps almost vertically from a big positive number to a smaller negative one. Positive to negative, because the ball's direction flipped from down to up in a few milliseconds. Smaller, because a real bounce pays a price — some energy leaves as sound and a squashed thump, so the ball leaves slower than it arrived.

Then the story simply repeats. Another straight line with the same slope as before — gravity's tax rate never changes — crossing zero at the top of the bounce, growing positive as the ball falls again, another jump at the floor, smaller again. The whole graph is a sawtooth: parallel straight teeth, each tooth shorter than the last, until the ball lies still.

Two checkpoints to read the graph like an examiner. Every crossing of the zero line is a top of flight — the ball hanging still for an instant in mid-air. Every sudden vertical jump is a floor strike. Between those events, every segment has the identical slope, because between touches the ball is a pure projectile with nothing acting on it but gravity.

Say that last sentence proudly in an exam: between bounces the acceleration is constant at 9,8 metres per second squared downward, and the graph's parallel lines prove it.

The last questions of the lesson are yours now — find the tops, find the bounces, and check that all the teeth are parallel.
