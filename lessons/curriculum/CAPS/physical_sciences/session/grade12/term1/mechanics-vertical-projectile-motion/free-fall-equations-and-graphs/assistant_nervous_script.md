Hey, it's Mandy — projectile motion looks like a topic full of falling objects and flying signs, and I know the minus signs are what make your stomach drop. Here is the secret: this is the most predictable question in the whole paper. Same three equations, same two graphs, every single year.

Start every solution the same way, no matter what the question says: write your sign convention. "Upward positive" or "downward positive" — one line. Then the acceleration is settled forever: 9,8 with the sign your convention gives it, and it NEVER changes during the flight, not even at the top. That one line protects every mark that follows.

Then mine the words for data. "Dropped" means initial velocity zero. "Maximum height" means final velocity zero. "Returns to the hand" means displacement zero. "Thrown down at 5" means initial velocity 5, downward sign. The examiner hides half the numbers inside ordinary words, and underlining them is not childish — it is how the top learners work.

Pick the equation that contains your three knowns and one unknown, quote it, substitute with signs, solve. If time is involved, use the first two equations; if time is missing, the velocity-squared equation is your friend. That is the entire decision tree.

For graphs, memorise one chain: position curves, velocity is a straight sloping line, acceleration is flat at 9,8. Gradient takes you left to right along that chain; area under the velocity graph gives displacement. The bouncing ball is just the falling graph photocopied smaller and smaller — parallel teeth, jumps at the floor, zero crossings at each top.

And the trap everyone falls into once: at the top, velocity is zero but acceleration is still 9,8 downward. Say it out loud before the exam. Say it twice.

Sign convention, word-mining, one equation, one graph chain. Four moves, practised until they are boring — and boring is exactly what you want in the exam room. You've got this!
