# Part 1 — Expert

Why does petrol evaporate while cooking oil sits in the pan? Why is butane a gas, yet an alcohol with almost the same mass a liquid? Every physical-property question in the organic section is one question in disguise: how strong are the forces BETWEEN the molecules? This session revises the three intermolecular forces, then applies them to the three structural levers the syllabus tests — chain length, branching, and functional group — and finishes by assembling the four-step explanation that earns full marks every time.

## Subtopic: The Forces Between Molecules

First, the distinction that anchors everything. The bonds INSIDE a molecule — the covalent bonds holding carbon to carbon and carbon to hydrogen — are interatomic forces, and they are strong. The attractions BETWEEN separate molecules are intermolecular forces, and they are far weaker. Boiling and melting break only the forces between molecules; the molecule itself sails into the gas phase intact. Any answer that speaks of "breaking bonds" during boiling has already lost the marks.

Three intermolecular forces run the whole topic, from weakest to strongest. LONDON forces — also called dispersion forces — act between ALL molecules, polar or not, and arise from momentary shifts of electron clouds. They are the only force nonpolar molecules have, so alkanes, alkenes and alkynes rely on them entirely. Their strength grows with molecular size: more electrons and more surface area mean stronger London forces.

DIPOLE-DIPOLE forces act between polar molecules — molecules with a permanent positive end and negative end. The carbonyl group makes aldehydes and ketones polar, so they attract each other more strongly than alkanes of the same size.

HYDROGEN BONDS are the strongest of the three: a special, extra-strong dipole attraction that occurs when hydrogen is bonded directly to nitrogen, oxygen or fluorine. The hydroxyl group of an alcohol qualifies exactly — its hydrogen sits on an oxygen — so alcohols hydrogen-bond to each other. Carboxylic acids qualify twice over, because the carboxyl group offers both an oxygen-bound hydrogen and a second oxygen to receive a neighbour's hydrogen; acid molecules typically bind in pairs, doubling the attraction.

So the ranking by family, for equal chain length: alkanes weakest, then aldehydes and ketones, then alcohols, then carboxylic acids strongest. Hold that ladder; the whole lesson climbs it.

This section's questions are with you now — name the force each family relies on, and keep interatomic and intermolecular firmly apart.

## Subtopic: Chain Length and Branching

Lever one: chain length. Walk up the alkanes and watch the boiling points climb: methane boils at minus 161,5 degrees Celsius, butane at minus 0,5, pentane at 36,1, hexane at 69, octane at 126. The pattern is perfectly steady, and the explanation is one sentence: as chain length increases, the molecules have larger surfaces and more electrons, so the London forces between them are stronger, and more energy is needed to separate the molecules. That is why the first four alkanes are gases at room temperature, the middle ones are liquids like the petrol fractions, and the very long ones are waxes and solids.

Lever two: branching, and it runs the other way. Compare the three isomers of C five H twelve. Pentane, the straight chain, boils at 36,1 degrees Celsius. 2-methylbutane, with one branch, boils at 27,8. And 2,2-dimethylpropane — a central carbon carrying four other carbons, the most compact arrangement possible — boils at 9,5. Same molecular formula, same molar mass, and a drop of nearly 27 degrees from straightest to most branched.

The explanation is geometric. London forces act over surfaces: two straight chains can lie alongside each other and attract along their entire length, like two rulers stacked face to face. Branching pulls the molecule into a compact, nearly spherical shape, and spheres touch only at a point. Less contact area means weaker London forces, means less energy to separate, means a lower boiling point. In the exam's words: MORE BRANCHING, LOWER boiling point, because the reduced surface area of contact weakens the dispersion forces.

Melting points and vapour pressures follow as mirror images, and we will formalise vapour pressure shortly: for now, note that whatever strengthens the intermolecular forces raises the boiling point and lowers the vapour pressure together.

Questions on the two levers are in front of you now — longer means higher, more branched means lower, and the reason is surface.

## Subtopic: Functional Groups and Boiling Points

Lever three, the strongest of all: the functional group. To isolate it, compare compounds of nearly equal molar mass, so that London forces start level and the functional group's contribution stands out.

Line up three compounds near sixty grams per mole. Butane — an alkane, molar mass 58 — boils at minus 0,5 degrees Celsius. Propanal — an aldehyde, also 58 — boils at 48,8. Propan-1-ol — an alcohol, molar mass 60 — boils at 97,2. Same size, wildly different boiling points, and the ladder of forces explains every rung. Butane is nonpolar: London forces only. Propanal's carbonyl group gives it a permanent dipole: London forces PLUS dipole-dipole attraction, so it boils about fifty degrees higher. Propan-1-ol's hydroxyl group hydrogen-bonds: London forces plus the strongest intermolecular attraction available, and the boiling point jumps another fifty degrees.

Now crown the ladder. Propanoic acid, molar mass 74, boils at 141 degrees Celsius — far above even the alcohol of comparable size. The carboxyl group hydrogen-bonds at two sites, and pairs of acid molecules clasp each other in a double hydrogen bond, so each "molecule" leaving the liquid must effectively tear itself out of a dimer. Strongest forces, highest boiling point, exactly as the ladder predicts.

The same reasoning explains solubility in water, which the syllabus touches lightly: water is a hydrogen-bonding liquid, so molecules that can hydrogen-bond with it — short alcohols, carboxylic acids — dissolve readily, while alkanes, offering water nothing but London forces, do not mix with it. Petrol floats on rain puddles for a reason.

One caution for comparisons: always state WHAT you are holding constant. Comparing propan-1-ol to octane proves nothing about functional groups, because chain length changed too. The examiner sets fair comparisons; your answers must too.

This section's questions arrive now — rank by family at equal mass, and name the specific force doing the lifting.

## Subtopic: Vapour Pressure and the Four-Step Answer

Vapour pressure is the pressure exerted by the vapour of a liquid in equilibrium with that liquid, in a closed container. It measures escape: molecules at the liquid's surface with enough energy break free into the space above, and the more easily they escape, the higher the vapour pressure. A liquid boils when its vapour pressure equals the surrounding atmospheric pressure.

The connection to everything before is a strict inversion: STRONG intermolecular forces hold molecules in the liquid, so few escape — LOW vapour pressure, HIGH boiling point. WEAK intermolecular forces let molecules stream out — HIGH vapour pressure, LOW boiling point, and we call such liquids volatile. One ladder of forces, two readouts facing opposite ways.

Test it on the compounds we know. Pentane: weak London forces only, vapour pressure high, boiling point 36,1 — splash it on a bench and it is gone in moments. Propan-1-ol: hydrogen-bonded, vapour pressure much lower, boils at 97,2. Esters, with dipole-dipole forces but no hydrogen bonding between their own molecules, sit in between — volatile enough that their fruit-scented molecules reach your nose, which is why esters do the perfume work in this syllabus.

Now the answer scheme, because these questions are marked on structure. Every "explain the difference in boiling point or vapour pressure" answer takes four steps. Step one: identify the relevant structural difference — chain length, branching, or functional group. Step two: name the intermolecular forces in each compound — London, dipole-dipole, hydrogen bonding. Step three: compare their strengths explicitly — stronger in this one, weaker in that one, and say why. Step four: link to energy — stronger forces need more energy to overcome, hence the higher boiling point or the lower vapour pressure. Four sentences, four marks, in that order, and the word "energy" must appear in the last one.

The final expert questions are with you now — define vapour pressure precisely, invert the ladder correctly, and answer in four steps.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the whole topic again with kitchen equipment: velcro, magnets and nail polish remover.

## Subtopic: Molecules Are Wearing Velcro

Every molecule in this topic is wearing a strip of velcro, and the story of boiling points is just the story of how much velcro touches.

The weakest velcro is the kind every molecule owns — the London force. It is feeble per touch, but it adds up with size: a long molecule is a long strip of velcro. Lay two long strips side by side and they grip along their whole length; pulling them apart takes real effort. That is why boiling points climb as chains grow — methane, a tiny scrap of velcro, boils at minus 161,5 degrees, while octane, a long strip, needs 126 degrees before its molecules can tear free. Boiling IS the tearing: heat until the molecules shake themselves apart from each other. The molecules themselves never break — only their grip on each other does.

Branching is what happens when you crumple the strip into a ball. Same amount of velcro, but two crumpled balls touch only where they happen to meet — a tiny patch. Compare the three molecules made of exactly five carbons and twelve hydrogens: the straight one boils at 36,1 degrees, the once-branched one at 27,8, and the tightest ball of the three at just 9,5. Nothing changed but the shape.

So two rules cost you nothing to remember. Longer chain: more velcro, higher boiling point. More branching: crumpled velcro, lower boiling point. And the reason behind both is the same word — contact.

Your first questions are with you now — measure the velcro, then check how much of it can touch.

## Subtopic: Some Molecules Also Carry Magnets

Velcro is only the starting kit. Some families upgrade.

Molecules with a lopsided charge — a slightly positive end and a slightly negative end — behave like weak fridge magnets: the positive end of one snuggles up to the negative end of the next. Aldehydes and ketones, the families with a carbon-oxygen double bond, carry exactly this kind of magnet. So at the same size, they out-grip the plain velcro of an alkane: butane, all velcro, boils below zero, while propanal, velcro plus magnets, needs almost 49 degrees.

Then comes the serious equipment. When a hydrogen is bonded straight onto an oxygen — as in every alcohol's O H group — it forms hydrogen bonds with the oxygen of the next molecule: the strongest intermolecular grip in the syllabus. That upgrade takes propan-1-ol, the same size as butane, all the way to 97,2 degrees. And carboxylic acids carry the grip TWICE — their molecules hold hands in pairs, each clasping the other with two hydrogen bonds — which is why propanoic acid tops the chart at 141.

This is also why alcohol mixes with water and petrol refuses. Water is itself a hydrogen-bonding liquid; an alcohol can join water's network of handshakes, so it dissolves. An alkane offers water nothing but velcro, and water is not interested — so petrol beads up and floats.

One ladder to carry into the exam, for molecules of similar size: alkane, then aldehyde or ketone, then alcohol, then carboxylic acid — each rung gripping harder and boiling higher than the one below.

Questions on the upgrades are coming to you now — velcro, magnets, handshakes, in rising order.

## Subtopic: Why Nail Polish Remover Disappears

Leave the cap off nail polish remover and the bottle empties itself within days. Leave the cap off cooking oil and it sits there for months. Both are liquids; the difference is how badly their molecules want to leave — and that is what vapour pressure measures.

Picture the liquid's surface as the exit gate of a taxi rank at the end of a shift. In a weakly gripping liquid — plain velcro, no magnets — molecules constantly get enough of a jiggle from heat to jump the gate and escape as gas. Lots of escapees means a high vapour pressure, and we call the liquid volatile. That is petrol, that is nail polish remover, that is pentane vanishing off a lab bench. In a strongly gripping liquid — hydrogen-bonded, like an alcohol or worse, like glycerine — hardly anyone makes it over the gate. Low vapour pressure, slow evaporation, higher boiling point.

So vapour pressure and boiling point are the same information stated backwards: strong grip means high boiling point AND low vapour pressure; weak grip means low boiling point AND high vapour pressure. Never let the exam trick you into raising both together.

You can even smell the physics. Esters — the sweet, fruity family — are volatile enough that their molecules leap out of the liquid and drift to your nose, which is exactly why they are used in flavourings and perfumes. A substance you can smell across the room is a substance with a decent vapour pressure. Cooking oil, gripping itself tightly, barely smells at all until you heat it — heat is the bribe that gets molecules over the gate.

And when the exam asks you to explain any of this, walk the same four steps every time: point at the structural difference, name each liquid's forces, say which grip is stronger, and finish with energy — stronger forces need more energy to overcome. The last questions of the lesson are yours now — weak grip leaves the bottle, strong grip stays.
