Hey, it's Mandy — physical properties questions look like they need a memory full of boiling points, and I promise you they do not. Nobody expects you to know that pentane boils at 36,1 degrees. The examiner gives you the data; your whole job is to explain the pattern, and there is exactly one pattern.

Everything — boiling point, melting point, vapour pressure — is decided by one question: how strongly do the molecules grip each other? Answer that, and the properties follow automatically. Strong grip: high boiling point, low vapour pressure. Weak grip: low boiling point, high vapour pressure, evaporates fast. Never both high together — they are opposites, always.

Three levers change the grip, and every question pulls exactly one of them. Longer chain: more surface, stronger London forces, higher boiling point. More branching: compact ball, less contact, LOWER boiling point — that one feels backwards, so drill it. Stronger functional group: climb the ladder — alkane, then aldehyde or ketone, then alcohol, then carboxylic acid at the top with its double handshake.

Then write your answer in the four steps, in order, every time. One: name the structural difference. Two: name the forces in each compound — London, dipole-dipole, or hydrogen bonding. Three: say which is stronger and why. Four: finish with the word energy — stronger forces need more energy to overcome, so the boiling point is higher. That fourth sentence is a guaranteed mark and it is the one everyone forgets.

And the golden vocabulary rule: molecules never break when something boils. Only the forces BETWEEN molecules are overcome. Write "intermolecular forces", never just "bonds".

One question, three levers, four steps, one forbidden word. That is the entire topic, and the data booklet does the memorising for you. You've got this!
