**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! The first one never stops arriving: learners look at a reaction and freeze — is this addition, elimination or substitution? Is there a sorting test that works even when the nerves kick in?

**Tutor:** There is, and it is nothing more than counting. Count the molecules going in, count the molecules coming out, and compare the saturation before and after — those two observations classify nearly every reaction in the syllabus before any deeper chemistry is needed. Addition: TWO reactants merge into ONE product, and the reactant's double bond disappears — unsaturated in, saturated out. Elimination is the exact mirror: ONE reactant fragments into TWO products, and a double bond APPEARS where there was none, alongside a small ejected molecule such as water or a hydrogen halide. Substitution: TWO in, TWO out, saturation untouched — one piece traded for another, with the displaced piece leaving attached to part of the incoming reagent, which is why substitution always makes that second product learners forget to write, the hydrogen chloride or the sodium bromide. So the drill is: count in, count out, compare double bonds. Two-to-one with a vanishing double bond is addition. One-to-two with an appearing double bond is elimination. Two-to-two with no saturation change is substitution. Then, and only then, confirm with the conditions — catalyst and mild for addition, hot and concentrated for elimination, light for alkane halogenation, dilute and aqueous for hydrolysis. The count decides, the conditions confirm, and together they hold even on your worst day.

**Mandy:** Next, the pair of rules that feels contradictory: in addition the hydrogen goes to the carbon with MORE hydrogens, but in elimination the hydrogen leaves the carbon with FEWER. Are those two rules fighting each other?

**Tutor:** They only look like rivals until you notice they serve the same master, which is the stability of the double bond. The underlying fact: a double bond is more stable when it sits deeper inside the chain, between carbons carrying more carbon neighbours — a more substituted alkene is a more contented alkene. Now watch both rules bow to that fact. In ADDITION the double bond is being consumed, and the question is which saturated product forms. The favoured pathway sends the incoming hydrogen to the doubled carbon already rich in hydrogens, leaving the halogen or hydroxyl to settle on the more substituted carbon — the rich get richer. In ELIMINATION a double bond is being created, and the question is where to put it. Taking the hydrogen from the neighbouring carbon with FEWER hydrogens plants the new double bond deeper inside the chain, between the more substituted carbons — building precisely the more stable alkene. Test it on 2-bromopentane: remove the hydrogen from carbon three, which holds two, rather than carbon one, which holds three, and the double bond forms between carbons two and three — pent-2-ene, internal, major. Remove from carbon one instead and you get pent-1-ene, terminal, minor. One sentence holds both rules: the product with the more internal, more crowded double bond wins, and each rule is just the route to it from its own side. No contradiction — one principle in two uniforms.

**Mandy:** The ester-naming disaster, next: learners run the reaction perfectly and then write propyl methanoate when the answer was methyl propanoate. What procedure makes the name come out right every time?

**Tutor:** Identify the parents FIRST, assemble the name LAST — every scrambled ester name in history comes from assembling first. Step one: find the two parent molecules. In a synthesis question they are given openly — the alcohol and the acid sitting in the reactants. In a structure question, find the ester linkage: a carbon carrying both a double-bonded oxygen and a single-bonded oxygen that runs on into a second chain. The chain containing that carbonyl carbon belongs to the ACID parent; the chain hanging off the single-bonded oxygen belongs to the ALCOHOL parent. Step two: name the alcohol's half first, its carbon count with a Y L ending — methanol gives methyl, ethanol gives ethyl, butan-1-ol gives butyl. Step three: name the acid's half second, its carbon count with the O A T E ending — propanoic acid gives propanoate, methanoic acid gives methanoate. Methanol plus propanoic acid: methyl propanoate, never the other way around. The check that exposes any swap: the O A T E half must contain the carbonyl carbon, and its carbon count includes that carbon — propanoate means three carbons counting the one wearing the double-bonded oxygen. Two final habits while we are here: write the catalyst as CONCENTRATED sulfuric acid, since the concentration is part of the required conditions, and never leave out the water — without it the equation does not balance, and a balanced equation is the easiest thing in the question to get right.

**Mandy:** Last one: the condition passwords — dilute aqueous versus concentrated ethanolic sodium hydroxide. Learners memorise them, but then a question asks WHY the conditions flip the product, and everything goes quiet. What is the why?

**Tutor:** There is a chemical why and a strategic why, and strong answers carry both. Chemically, the hydroxide ion has two talents: it can attack the carbon holding the halogen and slide into its place, which is substitution, or it can behave as a base and pluck a hydrogen off the neighbouring carbon, which sets off elimination. The conditions decide which talent takes the stage. In dilute AQUEOUS solution with gentle warming, the ion sits comfortably solvated in water, and the easiest available event is the direct swap at the halogen-bearing carbon — substitution wins, and the alcohol forms. In concentrated ETHANOLIC solution at high temperature, water is scarce, the base talent comes forward, and heat favours the pathway that splits one molecule into several — elimination wins, because higher temperatures generally reward reactions that increase the number of separate particles. So the passwords compress real physics: water and mildness serve the swap; ethanol, concentration and heat serve the ejection. Strategically, deploy it like this: whenever a question offers a haloalkane and sodium hydroxide, refuse to commit until you have underlined three words — the solvent, the concentration, and the temperature. Those three underlines are the answer: aqueous, dilute and warm points to the alcohol; ethanolic, concentrated and hot points to the alkene, with the major product chosen by the fewer-hydrogens rule. And when a question reverses the game and asks you to CHOOSE conditions for a target product, give all three explicitly — solvent, concentration, temperature — because a vague answer like heat it with sodium hydroxide tells the reader you know the reagent but not the levers. Conditions are the levers of organic chemistry, and this topic is one long test of whether you know which lever runs which machine.
