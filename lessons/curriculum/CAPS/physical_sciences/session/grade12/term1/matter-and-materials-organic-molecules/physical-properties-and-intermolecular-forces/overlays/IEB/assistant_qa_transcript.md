**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First, the misconception I mark in half the practice books: when hexane boils, learners write that the carbon-carbon bonds break. Why is that wrong, and how do we banish it for good?

**Tutor:** It is wrong because boiling changes a substance's STATE, never its identity — the hexane vapour above the liquid is still hexane, every molecule whole, every covalent bond intact. If those bonds had actually broken, you would not have hexane gas; you would have decomposition into entirely new substances, and that requires flames or catalysts, not a warm water bath. The energy scales tell the story: a covalent bond is enormously strong, while the London forces between hexane molecules are so feeble that a sunny bench top overcomes them. Boiling overcomes only the weak grip BETWEEN molecules. The habit that makes this permanent: in any answer about melting, boiling, evaporation or vapour pressure, never write the word bond by itself. Write intermolecular forces when you mean the attractions between molecules, and if hydrogen bonding must be mentioned, call it hydrogen bonding between molecules — it is the one intermolecular force with the word bond unhelpfully baked into its name. The claim that bonds break on boiling is one of the most reliably penalised sentences in the subject, so this single vocabulary habit protects every physical-property answer you will ever write.

***

**Mandy:** Next: comparison questions where the two compounds differ in more than one way — say a long alcohol against a short ketone. How do we answer those without getting tangled?

**Tutor:** By naming the levers before pulling any of them. The three levers are chain length, branching and functional group, and a fair comparison changes exactly one. When a question compares hexane with heptane, only chain length moved: answer through London forces growing with surface and electron count. When it compares hexane with 2,2-dimethylbutane, only branching moved: same formula, same mass, but the compact ball touches less, so the dispersion forces weaken. When it compares pentane with butan-1-ol, the masses are nearly level, so the functional group is the live lever: hydrogen bonding against bare London forces. Your tangled case — a long alcohol against a short ketone — changes TWO levers at once, and the honest answer says so and weighs them. The alcohol's hydrogen bonding beats the ketone's dipole-dipole attraction force for force, AND its longer chain piles extra London force on top, so both levers pull the same direction and the alcohol boils higher. If the levers pulled opposite ways, you would name the tension and reason toward the dominant one — hydrogen bonding usually outweighs modest chain differences. The sentence that shows a reader you are in control is the first one: these compounds differ in chain length and in functional group. Declare both levers, then pull them one at a time.

***

**Mandy:** Now the one that feels upside-down to everybody: why does a HIGH vapour pressure pair with a LOW boiling point? Learners keep wanting the two to rise together.

**Tutor:** Because the two numbers measure the same grip from opposite ends, and one picture settles it. Vapour pressure measures how easily molecules LEAVE the liquid on their own; boiling point measures how hard you must WORK to make all of them leave. Take hexane, weakly gripped by London forces alone: molecules escape its surface constantly, so in a closed bottle the vapour above becomes crowded and pushes hard — a high vapour pressure. Because escape is already that easy, only modest heating lifts the vapour pressure to atmospheric pressure, and boiling — which is precisely the moment vapour pressure equals atmospheric pressure — arrives early, at 68,7 degrees Celsius. Now take butan-1-ol, hydrogen-bonded: few molecules escape, the vapour stays thin, the vapour pressure sits low, and you must heat all the way to 117,7 degrees before its vapour pushes as hard as the atmosphere. Strong grip: low vapour pressure, high boiling point. Weak grip: high vapour pressure, low boiling point. And here is the self-check that catches the error before it costs you: if you ever find yourself writing that a compound has both a high vapour pressure and a high boiling point, stop — you have just described molecules that are desperate to leave and impossible to remove at the same time, and no substance manages both.

**Mandy:** Last one: the data table shows ethanol boiling at 78 degrees but water — a far smaller molecule — at 100. Doesn't that smash the rule that bigger molecules boil higher?

**Tutor:** It refines the rule rather than smashing it: the levers have a ranking, and the functional-group lever outranks the size lever whenever they disagree. Bigger boils higher is a statement about London forces, and it works beautifully when the compounds compared rely on the same kind of force — that is why it runs so cleanly up the alkane series. Water against ethanol is not that comparison. A water molecule is tiny, but it is essentially pure functional group: one oxygen carrying two hydrogens, both capable of donating hydrogen bonds, plus two lone pairs ready to receive them. Each water molecule can anchor itself into the liquid with up to four hydrogen bonds, weaving a dense three-dimensional network. Ethanol brings one hydroxyl group to that contest, with a two-carbon tail contributing only London forces. Molecule for molecule, water's network is harder to dismantle than ethanol's, and that beats ethanol's size advantage — one hundred against seventy-eight. The transferable lesson: when two compounds differ in BOTH size and hydrogen-bonding capacity, count the hydrogen-bonding sites first and let size settle ties, not the reverse. You have already seen the same principle inside the organic families: butanoic acid, with its double hydrogen-bond clasp, outboils alkanes far longer than itself — a second handshake beats a longer velcro strip every time.
