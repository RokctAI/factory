**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First, the misconception I see in half the practice answers: when butane boils, learners write that the carbon-carbon bonds break. Why is that wrong, and how do we keep it out of our answers forever?

**Tutor:** It is wrong because boiling changes the STATE of a substance, not its identity — the butane gas above the liquid is still butane, every molecule complete, all its covalent bonds intact. If the carbon-carbon and carbon-hydrogen bonds had actually broken, you would not have butane vapour; you would have chemical decomposition into different substances entirely, and that takes flames or catalysts, not a water bath. The energy scales settle the matter: a covalent bond is enormously strong, while the London forces between butane molecules are so weak that even the warmth of your hand overcomes them. What boiling overcomes is only the weak grip BETWEEN the molecules. The habit that keeps this permanent: in any answer about melting, boiling, evaporation or vapour pressure, never use the word "bond" on its own. Say "intermolecular forces" when you mean the attractions between molecules, and if you must mention hydrogen bonding, call it hydrogen bonding between molecules, since it is the one intermolecular force with the word bond unfortunately baked into its name. Markers are specifically instructed to penalise "bonds break when it boils", so this single vocabulary habit protects real marks in every physical-property question on the paper.

***

**Mandy:** Next: how do we answer the comparison questions fairly? Learners get confused when two compounds differ in more than one way — say, a long alcohol against a short ketone.

**Tutor:** The key is to recognise that a fair comparison changes one lever at a time, and to say explicitly which lever the question has changed. The three levers are chain length, branching and functional group. When the examiner compares pentane with hexane, only chain length changed: answer with London forces growing with molecular surface and electron count. When the examiner compares pentane with 2,2-dimethylpropane, only branching changed: same formula, same mass, but the compact shape touches less, so the dispersion forces weaken. When the examiner compares butane with propan-1-ol, the masses are nearly equal, so the functional group is the live lever: hydrogen bonding against London forces. Now, your harder case — a long alcohol against a short ketone — is exactly the kind of question where both chain length and functional group differ, and the honest answer names both effects and weighs them. The alcohol's hydrogen bonding is stronger per contact than the ketone's dipole-dipole attraction, AND its longer chain adds London force on top, so both levers pull the same way and the alcohol boils higher. If the levers pulled in opposite directions, you would say so and reason toward which dominates; hydrogen bonding usually outweighs modest chain differences. The sentence that earns the method mark is the opening one: "these compounds differ in chain length and in functional group" — showing the examiner you saw both levers before you pulled either.

***

**Mandy:** Here is the one that feels backwards to everyone: why does HIGH vapour pressure go with LOW boiling point? Learners keep wanting the two to rise together.

**Tutor:** They feel like siblings but they are opponents, and one picture fixes it: vapour pressure measures how badly molecules WANT to leave the liquid, while boiling point measures how hard you must WORK to make them all leave. A liquid with weak intermolecular forces — pentane, say — lets molecules escape from its surface constantly, so the vapour above it in a closed bottle becomes crowded and pushes hard: a high vapour pressure. Because escape is already so easy, only a little heating brings the vapour pressure up to atmospheric pressure, and boiling — which is precisely the moment vapour pressure equals atmospheric pressure — arrives early, at 36,1 degrees Celsius. A liquid with strong forces — propan-1-ol, hydrogen-bonded — holds its molecules back, so few escape, the vapour above it stays thin, and the vapour pressure sits low; you must heat all the way to 97,2 degrees before its vapour pushes as hard as the atmosphere. So the two quantities are the same underlying fact, the strength of the grip, read from opposite ends: strong grip means low vapour pressure and high boiling point; weak grip means high vapour pressure and low boiling point. If you ever catch yourself writing that a compound has both a high vapour pressure and a high boiling point, stop — you have just claimed its molecules are simultaneously desperate to leave and impossible to remove, and one of those must be wrong.

**Mandy:** Last one: the data booklet shows ethanol boiling at 78 degrees but water — a much smaller molecule — at 100. Doesn't that break the rule that bigger molecules boil higher?

**Tutor:** It breaks nothing; it demonstrates that the rules have a ranking, and the functional-group lever outranks the size lever when the two disagree. The rule "bigger boils higher" is a rule about London forces, and it holds when the compounds being compared rely on similar kinds of force — that is why it works so cleanly along the alkane series. Water against ethanol is not that comparison. A water molecule is tiny, but it is practically ALL functional group: one oxygen carrying two hydrogens, both of them capable of hydrogen bonding, and two lone pairs on the oxygen to receive hydrogen bonds from neighbours. Each water molecule can therefore anchor itself into the liquid with up to four hydrogen bonds, forming a dense three-dimensional network. Ethanol has one hydroxyl group doing that work, and the rest of the molecule is a two-carbon tail contributing only London forces. Per molecule, water's hydrogen-bond network is simply harder to pull apart than ethanol's, and that outweighs ethanol's size advantage — hence 100 against 78. The exam lesson is transferable: when two compounds differ in BOTH size and hydrogen-bonding capacity, count the hydrogen-bonding sites first and let size break ties, not the other way round. And notice the same logic inside the organic families themselves: propanoic acid outboils much longer alkanes for exactly the same reason — a double hydrogen-bond clasp beats a longer velcro strip.
