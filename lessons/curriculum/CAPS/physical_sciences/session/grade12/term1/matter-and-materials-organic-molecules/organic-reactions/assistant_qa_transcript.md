**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First, the one that comes up every single cycle: learners stare at a reaction and cannot decide — addition, elimination or substitution? Is there a foolproof sorting test that works under exam pressure?

**Tutor:** There is, and it needs nothing but counting. Count the reactant molecules and the product molecules, and check the saturation before and after — those two observations classify almost every reaction in the syllabus before any chemistry knowledge is consulted. Addition: TWO reactants collapse into ONE product, and a double bond in the reactant disappears — unsaturated in, saturated out. Elimination is the exact mirror: ONE reactant splits into TWO products, and a double bond APPEARS where there was none — saturated in, unsaturated out, plus a small ejected molecule like water or hydrogen bromide. Substitution: TWO in and TWO out, with saturation untouched — one piece swapped for another, and the displaced piece walking away bonded to part of the incoming reagent, which is why substitution always produces that second product learners forget, the hydrogen bromide or the sodium bromide. So the sorting routine is: count in, count out, compare double bonds. Two-to-one with a vanishing double bond, addition. One-to-two with an appearing double bond, elimination. Two-to-two with no change in saturation, substitution. Only after the count do you confirm with the conditions — catalyst and mild for addition, hot and concentrated for elimination, light for alkane halogenation, dilute and aqueous for hydrolysis. The count decides, the conditions confirm, and the two together are unshakeable even on a bad day.

**Mandy:** Next, the major-minor confusion: for addition the rule says hydrogen goes to the carbon with MORE hydrogens, but for elimination it says hydrogen leaves the carbon with FEWER. Learners feel the rules contradict each other. Do they?

**Tutor:** They feel contradictory only until you see that both rules are the same preference read in opposite directions, and the preference belongs to the double bond, not to the hydrogen. The underlying fact: double bonds are more stable when they sit between carbons that carry more alkyl neighbours — a more substituted double bond is a happier double bond. Now watch each rule serve that preference. In ADDITION, the double bond is being destroyed, and the question is which of the two possible saturated products forms. The pathway runs through an intermediate that is more stable when the incoming hydrogen lands on the already hydrogen-rich carbon — so hydrogen joins the carbon with more hydrogens, and the halogen or hydroxyl settles on the more substituted carbon. The rich get richer. In ELIMINATION, a double bond is being CREATED, and the question is where. Removing a hydrogen from the neighbouring carbon that has FEWER hydrogens plants the new double bond deeper inside the chain, between the more substituted carbons — building exactly the stabler alkene the preference demands. Take 2-bromobutane: strip the hydrogen from carbon three, which holds two hydrogens, rather than carbon one, which holds three, and the double bond forms between carbons two and three — but-2-ene, internal, major. Strip from carbon one instead and you get but-1-ene, terminal, minor. So teach it as one sentence: the product with the more crowded, more internal double bond wins, and each rule is just the recipe for reaching it from its own side. Contradiction dissolved — it was one principle wearing two uniforms.

**Mandy:** The ester question, every year: learners can perform the reaction but butcher the name — methyl butanoate becomes butyl methanoate and the marks evaporate. What is the reliable naming procedure?

**Tutor:** The procedure is to identify the parents FIRST and only then assemble the name, because every butchered ester name comes from assembling before identifying. Step one: find the two parent molecules. In a synthesis question they are handed to you — the alcohol and the carboxylic acid in the reactants. In a name-the-structure question, locate the ester linkage: a carbon holding a double-bonded oxygen AND a single-bonded oxygen that continues into a second chain. The chain containing that double-bond carbon belongs to the ACID parent; the chain hanging off the single-bonded oxygen belongs to the ALCOHOL parent. Step two: name the alcohol's contribution first — its carbon count with a Y L ending. One carbon from methanol: methyl. Two from ethanol: ethyl. Step three: name the acid's contribution second — its carbon count with the O A T E ending. Butanoic acid: butanoate. Assemble: methanol plus butanoic acid gives methyl butanoate, never the reverse order. The check that catches every swap: the O A T E half must contain the carbonyl carbon, and its carbon count must match the original acid's, counting that carbonyl carbon itself — butanoate means four carbons including the one wearing the double-bonded oxygen. Two further mark-savers while we are here: state the catalyst as CONCENTRATED sulfuric acid, because dilute loses the condition mark, and never forget water as the second product — the equation is not balanced without it, and the balancing mark is the cheapest one on the paper.

**Mandy:** Last one, the condition passwords: dilute aqueous versus concentrated ethanolic sodium hydroxide, same haloalkane, different products. Learners memorise the passwords but then a question asks WHY the conditions matter, and they have nothing. What is the why?

**Tutor:** The why has a chemical layer and a strategic layer, and the exam rewards both. The chemical layer: the hydroxide ion is a reagent with two talents — it can attack the carbon holding the halogen and take its place, which is substitution, or it can act as a base and pluck a hydrogen off the neighbouring carbon, which triggers elimination. The conditions tilt the stage between the two talents. In dilute AQUEOUS solution, at gentle warmth, the hydroxide approaches a well-solvated, calm system where the easiest event is the direct swap at the halogen-bearing carbon — substitution wins and the alcohol forms. In concentrated ETHANOLIC solution at high temperature, water is scarce, the hydroxide's base behaviour comes forward, and the added heat favours the pathway that fragments one molecule into two — elimination wins, because higher temperatures generally reward the reaction that creates more separate molecules. So the passwords encode real physics: water and mildness serve the swap; ethanol, concentration and heat serve the ejection. The strategic layer is how to deploy this in the paper. Whenever a question supplies a haloalkane and sodium hydroxide, refuse to answer until you have underlined three words in the question: the solvent, the concentration, and the temperature. Those three underlines ARE the answer — aqueous, dilute, warm points to the alcohol; ethanolic, concentrated, hot points to the alkene, with the major product chosen by the fewer-hydrogens rule. And if a question asks you to CHOOSE conditions to make a target product, run the passwords backwards: name the solvent, the concentration and the temperature explicitly, because vague answers like heat with sodium hydroxide score half marks at best. Conditions are not stage decoration in organic chemistry — they are the levers, and the paper is testing whether you know which lever makes which machine run.
