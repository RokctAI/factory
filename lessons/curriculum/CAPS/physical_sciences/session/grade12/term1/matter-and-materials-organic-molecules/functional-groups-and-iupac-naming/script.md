# Part 1 — Expert

Organic chemistry carries more marks than any other chemistry topic in the matric year, and every one of those marks begins with the same two skills: recognising which family a molecule belongs to, and naming it correctly. This session builds the vocabulary the definitions demand, tours the nine homologous series of the syllabus, sets out the IUPAC naming rules as a fixed procedure, and finishes with the three kinds of structural isomer. Since we are working in sound, every structure will be built in words — carbon by carbon — which is exactly the discipline that makes structures reliable on paper too.

## Subtopic: The Vocabulary of Organic Chemistry

Organic molecules are molecules containing carbon atoms. Carbon earns an entire branch of chemistry for one reason: each carbon atom forms exactly four bonds, and carbon bonds readily to itself, building chains, branches and rings of almost any length. Every structure in this topic obeys the four-bond rule without exception, and checking it is how you audit your own work.

A HYDROCARBON is a compound containing only carbon and hydrogen atoms. A FUNCTIONAL GROUP is a bond or an atom or a group of atoms that determines the physical and chemical properties of a group of organic compounds — the double bond in an alkene, the O H group in an alcohol, the halogen atom in a haloalkane. A HOMOLOGOUS SERIES is a series of organic compounds that can be described by the same general formula, in which each member differs from the previous one by a C H two unit — one carbon and two hydrogens.

SATURATED compounds contain only single carbon-to-carbon bonds; UNSATURATED compounds contain at least one double or triple carbon-to-carbon bond, and so can accept more atoms.

Then the three ways of writing a molecule. The MOLECULAR formula only counts atoms: butane is C four H ten, and that is all it says. The STRUCTURAL formula draws every atom and every bond so that the full skeleton is visible. The CONDENSED formula compresses each carbon and its hydrogens into a unit written in sequence: butane reads C H three, then C H two, then C H two, then C H three — four carbons in a row, the two end carbons carrying three hydrogens each, the two middle carbons carrying two each. Twelve bonds accounted for, four per carbon. When a question says "structural formula", every bond must appear; a condensed formula there loses the mark.

This section's questions are with you now: the five definitions word for word, and the four-bond rule applied to every structure you meet.

## Subtopic: Nine Families and Their Functional Groups

The syllabus works with nine homologous series, and each is identified by its functional group and named by its suffix.

The ALKANES have only single carbon-carbon bonds, general formula C n H two n plus two, and names ending in A N E: methane, ethane, propane, butane, and onward through pentane, hexane, heptane, octane — one to eight carbons, a spine you must know cold. The ALKENES carry a carbon-to-carbon double bond, general formula C n H two n, suffix E N E. The ALKYNES carry a carbon-to-carbon triple bond, general formula C n H two n minus two, suffix Y N E. Alkanes are saturated; alkenes and alkynes are unsaturated.

The ALCOHOLS carry the hydroxyl group — an oxygen bonded to a hydrogen, written O H — attached to a carbon of the chain, suffix A N O L. The HALOALKANES are alkanes in which one or more hydrogens are replaced by halogen atoms — fluorine, chlorine, bromine or iodine — named with the prefixes fluoro, chloro, bromo and iodo.

Four families carry the carbonyl group, a carbon double-bonded to an oxygen, and the difference between them is entirely about position and attachments. In the ALDEHYDES the carbonyl carbon sits at the END of the chain, bonded also to a hydrogen; suffix A L, as in propanal, and the carbonyl carbon is always carbon number one. In the KETONES the carbonyl carbon sits INSIDE the chain, bonded to carbon on both sides; suffix O N E, as in propanone. In the CARBOXYLIC ACIDS the end carbon carries both a double-bonded oxygen and a hydroxyl group — the carboxyl group, written C O O H; suffix A N O I C acid, as in ethanoic acid, the acid of vinegar. In the ESTERS the carbonyl carbon is bonded to a second oxygen which continues into another carbon chain — a chain, then C O O, then another chain; esters are named later from their parent alcohol and acid, as in ethyl ethanoate.

Given any structure, your first question is always: which functional group is present? That one identification fixes the suffix, the family, and — as the next lesson shows — most of the physical properties.

Questions on the families are in front of you now — match functional group, general formula and suffix until the links are automatic.

## Subtopic: IUPAC Naming, Step by Step

IUPAC naming is a procedure, not an art, and the same four steps name everything in the syllabus.

Step one: find the LONGEST continuous carbon chain that contains the functional group, and take the root name from its length. Step two: number the chain from the end that gives the FUNCTIONAL GROUP the lowest possible number; if there is no functional group, number from the end that gives the substituents the lowest numbers. Step three: name each substituent — methyl for a one-carbon branch, ethyl for a two-carbon branch, or a halogen prefix — with its position number. Step four: assemble alphabetically, prefixes before the root, using di and tri for repeated substituents, with commas between numbers and hyphens between numbers and words.

Build one in words. Take a chain of four carbons. On carbon two, hang a methyl group — a single carbon with its three hydrogens. Fill every remaining bond with hydrogen. Longest chain: four carbons, so the root is butane. Numbering from the end nearest the branch puts the methyl on carbon two. Name: 2-methylbutane. Numbering from the other end would have given three — higher, so wrong.

Now with a functional group. A chain of three carbons; on the MIDDLE carbon, a hydroxyl group. Root: propan. The O H sits on carbon two whichever end you count from. Name: propan-2-ol. Its cousin with the hydroxyl on the end carbon is propan-1-ol — same formula, different compound, and the number is what separates them.

An alkene, to see the priority rule. A chain of four carbons with a double bond between carbon one and carbon two, and a methyl group on carbon three. The double bond outranks the substituent, so numbering must start at the end nearest the double bond. Name: 3-methylbut-1-ene. The alkene's number states the FIRST carbon of the double bond.

And a halogen pair, since haloalkanes may carry two. A chain of two carbons with a chlorine on each: 1,2-dichloroethane — positions one and two, di for two chlorines.

Reverse the skill as well, because the exam asks both directions. Asked to draw 2-bromobutane: butane means a four-carbon chain; bromo on carbon two; fill the rest with hydrogen; then check every carbon for exactly four bonds.

This section's questions come to you now — four steps, in order, both directions.

## Subtopic: Isomers — Same Formula, Different Molecule

Structural isomers are molecules with the same molecular formula but different structural formulae, and the syllabus names three kinds.

CHAIN isomers differ in the shape of the carbon skeleton. C four H ten can be butane — four carbons in an unbranched chain — or 2-methylpropane — a three-carbon chain with a methyl branch on the middle carbon. Same ten hydrogens and four carbons, different skeleton, different boiling point, different name.

POSITIONAL isomers have the same chain and the same functional group, but the group sits on a different carbon. Butan-1-ol and butan-2-ol: a four-carbon chain in both, with the hydroxyl on the end carbon in one and on the second carbon in the other. But-1-ene and but-2-ene tell the same story with a double bond.

FUNCTIONAL isomers share a molecular formula while belonging to DIFFERENT homologous series. The pair to know: an aldehyde and a ketone of the same carbon count. Propanal — three carbons with the carbonyl at the end — and propanone — three carbons with the carbonyl in the middle — are both C three H six O, yet they answer to different suffixes, different families and different chemistry. Carboxylic acids and esters form the same kind of pair: propanoic acid and methyl ethanoate are both C three H six O two.

The exam verbs matter here. "Define structural isomers" wants the definition above, with molecular formula and structural formula both mentioned. "Draw a chain isomer of pentane" wants a genuinely different skeleton — 2-methylbutane or 2,2-dimethylpropane — not the same chain bent on the page, because a bent drawing of five carbons in a row is still pentane. Rotating a molecule does not make an isomer; only changing which atom is bonded to which does.

The final expert questions are with you now: classify each pair as chain, positional or functional, and defend the classification in a sentence.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same content with the chemistry stripped down to what it really is: a naming system less complicated than a home address.

## Subtopic: A Surname, a First Name and a House Number

Every organic name is just an address, read from right to left.

The end of the name — the suffix — is the family surname. A N E means the alkane family, plain and single-bonded. A N O L means the alcohols, the family with the O H group. A N O I C acid means the carboxylic acids, the vinegar family. Hear the surname and you already know the functional group, the way hearing a surname at a funeral tells you which family to greet.

The middle of the name tells you how many carbons long the main chain is, and it is a counting rhyme you already half-know from geometry: meth is one, eth is two, prop is three, but is four, then pent, hex, hept, oct — five, six, seven, eight. Butanol: but means four carbons, anol means an alcohol. Done.

The numbers are house numbers on that chain. Propan-2-ol: three-carbon street, O H living at number two. 2-bromobutane: four-carbon street, bromine at number two. And the one rule about numbering: the important resident — the functional group — must get the LOWEST house number available, so you count from whichever end of the street makes that number smallest. If counting from the left puts the O H at number two and counting from the right puts it at number three, the left wins and the name says two.

Front of the name, the prefixes: the lodgers. Methyl is a one-carbon lodger, ethyl a two-carbon lodger, chloro and bromo are halogen lodgers, each announced with its house number. Two identical lodgers get di, three get tri.

Your questions are with you now — read each name backwards: surname first, street length, then house numbers.

## Subtopic: Carbon Has Four Hands

To build any structure from a name, you need one fact and one habit.

The fact: every carbon atom has exactly four hands, and every hand must hold something — another carbon, a hydrogen, an oxygen, a halogen. Never five, never three. Hydrogen has one hand, oxygen two, and any halogen one.

The habit: build in three passes. First, lay down the carbon spine — the name told you how long. Second, attach the important things: the functional group at its numbered position, then each lodger at its number. Third, and only third, top up every carbon's spare hands with hydrogens.

Build 2-methylbutane out loud. Pass one: four carbons holding hands in a row. Pass two: on carbon two, a methyl group — one extra carbon. Pass three: count hands. Carbon one has one hand on carbon two and three hands free — three hydrogens. Carbon two holds carbon one, carbon three and the methyl — one hydrogen. Carbon three holds two carbons — two hydrogens. Carbon four — three hydrogens. The methyl carbon — three hydrogens. Total it and you get C five H twelve, and every carbon has exactly four hands. The molecule audits itself.

Now build propan-2-ol. Three carbons in a row; on the middle one, an oxygen holding a hydrogen — the O H group takes one of the middle carbon's hands. Top up with hydrogens: three on each end carbon, one on the middle. Every structure question in the paper is these same three passes, and the four-hands count at the end is your free error-checker: a carbon with five bonds means a mistake, found before the marker finds it.

Questions on building are coming to you now — spine, attachments, hydrogens, count.

## Subtopic: Spot the Twins

Take four carbon atoms and ten hydrogens. You can assemble them as a straight chain of four — that is butane — or as a chain of three with the fourth carbon hanging off the middle one — that is 2-methylpropane. Same box of atoms, two genuinely different molecules, with different names and even different boiling points. Chemists call them structural isomers: same molecular formula, different structure.

There are three ways to make twins like this. Change the SKELETON — straight versus branched — and you have chain isomers, like the pair above. Keep the skeleton but move the functional group to a different house number — butan-1-ol against butan-2-ol — and you have positional isomers. Or rearrange the atoms so the molecule lands in a completely different FAMILY — the same atoms making an aldehyde or a ketone, propanal or propanone — and you have functional isomers: same formula, different surname entirely.

One trap catches nearly everyone the first time. Draw four carbons in a row, then draw four carbons in a row with a kink in the drawing, like a bent elbow. Twins? No — the same molecule, photographed from a different angle. On paper or in your head, what matters is only WHO IS BONDED TO WHOM, not how the drawing bends. A chain of four is butane no matter how it snakes across the page. To make a genuine isomer you must change a bond — take a carbon off the end of the queue and bond it to the middle.

So when the exam says "draw an isomer", make one real change: re-route the skeleton, move the group's house number, or switch the family. Then run the four-hands check, and confirm your new molecule still uses exactly the same atom count as the old one — same formula, different molecule, that is the whole game.

The last questions of the lesson are yours now — same bricks, different building, and never be fooled by a bent photograph.
