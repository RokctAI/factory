**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First, the one that fills my inbox every year: how are we supposed to memorise nine families, nine suffixes and nine general formulae without drowning?

**Tutor:** By refusing to memorise them as nine separate things, because they are really three small groups with a logic each. Group one is the hydrocarbons, and they are a counting exercise: alkanes have only single bonds and the general formula C n H two n plus two; take one pair of hydrogens away to make room for a double bond and you have the alkenes at C n H two n; take another pair away for a triple bond and you have the alkynes at C n H two n minus two. One ladder, descending by two hydrogens per rung — that is three families for the price of one idea. Group two is the single-substitution pair: alcohols are a chain wearing a hydroxyl group, haloalkanes are a chain wearing a halogen. Group three is the carbonyl quartet — aldehyde, ketone, carboxylic acid, ester — and they differ only in where the carbon-oxygen double bond sits and what its carbon holds: at the end with a hydrogen, inside the chain, at the end with a hydroxyl as well, or bonded through an oxygen to a second chain. Learn the three logics, and then let the questions themselves drill the suffixes: every time you meet a name ending in O N E, say "carbonyl inside the chain" before you do anything else. Recognition practised beats lists memorised, and after twenty structures the families will feel like faces rather than facts.

**Mandy:** Next: when a chain has both a double bond and a methyl branch, learners never know which one gets the low number. What is the order of importance?

**Tutor:** The functional group always outranks the substituent, with no exceptions inside this syllabus. Number the chain so that the double bond — or the hydroxyl, or the carbonyl, or whatever the family's defining group is — receives the lowest possible number, and only use substituents to break a tie. Take the four-carbon chain with a double bond starting at one end and a methyl on the third carbon from that end. Numbering from the double-bond end gives the double bond position one and the methyl position three: 3-methylbut-1-ene. Numbering from the other end would give the methyl a flattering two but push the double bond to three, and that is simply wrong, because the group that defines the family defines the numbering. A useful mental order of precedence for everything you will meet: carboxylic acids are the strongest — their carbon is ALWAYS carbon one, so their names never even carry a position number; aldehydes likewise sit at carbon one automatically; then ketones, alcohols and double or triple bonds get the lowest number the chain allows; and alkyl groups and halogens queue up last, taking whatever numbers remain. When there is no functional group at all — a plain branched alkane — then and only then do the substituents themselves claim the lowest set of numbers. Say the priority out loud before numbering and the mark takes care of itself.

**Mandy:** This one causes real arguments: propan-1-ol and propan-2-ol — same atoms, same chain, same family. Why does the exam insist they are different compounds when they seem identical in every way that matters?

**Tutor:** Because bonding position is not bookkeeping — it changes the molecule's actual behaviour, and the examiner knows it. Physically, the two have different boiling points, different densities, different melting points: moving the hydroxyl from the end carbon to the middle carbon changes the molecule's shape, and shape controls how closely molecules can pack and how well their intermolecular attractions line up — the next lesson builds that machinery in detail. Chemically the difference is even sharper: in later reactions the two behave differently precisely because the hydroxyl carbon has different surroundings — the end carbon of propan-1-ol holds one other carbon, while the middle carbon of propan-2-ol holds two, and reaction products depend on that arrangement. So "same atoms" is a weaker statement than it sounds; chemistry is a science of arrangements, not ingredients. Charcoal and diamond are both pure carbon, and nobody proposes they are the same material. The examination consequence is strict: a structural formula with the hydroxyl on the wrong carbon is not "nearly right" — it is a different compound with a different name, and it earns zero for both the structure and any name built on it. Treat position numbers with the respect you give digits in a cell phone number: one digit out, entirely different destination.

**Mandy:** Last one: in the exam, learners confuse condensed, structural and molecular formulae and lose marks for giving the wrong kind. What is the clean way to keep the three apart?

**Tutor:** Match each type of formula to the question it answers, and then to the exam verb that requests it. The molecular formula answers only "how many of each atom?" — C four H ten and nothing more; it cannot distinguish butane from 2-methylpropane, which is exactly why isomer questions lean on it. The structural formula answers "which atom is bonded to which?" — every atom drawn, every bond shown as its own line, including every single carbon-hydrogen bond; it is the only representation with no ambiguity at all. The condensed formula sits between: it walks the chain carbon by carbon, writing each carbon with its own hydrogens — C H three, C H two, C H two, C H three for butane — with branches shown in brackets after the carbon that carries them, but no bond lines drawn. Now the exam grammar. "Write down the MOLECULAR formula" wants the count only, and showing structure there is wasted time. "Draw the STRUCTURAL formula" wants every bond — a condensed formula here forfeits the mark, and the most common forfeit in the whole topic is writing C H three dash C H three when the question asked for the full drawing with six separate carbon-hydrogen bond lines. "Write the CONDENSED formula" wants the compact walk, and a full drawing there, though not wrong in spirit, wastes minutes you need elsewhere. One habit locks all this in: whenever you finish any structural formula, run the four-bond audit on each carbon in turn. It catches missing hydrogens, impossible five-bond carbons, and halved marks before they happen.
