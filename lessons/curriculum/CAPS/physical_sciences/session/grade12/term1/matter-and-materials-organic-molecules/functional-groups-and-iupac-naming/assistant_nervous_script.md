Hey, it's Mandy — organic chemistry is the biggest topic in Paper 2, and I know that first glance at a page of structures feels like being asked to read another language. Here is the truth: it IS another language, but it is the most regular language ever invented. No irregular verbs, no exceptions to cram — one system, applied identically to every molecule you will ever meet.

Read every name backwards, in three pieces. The suffix is the surname: A N E is an alkane, A N O L is an alcohol, A N O I C acid is a carboxylic acid. The root is the chain length: meth, eth, prop, but, pent, hex, hept, oct — one through eight. The numbers are house numbers. Butan-2-ol: four-carbon street, alcohol family, O H at number two. Any name in the paper falls apart into those three pieces.

Building structures is three passes, always in the same order: lay the carbon spine, attach the functional group and branches at their numbers, then fill every spare bond with hydrogen. Never start with the hydrogens.

Then the free checking trick, and I want you to use it on every single structure: count to four on every carbon. Four bonds, no more, no less. A carbon with five bonds is the most common error in the country, and the count catches it in ten seconds.

Naming has one priority rule that settles every argument: the functional group gets the lowest number, and substituents only matter when there is a tie or no group at all. Say "group first, branches second" as you number.

And for isomers, ask one question: did a bond actually change? A bent drawing is the same molecule. A moved carbon, a moved group, or a changed family — those are real isomers.

Backwards reading, three passes, count to four, group first. Practise on five molecules tonight and the language starts speaking itself. You've got this!
