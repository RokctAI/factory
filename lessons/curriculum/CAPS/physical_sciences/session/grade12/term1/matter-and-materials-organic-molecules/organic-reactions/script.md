# Part 1 — Expert

Naming molecules is grammar; making them react is the actual language. The organic reactions section asks one question over and over: given this molecule and these conditions, what happens and why? This session covers the full syllabus set — combustion and esterification first, then the three reaction types that dominate the marks: addition across double bonds, elimination to create them, and substitution to swap one group for another. The conditions decide everything, and by the end they will decide it for you.

## Subtopic: Combustion and Esterification

Combustion first, the alkanes' claim to fame. In excess oxygen, any alkane burns completely to carbon dioxide and water, releasing large amounts of heat energy — which is the entire reason alkanes are the world's fuels: petrol, paraffin, liquid petroleum gas, diesel. Speak the smallest example: methane plus two oxygen molecules gives one carbon dioxide plus two water molecules. Balancing is a routine: balance carbons first, then hydrogens, then count oxygens last. Propane: three carbons need three carbon dioxides; eight hydrogens need four waters; that demands ten oxygen atoms, so five oxygen molecules. Propane plus five oxygen gives three carbon dioxide plus four water. The phrase EXCESS OXYGEN matters — the syllabus keeps combustion complete, and complete means only carbon dioxide and water.

Esterification is the perfume maker's reaction: a carboxylic acid and an alcohol join, with concentrated sulfuric acid as catalyst and gentle heating, to form an ESTER plus water. Follow the atoms: the acid contributes its carboxyl group minus its hydroxyl; the alcohol contributes its oxygen and chain, giving up only the hydrogen from its hydroxyl group. The discarded hydroxyl and hydrogen become the water molecule.

Work the standard example in words: ethanoic acid plus ethanol, warmed with concentrated sulfuric acid, gives ethyl ethanoate plus water. The naming rule: the alcohol donates the first half of the name, ending in Y L, and the acid donates the second half, ending in O A T E. Methanol with butanoic acid gives methyl butanoate. Reverse-reading works too: propyl ethanoate must have come from propan-1-ol and ethanoic acid. Esters carry the fruity smells of chemistry — pear drops, pineapple, banana — and questions about them are really bookkeeping: name the parents, name the ester, and never forget the water.

This section's questions are with you now — carbons, hydrogens, oxygens in that order for burning, and acid plus alcohol gives ester plus water for perfume.

## Subtopic: Addition: Opening the Double Bond

Addition reactions belong to the ALKENES, because addition needs somewhere to add — the double bond. One bond of the carbon-carbon double pair breaks open, and each of the two carbons gains a new partner. Unsaturated becomes saturated; two molecules become one; nothing is thrown away. Four versions run the syllabus, distinguished only by what is added.

HYDROGENATION adds hydrogen: an alkene plus hydrogen gas, with a platinum, palladium or nickel catalyst, gives the alkane. Ethene plus hydrogen gives ethane. This is the reaction that hardens vegetable oils into margarine. HALOGENATION adds a halogen: ethene plus bromine gives 1,2-dibromoethane, one bromine on each carbon, no catalyst needed — and the rapid fading of red-brown bromine is the standard laboratory test for unsaturation: alkenes decolourise bromine water quickly, alkanes do not.

HYDROHALOGENATION adds a hydrogen halide, and here position matters. Take propene — a three-carbon chain with the double bond between carbon one and carbon two. Add hydrogen bromide: the hydrogen goes to one double-bond carbon, the bromine to the other, and two products are possible: 1-bromopropane or 2-bromopropane. The syllabus rule for the MAJOR product: the hydrogen atom attaches to the double-bond carbon that ALREADY holds more hydrogens. Carbon one of propene carries two hydrogens, carbon two only one — so the incoming hydrogen joins carbon one, the bromine settles on carbon two, and 2-bromopropane is the major product. The rich get richer — that is the memory hook.

HYDRATION adds water: an alkene plus water, with an acid catalyst such as dilute sulfuric acid, gives an alcohol. The same richness rule applies: propene hydrates mainly to propan-2-ol, the hydroxyl landing on the middle carbon. Recognise addition instantly by its signature: reactant contains a double bond, two reactants become one product, and the conditions are mild — no intense heat required.

Questions on this section reach you now — one bond opens, two carbons gain partners, and hydrogen joins the carbon that already has more hydrogens.

## Subtopic: Elimination: Creating the Double Bond

Elimination is addition in reverse: one molecule EJECTS a small piece and forms a double bond where the pieces left. Two neighbouring carbons each lose something — one loses a halogen or hydroxyl, the other loses a hydrogen — and the freed bonding capacity snaps into a carbon-carbon double bond. One molecule in, two out: the alkene plus the small ejected molecule. The syllabus tests three versions.

DEHYDROHALOGENATION strips hydrogen and halogen from a haloalkane. Conditions are everything, so state them in full: the haloalkane is heated with CONCENTRATED sodium hydroxide dissolved in ETHANOL — hot, concentrated, ethanolic. Take 2-bromobutane: a four-carbon chain, bromine on carbon two. The bromine leaves carbon two, and a hydrogen must leave a NEIGHBOURING carbon — either carbon one or carbon three, giving but-1-ene or but-2-ene. The major-product rule: the hydrogen is removed from the neighbouring carbon that has FEWER hydrogens. Carbon three, inside the chain, holds two hydrogens; carbon one, at the end, holds three. Fewer means carbon three, so the double bond forms between carbons two and three: but-2-ene is the major product — the more substituted, more stable alkene.

DEHYDRATION of an alcohol ejects water: the hydroxyl leaves one carbon, a hydrogen leaves its neighbour. Conditions: heating with an excess of concentrated sulfuric acid, which serves as both acid catalyst and dehydrating agent. Ethanol dehydrates to ethene plus water — industrially useful as a route to alkenes wherever fermentation supplies ethanol.

CRACKING breaks long alkanes into shorter, more useful pieces — a smaller alkane plus an alkene — using high temperature and pressure, with or without a catalyst. It exists because crude oil delivers too many long chains and the market wants short ones: petrol-length alkanes and alkenes for plastics. Elimination's signature, for classification questions: one reactant, a double bond APPEARING in the product, and harsh conditions — heat, concentration, strength.

This section's questions arrive now — eject from neighbours, double bond in the gap, and the hydrogen leaves the carbon that has fewer to give.

## Subtopic: Substitution: the Straight Swap

Substitution is the polite reaction: one atom or group steps out, another steps into the exact same position, and no double bond forms or disappears. Saturated stays saturated. The syllabus tests two versions plus a critical contrast.

HALOGENATION of alkanes: an alkane plus a halogen, in the presence of LIGHT or heat, swaps one hydrogen for one halogen. Methane plus bromine, under light, gives bromomethane plus hydrogen bromide. Note both products: the displaced hydrogen does not vanish — it leaves bonded to the other halogen atom as a hydrogen halide. Light is the examinable condition: alkanes are saturated and stubborn, and without light or heat the bromine simply sits there — which is why alkanes decolourise bromine only slowly and only in sunlight, while alkenes do it instantly in the dark. Same reagent, different speed, different reaction type — a favourite comparison question.

HYDROLYSIS of haloalkanes runs the swap with water's pieces: the haloalkane reacts with water, or better with DILUTE aqueous sodium hydroxide, under mild warming, and the halogen is replaced by a hydroxyl group: a haloalkane becomes an alcohol. Two-bromopropane gives propan-2-ol — the hydroxyl landing exactly where the bromine sat, because substitution is a swap in place.

Now the contrast the examiners adore, because it separates understanding from memorisation. The SAME haloalkane with the SAME reagent, sodium hydroxide, gives two different products depending only on conditions. Dilute sodium hydroxide, in water, gently warmed: SUBSTITUTION — the alcohol forms. Concentrated sodium hydroxide, in ethanol, strongly heated: ELIMINATION — the alkene forms. Dilute, aqueous and mild favours the swap; concentrated, ethanolic and hot favours the ejection. Learn the condition pairs as passwords, because classification questions hand you conditions precisely so the conditions can hand you the answer.

The final expert questions are with you now — a swap keeps saturation, both products count, and the conditions are the verdict.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same chemistry as three stories: a fire and a perfume factory, a molecule with open hands, and a dance floor where the rules are set by the lighting.

## Subtopic: Burning Money and Bottling Fruit

Two reactions bracket this topic's usefulness: one destroys molecules for energy, the other builds them for beauty. Combustion is the destroyer. Every alkane — the gas in the braai cylinder, the petrol in the tank, the paraffin in the lamp — burns in plenty of oxygen down to the same two ashes: carbon dioxide and water. Nothing fancy survives; the carbon skeleton you learned to name is dismantled entirely, and what you harvest is the energy that held it together. That is why the alkane family IS the fuel family: their whole economic purpose is to be taken apart. Balancing the burn is a counting chore, always in the same order — carbons first, hydrogens second, oxygens last, because oxygen appears alone and adjusts freely.

Esterification is the builder, and it is closer to matchmaking than demolition. Take a carboxylic acid — vinegar's family, sharp-smelling — and an alcohol. Warm them with a drop of concentrated sulfuric acid as the matchmaker, and the two join at their oxygen ends, releasing one small water molecule as the ceremony's confetti. The product is an ester, and the family's party trick is fragrance: pear drops, pineapple, banana, apple — the smells of sweets and fruits are largely esters, and perfume and flavour houses build them exactly this way.

The name of an ester is its parentage read backwards: the alcohol's part first with a Y L ending, the acid's part second ending in O A T E. Ethanol plus ethanoic acid: ethyl ethanoate. Smell aside, the exam wants the bookkeeping: two parents, one catalyst, one ester, one water. Say all four and the marks are yours.

Your first questions are with you now — burn the skeleton for energy, or marry acid to alcohol and bottle the smell.

## Subtopic: The Molecule with Open Hands

Why do alkenes react so eagerly while alkanes sit inert? Picture the double bond as a molecule holding one of its own bonds like a spare, outstretched hand. An alkane has every hand gripped tight — four single bonds per carbon, everyone occupied, nobody available. An alkene keeps one bond of its double pair loosely held, ready to release and offer two fresh grips, one on each of the doubled carbons. Addition is simply the world shaking those open hands.

Offer hydrogen with a metal catalyst, and each carbon grips a hydrogen: the alkene becomes an alkane. That is how runny plant oil becomes spreadable margarine — factory hydrogen, nickel catalyst, open hands filled. Offer red-brown bromine and the colour vanishes as fast as the hands can grab: the instant decolourising of bromine is chemistry's quickest test — open hands present, alkene confirmed; colour lingering, alkane, hands full. Offer water with an acid helper, and one carbon takes the hydroxyl, the other takes the spare hydrogen: the alkene becomes an alcohol.

When the offered molecule has two DIFFERENT pieces — hydrogen bromide, say, one hydrogen and one bromine — the alkene must choose which carbon gets which. The rule sounds like gossip but scores like science: the incoming hydrogen joins the carbon that already holds more hydrogens. The rich get richer. On propene, the end carbon of the double bond owns two hydrogens, the middle carbon just one, so the newcomer hydrogen joins the end, the bromine takes the middle, and 2-bromopropane dominates the product bottle.

Questions on this section are coming to you now — full hands sit still, open hands grab, and the extra hydrogen always joins the richer carbon.

## Subtopic: The Dance Floor Rules

Here is the secret that unlocks classification questions: molecules do not choose their reactions — CONDITIONS choose for them. Same partners, different lighting, completely different dance. The exam hands you the conditions precisely because the conditions are the answer.

Watch one molecule prove it. Take 2-bromobutane and sodium hydroxide — one pairing, two possible fates. Serve the sodium hydroxide DILUTE, dissolved in water, with gentle warmth, and you get the polite swap: the hydroxide taps the bromine on the shoulder, takes its exact place on the chain, and the product is an alcohol. Substitution: calm conditions, courteous exchange, saturation untouched. Now serve the SAME sodium hydroxide concentrated, dissolved in ethanol, at a rolling boil, and the mood turns: instead of swapping in, the reagent rips a hydrogen off the neighbouring carbon while the bromine storms out the other side, and the chain snaps a double bond into the gap. Elimination: harsh conditions, dramatic exit, an alkene where a haloalkane stood. Dilute, watery and warm — swap. Concentrated, ethanolic and hot — eject. Learn those two passwords and the classification marks are banked.

The lighting rule runs the alkane floor too. Alkanes and bromine ignore each other in the dark — full hands, no interest. Switch on ultraviolet light and the swap begins: one hydrogen out, one bromine in, hydrogen bromide walking away with the leftovers. No light, no reaction: the condition IS the mechanism's permission slip.

So read every organic reaction question in this order: first the conditions, then the reactant, then the product type follows almost automatically. Heat with concentrated acid: dehydration, a double bond is coming. Light on an alkane: substitution. Catalyst and hydrogen: addition, hands being filled. The last questions of the lesson are yours now — the dance is chosen by the lighting, and the exam always prints the lighting.
