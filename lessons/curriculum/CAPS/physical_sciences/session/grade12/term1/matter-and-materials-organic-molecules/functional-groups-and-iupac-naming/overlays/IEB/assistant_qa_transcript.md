**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! The first one arrives every single year: nine families, nine suffixes, nine general formulae — how is anyone supposed to hold all of that in one head?

**Tutor:** By refusing to store them as nine unrelated facts, because the nine families are really three small clusters, each with its own internal logic. Cluster one is the hydrocarbons, and it is arithmetic: the alkanes carry only single bonds at C n H two n plus two; remove one pair of hydrogens to make space for a double bond and you have the alkenes at C n H two n; remove a second pair for a triple bond and you have the alkynes at C n H two n minus two. One staircase, two hydrogens shorter at each step — three formulae from one pattern. Cluster two is the decorated-chain pair: an alcohol is a chain wearing a hydroxyl group, a haloalkane is a chain wearing a halogen. Cluster three is the carbonyl quartet — aldehyde, ketone, carboxylic acid, ester — and the whole cluster turns on one question: where does the carbon-oxygen double bond sit, and what else does its carbon hold? At the end with a hydrogen, inside the chain, at the end with a hydroxyl, or bridged through an oxygen to a second chain. Learn those three logics and then let practice do the rest: every time a name ends in A L, say "carbonyl at the end" before anything else. After twenty structures the families stop being a list and start being faces you recognise.

**Mandy:** Next: when a molecule has both a double bond and a branch, learners freeze over which one controls the numbering. What is the ranking?

**Tutor:** The functional group wins, every time, with no exceptions at this level. Number the chain so the family's defining group — the double bond, the hydroxyl, the carbonyl — takes the lowest number available, and let the substituents pick up whatever numbers remain. Consider the five-carbon chain with a double bond between the second and third carbons and a methyl group two carbons further along. Numbering from the end nearest the double bond gives the double bond position two and the methyl position four: 4-methylpent-2-ene. Numbering from the other end would flatter the methyl with a two but push the double bond to three, and that name is simply wrong, because the group that names the family also anchors the numbering. A helpful order of precedence to carry with you: carboxylic acids and aldehydes are the strongest — their carbon is always carbon one, so their names never even need a position number; then ketones, alcohols and the double or triple bonds claim the lowest number the chain allows; and alkyl branches and halogens queue at the back, numbered last. Only in a plain branched alkane, where no functional group exists at all, do the substituents themselves get to choose the direction. Say the ranking aloud before you number, and the numbering never betrays you.

**Mandy:** This one starts arguments in my study group: pentan-1-ol and pentan-2-ol have the same atoms, the same chain, the same family. Why does chemistry insist they are two different compounds?

**Tutor:** Because chemistry is a science of arrangements, not of ingredient lists, and moving one group by one carbon genuinely changes what the substance does. Physically, the two alcohols have measurably different boiling points and densities: shifting the hydroxyl from the end of the chain to the second carbon changes the molecule's shape, and shape controls how neighbouring molecules pack together and how well their attractions line up — machinery the next lesson builds properly. Chemically the difference cuts deeper: the carbon carrying the hydroxyl has different surroundings in each compound — the end carbon of pentan-1-ol holds one carbon neighbour, while the second carbon of pentan-2-ol holds two — and later reactions produce different products because of exactly that difference. Think of carbon and its arrangements this way: graphite in your pencil and the diamond in a ring are both pure carbon, and nobody calls them the same material. The consequence for your written work is strict: a structure with the hydroxyl drawn on the wrong carbon is not nearly right — it is a different compound with a different name, and everything built on it collapses. Treat position numbers like the digits of a phone number: one digit out and you have reached somebody else entirely.

**Mandy:** Last one: molecular, condensed and structural formulae keep getting mixed up under time pressure, and the wrong kind gets drawn. What is the clean way to keep the three separate?

**Tutor:** Anchor each type of formula to the question it can answer, and then to the instruction word that asks for it. The molecular formula answers only "how many of each atom?" — C five H twelve, full stop; it cannot tell pentane from 2-methylbutane, which is precisely why isomer questions are built on it. The structural formula answers "which atom is bonded to which?" — every atom drawn, every bond drawn as its own line, including each carbon-hydrogen bond; it is the only representation with zero ambiguity. The condensed formula lives between the two: it walks the chain carbon by carbon, each carbon written with its own hydrogens — C H three, C H two, C H two, C H two, C H three for pentane — branches shown in brackets after the carbon that carries them, no bond lines at all. Now attach the instruction words. "Write down the molecular formula" wants the census and nothing more; drawing structure there is time thrown away. "Draw the structural formula" wants every bond as a line — offering a condensed formula there earns nothing, and the commonest loss in the whole topic is writing the condensed chain when the full drawing was demanded. "Write the condensed formula" wants the compact walk, and a full drawing there wastes minutes you will want later. Then one closing habit that guards all three: whenever you finish any structure, audit every carbon for exactly four bonds. The audit catches missing hydrogens and impossible five-bond carbons while they are still free to fix.
