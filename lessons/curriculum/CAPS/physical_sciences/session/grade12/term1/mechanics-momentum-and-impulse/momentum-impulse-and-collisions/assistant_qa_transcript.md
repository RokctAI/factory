**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First, the one that comes up every single year: momentum and kinetic energy both come from mass and velocity, so why do we need both? What is actually different about them?

**Tutor:** They measure different things and they obey different rules, and a collision is exactly where the difference shows. Momentum is mass multiplied by velocity — a vector, with a direction, and it is conserved in every collision in an isolated system, elastic or not. Kinetic energy is a half multiplied by mass multiplied by the velocity squared — a scalar, no direction, and it is only conserved in the special case of an elastic collision. The squaring is the key difference. Because velocity is squared, kinetic energy cannot cancel: two identical cars driving toward each other have a total momentum of zero, since the vectors point opposite ways, but an enormous total kinetic energy, since both squares are positive. When they collide head-on and stop dead, momentum is perfectly conserved — zero before, zero after — while nearly all the kinetic energy converts into heat, sound and crushed metal. So use momentum to find velocities after a collision, because it always survives; use kinetic energy as an auditor, to classify the collision as elastic or inelastic. One is the bookkeeping, the other is the health check, and the examiner routinely asks for both in the same question.

**Mandy:** Next: in the rebound problems, learners keep getting 1 instead of 9 for the change in momentum of the ball that hits the wall at 10 and comes back at 8. What is the reliable way to never make that mistake?

**Tutor:** Write the sign convention down as the first line of the solution, before any formula — "let toward the wall be positive" — and then refuse to write any velocity without its sign. The error comes from treating delta v as "big speed minus small speed", which subtracts magnitudes. But velocity is a vector, so the final velocity of the rebounding ball is negative 8, not 8. Delta p is m v final minus m v initial: 0,5 multiplied by negative 8 gives negative 4; minus 0,5 multiplied by positive 10 takes away another 5; total, negative 9 kilogram metres per second, which you then report as 9 kilogram metres per second away from the wall. Notice the rhythm: substitute WITH signs, calculate, and only translate the sign back into a direction in the final sentence. A useful self-check is physical: a ball that bounces back has had a bigger change of motion than a ball that merely stops, so the rebound answer must be BIGGER than m times the arrival speed. If your answer came out smaller, the signs went missing somewhere. The same discipline carries straight into conservation problems, where one object usually moves negatively after the collision, and into impulse, where the force direction comes out of the sign of your answer for free.

**Mandy:** Here is one from the safety section. If the change in momentum in a crash is fixed, what exactly does a crumple zone change — and why do questions insist we mention time and not just distance?

**Tutor:** The crumple zone changes only one thing the theorem cares about: the duration of the stop. The car and passenger arrive with a momentum fixed by their mass and speed, and that momentum must reach zero — nothing negotiates that. Newton's second law in momentum form says the net force equals the change in momentum divided by the time taken, so with the numerator locked, the only lever left is the denominator. A rigid car stops in a few hundredths of a second; a car whose front end folds progressively keeps decelerating over several tenths of a second. Multiply the time by ten and the average force drops by ten, and it is force that breaks bones. The reason your explanation must run through time is that the impulse-momentum theorem is stated in time — F net delta t equals m delta v — and the marking guideline awards marks for exactly that chain: delta p is constant, delta t increases, therefore F net decreases. Mentioning distance is not wrong physically, since stopping over a longer distance is how the longer time is achieved, but distance belongs to the work-energy way of seeing the same crash, which is next term's tool. In this topic, the currency is time. And add the quiet bonus: the crumpling also deliberately converts kinetic energy into deformation, which is why the collision is designed to be as inelastic as possible.

**Mandy:** Last one: the principle says momentum is conserved in an isolated system, but no real system is isolated — there is always friction somewhere. Why are we allowed to use conservation for car crashes at all?

**Tutor:** Because of the timescale, and this is a genuinely mature point to make in an answer. Conservation needs the net EXTERNAL force to be negligible during the interval you apply it over, and a collision lasts only a few hundredths of a second. In that sliver of time, the forces the colliding cars exert on each other are enormous — thousands of newtons, often far more — while friction from the road is comparatively tiny. The impulse of a force is force multiplied by time, so a modest external force acting for a very short time delivers almost no impulse, and the total momentum immediately before and immediately after the impact is as good as unchanged. That is why the standard method compares the instant before contact with the instant after contact, and no later. Once the collision is over and the wreckage slides, friction has all the time it wants, delivers real impulse, and the momentum drains away — which is why you may not apply conservation between the moment of impact and the place the cars finally stop. So read the phrase "ignore friction" in a question as permission to treat those few hundredths of a second as isolated, and keep your before and after snapshots tight against the moment of contact. Choosing the snapshots well is most of the skill.
