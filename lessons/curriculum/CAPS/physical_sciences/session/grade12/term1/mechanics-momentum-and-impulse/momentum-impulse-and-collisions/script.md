# Part 1 — Expert

Momentum opens the Grade 12 physics year and anchors the first section of Paper 1. This session defines momentum and impulse in the wording the marking guideline expects, works Newton's second law in its momentum form, and builds the two calculations that carry the topic: the impulse-momentum theorem for a single object, and conservation of momentum for two objects colliding in one dimension. Everything is a vector, so a stated positive direction sits at the top of every solution.

## Subtopic: Momentum and Newton's Second Law in Momentum Form

The definition, exactly: linear momentum is the product of an object's mass and its velocity. In symbols, p equals m v — momentum equals mass multiplied by velocity. The unit is kilogram metre per second, and momentum is a VECTOR, pointing in the same direction as the velocity. Both parts of the product matter: a delivery truck creeping through a parking area and a bullet leaving a rifle can carry comparable momentum, one through its enormous mass, the other through its enormous velocity.

Work the first calculation. A 1 200 kilogram bakkie travels at 20 metres per second east along the N1. Formula: p equals m v. Substitution: 1 200 multiplied by 20. Answer: 24 000 kilogram metres per second, east. The direction is part of the answer, and leaving it out costs the mark.

Change in momentum is where the vector nature bites. Delta p equals m v final minus m v initial, and because these are vectors, the signs do the work. A 0,5 kilogram ball strikes a wall at 10 metres per second and rebounds at 8 metres per second. Take the direction toward the wall as positive. Initial momentum: 0,5 multiplied by positive 10, which is 5 kilogram metres per second. Final momentum: 0,5 multiplied by negative 8, which is negative 4. The change: negative 4 minus 5, which is negative 9 — a change in momentum of 9 kilogram metres per second directed away from the wall. Learners who subtract 8 from 10 and answer 1 have treated vectors as numbers, and that error is the most common one in the whole topic.

Now the law. Newton's second law in terms of momentum: the net force acting on an object is equal to the rate of change of momentum of the object. In symbols, F net equals delta p divided by delta t — the change in momentum divided by the time over which it changes. This is the more general form of the second law, and the familiar F net equals m a falls out of it whenever the mass stays constant.

This section's questions are with you now: the definition word for word, a stated positive direction, and vector subtraction done with signs.

## Subtopic: Impulse and the Impulse-Momentum Theorem

Impulse is the product of the net force and the contact time: impulse equals F net multiplied by delta t. Its unit is the newton second, which is exactly the same as the kilogram metre per second — the units of momentum — and that is no accident, because the impulse-momentum theorem says F net delta t equals m delta v: the impulse delivered to an object equals its change in momentum.

Work it with a cricket ball. A bowler delivers a 0,16 kilogram ball at 30 metres per second, and the batsman drives it straight back at 40 metres per second. Take the direction back toward the bowler as positive, so the ball arrives at negative 30 and leaves at positive 40. Formula: impulse equals m v final minus m v initial. Substitution: 0,16 multiplied by 40, minus 0,16 multiplied by negative 30 — that is 6,4 plus 4,8. Answer: 11,2 newton seconds toward the bowler. If bat and ball touch for 0,01 seconds, the average net force is 11,2 divided by 0,01, which is 1 120 newtons — more than the weight of a full grown man, delivered in a hundredth of a second.

The theorem's real power is the trade between force and time. The change in momentum in a crash is fixed by the masses and velocities; nothing can alter it. What CAN be altered is how long the change takes, and since F net equals delta p divided by delta t, doubling the stopping time halves the average force. That single sentence explains every safety device in the syllabus. An airbag stretches the stopping time of your head from milliseconds to tenths of a second. A crumple zone folds up so the car stops over a longer distance and time. A gravel arrestor bed beside a steep pass stops a runaway truck slowly. A fielder pulls the hands back while catching, and a gymnast bends the knees on landing, for exactly the same reason.

The exam wants the reasoning in that order: delta p is constant, delta t increases, therefore F net decreases. Writing "the airbag is soft" earns nothing.

Questions on impulse are in front of you now — keep the signs honest and quote the theorem before substituting.

## Subtopic: Conservation of Linear Momentum

The principle, exactly: the total linear momentum of an isolated system remains constant, in both magnitude and direction. An ISOLATED system, sometimes called a closed system, is one on which no net external force acts. Internal forces — the forces the colliding objects exert on each other — occur in equal and opposite pairs by Newton's third law, so they can redistribute momentum inside the system but never change the total. External forces, like friction from outside or a push from a hand, are the ones that would break the balance, and the standard exam phrase "ignore the effects of friction" is the signal that the system may be treated as isolated.

The method: define the system, state the positive direction, write total momentum before equals total momentum after, and substitute with signs.

Work a recoil problem. A 4 kilogram rifle, initially at rest, fires a 0,02 kilogram bullet at 400 metres per second. Before firing, the total momentum is zero, so it must be zero afterwards as well. Take the bullet's direction as positive. Zero equals 0,02 multiplied by 400, plus 4 multiplied by the rifle's velocity. The bullet carries 8 kilogram metres per second forward, so the rifle must carry 8 backward: its velocity is negative 2 metres per second — a recoil of 2 metres per second opposite to the bullet. Notice the momenta are equal and opposite while the velocities are wildly different, because the masses differ by a factor of 200.

The same equation runs every explosion, every springing-apart of two trolleys, and every docking of two vehicles. Momentum conservation asks nothing about what happened during the contact — no forces, no times — which is exactly why it is so useful.

This section's questions arrive now: define the system, state the principle in full, and let zero totals do the heavy lifting.

## Subtopic: Collisions in One Dimension — Elastic or Inelastic

Two objects collide along a straight line; momentum conservation finds the missing velocity, and a kinetic energy check classifies the collision.

Work the standard case. A 2 kilogram trolley moving at 3 metres per second collides with a stationary 1 kilogram trolley, and they couple together. Take the initial motion as positive. Momentum before: 2 multiplied by 3, plus 1 multiplied by 0, which is 6 kilogram metres per second. Momentum after: the combined 3 kilograms multiplied by v. So v equals 6 divided by 3, which is 2 metres per second in the original direction.

Now classify it. An ELASTIC collision is one in which total kinetic energy is conserved; an INELASTIC collision is one in which it is not. Momentum is conserved in both — that is the point learners forget — so the only test is a kinetic energy calculation, done separately for before and after. Before: a half multiplied by 2 multiplied by 3 squared, which is 9 joules. After: a half multiplied by 3 multiplied by 2 squared, which is 6 joules. Kinetic energy fell from 9 joules to 6 joules, so the collision is inelastic, and the missing 3 joules became heat, sound and deformation. Objects that stick together always give an inelastic result.

One more, at road scale. A 1 000 kilogram car travelling at 15 metres per second runs into the back of a stationary 1 500 kilogram bakkie and the vehicles lock together. Momentum before: 15 000 kilogram metres per second. Combined mass: 2 500 kilograms. Velocity after: 6 metres per second. Kinetic energy before: a half multiplied by 1 000 multiplied by 225, which is 112 500 joules; after: a half multiplied by 2 500 multiplied by 36, which is 45 000 joules. Most of the energy went into crushed metal — inelastic, by calculation, not by guesswork.

The final expert questions are with you now: momentum equation first, kinetic energy audit second, verdict last, with every value signed and in its unit.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same physics from the taxi rank and the kitchen, where momentum is something you already understand with your hands.

## Subtopic: Moving Oomph — Why Mass and Speed Both Count

Imagine catching a tennis ball thrown across a room. Easy. Now imagine catching a cricket ball at the same speed. Your hands already know the difference: the cricket ball carries more of something, and that something is momentum — moving oomph, if you like. It is simply mass multiplied by speed, with a direction attached.

Both ingredients count. A fully loaded coal truck rolling at walking pace is carrying huge momentum, because of its mass. A pellet from an air rifle carries plenty too, because of its speed. Double either one and you double the oomph. That is the whole formula: p equals m v.

The direction matters more than it looks. A ball that hits a wall at 10 and bounces back at 8 has not changed its momentum by a little — it has changed it by a lot, because it first had to lose all of its forward oomph and then gain a new supply in the opposite direction. Going from positive 10 to negative 8 is a bigger change than going from 10 to zero. That is why physics insists on calling one direction positive and sticking to it: the signs are doing real work, keeping track of which way the oomph points.

And the deep rule underneath: the only thing that can change an object's momentum is a net force from OUTSIDE, and the faster the change must happen, the bigger the force has to be.

Your first questions are with you now — think of momentum as oomph with a direction, and mind those signs.

## Subtopic: The Egg and the Duvet

Here is an experiment you can picture without breaking anything. Throw an egg at a brick wall: it smashes. Throw the same egg, just as hard, at a duvet hanging on a washing line: it survives. Same egg, same speed, same change in momentum. So what saved it?

Time. Against the wall, the egg's momentum is wiped out in a millisecond, and cramming that change into a tiny time demands a huge force. The duvet gives way, and the same change in momentum happens over maybe half a second. Same total change, a thousand times more time, a thousand times less force. The egg's shell can handle that.

That trade — same change, more time, less force — is worth memorising as a sentence, because it is behind every safety question in the exam. An airbag is a duvet for your face. A crumple zone is a car agreeing to be squashed so that the stopping stretches out. The gravel bed at the bottom of a mountain pass is a duvet for a runaway truck. A fielder taking a hard catch lets the hands ride backwards; a netball player landing from a jump bends her knees. Every one of them is buying time to shrink the force.

When the question asks you to explain, give all three steps: the change in momentum is fixed; the safety device increases the time over which it happens; therefore the force is smaller. Three short sentences, full marks.

Questions on this section are coming to you now — find the duvet in each scenario.

## Subtopic: Trolleys That Crash and Stick

Push a loaded shopping trolley gently into a stationary empty one so that they lock and roll off together. Here is the remarkable thing: the total oomph just before they touch equals the total oomph just after. Momentum is not created or destroyed in the crash — it is only shared out.

Feel it in the numbers. Your loaded trolley, 2 units of mass at 3 of speed, carries 6 units of oomph. It hits a stationary trolley of 1 mass unit and they stick, so now 3 units of mass share the same 6 units of oomph — and 6 shared over 3 is a speed of 2. The pair rolls off slower than the original trolley, but nothing has been lost: less speed, more mass, same total momentum.

Why is the total protected? Because during the crash the trolleys only push on EACH OTHER — equal and opposite, by Newton's third law — so whatever momentum one loses, the other gains, cent for cent, like money changing pockets inside the same pair of trousers. Only an outside force, a hand or serious friction, can change the total.

Energy plays by a different rule. Check the movement energy before and after the trolley crash and some has vanished — into sound, into a jolt, into slightly bent wire. Collisions where movement energy survives are called elastic; collisions where it leaks away are inelastic, and anything that crumples or sticks is inelastic. So remember the split verdict: momentum, always conserved in the crash; kinetic energy, only sometimes — and only a calculation can tell you.

The last questions of the lesson are yours now — share out the oomph first, then audit the energy.
