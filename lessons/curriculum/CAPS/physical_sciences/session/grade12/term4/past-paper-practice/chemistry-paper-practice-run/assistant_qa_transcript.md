**Mandy:** Questions from the class about the chemistry practice run! First: the organic section opens every paper — what separates the candidates who finish it quickly from those who get stuck there?

**Tutor:** The quick candidates treat organic as recognition, not invention, because that is what the paper actually tests. Before writing anything they classify: which homologous series — read the functional group; which reaction type — count the bonds, double bond consumed is addition, double bond created is elimination, a straight swap on a saturated molecule is substitution. That classification takes seconds and dictates every mark that follows. The stuck candidates skip it and try to reason each molecule from scratch. Then two rehearsed lists finish the section: the naming algorithm — longest chain containing the group, lowest number to the group, substituents alphabetical — run confidently in both directions; and the conditions table — light for halogenating an alkane, concentrated acid and heat for dehydration, strong base in ethanol for elimination against dilute aqueous base for substitution. That last contrast is the modern favourite: same reagents, different conditions, different product, and the marks go to whoever quotes the conditions precisely. One more habit worth its weight: before calling anything an isomer, count the atoms — same formula first, different structure second, always in that order.

***

**Mandy:** Next: the stoichiometry chains — purity, yield, titrations. Learners know each step but lose the thread mid-question. What holds the chain together?

**Tutor:** Write the chain BEFORE the arithmetic, as a line of signposts: volume to moles, moles to moles, moles to mass, mass to percentage. The purity question in the walk is the template — gas volume through the molar volume at STP, the one-to-one ratio through the balanced equation, the molar mass assembled from the periodic table, then the percentage. Each signpost is a conversion with exactly one tool, and the memo pays per signpost, so the plan IS the mark scheme. The thread snaps in predictable places, and you can guard them all. First, the ratio must come from a BALANCED equation, read aloud from its coefficients — never assumed. Second, units must arrive standard before substitution: cubic centimetres to cubic decimetres, grams staying grams; the titration walk hid a mark exactly there, in 25 cubic centimetres becoming 0,025. Third, the final answer must pass its own logic — purity between nought and one hundred percent, a concentration that is positive and plausible. When an answer fails that test, walk the signposts backward; the broken link is usually the ratio or a unit, and it is faster to find than to redo the question.

***

**Mandy:** The equilibrium and titration questions both hid a divide-by-volume step. Why does that one line cost so many candidates so much?

**Tutor:** Because moles and concentration feel interchangeable right up until the moment they are not, and both questions place that moment mid-calculation, where habit runs ahead of thought. The RICE table is naturally a mole machine — initial moles, change in moles through the balanced ratios, equilibrium moles — and it should be, because ratios live in moles. But K c is defined on concentrations, moles per cubic decimetre, so between the table and the constant stands one compulsory line: divide every equilibrium amount by the vessel's volume. In the sulfur trioxide walk that line turned 0,2 moles into 0,1 moles per cubic decimetre, and skipping it changes the answer and forfeits everything downstream. My advice is mechanical: give the division its own labelled row in the table, every time, even when the volume is one cubic decimetre and the numbers do not change — because the habit, not the arithmetic, is what saves you. The titration mirror image is the volume in cubic centimetres: concentration times volume only yields moles when the volume speaks cubic decimetres. Two small lines, both pure discipline, and together they are worth more than any clever insight in the paper.

**Mandy:** Last one: electrochemistry lands at the end of the paper when everyone is tired. What is the survival routine for the galvanic cell question?

**Tutor:** Lean on the table of standard electrode potentials and let it make every decision, because tired reasoning is exactly what the table exists to replace. The routine has five fixed moves. One: compare the two reduction potentials; the higher one runs as written — reduction, cathode; the lower one reverses — oxidation, anode. In the silver-zinc cell, silver's plus 0,80 beats zinc's minus 0,76, so zinc reverses and is the anode. Two: write the half-reactions with the electrons visible, on the correct sides. Three: the emf — E cathode minus E anode — and speak the double negative deliberately: 0,80 minus, minus 0,76, is 1,56 volts; that sign slip is the most common error in the entire paper, and saying the line aloud in your head defeats it. Four: balance the electrons before adding the half-reactions — double the silver half-reaction so two electrons cancel two. Five: the audit — a positive emf must accompany a spontaneous cell, and the anode must be losing mass while the cathode gains. Add the two sentences that never change and are always worth marks: oxidation at the anode, reduction at the cathode; and the salt bridge completes the circuit by ion migration, keeping both solutions neutral. Five moves and two sentences — a routine strong enough to survive the last page of any paper.
