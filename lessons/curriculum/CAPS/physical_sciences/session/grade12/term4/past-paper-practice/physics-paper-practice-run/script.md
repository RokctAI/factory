# Part 1 — Expert

This session is a practice run through a Paper 1 built to the examination guideline: seven questions in the order the real paper uses them, from Newton's laws to the photoelectric effect. Each question is stated in full, attacked with a plan before any substitution, worked to the final answer, and priced — every step tagged with the mark it earns, exactly as the memo tags it. The aim is examination pace: read, plan, execute, audit, move on.

## Subtopic: Question Walk — Newton's Laws on an Incline

The question, as the paper would print it. An 8 kilogram block rests on a plane inclined at 30 degrees to the horizontal. A force of 80 newtons, parallel to the incline, pulls the block up the slope. The kinetic frictional force is 10 newtons. First, draw a labelled free-body diagram. Second, state Newton's second law in words. Third, calculate the acceleration of the block.

The attack plan: this is the force story. Isolate the block, list every force, split the weight into components along and perpendicular to the slope, then apply net force equals mass times acceleration along the slope.

The free-body diagram earns its marks by completeness: weight straight down, normal force perpendicular to the slope, applied force up the slope, friction down the slope because the block moves up. Four labelled arrows — typically one mark for the forces being present and correct, one for labels and directions.

The statement mark: when a net force acts on an object, the object accelerates in the direction of the force, the acceleration directly proportional to the net force and inversely proportional to the mass. Word-perfect recall — usually two marks.

Now the calculation. The weight component along the slope: m g sine theta, which is 8 times 9,8 times 0,5 — 39,2 newtons, pulling down the slope. Taking up the slope as positive, the net force is 80 minus 39,2 minus 10, which is 30,8 newtons. That line typically carries two marks: one for each opposing force correctly subtracted. Then Newton's second law: acceleration equals 30,8 divided by 8 — 3,85 metres per second squared, directed up the slope. Formula, one mark; substitution, one mark; answer with unit and direction, one mark. The audit before moving on: the answer is positive in the chosen direction and smaller than the frictionless value would be — sensible.

## Subtopic: Question Walk — Momentum, Impulse and the Collision

The question. Trolley A, mass 5 kilograms, moves at 4 metres per second to the right on a frictionless track and couples with stationary trolley B, mass 3 kilograms. First, state the principle of conservation of linear momentum. Second, calculate the velocity of the combined trolleys. Third, show by calculation whether the collision is elastic or inelastic. Fourth, calculate the magnitude of the impulse on trolley B.

The plan: a coupling collision is the momentum ledger — total before equals total after — then an energy comparison, then impulse as change of momentum of ONE trolley.

The statement: the total linear momentum of an isolated system remains constant. Two marks, and the word isolated must appear.

The velocity: total momentum before is 5 times 4 plus 3 times nought, which is 20 kilogram metres per second. After coupling the mass is 8 kilograms, so the shared velocity is 20 over 8 — 2,5 metres per second to the right. Formula and substitution, two marks; answer with direction, one.

Elastic or inelastic is decided by kinetic energy. Before: half times 5 times 16, which is 40 joules. After: half times 8 times 2,5 squared, which is half times 8 times 6,25 — 25 joules. Kinetic energy fell from 40 to 25 joules, so the collision is inelastic. Both calculations, one mark each; the conclusion drawn FROM the numbers, one mark — a conclusion without the comparison earns nothing.

Impulse on trolley B equals its change in momentum: 3 times 2,5 minus nought, which is 7,5 kilogram metres per second, or equivalently 7,5 newton seconds. One mark for the theorem, one for the answer. The quiet check: trolley A lost 5 times, 4 minus 2,5 — 7,5 of momentum. Equal and opposite, exactly as Newton's third law demands.

## Subtopic: Question Walk — Vertical Projectile Motion

The question. A ball is thrown vertically upward from the ground at 19,6 metres per second. Ignore air resistance. Calculate, first, the time to reach the highest point; second, the maximum height; third, the velocity of the ball at 3 seconds; and fourth, sketch the velocity-time graph for the full flight, back to the ground.

The plan: free fall throughout — the only force is gravity, acceleration 9,8 downward the entire time, including the top. Choose upward as positive and say so.

Time to the top: the velocity there is zero, so from v f equals v i minus g t, nought equals 19,6 minus 9,8 t, giving t equals 2 seconds. Formula, substitution, answer — three marks in most memos.

Maximum height: v f squared equals v i squared minus 2 g delta y with v f nought, so delta y equals 19,6 squared over twice 9,8, which is 384,16 over 19,6 — 19,6 metres. Two to three marks: formula, substitution, answer with unit.

Velocity at 3 seconds: v equals 19,6 minus 9,8 times 3, which is 19,6 minus 29,4 — minus 9,8 metres per second. The sign is the physics: the ball is already descending, so the answer reads 9,8 metres per second downward. Answer without direction forfeits the final mark.

The graph: one straight line starting at plus 19,6, falling with constant gradient minus 9,8, crossing zero at 2 seconds, reaching minus 19,6 at 4 seconds when the ball lands. Marks are paid for the straight single line, the correct intercepts, and the zero crossing at the top of the flight. The symmetry is the audit: up in 2 seconds, down in 2 seconds, landing speed equal to launch speed.

## Subtopic: Question Walk — Work, Energy and Power

The question. A 20 kilogram crate is dragged from rest across a rough horizontal floor by a horizontal force of 100 newtons. The frictional force is 30 newtons. Using energy methods, calculate the speed of the crate after 5 metres. Then, in a separate scenario, a motor lifts a 300 kilogram load through 12 metres at constant speed in 20 seconds; calculate the power output of the motor.

The plan for the crate: the work-energy theorem — net work equals change in kinetic energy — because the question links a distance to a speed and names energy methods. Marks are commonly lost by reaching for equations of motion instead; the instruction using energy methods is binding.

Net force on the crate: 100 minus 30, which is 70 newtons. Net work over 5 metres: 70 times 5, which is 350 joules — and with cosine of nought degrees equal to one, the full force counts. The theorem: 350 equals half times 20 times v squared. Then v squared equals 35, and v equals the square root of 35 — 5,92 metres per second. The mark trail: net work calculated, two marks; theorem stated or used, one; substitution, one; answer with unit, one.

The motor: at constant speed the lifting force equals the weight, 300 times 9,8, which is 2 940 newtons. Work done: 2 940 times 12 — 35 280 joules. Power: work over time, 35 280 divided by 20, which is 1 764 watts. Three to four marks: force or work line, power formula, substitution, answer. The audit: just under 2 kilowatts to lift three hundred kilograms through four storeys in twenty seconds — a plausible industrial motor, so the magnitude passes the sanity test.

## Subtopic: Question Walk — The Doppler Effect

The question. An ambulance approaches a stationary listener at 30 metres per second, siren emitting 800 hertz. Take the speed of sound as 340 metres per second. Calculate the frequency the listener hears, first as the ambulance approaches, then after it passes and recedes. Finally, state ONE medical application of the Doppler effect.

The plan: one equation covers both parts — observed frequency equals, v plus or minus v listener, over, v minus or plus v source, times the source frequency. The listener is stationary, so the numerator stays 340. The only decision is the sign in the denominator, and the reasoning that never fails: approach must raise the frequency, so the denominator must shrink — subtract the source speed; recession must lower it — add the source speed.

Approaching: observed frequency equals 340 over, 340 minus 30, times 800, which is 340 over 310 times 800. That is 272 000 over 310 — 877,42 hertz. Higher than 800, as approach demands. Formula, one mark; correct sign choice and substitution, two; answer with unit, one.

Receding: 340 over 370, times 800, which is 272 000 over 370 — 735,14 hertz. Lower than the source, as it must be. The paired audit is free and powerful: approach answer above 800, recession answer below, and the two answers are not symmetric about 800 — nor should they be, because the denominators differ.

The application mark: the measurement of blood flow speed by ultrasound — Doppler flow meters — or the monitoring of a foetal heartbeat. One clear sentence, one mark. And the red shift cousin belongs in the same drawer: light from distant galaxies shifted toward lower frequencies tells us the galaxies recede, the evidence for an expanding universe.

## Subtopic: Question Walk — The Circuit with Internal Resistance

The question. A battery of emf 20 volts and internal resistance 1 ohm is connected to a 5 ohm resistor in series with two 8 ohm resistors in parallel. Calculate, first, the total resistance of the circuit; second, the main current; third, the terminal voltage; fourth, the current through one 8 ohm resistor; and fifth, the power dissipated in the 5 ohm resistor.

The plan: outside-in, always. Collapse the network, find the main current with the internal resistance included, then walk back into the branches, and audit the books at the end.

Total resistance: the parallel pair first — product over sum, 64 over 16, which is 4 ohms. External resistance: 5 plus 4 — 9 ohms. Total including the battery's insides: 9 plus 1, which is 10 ohms. Three marks, one per stage, and the parallel answer passing its own check — 4 is smaller than 8, as parallel demands.

Main current: emf over total resistance, 20 over 10 — 2 amperes. Formula and answer, two marks.

Terminal voltage: emf minus the lost volts, 20 minus 2 times 1, which is 18 volts. Two marks, and the concept behind it is worth rehearsing aloud: 2 volts of the emf are spent inside the battery as heat.

The branch current: the 5 ohm resistor takes 2 times 5 — 10 volts — leaving 18 minus 10, which is 8 volts across the parallel pair. Each 8 ohm branch carries 8 over 8 — 1 ampere. Two to three marks along that chain. The audit: 1 plus 1 equals the main current, 2 amperes, exactly; the books balance.

Power in the 5 ohm resistor: I squared R, which is 4 times 5 — 20 watts. Formula, substitution, answer with unit. Choose the power formula whose quantities you have already found; chasing new quantities invites new errors.

## Subtopic: Question Walk — The Photoelectric Effect

The question. Light of wavelength 400 nanometres strikes a metal whose work function is 3,68 times 10 to the minus 19 joules. First, define work function. Second, calculate the energy of one photon. Third, calculate the maximum kinetic energy of an ejected photoelectron. Fourth, state, with a reason, what happens to this maximum kinetic energy if the light's intensity is doubled.

The plan: the photoelectric equation, E equals W nought plus maximum kinetic energy, with the photon energy found from E equals h c over lambda since a wavelength is given, not a frequency.

The definition: the work function is the minimum energy an electron in the metal needs to be emitted from the metal surface. Two marks, and minimum is the non-negotiable word.

Photon energy: h c over lambda — 6,63 times 10 to the minus 34, times 3 times 10 to the 8, over 400 times 10 to the minus 9. The numerator is 1,989 times 10 to the minus 25; divided by 4 times 10 to the minus 7 it gives 4,97 times 10 to the minus 19 joules. Formula, one mark; substitution with the wavelength converted to metres, one; answer, one. The conversion of nanometres is where this question quietly fails candidates.

Maximum kinetic energy: photon energy minus work function, 4,97 minus 3,68, times 10 to the minus 19 — 1,29 times 10 to the minus 19 joules. Positive, so emission genuinely happens — a built-in audit; a negative answer here means no emission, and quoting one as an energy is a physics error the memo punishes.

The intensity question: the maximum kinetic energy stays UNCHANGED. Reason: intensity sets the number of photons arriving per second, not the energy of each photon; each ejection is one photon giving its energy to one electron, so doubling intensity doubles the number of ejected electrons per second while each electron's energy budget is untouched. Statement, one mark; reason, one to two marks. Close the paper the way the guideline closes the topic: the photoelectric effect demonstrates the particle nature of light.
