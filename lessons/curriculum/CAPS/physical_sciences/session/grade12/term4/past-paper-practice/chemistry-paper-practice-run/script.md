# Part 1 — Expert

This session is a practice run through a Paper 2 built to the examination guideline: seven questions in the paper's own order, from organic naming through stoichiometry, rates, equilibrium and acids to the electrochemical cells. Each question is stated as the paper would print it, attacked with a plan before any writing, worked to the final answer, and priced step by step — every line tagged with the mark it earns. The aim is examination pace: read, plan, execute, audit, move on.

## Subtopic: Question Walk — Naming and Isomers

The question. Consider a compound with a four-carbon chain, a hydroxyl group on carbon one, and a methyl branch on carbon two. First, write down its IUPAC name. Second, name the homologous series. Third, draw or describe a chain isomer of butane. Fourth, define the term structural isomer.

The plan: run the naming algorithm in order — longest chain containing the functional group, lowest number to the group, substituents alphabetical with positions — and keep the definitions word-perfect.

The name: the parent chain has four carbons carrying an alcohol — butanol. The hydroxyl takes the lowest number, carbon one; the methyl branch sits on carbon two. The name assembles as 2-methylbutan-1-ol. The memo typically pays one mark for the correct parent and suffix, one for the hydroxyl position, one for the branch with its position. The audit: number from the other end and the hydroxyl would sit on carbon four — higher, so the chosen numbering stands.

The series: alcohols — one mark, and the general recognition sentence is worth having ready: a homologous series is a family described by one general formula, each member differing from the next by C H two.

The chain isomer of butane: same molecular formula, C four H ten, different skeleton — 2-methylpropane, the three-carbon chain wearing a methyl branch. One mark for the name or a correct structure. The definition mark: structural isomers are molecules with the same molecular formula but different structural formulae. Two marks, and both halves of the sentence must appear — same formula, different structure. The classic forfeit is naming a compound with a DIFFERENT formula, which is not an isomer at all; count atoms before answering, always.

## Subtopic: Question Walk — The Reaction Scheme

The question. A flow diagram shows propene reacting with hydrogen bromide to form compound X, the major product. X then reacts with a concentrated strong base in hot ethanol to form compound Y. First, name the type of reaction in each step. Second, identify X, with a reason. Third, identify Y.

The plan: bonds first. Step one consumes a double bond — addition, specifically hydrohalogenation. Step two removes hydrogen and a halogen from a saturated molecule to rebuild a double bond — elimination, specifically dehydrohalogenation. Two marks, one per type, and the specific names earn where the paper asks for them.

Compound X, with the reason the memo demands: the hydrogen atom adds preferentially to the carbon that already holds more hydrogen atoms — for propene, the end carbon of the double bond — so the bromine lands on the middle carbon. X is 2-bromopropane, the major product; 1-bromopropane is the minor. Identification, one mark; the rule stated as the reason, one to two marks. An answer naming the right compound without the rule leaves the reason marks on the table.

Compound Y: eliminating hydrogen and bromine from 2-bromopropane rebuilds the double bond — propene returns. One mark. The scheme is deliberately a round trip, and noticing that IS the audit: addition then elimination should hand back the original alkene.

The conditions line is a favourite two marks in this territory, so rehearse it as a chant: addition of hydrogen bromide needs no light and no catalyst; elimination wants concentrated strong base, dissolved in ethanol, with heat. Swap those conditions and the same reagents give substitution instead — the dilute aqueous base swaps bromine for hydroxyl and delivers propan-2-ol. Conditions are not decoration; they choose the product.

## Subtopic: Question Walk — Stoichiometry and Percentage Purity

The question. A 2,5 gram sample of impure calcium carbonate reacts with excess hydrochloric acid. The carbon dioxide released occupies 0,448 cubic decimetres at STP. Calculate the percentage purity of the sample. The equation: calcium carbonate plus two hydrochloric acid gives calcium chloride plus water plus carbon dioxide.

The plan: gas volume to moles through the molar volume, moles of gas to moles of carbonate through the balanced equation, moles to mass through the molar mass, and mass to percentage. Four conversions, one bridge each — write the plan as a chain before computing, because the chain is the marks.

Moles of carbon dioxide: volume over molar volume, 0,448 divided by 22,4 — 0,02 moles. Formula, one mark; answer, one. The balanced equation says one mole of carbonate releases one mole of carbon dioxide, so moles of pure calcium carbonate in the sample: 0,02 moles. That ratio line is its own mark, and quoting it explicitly — one to one, from the equation — is what secures it.

Mass of pure carbonate: moles times molar mass. Calcium 40, carbon 12, three oxygens 48 — molar mass 100 grams per mole. Mass equals 0,02 times 100 — 2 grams. Two marks: molar mass assembled, mass computed.

Percentage purity: pure mass over sample mass, times 100 — 2 over 2,5, times 100, which is 80 percent. Final answer with the percent sign, one mark. The audit: purity must land between nought and 100, and an answer above 100 means the chain broke — usually at the molar volume, used at the wrong temperature, or a ratio inverted. Eighty percent of a seashell being carbonate is chemically sensible; the number passes.

## Subtopic: Question Walk — Rates and the Collision Story

The question. A learner reacts 2 grams of powdered zinc with excess dilute acid and measures the gas collected. The reaction produces 0,06 moles of hydrogen in the first 30 seconds. First, calculate the average rate of hydrogen production over this interval. Second, the experiment is repeated with zinc granules of the same mass; state and explain, in terms of the collision theory, how the rate changes. Third, sketch what a catalyst does to the potential energy diagram.

The plan: rate is change over time in whatever currency the question hands you — here, moles per second — then collision theory for the explain marks, told with its two levers: collision frequency and the fraction of particles above the activation energy.

The rate: 0,06 moles over 30 seconds — 0,002 moles per second. Formula, substitution, answer with unit — commonly three marks, and the unit moles per second is where the third one hides.

The granules: same mass, larger lumps, smaller total surface area. Fewer acid particles can collide with zinc per second, so the frequency of effective collisions drops, and the rate DECREASES. The memo pays this as a chain — surface area down, collision frequency down, rate down — one mark per link, and the phrase effective collisions, meaning collisions with at least the activation energy and correct orientation, is the vocabulary the marker is scanning for. Note what does not change: the total hydrogen eventually produced stays the same, because the same moles of zinc react — slower, not less, and papers love asking exactly that.

The catalyst sketch: the potential energy diagram keeps its reactant level, its product level and its heat of reaction untouched; only the hump between them drops. A catalyst provides an alternative path of lower activation energy — more particles clear the lower bar, the rate rises, and the catalyst emerges unchanged. Marks: lower hump drawn on the same axes, one; labels for both activation energies, one; delta H unchanged, one. The audit is the untouched delta H — a catalyst that appears to change the heat of reaction is a drawing error, every time.

## Subtopic: Question Walk — The Equilibrium Calculation

The question. Sulfur dioxide and oxygen react in a closed 2 cubic decimetre vessel: two S O two plus O two in equilibrium with two S O three. Initially 0,4 moles of sulfur dioxide and 0,3 moles of oxygen are sealed in; at equilibrium the vessel holds 0,2 moles of sulfur trioxide. Calculate the equilibrium constant.

The plan: the RICE table in moles, ratios from the balanced equation, then — the step the memo watches — divide by the volume for concentrations before touching the constant.

The table. Initial: 0,4 and 0,3 and nought. Change, anchored on the product: forming 0,2 moles of sulfur trioxide consumes 0,2 moles of sulfur dioxide — the ratio is two to two — and 0,1 moles of oxygen, the two-to-one ratio. Equilibrium moles: sulfur dioxide 0,4 minus 0,2 — 0,2; oxygen 0,3 minus 0,1 — 0,2; sulfur trioxide 0,2. The memo pays the change row and the equilibrium row, typically two marks, and the ratio reasoning must be visible.

Concentrations: each entry over 2 cubic decimetres — sulfur dioxide 0,1; oxygen 0,1; sulfur trioxide 0,1 moles per cubic decimetre. One mark, and it is the most forfeited line in the whole calculation: moles poured straight into the constant score nothing past this point.

The expression, from the balanced equation: K c equals sulfur trioxide concentration squared, over, sulfur dioxide concentration squared times oxygen concentration. Writing it earns its own mark. Substituting: 0,1 squared over, 0,1 squared times 0,1 — which is 0,01 over 0,001 — 10. No units on K c, and the answer mark closes the question. The audit: K c of 10 means an equilibrium leaning toward products, consistent with a healthy amount of sulfur trioxide in the vessel — the number and the story agree.

## Subtopic: Question Walk — Titration and pH

The question. A learner titrates 25 cubic centimetres of a 0,1 molar oxalic acid solution with sodium hydroxide from a burette, using phenolphthalein. The endpoint arrives after 20 cubic centimetres of base. The equation: oxalic acid plus two sodium hydroxide gives sodium oxalate plus two water. First, calculate the concentration of the sodium hydroxide. Second, calculate the pH of a 0,001 molar hydrochloric acid solution. Third, explain why the salt formed in the titration gives a solution with a pH above 7.

The plan: titration is stoichiometry in glassware — moles of the known, ratio from the equation, concentration of the unknown — then the pH definition, then hydrolysis.

Moles of oxalic acid: concentration times volume in cubic decimetres, 0,1 times 0,025 — 0,0025 moles. One to two marks, and the volume conversion to cubic decimetres is the quiet trap. The equation demands two moles of base per mole of acid, so moles of sodium hydroxide: 0,005. The ratio line, one mark. Concentration: moles over volume, 0,005 over 0,020 — 0,25 moles per cubic decimetre. Formula, substitution, answer with unit — the standard trio.

The pH: hydrochloric acid is strong, fully ionised, so the hydronium concentration equals 0,001, which is 10 to the minus 3. The definition does the rest: pH equals minus the log of the hydronium concentration — minus log of 10 to the minus 3 — pH 3. Two to three marks, and the memo wants the definition line written, not just the answer.

The hydrolysis explanation: sodium oxalate is the salt of a WEAK acid and a STRONG base. The oxalate ion reacts with water, accepting protons and releasing hydroxide ions, so the solution turns alkaline — pH above 7 — which is exactly why phenolphthalein, changing colour in the alkaline range, was the right indicator. Salt classified, one mark; the hydrolysis reaction described, one; the pH conclusion tied to the indicator, one. The audit: strong-strong titrations sit near 7, weak-acid-strong-base above 7, strong-acid-weak-base below — the answer must match the pattern.

## Subtopic: Question Walk — The Galvanic Cell

The question. A standard galvanic cell couples a zinc half-cell with a silver half-cell. Standard reduction potentials: silver plus 0,80 volts, zinc minus 0,76 volts. First, identify the anode, with a reason. Second, write the half-reaction at each electrode. Third, calculate the standard cell emf. Fourth, write the overall cell reaction. Fifth, state the function of the salt bridge.

The plan: the table decides everything — the half-cell higher on the reduction list runs as reduction and is the cathode; the other reverses into oxidation at the anode. Then the emf from E cathode minus E anode, and the overall reaction from electron bookkeeping.

The anode: zinc, because zinc has the lower reduction potential — it is the stronger reducing agent, so zinc is oxidised, and oxidation defines the anode. Identification with reason, two marks; naming zinc without the reasoning earns one at most.

The half-reactions. At the anode: zinc gives zinc two plus, plus two electrons. At the cathode: silver ion plus one electron gives silver. One mark each, arrows and electrons on the correct sides.

The emf: E cell equals E cathode minus E anode — 0,80 minus, minus 0,76 — 1,56 volts. Formula, substitution, answer: three marks, and the double negative is the single most common slip in the entire paper; rehearse the line aloud — nought comma eight nought minus minus nought comma seven six.

The overall reaction balances the electrons: the silver half-reaction doubles, so zinc plus two silver ions gives zinc two plus, plus two silver. Two marks — species and balance. The audit: electrons must cancel exactly, and the positive emf confirms a spontaneous reaction, which is what galvanic means.

The salt bridge: it completes the circuit by allowing ions to migrate between the half-cells, maintaining electrical neutrality in both solutions. One to two marks. Close the run with the machine's fixed labels, good for both cells in any examination: oxidation at the anode, reduction at the cathode — the energy direction changes between galvanic and electrolytic, the definitions never do.
