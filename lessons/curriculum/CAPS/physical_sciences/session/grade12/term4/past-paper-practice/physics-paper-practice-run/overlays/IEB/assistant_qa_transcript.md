**Mandy:** Questions from the class about the physics practice run! First: every mechanics question seems to start with a free-body diagram. Is it really worth the drawing time under exam pressure?

**Tutor:** It is the single best investment in the whole set, and for two separate reasons. The first is direct: the diagram itself carries marks — forces present, labelled, pointing the right way — and in our incline walk that was two marks banked before any arithmetic. The second is protective: the diagram is where errors get caught while they are still cheap. The block moving up the slope forces you to draw friction pointing down the slope, and once that arrow exists you cannot accidentally add 8,6 instead of subtracting it. The routine is fixed: isolate the object as a dot, attach weight straight down, the normal force perpendicular to the surface, friction opposing the motion, and every applied force. On an incline, add one more habit — split the weight immediately, m g sine theta along the slope, m g cosine theta into it, and write both components next to the diagram before touching the net-force line. Thirty seconds of drawing, and the entire question becomes bookkeeping. The candidates who skip the diagram are not saving time; they are borrowing it at interest, because a sign error found at the final answer costs the whole question to repair.

**Mandy:** Next: the momentum question asked us to PROVE the collision was inelastic. Why is stating it not enough, and where do the proof marks actually sit?

**Tutor:** Because the definition is quantitative, so the answer must be too. Elastic means kinetic energy is conserved; inelastic means it is not — and the only way to know is to compute kinetic energy on both sides of the collision and compare. In the walk that was half times 4 times 9, giving 18 joules before, half times 6 times 4, giving 12 joules after, and then the sentence that earns the conclusion mark: kinetic energy decreased, therefore the collision is inelastic. Three separate marks — before, after, conclusion — and the conclusion mark is only paid when it points at the numbers. Two traps orbit this question. The first is thinking momentum conservation settles it; momentum is conserved in BOTH kinds of collision, so it proves nothing about elasticity. The second is halving wrongly — after coupling, the mass is the combined 6 kilograms and the velocity is the shared 2 metres per second, not either original value. And keep the impulse follow-up connected: impulse on one trolley is that trolley's change in momentum, and the two trolleys' changes must come out equal and opposite. When they do, you have audited the whole question for free.

**Mandy:** The circuit walk kept repeating outside-in. What does that discipline actually protect you from?

**Tutor:** It protects you from the two ways circuit questions collapse: using the emf where the terminal voltage belongs, and diving into a branch before the main current exists. Outside-in means the network collapses first — parallel pair to a single value, 144 over 24 giving 6 ohms, then the series 4, then the internal resistance 2, for a total of 12. Only now does the main current appear: 24 over 12, 2 amperes, computed with the FULL total. That order matters because the internal resistance is invisible on the diagram's edge and forgetting it inflates the current. Then the walk back in: lost volts, 2 times 2, so terminal voltage 20; the series resistor takes 8; the parallel pair shares the remaining 12; each branch carries 1 ampere. Every line uses a value the previous line produced — nothing is guessed, nothing is fetched from the question blindly. And the final audit is not decoration: branch currents summing to the main current is the one check that catches an upstream slip in seconds. If 1 plus 1 had not equalled 2, the error would be upstream, and walking the chain backward finds it faster than redoing the page.

**Mandy:** Last one: the modern physics question always looks short but scores badly. What goes wrong in the photoelectric effect, and what is the fix?

**Tutor:** Three small failures, all preventable. Failure one is the definition: the work function needs the word minimum — the minimum energy an electron needs to be emitted from the surface — and paraphrases without it drop a mark. Failure two is the unit conversion: the wavelength arrives in nanometres and the equation wants metres, so 450 becomes 4,5 times 10 to the minus 7 before anything else happens; our walk put the photon energy at 4,42 times 10 to the minus 19 joules, and that answer is unreachable with the wavelength left raw. Failure three is the conceptual finish: intensity against frequency. The picture that settles it is one photon meeting one electron. Intensity is how MANY photons arrive per second — more electrons ejected, same energy each. Frequency is how BIG each photon is — same number, more energetic electrons. So halving intensity halves the electron count and leaves the maximum kinetic energy untouched, which is exactly what the walk asked. Bank the closing sentence too, because it is a standing mark: the photoelectric effect demonstrates the particle nature of light. Short question, fixed script — learn the script and the marks are mechanical.
