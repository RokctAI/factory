**Mandy:** Chemistry revision questions from the class! First: organic chemistry feels like a mountain of names and reactions. What is the smallest set of things to master that unlocks the most marks?

**Tutor:** Two skills unlock most of the organic marks, and both are mechanical. The first is the naming algorithm run in both directions: longest chain containing the functional group, lowest number to that group, substituents alphabetical with positions. Practise it forwards — structure to name — and backwards — name to structure — because the paper asks both, and the backwards direction is where the shaky candidates fall. The second is the reaction map read through bonds: count the bonds before anything else. A double bond consumed means addition; a double bond created means elimination; a saturated molecule swapping one atom for another means substitution. With those two skills, most organic questions become bookkeeping. What remains is a short memorised list: the reaction conditions — light for halogenating alkanes, concentrated acid and heat for dehydration, strong base dissolved in ethanol for dehydrohalogenation, sulfuric acid catalyst for esterification — and the major-product rule, hydrogen to the carbon already richer in hydrogen. Write the map on one page, conditions on the arrows, and rehearse it until you can redraw it blank. One page, most of the section.

**Mandy:** Next: the Kc calculation. Learners set up the expression correctly and still lose the middle marks. Where does it go wrong?

**Tutor:** It goes wrong between the moles and the concentrations, and the RICE table exists precisely to guard that gap. The examiners rarely hand you equilibrium concentrations directly; they hand you initial amounts and one equilibrium amount, and the rest must be reasoned through the mole ratios. Take hydrogen plus iodine forming hydrogen iodide, starting with 1,0 mole of each in a 2 decimetre cubed vessel, with 0,2 moles of hydrogen left at equilibrium. The table runs: initial, 1,0 and 1,0 and nought; change, minus 0,8 and minus 0,8 and plus 1,6 — the ratios doing the work, one to one to two; equilibrium, 0,2 and 0,2 and 1,6. The classic error is jumping into the Kc expression with those MOLES. Kc is built from concentrations, so every entry must first be divided by the volume: 0,1 and 0,1 and 0,8. Then Kc equals 0,8 squared over 0,1 times 0,1 — 64. Moles in the table, concentrations in the constant, and the division by volume as its own visible line. That one line is the mark learners donate most often.

**Mandy:** The acids question that returns every year: strong versus concentrated. Why do these two words keep costing marks, and what fixes it?

**Tutor:** They cost marks because everyday language treats them as synonyms and chemistry does not. Strength describes what a FRACTION of the acid does in water: a strong acid like hydrochloric ionises completely — every molecule donates its proton; a weak acid like ethanoic ionises incompletely, a small percentage at any moment. Concentration describes how MUCH acid was dissolved per volume, and says nothing about what it does once dissolved. The two axes are independent, so all four combinations exist, and the paper loves the strange corners: a dilute strong acid — few molecules, all ionised; a concentrated weak acid — many molecules, few ionised. The fix is to answer a different question in your head: asked about strength, talk about the fraction ionised and the equilibrium; asked about concentration, talk about moles per cubic decimetre. The same discipline earns the comparison marks: a weak acid of equal concentration shows a higher pH, lower conductivity and a slower initial reaction rate than a strong one — three observable differences, all traceable to the fraction ionised, none to the amount dissolved.

**Mandy:** Last one: electrochemistry sits at the end of the paper, and learners say the two cells blur together by then. What keeps them apart under pressure?

**Tutor:** Keep one machine in mind with a gear lever, and hold on to what never changes. In both cells, oxidation happens at the anode and reduction at the cathode — that definition is welded down. What changes is the energy direction: the galvanic cell runs spontaneously, chemical energy pushing electrons through the wire; the electrolytic cell is forced, electrical energy driving a reaction that refuses to run alone. So identify the cell first — is there a battery in the diagram doing the pushing, or is the cell itself the battery — and then place the labels by definition, not by memory of a picture. For the galvanic cell, the table of standard electrode potentials settles everything: the half-cell higher on the reduction list runs as reduction and is the cathode; the other reverses; emf equals E cathode minus E anode, and the zinc-copper pair gives 0,34 minus, minus 0,76 — 1,10 volts, positive, confirming spontaneity. Watch the double negative there; it is the single most common slip in the whole calculation. For the electrolytic processes, learn the four the syllabus names — copper chloride decomposition, electroplating, copper refining, and concentrated sodium chloride giving chlorine at the anode and hydrogen at the cathode. Same machine, two gears, labels welded in place.
