**Mandy:** Questions from the class on the physics final sweep! First: with the whole year's physics to revise, where should the last weeks of preparation actually go?

**Tutor:** Follow the map from the lesson: three territories, weighted by breadth. Mechanics is the broadest stretch of the syllabus — forces, momentum, projectiles, energy — so it earns the biggest share of your remaining hours, and within it the two highest-value drills are the free-body diagram and the three ledgers: net force for instants, energy for journeys, momentum for crashes. Electricity and magnetism comes next, and its single most bankable skill is the internal-resistance circuit walked outside-in, because that one question type packs five or six connected marks. The smaller strips — Doppler and the photoelectric effect — are the best marks-per-hour in the whole subject: a handful of fixed definitions and two calculations that barely change their shape. So a sensible week looks like this: every day starts with five minutes of definitions spoken aloud from memory — Newton's laws, free fall, impulse, emf, rms, threshold frequency — because those sentences are fixed in advance and are the cheapest marks you will ever collect. Then one full mechanics problem, one full circuit, and alternate the small-territory topics. Doing, not reading — a page you solved cold is worth ten pages you highlighted.

**Mandy:** Next: several learners can quote all three of Newton's laws but still lose the calculation marks. What is going wrong between the statement and the substitution?

**Tutor:** The missing middle is almost always the free-body diagram, and skipping it is the most expensive shortcut in mechanics. The statement mark and the calculation mark are connected by a drawing: isolate the object as a dot, attach every force as a labelled arrow — weight down, normal force from the surface, friction opposing the motion, every push or pull — and only then write net force equals mass times acceleration. In the lesson's crate, the diagram is what makes 100 minus 28 inevitable rather than hopeful; on an incline, it is what forces the weight to split into m g sine theta along the slope and m g cosine theta into it before anything else happens. The second failure is the sign convention. The rebounding ball is the cleanest test: take towards the wall as positive, and the rebound velocity MUST enter as minus 9 — giving a change of momentum of minus 8,4 kilogram metres per second and a force of 168 newtons away from the wall. Candidates who skip the declaration add 9 instead of subtracting it and lose every mark downstream. Two habits, both free: draw the dot, declare the positive direction. They are not decorations; they are the difference between knowing the law and scoring with it.

**Mandy:** The circuit question with internal resistance keeps appearing in our practice sets. What is the reliable order of operations?

**Tutor:** Outside-in, then back out, with an audit at the end — the same walk every time, whatever the numbers. Stage one: collapse the network. Parallel pairs first, product over sum — in the lesson, 16 over 8 gave 2 ohms — then add the series resistors for the external resistance, then, crucially, add the battery's own internal resistance for the true total. That last addition is the trap: leave out the half ohm and every number afterwards inflates. Stage two: one division gives the main current — emf over total resistance, 12 over 4, 3 amperes. Stage three: walk back in. Lost volts first, I times r, so the terminal voltage is 10,5; then each series resistor takes its share, and whatever remains sits across the parallel pair, splitting between branches by Ohm's law. Stage four is the audit, and it is not optional: the branch currents must sum to the main current — 1,5 plus 1,5 equals 3, exactly — and the voltages around the loop must sum to the emf. When the books refuse to balance, the error is upstream, and walking the chain backward finds it in seconds. One more discipline: never use the emf where the terminal voltage belongs; the emf only appears in two places — the total-resistance division and the lost-volts line.

**Mandy:** Last one: the modern physics section is short but learners mix up intensity and frequency every time. Is there a picture that makes it stick?

**Tutor:** One picture: photons as coins, electrons as vending machines. Each electron is freed by exactly one coin, and the machine has a minimum price — the work function, the minimum energy an electron needs to escape the surface, with minimum being the word the definition mark hangs on. Frequency is the SIZE of each coin: h times f. Intensity is HOW MANY coins arrive per second. Now every question answers itself. Brighter light of the same colour? More coins, same size — more electrons per second, each with the same maximum kinetic energy, exactly as the lesson's numbers showed: photon energy 4,97, work function 3,7, leftover 1,27 times 10 to the minus 19 joules, regardless of how many photons rain down. Bluer light? Bigger coins — same number of electrons, each leaving with more energy. Coins too small — below the threshold frequency? Nothing is freed, no matter how many arrive, and that is the whole reason the photoelectric effect matters: it demonstrates the particle nature of light, because a wave would simply accumulate energy until any electron escaped. Close every photoelectric answer with that sentence when significance is asked; it is a standing mark, and the vending machine will not let you forget it.
