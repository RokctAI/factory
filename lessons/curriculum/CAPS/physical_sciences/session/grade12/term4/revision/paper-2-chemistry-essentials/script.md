# Part 1 — Expert

Paper 2 is chemistry, and its marks divide between two great blocks: chemical change — rates, equilibrium, acids and bases, electrochemistry — and matter and materials, which is organic chemistry. This session is the final sweep of both: the naming rules and reaction map of organic molecules, the collision theory and Le Chatelier machinery, the acid-base definitions and calculations, and the two electrochemical cells that close the syllabus. Every definition in its memo wording, every calculation rebuilt in full.

## Subtopic: Organic Molecules — Naming, Isomers and Properties

Organic chemistry begins with recognition: nine homologous series, each stamped by its functional group. Alkanes carry only single bonds; alkenes a carbon-carbon double bond; alkynes a triple bond. Haloalkanes carry a halogen atom on the chain. Alcohols carry the hydroxyl group; aldehydes the formyl group at the end of a chain; ketones the carbonyl group between two carbons; carboxylic acids the carboxyl group; and esters the alcohol-acid link. A homologous series is a family described by one general formula, each member differing from the next by C H two.

The naming algorithm never changes. One: find the longest chain containing the functional group. Two: number the chain so the functional group takes the lowest number. Three: name substituents with their positions, listed alphabetically. Speak one through: a chain of four carbons with a hydroxyl group on carbon one and a methyl branch on carbon two is 2-methylbutan-1-ol. Reverse direction works too: given the name, build the skeleton first, hang the groups, then fill with hydrogens.

Isomers share a molecular formula but differ in structure, and the syllabus wants three kinds by name. Chain isomers differ in the carbon skeleton — butane against 2-methylpropane. Positional isomers move the group along the chain — but-1-ene against but-2-ene. Functional isomers swap the group itself — methyl methanoate against ethanoic acid, both C two H four O two.

Physical properties follow the forces between molecules. Longer chains mean larger molecules, stronger induced dipole forces, higher boiling points — so boiling point climbs through a homologous series. Branching shrinks the contact surface, weakens those forces, lowers the boiling point. And hydrogen bonding lifts alcohols and carboxylic acids above the alkanes of similar size, because hydrogen bonded to oxygen builds the strongest intermolecular force on the list. Stronger forces also mean lower vapour pressure — the pair travel in opposite directions, always.

This section's questions are with you now — groups recognised on sight, chains numbered from the right end, and boiling points argued from forces, never memorised.

## Subtopic: The Organic Reaction Map

The examiners test organic reactions as a map: which type, which conditions, which product. Three types rule. SUBSTITUTION swaps one atom or group for another, and it belongs to saturated compounds — alkanes with halogens in light, haloalkanes hydrolysed to alcohols, alcohols converted back to haloalkanes. ADDITION breaks a double bond open, and it belongs to unsaturated compounds — alkenes adding hydrogen with a platinum catalyst, adding halogens directly, adding hydrogen halides, or adding water with an acid catalyst. ELIMINATION creates a double bond by removing atoms — dehydrohalogenation of a haloalkane with concentrated strong base in ethanol and heat, or dehydration of an alcohol with concentrated acid, and cracking of long alkanes into shorter useful pieces.

The recognition trick: count the bonds first. Saturated reactant plus something entering — substitution. Double bond consumed — addition. Double bond created — elimination. The bromine water test hangs on the same idea: an unsaturated compound decolourises bromine water quickly through addition, a saturated one barely reacts.

Major and minor products follow one rule spoken plainly: when hydrogen adds across an unsymmetrical double bond, it goes preferentially to the carbon that already holds more hydrogens, and the halogen or hydroxyl lands on the more substituted carbon — that product is the major one. Propene plus hydrogen bromide gives 2-bromopropane as major, 1-bromopropane as minor.

Esterification is its own small ceremony: a carboxylic acid warms with an alcohol over concentrated sulfuric acid as catalyst, and they join, releasing water. Ethanoic acid plus ethanol gives ethyl ethanoate. The naming rule: the alcohol donates the first half of the name, the acid the second — and the memo expects reactants, conditions, ester name, and water, all four.

Take this section's questions now — type identified from the bonds, conditions quoted exactly, and the major product placed on the crowded carbon.

## Subtopic: Rates, Equilibrium and Le Chatelier

Reaction rate is the change in concentration of reactants or products per unit time. Five factors move it: nature of the reactants, surface area, concentration — pressure for gases — temperature, and a catalyst. The explanation currency is collision theory: reactions happen when particles collide with at least the activation energy, so anything that raises the frequency of effective collisions, or the fraction of particles above the activation energy, raises the rate. Temperature does both jobs but its main lever is the second: the Maxwell-Boltzmann curve flattens and shifts right, and the tail beyond the activation energy grows. A catalyst increases the rate without being consumed, by providing an alternative path of lower activation energy — the curve does not move; the finish line does.

Chemical equilibrium is a dynamic state in a closed system where the rate of the forward reaction equals the rate of the reverse reaction. The equilibrium constant K c is built from the balanced equation: products over reactants, each concentration raised to its coefficient, solids and pure liquids left out. Only temperature changes the value of K c — concentration and pressure shift the position, never the constant.

Work the standard calculation. Hydrogen and iodine form hydrogen iodide: H two plus I two in equilibrium with 2 H I. Start with 1,0 mole of each gas in a 2 decimetre cubed vessel; at equilibrium 0,2 moles of hydrogen remain. The RICE table thinks for you. Reacted: 0,8 moles of hydrogen, so 0,8 moles of iodine, forming 1,6 moles of hydrogen iodide. Equilibrium concentrations: hydrogen 0,2 over 2, which is 0,1; iodine 0,1; hydrogen iodide 1,6 over 2 — 0,8. K c equals 0,8 squared over 0,1 times 0,1, which is 0,64 over 0,01 — 64. A large K c signals an equilibrium lying far towards products.

Le Chatelier's principle: when the equilibrium in a closed system is disturbed, the system re-instates a new equilibrium by favouring the reaction that opposes the disturbance. Add reactant — forward favoured. Raise the pressure — the side with fewer gas moles favoured. Raise the temperature — the endothermic direction favoured, and here K c itself changes. Every explain-answer follows the same three-sentence shape: name the disturbance, name the opposing reaction, state the shift and its result.

Questions on this section reach you now — collisions counted, the RICE table run in moles then converted, and every shift argued in Le Chatelier's three sentences.

## Subtopic: Acids, Bases and Electrochemistry

Two definitions open the acid-base marks. Arrhenius: acids produce hydronium ions in water, bases produce hydroxide ions. Lowry-Brønsted, the sharper tool: an acid is a proton donor, a base is a proton acceptor. A conjugate pair differs by exactly one proton, and an ampholyte — water is the star example — can act as either. Strong acids like hydrochloric, sulfuric and nitric ionise completely; weak acids like ethanoic and oxalic ionise incompletely. Strong is about the FRACTION ionised, concentrated is about the AMOUNT dissolved — a dilute strong acid is entirely possible, and telling those words apart is a perennial mark.

The pH machinery: pH equals minus the log of the hydronium concentration, and K w, the ion product of water, is 1 times 10 to the minus 14 at 298 kelvin. A 0,01 molar solution of hydrochloric acid: pH equals minus log of 10 to the minus 2 — pH 2. A 0,01 molar sodium hydroxide solution: hydroxide is 10 to the minus 2, so hydronium is 10 to the minus 12, and the pH is 12. Titrations turn stoichiometry into glassware: at the equivalence point the acid and base have reacted completely in their mole ratio. If 25 centimetres cubed of hydrochloric acid neutralises 20 centimetres cubed of 0,1 molar sodium hydroxide, then moles of base are 0,002, the ratio is one to one, and the acid concentration is 0,002 over 0,025 — 0,08 mole per decimetre cubed. Salt hydrolysis names the leftover solution: salt of weak acid and strong base, alkaline; salt of strong acid and weak base, acidic; strong-strong, neutral.

Electrochemistry closes the paper with two cells that mirror each other. The GALVANIC cell converts chemical energy to electrical energy spontaneously: oxidation at the anode, reduction at the cathode, electrons flowing through the external circuit from anode to cathode, the salt bridge keeping both solutions neutral. The zinc-copper cell: zinc is the stronger reducing agent, so zinc is oxidised at the anode; cell emf from the table equals E cathode minus E anode, which is 0,34 minus, minus 0,76 — 1,10 volts. A positive emf confirms spontaneity. The ELECTROLYTIC cell runs the mirror image: electrical energy forces a non-spontaneous reaction — electroplating, refining copper, and the electrolysis of concentrated sodium chloride solution which delivers chlorine gas at the anode and hydrogen at the cathode. Oxidation is loss of electrons, reduction is gain, and in both cells the anode is where oxidation lives — the definitions never move even when the energy direction does.

The final questions of this part are with you now — donors told from acceptors, pH logs run both ways, and the anode defined by oxidation in either cell.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same paper with softer edges — carbon as a family tree, equilibrium as a seesaw, and the two cells as one machine running forwards and backwards.

## Subtopic: Carbon Families and the Reaction Compass

Think of organic chemistry as one enormous family tree. The surname is the chain — meth, eth, prop, but, and onward to eight carbons — and the first name is the functional group: ane, ene, yne, ol, al, one, oic acid. Reading a molecule is reading a name tag: longest chain gives the surname, functional group gives the first name, branches are the middle names, each with a house number. Number the houses from the end that puts the important resident at the lowest address.

Reactions become a compass with three directions. Saturated molecules are full buses — no seats free — so the only move is substitution: one passenger off, one on. Unsaturated molecules hold folded seats — the double bond — so their move is addition: the bond unfolds and two new passengers board. And elimination is the reverse ride: two passengers leave and the folded seat reappears. Ask one question of every reaction — are seats being made, filled, or swapped — and the type names itself. Conditions are the ticket price: light for halogenating an alkane, acid and heat for dehydration, base in ethanol for dehydrohalogenation.

Boiling points read straight off the family photo: longer molecules touch more, stick more, boil higher; branched molecules are rounder, touch less, boil lower; and any molecule holding an O H group brings hydrogen bonding, the superglue of intermolecular forces, boiling far above its size class.

Your questions for this section are ready — read the name tag, point the compass, and let the forces explain the boiling.

## Subtopic: The Seesaw and the Stubborn Opponent

Equilibrium is a seesaw that never stops moving. Forward reaction on one side, reverse on the other, and at equilibrium the two rates balance — not the amounts, the RATES. The mixture looks still from the outside, but both reactions run flat out underneath; that is what dynamic means, and writing the word rates instead of amounts is worth a mark every time it appears.

Le Chatelier's principle is easiest as a stubborn opponent: whatever you do to the system, it leans the other way. Add more reactant, and the seesaw tips forward to use it up. Squeeze the gases into less space, and the system escapes toward the side with fewer gas molecules. Heat it, and the system spends the heat on the endothermic direction. The opponent never wins completely — it only softens the blow, settling at a new balance point. Three sentences answer every shift question: here is the disturbance, here is the direction that opposes it, here is the new position and its visible result.

K c is the seesaw's factory setting. Concentration changes and pressure changes tilt the plank but never touch the setting; only temperature reaches into the factory, because heating literally changes how far the reaction can go. So when a question asks whether K c changed, ask one thing only: did the temperature change? If not, K c stands, whatever else was poured, squeezed or removed.

Here are this section's questions — rates equal at balance, the opponent leans against you, and only temperature reprograms the constant.

## Subtopic: One Machine, Two Directions — and the Last Audit

Electrochemistry is one machine with a gear lever. Forward gear is the galvanic cell: the chemistry wants to happen, and its wanting pushes electrons through the wire — chemical energy becomes electrical energy, a battery. Reverse gear is the electrolytic cell: the chemistry does not want to happen, so you push electrons in from outside and force it — electrical energy becomes chemical change, electroplating and refining. The gear changes; the labels do not. Oxidation happens at the anode in both cells, reduction at the cathode in both cells. Learn it as a chant — an ox, red cat — oxidation at the anode, reduction at the cathode, forever.

The table of standard electrode potentials is the machine's instruction card. The half-reaction higher on the reduction list is the one that runs as reduction; the other reverses into oxidation. Cell emf equals E cathode minus E anode, and a positive answer means forward gear works on its own.

Now the last audit, the traps that harvest Paper 2 marks. Trap one: strong against concentrated — strength is the fraction ionised, concentration is the amount dissolved; the words are not cousins, they are strangers. Trap two: the equilibrium amounts illusion — equal RATES, almost never equal amounts. Trap three: touching K c without a temperature change — pouring in reactant shifts the position and leaves the constant alone. Trap four: swapping the electrodes — if electrons flow OUT of it into the wire, it is the anode, whatever the cell. And the standing disciplines: balanced equations before any stoichiometry, decimal commas tidy, units on every concentration, and states of matter written where the memo shows them.

The final questions of the lesson are with you now — the machine shifted through both gears, four traps named, and the audit run before the marker runs it for you.
