# Part 1 — Expert

Paper 1 is physics, and its marks live in three territories: mechanics is the largest, electricity and magnetism close behind, and the smaller coastal strips of waves and matter still decide symbols. This session is the final sweep of the paper's core — Newton's laws and momentum, projectile motion and the energy theorems, electrostatics through circuits to electrodynamics, and the photoelectric effect — every idea in its exam-day form, with the definitions the memo pays for and the calculations rebuilt from scratch.

## Subtopic: Newton's Laws and Momentum — the Mechanics Core

Three laws carry the biggest questions in the paper, and each is worth marks as a bare statement, so say them precisely. Newton's first law: a body remains in its state of rest or motion at constant velocity unless a non-zero net force acts on it. Newton's second: when a net force acts on an object, the object accelerates in the direction of the force, with acceleration directly proportional to the force and inversely proportional to the mass. Newton's third: when object A exerts a force on object B, object B simultaneously exerts an oppositely directed force of equal magnitude on A.

The method behind every force question is the free-body diagram: isolate the object, draw every force on it as an arrow from a dot, then apply F net equals m a along each axis. A 10 kilogram crate is pulled along a floor by an 80 newton horizontal force against 20 newtons of friction. Net force: 80 minus 20, which is 60 newtons. Acceleration: 60 divided by 10 — 6 metres per second squared. On an incline the weight splits into components: m g sine theta along the slope, m g cosine theta perpendicular to it, and that split is the single most rehearsed move in the whole paper.

Momentum is mass times velocity, p equals m v, a vector aimed along the velocity. Newton's second law in momentum language: net force equals the rate of change of momentum. Impulse is F net times delta t, and the impulse-momentum theorem says F net delta t equals m delta v. Work it once with signs. A 0,5 kilogram ball strikes a wall at 10 metres per second and rebounds at 8 metres per second. Take towards the wall as positive: change in momentum is 0,5 times, minus 8 minus 10, which is minus 9 kilogram metres per second. Over a contact time of 0,1 seconds the net force is minus 90 newtons — 90 newtons directed away from the wall. That sign discipline, the rebound velocity taken as negative, is where the marks hide.

Conservation of momentum closes the set: in an isolated system, total linear momentum is constant. A 2 kilogram trolley at 3 metres per second couples with a stationary 1 kilogram trolley. Total momentum before: 6 kilogram metres per second. Shared over 3 kilograms after coupling: 2 metres per second. Elastic or inelastic is decided by calculation, never by guess: kinetic energy before is half times 2 times 9, which is 9 joules; after, half times 3 times 4 — 6 joules. Energy lost, so the collision is inelastic.

This section's questions are with you now — laws stated exactly, signs declared before substituting, and elastic decided by arithmetic.

## Subtopic: Projectile Motion, Work, Energy and Power

A projectile is an object given an initial velocity that then moves under gravity alone, and free fall means the only force acting is gravitational. The acceleration is 9,8 metres per second squared downward at every instant — at the top of the flight the velocity is zero but the acceleration is still 9,8, and that exact statement appears in the paper year after year.

Run the standard flight. A ball is thrown straight up at 19,6 metres per second. Time to the top: velocity reaches zero, so 19,6 divided by 9,8 — 2 seconds. Maximum height from v f squared equals v i squared minus 2 g delta y: 19,6 squared divided by twice 9,8, which is 384,16 over 19,6 — 19,6 metres. Velocity after 3 seconds: 19,6 minus 9,8 times 3, which is minus 9,8 metres per second — already moving downward. The graphs tell the same story: the velocity-time graph is one straight line of constant gradient minus 9,8, crossing zero at the top, and for a bouncing ball the position graph is a chain of shrinking arches.

Work is F delta x cosine theta — the force, the displacement, and the cosine of the angle between them. The work-energy theorem: the net work done on an object equals the change in its kinetic energy. And the master equation for rough surfaces: W non-conservative equals delta E k plus delta E p. A 4 kilogram box slides from rest down a rough ramp of height 5 metres and arrives at the bottom at 8 metres per second. Potential energy surrendered: 4 times 9,8 times 5, which is 196 joules. Kinetic energy gained: half times 4 times 64 — 128 joules. The friction work is the gap: 128 minus 196, which is minus 68 joules. Negative, as friction work must be, and those 68 joules left as heat.

Power is the rate at which work is done, P equals W over delta t, and for constant speed on a rough surface, P average equals F times v average. The pump calculation: a pump raises 200 kilograms of water through 15 metres at constant speed in 10 seconds. Work: 200 times 9,8 times 15, which is 29 400 joules. Power: 29 400 divided by 10 — 2 940 watts.

Take this section's questions now — g never switches off at the top, and the energy budget closes to the joule.

## Subtopic: Electrostatics and Circuits

Coulomb's law: the electrostatic force between two point charges is directly proportional to the product of the charges and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between their centres — F equals k Q one Q two over r squared. Numbers: charges of 2 microcoulombs and 3 microcoulombs, 0,1 metres apart. Force: 9 times 10 to the 9, times 2 times 10 to the minus 6, times 3 times 10 to the minus 6, all over 0,1 squared. The top multiplies to 5,4 times 10 to the minus 2, the bottom is 10 to the minus 2, and the force is 5,4 newtons. The electric field at a point is the force per unit positive charge placed there, E equals F over q, and a single charge builds a field E equals k Q over r squared. For several charges, calculate each contribution and add them with directions — fields from positive charges point away, from negative charges point toward.

Circuits in Grade 12 mean internal resistance. The emf is the maximum energy the battery provides per unit charge; the internal resistance r behaves as a small resistor hidden inside the cell. The master equation: emf equals I times R external plus I times r. Work the full staple. A battery of emf 18 volts and internal resistance 0,5 ohms drives a 2 ohm resistor in series with two 4 ohm resistors in parallel. The parallel pair: product over sum, 16 over 8 — 2 ohms. External resistance: 2 plus 2, which is 4 ohms. Total including the inside of the battery: 4,5 ohms. Current: 18 divided by 4,5 — 4 amperes. Terminal voltage: 18 minus 4 times 0,5, which is 16 volts. The series resistor takes 4 times 2 — 8 volts — leaving 8 volts across the parallel pair, and each 4 ohm branch carries 8 divided by 4, which is 2 amperes. Audit: 2 plus 2 equals the main current, 4 amperes, exactly. Every circuit answer should close its books like that.

Questions on this section reach you now — inverse square respected, fields added as vectors, and the battery's budget balanced to the volt.

## Subtopic: Electrodynamics and Optical Phenomena

Generators convert mechanical energy to electrical energy through electromagnetic induction: rotate a coil in a magnetic field and the changing flux induces an emf. An AC generator uses slip rings and delivers a voltage that alternates as a sine wave; a DC generator swaps the slip rings for a split-ring commutator, which reverses the connections every half turn so the output keeps one polarity. A motor is the same machine run backward — electrical energy in, mechanical energy out, driven by the force on a current-carrying coil in a magnetic field.

Alternating current brings the rms pair. The rms potential difference is the AC potential difference that dissipates the same energy as an equivalent DC potential difference, and V rms equals V max over the square root of 2. South African mains peaks near 311 volts, so the rms value is 311 over 1,41 — about 220 volts, the number on every appliance. Average power in a resistive circuit: P average equals I rms times V rms.

Then the paper crosses into modern physics. The photoelectric effect is the process whereby electrons are ejected from a metal surface when light of suitable frequency shines on it — and its significance is that it demonstrates the particle nature of light. The threshold frequency f nought is the minimum frequency that ejects electrons; the work function W nought is the minimum energy an electron needs to escape, with W nought equals h f nought. The photoelectric equation: E equals W nought plus maximum kinetic energy, where E equals h f. Numbers: light of frequency 6,0 times 10 to the 14 hertz strikes a metal of work function 3,0 times 10 to the minus 19 joules. Photon energy: 6,63 times 10 to the minus 34, times 6,0 times 10 to the 14, which is 3,98 times 10 to the minus 19 joules. Maximum kinetic energy: 3,98 minus 3,0, times 10 to the minus 19 — 9,8 times 10 to the minus 20 joules. And the conceptual pair the examiners adore: increasing INTENSITY ejects more electrons per second but leaves each electron's maximum kinetic energy unchanged; increasing FREQUENCY raises the energy of each ejected electron. Intensity counts photons; frequency sizes them.

The final questions of this part are with you now — commutator against slip rings, rms defined not just divided, and intensity never confused with frequency.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same paper as a journey with a map — three territories, a handful of border posts, and the small habits that keep marks from leaking at each one.

## Subtopic: The Paper as a Map of Three Territories

Picture Paper 1 as a country you have toured all year. The largest territory is mechanics — forces, momentum, projectiles, energy — and because it is the largest, it is where preparation pays the highest rent. The second territory is electricity and magnetism: electrostatics, circuits with the battery's hidden resistor, generators and motors. The remaining strips — the Doppler effect and the photoelectric effect — are smaller but fiercely predictable: the same definitions and the same two or three calculations, almost every year.

The territories share one border control: the definition. Every topic opens its marks with a statement learned word for word — Newton's laws, free fall, impulse, emf, rms, threshold frequency. These are the cheapest marks in the paper because they are printed in the syllabus in advance. Recite them while walking, while washing dishes, until the sentences say themselves.

And they share one currency: the formula sheet. Every equation in this lesson is printed on the data sheet in the exam room — nothing needs memorising except what each symbol means and when each equation applies. Revision, then, is not about storing formulas; it is about rehearsing the choice of formula. Read a question, name the territory out loud, name the equation before touching the calculator — that habit alone straightens most wandering solutions.

Your questions for this section are ready — name the territory, quote the definition, then choose the tool.

## Subtopic: Force Stories and Energy Budgets

Every mechanics problem is one of two stories, and choosing the right story is the real skill. The first story is the force story — told in newtons, frame by frame. Draw the object alone, as a dot. Attach every force arrow: weight down, normal force from the surface, friction opposing motion, any pull or push. Then the accounting line: net force equals mass times acceleration. If the object sits on a slope, tilt your bookkeeping, not your head — the weight splits into a part along the slope and a part pressing into it, and everything else stays routine.

The second story is the energy budget — told in joules, and it skips the frames entirely. Energy arrives, energy leaves, and the books must balance: what the object loses in height it gains in speed, unless friction takes its cut as heat. The box sliding down the rough ramp told it perfectly: 196 joules of height money spent, only 128 joules of speed banked, so friction pocketed 68 — and the negative sign on friction's work is simply the receipt.

How to choose? If the question asks about acceleration, forces or tension at an instant, tell the force story. If it connects a starting state to an ending state — a height to a speed, a speed to a stopping distance — tell the energy budget and save yourself the frames in between. Momentum adds the third ledger for collisions: before equals after, always, in an isolated system. Three ledgers, one rule each — net force for instants, energy for journeys, momentum for crashes.

Here are this section's questions — pick the ledger first, and the arithmetic falls in line.

## Subtopic: The Final Sweep — Symbols, Signs and the Classic Traps

The traps in Paper 1 are old friends wearing new numbers, so meet them by name. Trap one: the top of the flight. Velocity is zero there, and the trap whispers that acceleration is too — it is not; gravity works full shifts, 9,8 metres per second squared, all the way up and all the way down. Trap two: the missing sign convention. Momentum and projectile answers collapse when up and down, towards and away, are never declared; write the chosen positive direction before the first substitution, every time. Trap three: the battery under load. A voltmeter across the terminals reads the full emf only when no current flows; close the switch and the reading drops by the lost volts, I times r. Trap four: intensity against frequency in the photoelectric effect — brighter light means more electrons, never faster ones; only higher frequency raises each electron's energy.

Then the sweep of small disciplines that convert answers into full marks. Units on every final answer — a number without its unit is a mark donated back. Decimal commas kept tidy, answers rounded to two decimal places unless told otherwise. Free-body diagrams drawn even when not asked, because they earn method marks and catch errors before they spread. Vector answers finished with both magnitude and direction — 90 newtons away from the wall, not just 90 newtons. And the final audit on every circuit: branch currents summing to the main current, voltages summing to the emf.

Revision now is doing, not reading. Work past questions cold, mark against the memo, and keep a one-page museum of your own personal traps. The paper is predictable; make your preparation just as predictable.

The final questions of the lesson are with you now — four traps named, every unit attached, and every budget closed before the marker opens it.
