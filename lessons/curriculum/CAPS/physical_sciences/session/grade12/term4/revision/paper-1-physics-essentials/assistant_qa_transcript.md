**Mandy:** Revision questions from the class! First: with mechanics, electricity and the modern physics all in one paper, how should the final weeks of Paper 1 preparation actually be structured?

**Tutor:** Structure it by weight and by kind. Mechanics carries the largest share of the marks, electricity and magnetism the next largest, so those two territories take the lion's share of your practice — but do not spend it rereading notes. Take a past paper, work its mechanics section cold, then mark yourself against the memo; the gap between your answer and the memo's is your genuine revision list, and it is usually much shorter and more specific than the syllabus. Then split your remaining time by kind: definitions and calculations are different muscles. Definitions are pure recall — Newton's laws, free fall, emf, rms, threshold frequency — and they are announced in advance, so drill them until word-perfect; those are the cheapest marks in the paper. Calculations are pure method — free-body diagram, sign convention, formula from the data sheet, substitute, unit — and the only way to install a method is to run it, cold, repeatedly. The smaller topics, Doppler and the photoelectric effect, are the best value for effort of anything in the paper: a handful of definitions and two calculation patterns cover almost every question ever asked about them.

**Mandy:** Next: learners say momentum questions feel like a lottery — sometimes the answer is positive, sometimes negative, and the memo seems to switch. What is going wrong?

**Tutor:** The lottery feeling comes from skipping the one line that makes everything deterministic: the sign convention. Momentum is a vector, so before any substitution you write one short sentence — take towards the wall as positive, or take downward as positive — and from that moment every velocity in the problem inherits a sign automatically. The ball that strikes a wall at 10 metres per second and rebounds at 8 is the perfect test: with towards-the-wall positive, the initial velocity is plus 10, the rebound is minus 8, and the change in momentum is mass times, minus 8 minus 10 — minus 9 kilogram metres per second for a half-kilogram ball. Learners who lose marks here almost always subtracted 8 from 10 because both numbers looked positive on the page. The memo is not switching; it is following the declared convention, and a candidate who declares none is guessing. There is a bonus, too: the final sign is not decoration, it is the direction of your answer, so the closing sentence writes itself — 9 kilogram metres per second, directed away from the wall. Declare, substitute, interpret. Three steps, no lottery.

**Mandy:** The circuits question: why do learners who can do Grade 11 circuits perfectly still lose marks the moment internal resistance appears?

**Tutor:** Because internal resistance changes where the problem starts, and they keep starting in the old place. The Grade 11 habit is to reach straight for a branch — find a current here, a voltage there. With a real battery that fails, because every branch depends on the main current, and the main current depends on the WHOLE circuit, internal resistance included. So the routine must run outside-in. First collapse the external network to a single resistance — parallel clusters first, product over sum for pairs, then add along the series line. Second, find the main current from the emf over the TOTAL resistance, external plus internal — this is the step the old habit skips. Third, walk back into the network, splitting voltage across series elements and current between parallel branches. Fourth, audit: branch currents must add to the main current, and terminal voltage plus lost volts must equal the emf. The other mark-leak is conceptual: the voltmeter across the terminals reads the emf only with the switch open; under load it reads less by I times r, and explain-questions expect that exact chain — current flows, lost volts appear, terminal voltage drops.

**Mandy:** Last one: the photoelectric effect — what is the cleanest way to keep intensity and frequency from blurring together under exam pressure?

**Tutor:** Give each word one job and one image. Light arrives as photons — energy parcels. FREQUENCY sets the size of each parcel, through E equals h f. INTENSITY sets how many parcels arrive per second. Now every exam question answers itself. Can dim light of high frequency eject electrons? Yes — each parcel is big enough, there are just fewer of them, so fewer electrons per second, each energetic. Can blindingly bright light below the threshold frequency eject electrons? No — millions of parcels, every one too small, and parcels do not club together. Turn up intensity above threshold: more electrons per second, same maximum kinetic energy each. Turn up frequency: the same count of electrons, each with more energy, because the surplus above the work function grows. Anchor it with the equation: h f equals W nought plus maximum kinetic energy — frequency appears in it, intensity does not, and that absence is the whole story. One sentence for the significance mark: the photoelectric effect demonstrates the particle nature of light, because a wave would eventually deliver enough energy at any frequency, and experiment says it never does below threshold.
