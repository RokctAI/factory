**Mandy:** Question time before we wrap! The first one came in from half the class: slip rings versus the split ring. Learners can recite "slip rings give AC, commutator gives DC", but they say that in a diagram they cannot tell WHY the split changes anything. Can you make the mechanism visible?

**Tutor:** Freeze the machine at the critical instant and the mechanism shows itself. Start inside the coil: as it rotates, each long side sweeps up through the field for half a revolution and down for the other half, so the emf generated INSIDE the coil reverses every half turn — that is true in both machines, always; nothing about rings changes the coil's own behaviour. The rings only decide how that internal reversal is presented to the outside world. With slip rings, each end of the coil is bonded to its own complete ring for life: brush A listens to the same coil end forever, so when that end's emf reverses, brush A faithfully reports the reversal, and the external circuit receives alternating current — the internal truth, unedited. Now the split ring. There is only one ring, sawn into two half-cylinders, and each brush touches whichever half happens to be facing it. Here is the choreography that does all the work: the gap between the halves is aligned so the swap of brushes happens at exactly the instant the coil passes vertical — which is precisely the instant the coil's emf reverses. So just as coil end one turns negative, it is handed over to the other brush, and brush A finds itself connected to coil end two, which has just turned positive. Two reversals, perfectly simultaneous, cancel: brush A is always positive, brush B always negative, and the outside sees direct current. The exam sentence: the commutator swaps the coil's connections to the brushes each half revolution, at the moment the emf reverses, so the external current never changes direction. It still pulses in size — the commutator edits direction, not magnitude.

**Mandy:** Next, the energy question that sounds like a trick: a learner asked why pedalling gets harder when a bicycle dynamo is switched on. Surely the magnet doesn't weigh more just because a lamp is glowing?

**Tutor:** The magnet weighs the same — what changes is that the rider has started paying for light, and the payment is collected as a force. When the dynamo is disengaged, the coil spins with no complete circuit or no load, and pedalling only fights friction. Engage the lamp and current begins to flow in the spinning coil — and a current-carrying coil in a magnetic field feels a force: the motor effect, appearing inside a generator. By Lenz's principle, that induced current always flows in the direction whose magnetic effect OPPOSES the rotation that created it — nature never induces a current that helps its own cause, because that would be a free-energy machine. So the coil experiences a drag torque, the rider's legs must overcome it, and the extra muscular work done against that drag is numerically the electrical energy lighting the lamp. Brighter lamp, bigger current, bigger drag: sprint with a dynamo and you can feel the brightness in your calves. This is the honest face of energy conservation, and it scales without mercy: in a power station, when a city's demand rises, the generator coils carry more current, the opposing torque on the rotor grows, and the turbine must be fed more steam or more water to hold its speed. The grid's control rooms are, in effect, watching how hard the national bicycle is to pedal. So give the two-layer answer: mechanically, the induced current makes the coil a motor pushing backwards; energetically, the lamp's joules must come from somewhere, and the only account available is the rider's legs.

**Mandy:** Third: the transmission argument. A learner wrote "high voltage is used because it makes the current travel faster" and lost the marks. What is the correct chain, and where does the square make its appearance?

**Tutor:** Speed has nothing to do with it — the correct chain is about how much of the cargo survives the journey, and the square is the whole plot. Set it up with the two powers that share one line. The useful power being delivered is the product of transmission voltage and current: P equals V times I. The wasted power, heating the cable itself, is I squared times R, where R is the cable's fixed resistance. Notice the asymmetry: the delivery depends on the PRODUCT of voltage and current, but the waste depends on the current ALONE, and on its SQUARE. That asymmetry is exploitable. Suppose you must deliver a fixed amount of power. Double the voltage and you may halve the current — same product, same delivery. But the waste is the current squared times R: halve the current and the waste falls to a QUARTER. Step the voltage up tenfold, current drops tenfold, and losses collapse a hundredfold. That is why the grid transmits at hundreds of kilovolts: not speed, but survival of the payload. Then the second half of the argument, which learners often forget to attach: none of this is usable unless voltage can be changed cheaply at both ends — up at the power station, down in stages to the safe 220 at the socket — and the transformer, which does exactly that, works only on a CHANGING current. AC changes constantly; historical DC did not, and could not be transformed efficiently. So the full answer is a two-part weld: I squared R makes high-voltage transmission overwhelmingly efficient, and transformers — an AC-only technology at the time the grids were built — make high-voltage transmission possible. Either half alone is half marks.

**Mandy:** Last one — rms. A learner measured the mains with a peak-reading instrument, got about 311 volts, and is now convinced every appliance label in the country is wrong. Are the labels lying?

**Tutor:** The labels are telling a different truth — the energy truth — and both numbers are correct descriptions of the same wave. The instrument reported the summit of the sine wave: the mains genuinely does climb to about plus 311 volts and dive to minus 311, fifty round trips per second. The label's 220 is the root mean square value: the steady DC voltage that would deliver energy to a resistor at the same average rate as that swinging wave. Why is the honest average not zero, given the wave is negative half the time? Because heating does not care about direction — it follows current squared, and squaring makes both half-cycles positive. Do the averaging on the SQUARED wave, then take the square root to come back to a voltage, and for a pure sine wave the result is the peak divided by root two: 311 over 1,414 is 220. Now, who uses which number? The rms runs all the energy accounting: an 1100 watt heater on 220 volts draws five amperes rms, its label, your bill, and your calculations all agree. The peak runs the survival engineering: insulation, switch gaps and component ratings must withstand 311 volts and current crests near seven amperes, because the wave truly visits those summits twice every cycle. One more anchor worth keeping: the instantaneous power at the crest is V max times I max, exactly DOUBLE the average power — the wave works hardest at its peaks and rests at its zero crossings, and the average sits precisely halfway. So when a question hands you a maximum, divide by root two before touching any power formula; when it hands you a label value, it is already rms and ready to use. The two numbers never disagree — they answer different questions about the same wave.
