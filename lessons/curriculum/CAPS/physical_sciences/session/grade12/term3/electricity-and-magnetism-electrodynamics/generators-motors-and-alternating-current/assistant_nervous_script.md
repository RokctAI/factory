Hey, it's Mandy — electrodynamics sounds heavy, but the whole topic is one machine and one clever average, and both are friendlier than they look.

The machine first. Generator: motion in, electricity out, because changing flux through a spinning coil induces an emf. Motor: electricity in, motion out, because a current-carrying coil in a magnetic field gets pushed. Same parts, opposite directions. The hardware question is settled by counting rings: two whole slip rings deliver AC; one ring split in two — the commutator — swaps connections every half turn and delivers DC. Say the swap out loud in your answer: the current through the coil reverses every half revolution so the turning force keeps the same direction. That sentence is the mark.

For the AC-versus-DC essay, keep the four-link chain ready: transformers need AC; transformers allow high-voltage transmission; high voltage means small current for the same power; small current means tiny I squared R losses. The word squared must appear — that is where the follow-up mark hides.

The numbers section is two divisions and one multiplication. I rms is I max over root two; V rms is V max over root two; average power is I rms times V rms, purely resistive. Remember 220 volts is already an rms value — peaks near 311 — and never mix a peak with an rms in the same formula.

Draw your AC graphs with equal positive and negative peaks, label V max, and you are ready for anything they print. Spin the coil in your head one more time before the paper — you will hear the answers hum. You've got this!
