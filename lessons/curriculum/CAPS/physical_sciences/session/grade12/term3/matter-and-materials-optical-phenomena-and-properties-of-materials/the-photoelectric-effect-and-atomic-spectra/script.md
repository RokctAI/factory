# Part 1 — Expert

Shine light on a metal and, under the right conditions, electrons leap out of the surface. That simple observation broke classical physics, earned Einstein his Nobel Prize, and sits in Paper 1 every year as the photoelectric effect. This session defines the effect and its significance, masters the photoelectric equation and its calculations, separates the roles of intensity and frequency — the conceptual heart of the topic — and closes with atomic spectra, where the same photon idea explains the barcode of light every element carries.

## Subtopic: Light That Ejects Electrons

The photoelectric effect is the process whereby electrons are ejected from a metal surface when light of suitable frequency shines on it. Two words in that definition carry the physics: SUITABLE FREQUENCY. Every metal has a minimum frequency of light, its threshold frequency, symbol f nought, below which no electrons are ejected at all — no matter how bright the light, no matter how long it shines. Above the threshold, electrons leave the surface immediately, even in the dimmest light.

Classical wave physics cannot explain that. A wave delivers energy continuously, so a bright enough wave of ANY frequency should eventually shake electrons loose — and a dim light should need a waiting time to accumulate energy. Neither happens. Einstein's explanation: light energy arrives in packets called PHOTONS, each carrying an energy of E equals h f — Planck's constant, 6,63 times 10 to the minus 34 joule seconds, multiplied by the frequency. One photon interacts with one electron, delivering its whole packet or nothing. This is why the photoelectric effect matters beyond the laboratory: it is the evidence that light has a PARTICLE nature. Interference and diffraction show light behaving as a wave; the photoelectric effect shows it behaving as a stream of particles — together, the dual nature of light.

The escape cost is the WORK FUNCTION, symbol W nought: the minimum energy needed to eject an electron from the surface of a particular metal. It is a property of the metal — sodium's differs from caesium's — and it links directly to the threshold frequency: W nought equals h f nought. A photon at exactly the threshold frequency has precisely the energy to free an electron, with nothing left over.

This section's questions are with you now — one photon, one electron, and no frequency below the threshold moves anything.

## Subtopic: The Photoelectric Equation

Energy bookkeeping for one photon meeting one electron gives the equation of the topic: E equals W nought plus E k max. The photon's energy, h f, is spent in two parts — the work function pays the exit cost, and whatever remains becomes the maximum kinetic energy of the ejected electron, E k max equals half m v max squared. Written in full: h f equals h f nought plus half m v max squared. The word maximum matters: electrons deeper in the metal pay more than the minimum to escape, so they emerge slower; the equation tracks the luckiest electron, ejected from the very surface.

Now a full calculation, examination style. Light of frequency 6,0 times 10 to the 14 hertz falls on a caesium surface with work function 3,36 times 10 to the minus 19 joules. First, the photon energy: E equals h f, which is 6,63 times 10 to the minus 34, multiplied by 6,0 times 10 to the 14 — 3,978 times 10 to the minus 19 joules. Compare with the work function: the photon carries more than the exit cost, so emission happens. Maximum kinetic energy: E k max equals E minus W nought, which is 3,978 minus 3,36, times 10 to the minus 19 — 6,18 times 10 to the minus 20 joules.

Two standard variations. Threshold frequency: f nought equals W nought over h, which is 3,36 times 10 to the minus 19 divided by 6,63 times 10 to the minus 34 — 5,07 times 10 to the 14 hertz. Any light below that frequency ejects nothing from caesium. And when the question supplies wavelength instead of frequency, convert first: frequency equals the speed of light over the wavelength. A wavelength of 500 nanometres gives 3 times 10 to the 8 divided by 5,0 times 10 to the minus 7 — exactly the 6,0 times 10 to the 14 hertz we used. Wavelength to frequency, photon energy, subtract the work function: three moves, full marks.

Questions on this section reach you now — budget the photon: exit cost first, kinetic energy with the change.

## Subtopic: Intensity versus Frequency

The conceptual heart of the topic, and the marker's favourite discriminator, is what intensity does and what frequency does — because they refuse to do each other's jobs. Increasing the INTENSITY of the light means more photons arrive per second. Each photon still carries the same energy, h f, unchanged. So above the threshold frequency, brighter light ejects MORE electrons per second — a larger photocurrent — but every ejected electron has the SAME maximum kinetic energy as before. Below the threshold, brighter light achieves nothing at all: a million weak photons are still a million individual failures, because no electron can pool the energy of two photons.

Increasing the FREQUENCY raises the energy of each individual photon. Above threshold, each ejected electron now receives a bigger energy packet, pays the same work function, and leaves with GREATER maximum kinetic energy. But if the number of photons per second is unchanged, the number of electrons per second does not rise. Frequency controls the energy per electron; intensity controls the number of electrons. Two dials, two separate effects — cross them in an answer and the marks vanish.

This division of labour is precisely the evidence for the particle nature of light. On the wave picture, energy is spread continuously across the wavefront, so intensity should control everything, and any frequency should work given enough brightness. The observations say otherwise: an instant response, a hard frequency threshold, and kinetic energy that climbs with frequency but ignores brightness. Only energy arriving in single, indivisible packets — one photon absorbed by one electron — explains all three at once. That argument, stated in full sentences, is a standing four-mark question.

This section's questions arrive now — intensity counts photons, frequency sizes them, and no electron can save up two packets.

## Subtopic: Atomic Spectra

Photons also explain the light atoms emit. The electrons of an atom can only occupy specific energy levels — rungs on a ladder unique to that element, with no standing between rungs. When an electron drops from a higher level to a lower one, the atom emits a single photon carrying exactly the energy difference: E equals h f equals E two minus E one. Each possible downward jump produces its own precise frequency, so a hot, glowing gas emits light only at a specific set of frequencies. Pass that light through a prism and instead of a rainbow you see an ATOMIC EMISSION SPECTRUM: bright, sharp lines of colour on a black background, each line one type of jump.

Absorption runs the film backwards. Shine white light — all frequencies — through a cool gas, and its atoms absorb exactly those photons whose energies match an upward jump between levels. The transmitted light then shows an ABSORPTION SPECTRUM: a continuous rainbow interrupted by dark lines, at precisely the same frequencies where the hot gas would emit. Same element, same ladder, same set of lines — bright on dark for emission, dark on bright for absorption.

Because every element has its own ladder of energy levels, every element has its own line pattern — a fingerprint in light. That is how helium was discovered in the Sun's spectrum before it was found on Earth, how astronomers identify the composition of stars they will never touch, and how the red shifts of the previous term's work could be recognised at all: the fingerprint pattern is rigid, even when the whole pattern slides.

Contrast the two spectrum types the syllabus names: a continuous spectrum — the full unbroken rainbow — comes from hot solids and dense glowing matter; line spectra come from atoms in a gas, where the energy ladder shows through. Discrete lines are the visible proof that atomic energy levels are quantised: if electrons could hold any energy, the lines would smear into a continuous band.

The final expert questions are with you now — one jump, one photon, one line, and every element signs its light.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same physics with a bouncer, a carnival game, and a barcode scanner aimed at the stars.

## Subtopic: The Bouncer at the Metal's Door

Imagine each electron in a metal as a guest inside a club, and the way out is past a bouncer who demands an exit fee. That fee is the work function: a fixed price, different for each metal. Light arrives not as a smooth wave of wealth but as individual customers — photons — each carrying a fixed amount of cash decided ONLY by the light's frequency. Blue-end photons are rich; red-end photons are poor.

The club has strict rules. One photon deals with one electron — no whip-rounds, no pooling, no photon lending another its cash. If a photon's cash covers the exit fee, its electron walks out immediately, keeping the change as speed — kinetic energy. If the cash falls short, nothing happens at all: the electron stays, the photon's energy is gone, and the next poor photon fails identically. That is why there is a threshold frequency: below it, every photon is individually too poor, and sending millions of them — turning up the brightness — just queues up millions of failures. A stadium full of people each holding five rand cannot pay one fifty rand exit fee, because the bouncer only accepts single payments.

Above the threshold, everything works instantly: even the dimmest light ejects electrons the moment it arrives, because the first rich photon through the door pays in full. Dim just means few customers per second — few electrons out per second — never a waiting time. That instant response was one of the observations classical waves could not explain, and the bouncer explains it without effort.

Your first questions are with you now — one customer, one payment, keep the change as speed.

## Subtopic: Ten Soft Throws Never Beat One Hard Throw

Picture a carnival strength game: swing the hammer hard enough and the puck rings the bell; too soft, and it climbs partway and falls back. Now the crucial rule — the machine resets completely between swings. Nothing accumulates. Ten soft throws, a hundred soft throws, a thousand: if each individual swing is below the mark, the bell never rings. The only thing that rings it is ONE swing hard enough.

That is intensity versus frequency, in a single game. Each hammer swing is a photon; the hardness of the swing is the frequency — the energy per photon. The number of swings per minute is the intensity — photons per second. Ringing the bell is ejecting an electron. Below the threshold, more swings per minute changes nothing, because no single swing suffices and the machine keeps resetting. Above the threshold, every swing rings the bell: more swings per minute now means more rings per minute — brighter light, more electrons per second — but each ring is only as energetic as ITS OWN swing. Harder swings make the puck fly past the bell with energy to spare; that spare is the electron's kinetic energy.

Keep the two dials separated in your head exactly like that. The frequency dial sets how hard each individual packet hits — it decides WHETHER emission happens and how fast each electron leaves. The intensity dial sets how many packets arrive — it decides HOW MANY electrons leave per second, and nothing else. Exam answers fail when the dials get crossed; the carnival game keeps them apart.

Questions on this section are coming to you now — the machine resets between swings, and only the single hard hit ever rings the bell.

## Subtopic: Every Element Signs Its Light

Stare at a burning streetlamp of the older orange kind and you are reading sodium's signature. Heat any element's gas until it glows, split the glow with a prism, and you do not get a rainbow — you get a handful of razor-sharp coloured lines on blackness, always the same lines for that element, in the same places, every time. Sodium's pair of orange lines, hydrogen's red and blue-green set: signatures, written in light.

Why lines, and not a smear? Because the electrons in an atom live on a ladder with fixed rungs and forbidden gaps. An electron cannot hover between rungs; it can only sit on one, and when it drops to a lower rung the atom emits one photon whose energy is exactly the height of the drop. Fixed rungs, fixed drops, fixed photon energies — and a fixed photon energy is a fixed colour. Every line in the spectrum is one particular staircase step, taken by billions of atoms at once. If electron energies could be anything, the lines would blur into a full rainbow; the sharp lines are quantum physics visible to the naked eye.

The signature works in reverse too. Shine a full rainbow through the same element's cool gas, and its atoms steal exactly the colours they would otherwise emit — the electrons jump UP their rungs, swallowing matching photons — leaving dark gaps in the rainbow at the signature positions. Bright lines from hot gas, dark lines from cool gas, same positions: two versions of one autograph.

And that autograph reads across the universe. Point a spectroscope at any star and the dark lines in its rainbow name the elements in its atmosphere — helium was found in sunlight before anyone found it on Earth. Slide the whole autograph toward the red, and you have measured the star running away. One ladder inside the atom, one barcode in the light, and suddenly chemistry can be done on things forever out of reach. The last questions of the lesson are yours now — fixed rungs make fixed colours, and fixed colours name the element.
