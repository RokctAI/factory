**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First, the one that fills my inbox each year: learners can quote E equals W nought plus E k max, but the units destroy them — joules, electron volts, and questions that give the work function in one and want the answer in the other. What is the survival routine?

**Tutor:** The routine is to make joules the home currency and treat the electron volt as a foreign note you exchange at the door. The electron volt exists because atomic energies are absurdly small in joules: one electron volt is the energy an electron gains crossing one volt of potential difference, and the exchange rate is fixed — one electron volt equals 1,6 times 10 to the minus 19 joules, the same number as the electron's charge, which is no coincidence, since energy is charge times voltage. So the survival routine has three steps. Step one: the moment a work function arrives in electron volts, convert it — multiply by 1,6 times 10 to the minus 19 — and write the joule value down before touching the photoelectric equation. Caesium's roughly 2,1 electron volts becomes 3,36 times 10 to the minus 19 joules, and now every term in the equation speaks the same language, because h f always emerges in joules when h is in joule seconds. Step two: do all the physics in joules — photon energy, subtraction, kinetic energy. Step three: only at the very end, if the question explicitly asks for electron volts, divide back by 1,6 times 10 to the minus 19. The classic mark-loser is mixing currencies mid-calculation — subtracting an electron-volt work function from a joule photon energy produces nonsense that still looks like a number. And one habit protects you from a related trap: always compare the photon energy with the work function BEFORE calculating kinetic energy. If the photon energy is smaller, the honest answer is that no emission occurs — writing a negative kinetic energy and moving on is an automatic zero for that part, because a negative kinetic energy is not physics.

**Mandy:** Next, the graph that decides distinctions: maximum kinetic energy plotted against frequency. Learners see it in past papers and cannot say what the slope, the intercepts, or the flat nothing before the threshold mean. Unpack it?

**Tutor:** That graph is the photoelectric equation drawn as a picture, and once you see the translation, every feature labels itself. Rearrange the equation: E k max equals h f minus W nought. Compare with a straight line, y equals m x plus c, and read off the anatomy. The gradient is h — Planck's constant itself, the same for every metal in the universe, which is the deep punchline: plot the experiment for caesium, for zinc, for platinum, and you get parallel lines, all climbing at exactly h joules per hertz. Millikan did precisely this experiment and extracted Planck's constant from the slope, confirming Einstein's equation against his own expectations. The horizontal intercept, where the line meets the frequency axis, is the threshold frequency f nought — the frequency at which the photon exactly pays the work function with nothing left over. Below it the graph shows no line at all, just nothing, because no electrons emerge; the flat absence IS the threshold made visible. Extend the line backwards past the axis, and it strikes the vertical axis at negative W nought — the work function appears as the magnitude of that intercept. So one graph hands you all three quantities: slope gives h, frequency intercept gives f nought, extrapolated energy intercept gives the work function. Different metals shift the line sideways — bigger work function, bigger threshold, line further right — but never tilt it. When the exam shows two lines for two metals and asks which has the larger work function, the answer is the one crossing the frequency axis further to the right, and the reason is that its electrons charge a higher exit fee while physics charges the same slope.

**Mandy:** The conceptual killer: a question doubles the intensity and asks what happens to the maximum kinetic energy, and half the class doubles it. Why is the wrong answer so magnetic, and what unhooks it?

**Tutor:** The wrong answer is magnetic because everyday intuition says more light means more of everything — brighter sunshine feels hotter, so surely brighter light kicks electrons harder. The unhooking move is to make learners say precisely what doubling the intensity doubles. It doubles the number of photons arriving per second. It does not touch any individual photon, because a photon's energy is h f and f has not changed. Now walk one electron through its ejection: the electron's entire energy budget comes from the ONE photon it absorbs — the whole packet or nothing, never one and a half packets, never help from a neighbouring photon. Its exit cost is the work function, fixed by the metal. So its maximum kinetic energy is that one photon's energy minus that fixed cost — a transaction between one photon and one electron in which the crowd of other photons plays no part whatsoever. Doubling the crowd doubles how many such transactions happen per second, which doubles the photocurrent, and changes nothing about any single transaction. The examiner's follow-up traps are all variations on this one lock: dim light above threshold still ejects electrons instantly, because one sufficient photon is one instant success; blinding light below threshold ejects nothing forever, because a million insufficient photons are a million separate failures. If a learner can complete the sentence "each electron's energy comes from exactly one photon, therefore..." three different ways — therefore intensity changes the count, therefore frequency changes the energy, therefore below threshold nothing happens — the topic is beaten. And that sentence is also the answer to "why does this support the particle nature of light" — a wave would share its energy across all electrons at once, and these transactions refuse to be shared.

**Mandy:** Last one, on spectra: learners see that emission lines are bright and absorption lines are dark, but they ask a sharp question — after a cool gas absorbs those photons, its electrons must fall back down and re-emit them. So why do the absorption lines stay dark instead of filling in?

**Tutor:** That is a genuinely excellent question, and the answer is about geometry, not energy. The absorbed photon is indeed re-emitted when the electron falls back — energy is conserved, nothing is destroyed. But direction is not conserved in re-emission. The original beam travels one definite path: from the light source, through the gas, toward your spectroscope. Absorption removes photons of the signature frequencies specifically from that beam. When an atom re-emits, the new photon departs in a RANDOM direction — up, sideways, backwards, anywhere on the sphere — because the atom has no memory of where its photon came from. Only a minuscule fraction of the re-emitted photons happen to rejoin the exact line toward your instrument. So along your line of sight, those frequencies arrive drastically depleted, and the lines look dark against the bright continuum — not black, if you measure carefully, but starved. Meanwhile an observer standing off to the SIDE of the gas cloud, out of the beam, sees the opposite: nothing but the re-emitted scattered light, which is an emission spectrum — bright lines on darkness. Same gas, same transitions, same frequencies; which spectrum you see depends only on where you stand relative to the beam. That is worth stating in an exam answer about stars: the star's dense interior supplies the continuous rainbow, the cooler atmosphere absorbs its signature frequencies out of the outgoing light, scatters them in all directions, and the thin dark lines we photograph name every element the light pushed through. The universe fills in its own paperwork — you only need to know which side of the beam you are reading it from.
