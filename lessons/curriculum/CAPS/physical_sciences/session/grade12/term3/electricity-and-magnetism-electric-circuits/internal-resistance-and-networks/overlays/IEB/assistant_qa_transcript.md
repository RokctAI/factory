**Mandy:** Class questions before we close! First up, the one I got three times in different words: a learner measured a battery at nine volts with a voltmeter, connected it to a circuit, measured again and got eight volts. They want to know where the missing volt physically went — and whether the voltmeter is lying.

**Tutor:** The voltmeter is telling the truth twice; it is just answering two different questions. The first reading, nine volts, was taken with no circuit connected, so no current flowed — and with zero current, the term I times r in the budget equation is zero. The meter therefore saw the full emf, the entire energy per coulomb the chemistry can raise. The second reading was taken while current flowed, and a flowing current must push through the battery's own interior — electrolyte, plates, connections — which resists like any material does. Crossing that internal resistance costs each coulomb some energy, and that cost is the lost volt: current times internal resistance, in this case two amperes times nought comma five ohms, exactly one volt. Where did it physically go? Into heat, generated inside the battery casing — which is why a hard-working battery is warm to the touch. Nothing vanished; the energy audit balances to the cent: nine volts raised, eight delivered to the circuit, one converted to internal heat. And notice what this hands you experimentally: the difference between the open-circuit reading and the loaded reading, divided by the current, IS the internal resistance. Two voltmeter readings and an ammeter reading, and you have measured a quantity hidden inside a sealed battery — that is the whole logic of the practical investigation, compressed into one subtraction and one division.

***

**Mandy:** Next, the parallel paradox. A learner refuses to believe that adding a resistor can LOWER the total resistance — their instinct says more resistors, more resistance, full stop. How do you talk them out of the instinct?

**Tutor:** By showing them the instinct is right for one geometry and wrong for the other, and the geometry is the whole story. In series, the instinct is perfect: every extra resistor is another obstacle on the only road, so the total can only climb. But parallel changes what "adding" means. A parallel resistor is not a new obstacle on the existing road — it is a NEW ROAD between the same two points. Ask the learner this: a stadium has two exit gates and the crowd takes twenty minutes to leave; the club opens a third gate — even a narrow one. Does the crowd leave faster or slower? Faster, always, because some people who would have queued at the old gates now stream through the new one, which relieves every queue simultaneously. No gate was made worse; an alternative was created. That is why the combined resistance of a parallel cluster must be smaller than its smallest branch — the small branch alone would carry everything, and the other branches can only help it. Turn that into the standing sanity check: compute any parallel combination, compare it with the smallest member, and if it is not smaller, redo the arithmetic — usually a reciprocal was forgotten or the product-over-sum shortcut was applied to three resistors, which it does not cover. And anchor the physics with the house: every appliance in a home hangs on its own parallel branch precisely so that switching on the kettle opens a new road for current instead of throttling the road the lights are using.

***

**Mandy:** Third: the four-link chain. Learners can recite "resistance down, current up, lost volts up, terminal voltage down", but one asked what happens at the extreme — if you keep adding parallel branches forever, does the terminal voltage fall all the way to zero?

**Tutor:** It does, and chasing that extreme is one of the best stress-tests of understanding this topic offers. Push the thought experiment: each added branch lowers the external resistance, and in the limit — a thick copper bar straight across the terminals, the infamous short circuit — the external resistance approaches zero. The budget equation becomes emf equals current times internal resistance, near enough, because there is nothing else left in the loop. The current now takes the largest value that battery can ever deliver: the emf divided by its own internal resistance alone. For our nine-volt cell with nought comma five ohms inside, that ceiling is eighteen amperes — and the terminal voltage, emf minus current times internal resistance, collapses toward zero: virtually the entire budget is being spent inside the battery as heat. That is why a shorted battery becomes dangerously hot and drains in minutes: it has become a heater with itself as the only element. Two insights fall out of the extreme. First, internal resistance is the battery's own overload protection of last resort — it is the only thing capping the short-circuit current, which is why a car battery, with milliohms inside, can push hundreds of amperes and weld a dropped spanner. Second, the terminal voltage is best read as a negotiation: the smaller the external resistance, the larger the share the interior claims, until at short circuit the interior claims everything. The four-link chain is just the first few steps down that slope; the short circuit is where the slope ends.

***

**Mandy:** Last one — the graph. A learner plotted terminal voltage against current from a practical and got a lovely straight line, but then wrote "gradient equals r" and lost a mark for the sign. Why is the gradient negative, and does the line ever bend?

**Tutor:** Rearrange the budget once and the whole graph explains itself. From emf equals I R external plus I r, the terminal voltage — which is the I R external part — equals emf minus I times r. Read that as a straight-line recipe, y equals c plus m x: the vertical intercept c is the emf, and the coefficient of the current, the gradient m, is MINUS r. The line slopes downhill because every extra ampere of demand raises the internal toll by r volts, and the terminals surrender exactly that much. So the correct statement is: gradient equals negative r, and therefore r equals the magnitude of the gradient — the sign belongs to the slope, not to the resistance, since a resistance can never itself be negative. On the reading side, use the two anchors: where the line meets the voltage axis, current is zero, no toll is paid, and you read the pure emf; extend the line the other way to where the terminal voltage would hit zero, and you find the short-circuit current, emf over r — the battery's absolute ceiling. Does the line bend in practice? At the far right it can: a battery driven very hard warms up, its chemistry and interior change, and r itself starts creeping upward, curving the tail of the plot downward faster than the straight line predicts. In your data range for the practical, treat it as straight, fit the best line, and quote both constants from it: intercept for the emf, negative gradient for r. One plot, both numbers, and the sign convention stated cleanly — that is the full-mark version.
