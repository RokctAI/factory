**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First, the one that comes up every single year: learners can recite that emf equals I R external plus I r, but they still cannot say what emf actually IS. Is it a voltage or an energy? And why does the voltmeter sometimes read it and sometimes not?

**Tutor:** It is both, and the bridge between the two meanings dissolves the confusion. The emf is the energy the battery's chemistry gives to each coulomb of charge that passes through it — joules per coulomb — and joules per coulomb is exactly what a volt is, so we state emf in volts. Think of it as the battery's gross salary: every coulomb is paid that many joules, without exception, whether current flows or not. The subtlety is what happens to the salary. While a coulomb travels through the inside of the battery, it must push through the battery's own internal resistance, and that costs it I times r joules per coulomb — a deduction taken before the coulomb ever leaves the building. What emerges at the terminals is the net salary: the terminal voltage, emf minus I times r. Now the voltmeter's behaviour explains itself. A voltmeter across the terminals can only ever read the NET amount — what the charge has left after the internal deduction. When the switch is open, no current flows, the deduction I times r is zero because I is zero, and net equals gross: the voltmeter reads the full emf. Close the switch and current flows, the deduction becomes real, and the reading drops below the emf by exactly I times r. So there is no mystery reading and no faulty meter — one instrument, one quantity, and the difference between its two readings is the most direct measurement of the lost volts you will ever make. That, incidentally, is precisely how this term's formal practical extracts the internal resistance from a table of voltmeter and ammeter readings.

***

**Mandy:** Next, the parallel paradox: learners accept the formula but refuse to believe it. How can ADDING a resistor — an obstacle — make the total resistance SMALLER? It feels like adding a roadblock and getting faster traffic.

**Tutor:** The picture that misleads them is a roadblock ON the existing road, and the fix is to see that a parallel resistor is never on the existing road — it is a NEW road. Nothing about the original branch changes when you connect another branch beside it: same resistor, same voltage across it, same current through it as before. The new branch simply opens alongside, and whatever current it carries is EXTRA, drawn on top of what already flowed. Total current up at the same battery voltage means, by the definition of resistance as voltage over current, that the effective resistance of the network went down. It cannot go any other way: the new branch can only add current, never subtract it, so total resistance can only fall, even if the new branch is a very poor conductor. A thin footpath beside a highway still carries a few pedestrians the highway no longer must. The place where intuition then needs one more correction is the battery. With an ideal battery the old branches genuinely would not notice the new one. With a REAL battery — internal resistance included — the extra current pays extra toll inside the battery, the terminal voltage sags, and the old branches now feel a slightly smaller voltage and dim slightly. So the full truth has two layers: parallel addition always lowers network resistance and raises total current; and in a real circuit that extra current slightly starves everyone through the shared internal resistance. Keep the layers separate and both examination questions — the ideal one and the real one — fall to you.

**Mandy:** The graph question: past papers give a straight line of terminal voltage against current and ask for emf and internal resistance. Learners read the intercept fine, but the gradient trips them — sign, units, meaning. What is the clean way through?

**Tutor:** Start from the equation the line is secretly drawing. Terminal voltage equals emf minus I times r — rearrange the master equation and it is already in straight-line form: V on the vertical axis, I on the horizontal, so the intercept on the V axis is the emf and the coefficient of I, which is negative r, is the gradient. Everything the learners fumble is contained in that mapping. The gradient is negative because the line slopes downward — each extra ampere of current costs r more volts in internal toll, so the delivered voltage falls as the load current grows. When you compute the gradient from two points, you will get a negative number with units of volts per ampere, and volts per ampere is ohms — the internal resistance is the SIZE of that gradient, so state r as a positive number of ohms and mention that the negative sign encodes the drooping line, not a negative resistance. Two further readings hide in the same graph and both have appeared in papers. The intercept on the CURRENT axis, where the terminal voltage would fall to zero, is the short-circuit current, emf divided by r — the absolute maximum the battery can deliver, with every volt of the emf consumed internally. And a second battery with the same emf but larger internal resistance draws the same starting point on the V axis with a STEEPER downhill line — same promise, faster collapse. Read a battery graph as a character sketch: where it starts is its ambition, how fast it droops is its age.

***

**Mandy:** Last one, the exam classic: a circuit has a bulb in series with the battery and a resistor in parallel with the bulb. The parallel resistor is then removed. Learners guess in every direction about what the bulb does. What is the disciplined way to reason it out?

**Tutor:** The discipline is to refuse to talk about the bulb until you have talked about the whole circuit, because every local change is really a global change wearing a disguise. Removing the resistor that sat in parallel with the bulb closes a channel: the network loses a path, so the external resistance RISES. That is step one, and it is where guessing usually goes wrong, because removal feels like it should lower resistance — but removing a parallel branch is the exact opposite of adding one. Step two goes to the loop: higher total resistance with the same emf means the main current FALLS. Step three visits the battery: smaller current pays less internal toll, so the terminal voltage RISES a little. Step four finally reaches the bulb, and here you must notice who its neighbours are. The bulb now carries the entire main current instead of sharing it, and the voltage across the bulb is the improved terminal voltage minus nothing else, if the bulb is the only external element left. Depending on the original arrangement, the bulb can end up brighter even though the total current fell — because sharing ended. That is why guessing fails: two effects pull in opposite directions, and only the sequence — network resistance, then main current, then terminal voltage, then the component's own share — settles which one wins. Train that four-step walk until it is reflex, and every add-a-resistor, remove-a-resistor, close-a-switch question in the paper becomes the same question in different clothes.
