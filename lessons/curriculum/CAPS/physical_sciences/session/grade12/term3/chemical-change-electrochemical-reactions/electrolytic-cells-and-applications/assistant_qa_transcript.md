**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First, the polarity panic: in the galvanic lesson the anode was negative, and now in electrolysis the anode is suddenly positive. Learners are convinced one of the lessons must be wrong. What settles it?

**Tutor:** Both lessons are right, and the settling insight is that anode was never a polarity word in the first place — it is a CHEMISTRY word. The anode is defined as the electrode where oxidation happens, full stop, in every electrochemical device ever built. The polarity is a separate fact that depends on who is in charge of the electrons. In a galvanic cell, chemistry is in charge: the spontaneous reaction pumps electrons OUT of the oxidation electrode, so electrons accumulate there and that electrode sits negative — the anode happens to be the negative terminal. In an electrolytic cell, the power source is in charge: its positive terminal grips one electrode and SUCKS electrons out of whatever touches it, forcing oxidation to happen there — so oxidation now occurs at the positive electrode, and the anode happens to be positive. Same definition, different boss, opposite polarity. The exam-proof routine is therefore to work in this order: first find the power source, if there is one — its presence announces an electrolytic cell. Second, trace the chemistry: where are electrons being stripped from a species? Label that electrode the anode. Third, and only third, read off the polarity from the wiring. Learners who start from the plus and minus signs are gambling on memory; learners who start from the oxidation are reading the definition. And the ion traffic never needs memorising at all, because it is pure electrostatics: negative ions drift to whichever electrode is positive, positive ions to whichever is negative, and the names cooperate — anions end up at the anode, cations at the cathode, in both kinds of cell.

***

**Mandy:** Next, the brine surprise: the solution is full of sodium ions, yet hydrogen gas comes off the cathode. Learners feel cheated — why does the ion that is actually queueing at the electrode get refused?

**Tutor:** Because an electrode is not a queue — it is an auction, and the electrons go to the highest bidder, not the nearest one. At the cathode, every reducible species in reach is bidding for the arriving electrons, and in brine there are two bidders: sodium ions and water molecules. The bid each makes is its electrode potential — how strongly it wants to be reduced. Sodium's bid is spectacularly weak: sodium sits at the extreme reducing end of the table, around minus two point seven volts, which is chemistry's way of saying sodium metal desperately prefers to exist as an ion. Asking the cathode to produce sodium metal in water is asking it to build the most eager electron-donor in the room while surrounded by things happy to take electrons — and even if you somehow succeeded, the fresh sodium would react violently with the water on contact and undo itself instantly. Water's bid is far stronger, so water wins the electrons: two water molecules plus two electrons give hydrogen gas and two hydroxide ions. That is the general rule for every aqueous electrolysis question: list ALL the candidates at each electrode, including water, then let the electrode potentials pick the winner. It also explains the anode's subtlety in the same cell. By pure potentials, water should beat chloride at the anode too, and in dilute solution it does — oxygen forms. But in CONCENTRATED brine, the enormous chloride population tilts the contest and chlorine wins instead. So the full answer has both layers: potentials set the default winner, and concentration can overturn a close contest. Sodium versus water is not close — which is why no concentration of brine ever yields sodium metal at a cathode in water.

**Mandy:** The refining question: learners follow why copper dissolves off the impure anode and plates onto the pure cathode, but the impurities confuse them — why does the silver in the slab fall to the floor while the iron in the slab stays dissolved in the solution?

**Tutor:** Track each impurity through its two possible gates, one at each electrode. Gate one is the anode: to leave the slab as an ion, an atom must be OXIDISED at the cell's gentle operating voltage — a voltage deliberately tuned so that copper just dissolves. Metals less reactive than copper — silver, gold, platinum — hold their electrons more tightly than copper does, so at that voltage the anode cannot pry them loose. They never dissolve at all; as the copper matrix around them washes away ion by ion, they simply lose their footing and drop off the slab as solid grains: the anode sludge, physically fallen, never chemically changed. Gate two is the cathode: an ion in solution must be REDUCED to rejoin the metallic world. Metals more reactive than copper — iron, zinc, nickel — do pass gate one readily; they oxidise even more easily than copper and enter the solution as ions. But at the cathode the auction logic from the brine question returns: copper ions are easier to reduce than iron or zinc ions, so the cathode reduces copper preferentially and turns the reactive impurities away. They pass the entry gate and fail the exit gate, so they accumulate harmlessly in the electrolyte, which the refinery periodically cleans. So the slab sorts itself into three destinies by one property, reactivity relative to copper: nobler metals fall as treasure, baser metals stay dissolved as tenants, and only copper itself completes the full journey from slab to solution to ninety-nine point nine nine percent pure cathode. One voltage, tuned to one metal, and the periodic table does the sorting.

**Mandy:** Last one, and it is the wording trap: a question says the same half-reaction, copper two plus gaining two electrons, occurs in a galvanic cell in one paper and an electrolytic cell in another. Learners think it must behave differently in the two devices. Does it?

**Tutor:** The half-reaction is identical in both devices, down to the last electron — what differs is why it is happening and what pays for it, and that distinction is exactly what the examiners are probing. Copper two plus, plus two electrons, gives copper: in the galvanic Daniell cell, this runs at the cathode because the reaction WANTS to run — copper's electrode potential beats zinc's, the overall reaction is spontaneous, chemical energy flows out as electrical energy, and the copper electrode grows while a bulb glows for free. In the electrolytic refining tank or the copper chloride demonstration, the very same reduction runs at the cathode because the power source FORCES electrons onto that electrode at a pressure the ions cannot refuse — the overall reaction is non-spontaneous, electrical energy flows in and is stored as chemical change, and every gram of copper deposited appears on someone's electricity bill. So when a question asks you to compare, structure the answer as three sames and three differents. Same: the half-reaction equations, the definition that reduction happens at the cathode, and the direction ions move relative to their electrode. Different: the energy conversion — chemical to electrical versus electrical to chemical; the spontaneity — willing versus forced; and the polarity of the electrodes — the galvanic cathode is positive while the electrolytic cathode is negative. Learners who can produce that three-and-three table have the entire electrochemistry topic folded into one comparison, and it is the single most efficient piece of revision this section offers.
