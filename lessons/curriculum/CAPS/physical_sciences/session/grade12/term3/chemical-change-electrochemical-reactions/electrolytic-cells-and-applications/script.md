# Part 1 — Expert

A galvanic cell is chemistry that happens because it wants to. An electrolytic cell is chemistry that happens because you PAY for it: electrical energy forced into a reaction that would never run on its own. That single reversal powers a surprising share of the modern world — the copper in every cable, the chrome on every tap, the chlorine in every water treatment plant. This session defines electrolysis and contrasts the two cell types, works through the decomposition of copper chloride, masters electroplating and the refining of copper, and closes with the electrolysis of concentrated sodium chloride, the reaction behind an entire industry.

## Subtopic: Galvanic versus Electrolytic

ELECTROLYSIS is the chemical process in which electrical energy is used to drive a non-spontaneous redox reaction — the direct opposite of the galvanic cell's energy story. Galvanic: chemical energy becomes electrical energy, spontaneously, and the cell IS the battery. Electrolytic: electrical energy becomes chemical energy, non-spontaneously, and the cell NEEDS a battery or power source in the circuit to force the reaction along.

Three anchors survive the crossing between the two cell types, and three things flip. The anchors: oxidation still happens at the anode, reduction still happens at the cathode, and the electrolyte still conducts through moving ions — those definitions never bend. Now the flips. First, the energy direction, as stated. Second, the polarity labels: in a galvanic cell the anode is negative, but in an electrolytic cell the anode is POSITIVE, because it is wired to the positive terminal of the power source, and the cathode is negative. The rescue rule when confusion strikes: forget plus and minus, and ask where oxidation happens — that electrode is the anode, in either cell, every time. Third, the driver: a galvanic cell runs downhill from a willing reaction; an electrolytic cell is pushed uphill by the external source.

Ion traffic in the electrolytic cell follows the wiring. The positive electrode attracts the negative ions, which surrender electrons there — oxidation at the anode. The negative electrode attracts the positive ions, the cations, which collect electrons there — reduction at the cathode. Handily, the names agree: ANions travel to the ANode, CATions to the CAThode.

This section's questions are with you now — same definitions, flipped polarity, and the power source is the engine of everything.

## Subtopic: Decomposing Copper Chloride

The syllabus names the electrolysis of a copper two chloride solution as the model reaction, so command it completely. The setup, described in words: a beaker of copper chloride solution — blue-green from the copper ions — with two inert carbon electrodes standing in it, connected through a battery. Inert matters: carbon conducts but does not join the chemistry; it is a stage, not an actor. The solution contains copper two plus cations and chloride anions, both free to move.

Switch on and follow each ion family. The copper two plus ions drift to the negative electrode, the cathode. Each collects two electrons and is reduced: copper two plus, plus two electrons, gives copper solid. Watch the electrode: a salmon-brown layer of copper metal grows on it. The chloride ions drift to the positive electrode, the anode, where each pair of chloride ions gives up two electrons — oxidation: two chloride ions give chlorine gas plus two electrons. Watch this electrode too: bubbles rise, and the sharp swimming-pool smell of chlorine confirms the product.

Add the two half-reactions and the electrons cancel: copper two plus, plus two chloride ions, gives copper solid plus chlorine gas. The compound has been DECOMPOSED — pulled apart into its elements by electrical force, which is exactly what the reaction refuses to do spontaneously; left alone, copper and chlorine would rather combine than separate. The energy for the separation came entirely from the battery.

The observable checklist for exam answers: copper plating on the cathode, chlorine bubbling and smelling at the anode, and the blue-green colour of the solution fading as copper ions are steadily removed. Three observations, two half-reactions, one net equation — that package answers any version of the question.

Questions on this section reach you now — cations to the cathode for their electrons, anions to the anode to give theirs up, and the battery pays for the divorce.

## Subtopic: Electroplating and Refining Copper

Electrolysis earns its keep by putting metal exactly where industry wants it, one ion at a time. ELECTROPLATING coats an object with a thin layer of another metal. The recipe never varies, and the syllabus loves to examine it: the OBJECT to be plated is the CATHODE — connected to the negative terminal; the ANODE is a bar of the PLATING metal itself; the electrolyte is a solution containing ions of the plating metal. To silver-plate a spoon: the spoon is the cathode, a silver bar is the anode, and silver nitrate solution is the bath.

Run the current and a perfect relay operates. At the anode, silver atoms are oxidised and dissolve: silver gives silver ion plus one electron. At the cathode, silver ions from the solution are reduced onto the spoon: silver ion plus one electron gives silver metal. One dissolves, the other deposits, and the solution's concentration stays constant — the anode continuously replaces exactly the ions the cathode removes. The spoon gains a silver skin; the bar quietly shrinks. Why plate at all? Protection against corrosion, and appearance — a steel spoon with silver's face, chrome-plated taps, gold-plated connectors: cheap strength dressed in expensive surface.

The REFINING of copper uses the same relay to purify. Copper fresh from the smelter is about ninety-nine percent pure, and the stray one percent ruins its electrical conductivity — power cables demand better. So: the IMPURE copper slab is the anode, a thin sheet of PURE copper is the cathode, copper sulfate solution the electrolyte. Current on: copper atoms oxidise out of the impure anode into solution, and copper ions plate onto the pure cathode, which grows into a slab of copper at ninety-nine point nine nine percent purity. The impurities take two exits: metals less reactive than copper — silver, gold, platinum — never oxidise, and simply drop off the shrinking anode as sludge, an anode mud so rich in precious metals that selling it helps fund the refinery. Impurities more reactive than copper do dissolve, but they stay dissolved: at the modest operating voltage they are harder to reduce than copper ions, so the cathode selects copper and leaves them in solution.

This section's questions arrive now — object at the cathode, plating metal at the anode, and the refinery's profit falls off the anode as mud.

## Subtopic: Electrolysis of Concentrated Sodium Chloride

The syllabus's industrial centrepiece: pass current through concentrated sodium chloride solution — brine — and one cheap raw material yields three valuable products. This is the chlor-alkali industry, a pillar of chemical manufacturing worldwide.

The solution holds sodium ions, chloride ions, and — easy to forget, essential to remember — water molecules, which can themselves be oxidised or reduced. Every electrode is therefore a competition. At the anode, chloride ions and water both offer electrons; in CONCENTRATED solution, the sheer abundance of chloride wins the contest, and chlorine gas bubbles off: two chloride ions give chlorine gas plus two electrons. At the cathode, sodium ions and water both ask for electrons — and here water wins outright, because sodium ions are among the hardest of all cations to reduce; sodium metal is far too eager to STAY ionised. So water is reduced instead: two water molecules plus two electrons give hydrogen gas plus two hydroxide ions.

Audit what remains in the vessel. Chloride leaves as chlorine at one electrode; water leaves as hydrogen at the other, depositing hydroxide ions as it goes. Sodium ions remain in solution, now partnered with the fresh hydroxide: the liquid left behind is sodium hydroxide solution. Three products from salt water: chlorine gas at the anode, hydrogen gas at the cathode, sodium hydroxide in the cell.

All three earn their keep. Chlorine disinfects drinking water and swimming pools, bleaches, and feeds the manufacture of PVC plastic. Hydrogen fuels, hardens oils in the food industry, and feeds ammonia synthesis. Sodium hydroxide — caustic soda — makes soap, paper, and textiles. And the concentration condition is examinable in itself: in DILUTE salt solution, water out-competes chloride at the anode and oxygen gas forms instead of chlorine; industry keeps the brine concentrated precisely because chlorine, not oxygen, is the prize.

The final expert questions are with you now — water fights at both electrodes, winning at the cathode and losing at the concentrated anode, and brine becomes three industries.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same chemistry from the payback side: running the battery backwards, dressing cheap metal in precious skin, and turning salt water into an industry.

## Subtopic: Running the Reaction Backwards

A galvanic cell is like water flowing downhill: the reaction wants to happen, and you harvest the flow as electricity on the way down. An electrolytic cell is the pump pushing water back UP the hill: the reaction does not want to happen, so you buy electricity and force it. Same hill, same water, opposite direction — and someone must pay for every trip uphill.

You own this exact machinery. Using your phone, the battery runs downhill — chemistry spending itself to light the screen. Plug in the charger and the wall socket shoves the same reaction backwards up the hill, rebuilding the chemicals so they can fall again tomorrow. A rechargeable battery is simply a cell that commutes: galvanic all day, electrolytic all night.

Keep two anchors and one flip from this picture. The anchors: oxidation still happens at the anode and reduction at the cathode — those words follow the CHEMISTRY, not the wiring, and they never flip. The flip: in the forced-uphill cell, the anode is the POSITIVE electrode, because the power source's positive terminal grips it, while in the battery running downhill the anode is negative. When a diagram confuses you, ignore the plus and minus signs entirely and ask one question: where are electrons being torn away? That is oxidation; that is the anode; everything else follows. The ions never get confused: negative ions drift to the positive electrode, positive ions to the negative one — opposites attract, and the vocabulary even rhymes to help you: anions to the anode, cations to the cathode.

Your first questions are with you now — downhill for free, uphill for a fee, and the anode is wherever electrons are being taken.

## Subtopic: A Silver Skin on a Steel Spoon

Why does a jeweller pass electricity through a spoon? Because electrolysis is the only painter that works one atom at a time. Electroplating dresses a cheap object in a precious skin — and the recipe is worth memorising as a rhythm: the object goes to the NEGATIVE electrode, the precious metal bar goes to the POSITIVE one, and the bath is a solution of the precious metal's ions.

Feel the logic instead of memorising blindly. Metal ions in the bath are positive, so they drift toward the negative electrode — the spoon — and on arrival each collects electrons and becomes solid metal, painted on. Silver falls on the spoon atom by atom, an even coat creeping over every curve, including crevices no brush could enter. Meanwhile the silver bar at the positive electrode dissolves at exactly the same rate, feeding fresh ions into the bath. The bar is both paint tin and refill: the bath never runs dry, and the coat grows as thick as the time you leave the current on.

The refinery plays the same game with a sneaky twist: both electrodes are copper — one filthy, one pure. The dirty slab dissolves at the positive electrode; pure copper rains onto the clean sheet at the negative one. And the dirt? The precious impurities — silver, gold, platinum hiding in the raw copper — refuse to dissolve, and simply drop off the shrinking slab into a sludge on the tank floor. Refineries sell that mud for a fortune: it is the only dirt on Earth worth more than the metal it fell from. Purification by preference: the cell dissolves what it can, deposits only what it chooses, and lets treasure fall out of the way.

Questions on this section are coming to you now — spoon at the negative, bar at the positive, and the sludge pays the refinery's bills.

## Subtopic: Salt Water In, Three Industries Out

Take the cheapest chemical on the planet — salt dissolved in water — and force a current through it. What comes out funds factories on every continent: chlorine gas at one electrode, hydrogen gas at the other, and caustic soda left in the tank. One input, three saleable outputs; electrolysis as a money machine.

The clever part is what does NOT happen. You might expect sodium metal at the negative electrode — sodium ions are right there, positively charged, queueing. But sodium clings to its ionised state more stubbornly than almost anything in chemistry, so the electrode turns it away and reduces WATER instead: hydrogen gas bubbles up, leaving hydroxide ions behind. The rejected sodium ions and the new hydroxide ions make sodium hydroxide — the caustic soda that becomes soap and paper. At the positive electrode, chloride wins the contest instead — but only because the brine is CONCENTRATED; the crowd of chloride ions overwhelms the water's competing claim, and chlorine gas streams off to purify drinking water, bleach, and become PVC pipes. Dilute the brine and water snatches that electrode too, giving oxygen — which is why the industry keeps its salt strong.

Look around and the products are everywhere: the pool smelling faintly of chlorine, the soap in the dish, the plastic pipes in the wall. All of it starts as salt water and a current — chemistry that never happens on its own, bought and paid for at the socket, and worth every cent. The last questions of the lesson are yours now — water wins the negative electrode, concentrated chloride wins the positive one, and the leftovers are a product too.
