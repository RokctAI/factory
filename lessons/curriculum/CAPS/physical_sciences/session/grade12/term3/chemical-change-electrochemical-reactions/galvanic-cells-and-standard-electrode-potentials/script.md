# Part 1 — Expert

Every battery ever made runs on one idea: a chemical reaction split in half, with the electrons forced to travel through a wire to get from one half to the other. That is the galvanic cell, and it anchors the electrochemistry that Paper 2 tests every year. This session sharpens the redox language the topic depends on, builds the zinc-copper cell piece by piece, reads the table of standard electrode potentials with the standard hydrogen electrode as its anchor, and finishes with cell notation and emf calculations — the reliable marks of the section.

## Subtopic: Redox: the Electron Bookkeeping

Electrochemistry is redox chemistry, so the vocabulary must be automatic. OXIDATION is the loss of electrons; REDUCTION is the gain of electrons. Keep them straight with the old friend OIL RIG: oxidation is loss, reduction is gain. The two never happen alone — every electron lost must be gained by something — so the full event is always a redox reaction, and it splits neatly into two half-reactions, one shedding electrons, one collecting them.

Now the agents, where signs get crossed under pressure. The OXIDISING agent is the substance that causes oxidation in the other substance — and does so by taking its electrons, so the oxidising agent is itself REDUCED. The REDUCING agent donates electrons to the other substance, and is itself OXIDISED. The agent is named for what it does to the partner, not for what happens to itself. In the reaction between zinc metal and copper ions, zinc loses two electrons and is oxidised, so zinc is the reducing agent; the copper ion gains those electrons and is reduced, so the copper ion is the oxidising agent.

Oxidation numbers make the bookkeeping visible in bigger formulas: an element's oxidation number rises when it is oxidised and falls when it is reduced. Uncombined elements sit at zero; simple ions carry their charge as their number. Zinc metal at zero becoming zinc two plus has climbed from nought to plus two — oxidation, confirmed by arithmetic.

Three definitions of place complete the toolkit, and they hold for every electrochemical cell: the ANODE is the electrode where oxidation occurs; the CATHODE is the electrode where reduction occurs; the ELECTROLYTE is the solution that conducts electricity through the movement of its ions. Anode and oxidation both begin with vowels, cathode and reduction with consonants — a small anchor that has rescued many exam answers.

This section's questions are with you now — oxidation is loss, the agent is named for what it does to the other substance, and the anode is always where oxidation happens.

## Subtopic: Anatomy of the Galvanic Cell

Build the classic zinc-copper cell in your mind's eye, component by component. Two beakers. In the left one, a strip of zinc metal stands in a solution of zinc sulfate. In the right, a strip of copper stands in copper sulfate solution — recognisable by its blue colour. A wire connects the two metal strips through a voltmeter or a bulb. And bridging the two beakers, an upside-down U-shaped tube filled with a salt solution such as potassium nitrate: the salt bridge. That is a galvanic cell — also called a voltaic cell — a device that converts chemical energy to electrical energy through a spontaneous redox reaction.

Now run it. Zinc holds its electrons more loosely than copper, so at the zinc electrode, zinc atoms are oxidised: zinc becomes zinc two plus, plus two electrons. Zinc is the anode, and in a galvanic cell the anode is the NEGATIVE electrode, because the electrons pile up there. Those electrons travel through the external wire — through the voltmeter, the bulb, your calculator — to the copper strip, where copper two plus ions from the solution collect them and are reduced to copper metal, plating onto the electrode. Copper is the cathode, the positive electrode. Electrons flow through the wire from anode to cathode — from zinc to copper — and that flow is the current the cell exists to deliver.

Watch the consequences accumulate. The zinc electrode slowly dissolves and loses mass. The copper electrode gains mass as fresh copper plates on. The blue of the copper solution fades as its ions are consumed. And in the left beaker, positive zinc ions keep entering the solution, threatening a positive charge build-up that would halt the reaction almost immediately.

The salt bridge is the rescue. It completes the circuit and maintains electrical neutrality in both half-cells: negative ions from the bridge drift toward the anode beaker to balance the accumulating zinc ions, and positive ions drift toward the cathode beaker to replace the departing copper ions. Remove the salt bridge and the voltmeter drops to zero at once — no neutrality, no flow, no cell. Inside the solutions, current is carried by ions; inside the wire, by electrons; the salt bridge is where the two circuits close.

Questions on this section reach you now — electrons leave the anode through the wire, ions close the loop through the bridge, and the salt bridge keeps both beakers neutral.

## Subtopic: Standard Electrode Potentials

Which metal gives up electrons more willingly? The half-reaction table answers that question with numbers. Each entry is a reduction half-reaction with a STANDARD ELECTRODE POTENTIAL, symbol E standard, in volts — measured under standard conditions: solution concentrations of one mole per cubic decimetre, a temperature of twenty-five degrees Celsius, and gases at standard pressure.

A potential can only be measured against a reference, and chemistry's chosen zero is the STANDARD HYDROGEN ELECTRODE: hydrogen gas bubbling over a platinum electrode in a one molar solution of hydrogen ions, defined as exactly nought volts. Every other half-cell is measured connected against it, so every number in the table is a comparison with hydrogen. Copper's reduction potential of plus 0,34 volts means the copper half-cell pulls electrons harder than hydrogen; zinc's minus 0,76 volts means zinc pulls more weakly than hydrogen — equivalently, zinc pushes electrons away more readily.

Reading the table is a skill worth marks on its own. The more POSITIVE the electrode potential, the stronger the substance on the left of that half-reaction is as an OXIDISING agent — the more fiercely it grabs electrons. The more NEGATIVE the potential, the stronger the substance on the right is as a REDUCING agent — the more readily it donates. Fluorine sits at the strong-oxidiser extreme; lithium metal at the strong-reducer extreme. Two rules of thumb follow. First: in any galvanic pairing, the half-cell with the HIGHER electrode potential runs forward as the reduction — it becomes the cathode — and the lower one is forced backwards into oxidation, becoming the anode. Second: a spontaneous reaction needs the oxidising agent of a higher half-reaction meeting the reducing agent of a lower one; that prediction question appears in almost every paper.

This section's questions arrive now — every number is a comparison with hydrogen's zero, and the higher potential takes the cathode's job.

## Subtopic: Cell Notation and EMF Calculations

Chemists compress a whole cell into one line of notation, and the syllabus requires you to read and write it. The convention: anode on the left, cathode on the right. A single vertical line marks a phase boundary — solid electrode against solution — and a double vertical line marks the salt bridge. The zinc-copper cell reads: zinc solid, line, zinc two plus aqueous, double line, copper two plus aqueous, line, copper solid. Read any cell notation from the outside in: oxidation story on the left of the double line, reduction story on the right.

The emf calculation is the most reliable mark in the section. The formula from the data sheet: E standard of the cell equals E standard of the cathode minus E standard of the anode. For zinc-copper: the cathode is copper at plus 0,34 volts, the anode is zinc at minus 0,76 volts. E cell equals 0,34 minus negative 0,76 — subtracting a negative adds — giving 1,10 volts. The double negative is the classic slip: write the subtraction out in full with brackets before computing, every time.

One more example locks the method. Magnesium and silver: magnesium's potential is minus 2,36 volts, silver's plus 0,80 volts. Silver, higher, is the cathode; magnesium the anode. E cell equals 0,80 minus negative 2,36 — 3,16 volts. A positive cell emf confirms the reaction is spontaneous as written; if your subtraction produces a negative emf, you have the electrodes the wrong way around — swap them, because a galvanic cell runs only in the direction that gives a positive emf.

Finally, the fate of every battery: as the cell operates, reactant concentrations fall and the emf drops, until the reaction reaches EQUILIBRIUM — forward and reverse rates equal, no net electron push, emf zero. That is a flat battery: nothing is empty, nothing leaked out; the chemistry has simply arrived at equilibrium and has no further tendency to drive electrons anywhere.

The final expert questions are with you now — cathode minus anode with brackets on the negatives, and flat means equilibrium, not empty.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same chemistry as a story about generosity, a bridge that keeps the peace, and a league table that predicts every match.

## Subtopic: Chemistry That Pays in Electricity

Some metals are generous with their electrons; some are grasping. Zinc is a giver — it parts with two electrons without much of a fight. Copper ions are takers — they snatch electrons eagerly. Drop a strip of zinc directly into a blue copper solution and the exchange happens on the spot: zinc dissolves, brown copper crusts onto the strip, the blue fades, and the beaker warms slightly. A real chemical transaction — but the energy escapes as useless heat, like money burned.

A galvanic cell is the same transaction with the payment routed through YOUR wire. Separate the giver and the taker into different beakers so the electrons cannot jump across directly. Now the only route from zinc to copper ion runs through the external wire — and electrons marching through a wire ARE an electric current. Put a bulb in their path and they light it; put a motor, and they turn it. The cell converts chemical energy to electrical energy not by making a new reaction, but by making an old reaction commute through your circuit.

Every battery in your life is this trick in different packaging: two substances that want to trade electrons, kept apart so the trade must pass through the device you are powering. The wanting is the voltage — the harder the two sides want the trade, the bigger the push on each electron. And when the wanting runs out, the trade stops: a flat battery is a finished transaction, chemistry that has reached its resting point, not a container that leaked something out.

Your first questions are with you now — separate the giver from the taker, and make the electrons commute through your device.

## Subtopic: The Bridge That Keeps the Peace

The two-beaker design has a fatal flaw hiding in it. Follow the charges. Every zinc atom that donates electrons enters the left-hand solution as a positive ion — so the left beaker grows more positive with every transaction. Meanwhile the right beaker keeps consuming its positive copper ions — so it drifts negative. Within moments, the growing positive charge on the left would drag on every departing electron like a rubber band, and the whole enterprise would seize up. Charge imbalance is the strike that shuts the factory.

Enter the salt bridge: an upside-down U of tubing filled with a neutral salt solution, one arm dipped in each beaker. It is not decoration and it is not a wire — it is a corridor for IONS. As positive charge builds up in the anode beaker, negative ions stroll out of the bridge into that beaker and neutralise the excess. As the cathode beaker runs short of positive ions, positive ions stroll out of the other arm to top it up. Both beakers stay electrically neutral, nobody strikes, and the electrons keep flowing through the wire upstairs.

That gives the circuit its beautiful two-storey structure: upstairs, electrons run through the metal wire from zinc to copper; downstairs, ions shuffle through the solutions and the bridge to balance the books. Neither storey works without the other. Pull the salt bridge out and the voltmeter slams to zero instantly — the fastest, cleanest demonstration in the chapter, and a guaranteed exam explanation: the bridge completes the circuit and maintains neutrality in both half-cells. No bridge, no balance; no balance, no current.

Questions on this section are coming to you now — electrons upstairs, ions downstairs, and the bridge keeps both beakers calm.

## Subtopic: The League Table of Electron Grabbers

Why zinc and copper, and not copper and zinc the other way around? Chemistry publishes a league table that settles every such contest before it is played: the table of standard electrode potentials. Think of it as a ranking of electron GRABBERS. At one end sit the ferocious grabbers — fluorine the undisputed champion — with big positive voltages next to their names. At the other end sit the metals that barely hold their electrons at all — lithium, potassium, the pushovers — with big negative numbers.

Every number answers one question: compared with hydrogen — the agreed zero of the table — how hard does this substance pull on electrons? Copper at plus 0,34 pulls harder than hydrogen. Zinc at minus 0,76 pulls more weakly — which is the polite way of saying zinc would rather give its electrons away. Pair any two substances, and the match plays out by one rule: the stronger grabber grabs, the weaker one surrenders. Copper ions outgrab zinc, so copper ions are reduced and zinc metal is oxidised — never the reverse, not spontaneously, no matter how long you wait.

The table even quotes the score in advance. The gap between the two rankings is the cell's voltage: copper's plus 0,34 minus zinc's minus 0,76 gives 1,10 volts — a mid-table match, a modest push. Pair silver with magnesium, two names from opposite ends, and the gap yawns to 3,16 volts — a mismatch, a big push on every electron. Choose two neighbours from the table and you get almost nothing at all. Battery designers are effectively fixture managers: they hunt for pairings with big gaps that are also cheap, safe, and long-lived.

So one printed page settles direction and voltage for every pairing in the paper: the higher-ranked side takes the electrons and the gap between them is the emf. The last questions of the lesson are yours now — read the rankings, name the grabber, and quote the gap.
