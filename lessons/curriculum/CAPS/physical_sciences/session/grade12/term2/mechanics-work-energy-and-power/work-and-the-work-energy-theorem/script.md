# Part 1 — Expert

Work, energy and power form the second great block of Paper 1 mechanics, and they reward a different way of thinking: instead of forces and accelerations instant by instant, we track energy flowing in and out of a moving object over a whole journey. This session defines work precisely, distinguishes positive from negative and zero work, builds net work from a free-body diagram, and then proves the tool that makes the topic powerful — the work-energy theorem — on flat ground and on inclines.

## Subtopic: Work Done by a Force

The definition: the work done on an object by a force is the product of the magnitude of the force, the magnitude of the displacement, and the cosine of the angle between the force and the displacement. In symbols, W equals F delta x cosine theta. Work is a SCALAR — no direction — and its unit is the joule: one joule is the work done when a force of one newton acts through one metre along its own direction.

The cosine factor is the heart of the definition, and it sorts every force into three classes. When the force points along the displacement, theta is zero, cosine is one, and the work is positive — the force feeds energy INTO the object. When the force points opposite to the displacement, theta is 180 degrees, cosine is negative one, and the work is negative — the force drains energy OUT. And when the force is perpendicular to the displacement, theta is 90 degrees, cosine is zero, and the force does NO work at all, however large it is.

Work each class on one crate. A worker drags a 10 kilogram crate 5 metres across a level warehouse floor with a horizontal force of 50 newtons, against friction of 20 newtons. The applied force: W equals 50 multiplied by 5 multiplied by cosine zero, which is 250 joules — positive. Friction acts backward: 20 multiplied by 5 multiplied by cosine 180, which is negative 100 joules. The weight and the normal force both stand perpendicular to the motion: cosine 90, zero joules each. A force can be enormous and still do nothing — the normal force carries the crate's whole weight and contributes not one joule.

The classic trap: a waiter carrying a tray at constant height across a room does no work on the tray, because the upward force is perpendicular to the horizontal displacement. Effort is not work; only force along displacement is work.

This section's questions are with you now — quote the definition, compute the cosine, and classify each force's work as positive, negative or zero.

## Subtopic: Net Work from the Free-Body Diagram

Net work is the total work done on an object by ALL forces acting on it, and there are two equivalent routes to it. Route one: calculate the work done by each force separately and add them, signs included. Route two: find the net force first, then apply W net equals F net delta x cosine theta once.

Take route one through the crate. Applied force: positive 250 joules. Friction: negative 100 joules. Normal force and weight: zero each. Net work: 250 minus 100, which is 150 joules. Now route two: the net force is 50 minus 20, which is 30 newtons forward; net work equals 30 multiplied by 5, which is 150 joules. Same answer, as it must be.

The free-body diagram from Newton's laws is not optional here — it is the inventory. Draw the dot, draw every force, and every arrow on that diagram owes you a work calculation, even if the answer is zero. Most lost marks in this section are forces that never made it onto the diagram, usually friction or the component of gravity along a slope.

Sign discipline matters more than in any earlier topic, because the signs now carry physical meaning. Positive net work means the forces are, on balance, feeding energy into the object's motion — it speeds up. Negative net work means the object is being drained — it slows down. Zero net work, as with a crate dragged at constant velocity, means deposits and withdrawals balance exactly.

One more subtlety the examiner likes: work is done BY a force ON an object. The question "how much work is done by friction on the crate" and "how much work is done by the worker on the crate" are different questions with different answers, and reading which force and which object the sentence names is half the mark.

Questions on this section are in front of you now — inventory first, then one work term per arrow, then the signed sum.

## Subtopic: The Work-Energy Theorem

Here is the payoff. The work-energy theorem: the net work done on an object is equal to the change in the object's kinetic energy. In symbols, W net equals delta E k, which is a half m v final squared minus a half m v initial squared. The theorem is completely general — flat ground, slopes, curves, any combination of forces.

Apply it to the crate. Net work was 150 joules, and the crate started from rest. So 150 equals a half multiplied by 10 multiplied by v final squared, giving v final squared equals 30, and v final equals 5,48 metres per second. No kinematics equations, no acceleration calculated — the energy bookkeeping went straight from forces to final speed.

Run it in reverse for braking. A 1 000 kilogram car travelling at 20 metres per second brakes to a stop in 50 metres. Change in kinetic energy: zero minus a half multiplied by 1 000 multiplied by 400, which is negative 200 000 joules. That change was produced by the friction force alone, acting backward over 50 metres: negative F multiplied by 50 equals negative 200 000, so F equals 4 000 newtons. Notice the theorem handled a question about force using energy — the two languages translate into each other, and choosing the energy language is usually faster whenever a distance is given rather than a time.

When is the theorem the wrong tool? When the question asks about TIME. Work knows about forces and displacements; it is silent about how long anything took. Time questions belong to the impulse-momentum theorem from Term 1 — force multiplied by time — while distance questions belong here — force multiplied by displacement. Reading which one the question gives you is the topic's core skill.

This section's questions arrive now — state the theorem in words and symbols, then let it carry you between forces, distances and speeds.

## Subtopic: Work on an Inclined Plane

Inclines merge this topic with Newton's laws, and one worked example contains the entire method.

A 20 kilogram crate is pulled 4 metres up a ramp inclined at 30 degrees, at CONSTANT velocity, by a force parallel to the ramp, against friction of 30 newtons. Free-body diagram: the applied force up the slope; friction down the slope, opposing motion; the normal force perpendicular to the surface; and the weight, straight down, which we split into two components — one along the slope, m g sine theta, pulling the crate back down the ramp, and one perpendicular to it, m g cosine theta, pressed into the surface.

The component along the slope: 20 multiplied by 9,8 multiplied by sine 30 degrees, which is 20 multiplied by 9,8 multiplied by 0,5 — 98 newtons down the ramp. Constant velocity means zero net force, so the applied force must balance both opponents: 98 plus 30, which is 128 newtons.

Now the work audit over the 4 metres. Work by the applied force: 128 multiplied by 4, positive 512 joules. Work by gravity: the along-slope component opposes the motion, so negative 98 multiplied by 4, which is negative 392 joules. Work by friction: negative 30 multiplied by 4, negative 120 joules. Work by the normal force: perpendicular, zero. Net work: 512 minus 392 minus 120 — zero joules. And the theorem nods in agreement: constant velocity means no change in kinetic energy, so the net work HAD to be zero. That cross-check — net work against delta E k — is free insurance, and top candidates run it every time.

Notice also where gravity's negative 392 joules went: the crate rose 4 multiplied by sine 30, which is 2 metres, and 20 multiplied by 9,8 multiplied by 2 is 392 joules of gravitational potential energy gained. Nothing is lost; the ledger just moved energy from one account to another — which is exactly where the next lesson begins.

The final expert questions are with you now — split the weight, audit every force over the same displacement, and let delta E k check your total.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same ideas without the cosines: a bag of cement, a bank account, and a ramp at a taxi rank.

## Subtopic: Work Is Not Effort

Here is the strangest sentence in this topic, and it is pure physics: you can sweat all afternoon and do zero work.

Hold a 50 kilogram bag of cement on your shoulders, standing perfectly still, for ten minutes. Your legs burn, your back aches — and the work you have done on that bag, by the physics definition, is exactly zero. Why? Because work only counts when a force moves something ALONG the force's own direction. Your push is upward; the bag went nowhere. No displacement, no work.

Now walk across the yard carrying the bag at the same height. Still zero work on the bag. Your force is upward; the movement is sideways; the two are at right angles, and a force at right angles to the motion contributes nothing — the same way pushing down on a bicycle's handlebars does not make it go faster.

Only when you lift the bag ONTO the bakkie does physics start paying: force up, movement up, work done. And when you lower it gently to the ground, something stranger happens — the bag moves down while your hands push up, force against motion, and your work on the bag is NEGATIVE. You are taking energy away from it, which is precisely why it lands softly instead of crashing.

So the rule of thumb: along the motion, positive work — you are feeding the object energy. Against the motion, negative work — you are draining it. Sideways to the motion, zero — you are just holding on.

Your first questions are with you now — ask "did my force point along the movement?" before anything else.

## Subtopic: The Energy Bank Account

Every moving object carries a bank account, and the balance is its kinetic energy — energy of motion. The work-energy theorem is just the bank statement: net work done on the object equals the change in its balance. Deposits are positive work; withdrawals are negative work.

Watch a taxi pull away from the rank. The engine, through the wheels, does positive work — deposit after deposit — and the balance climbs: the taxi speeds up. The balance grows with the SQUARE of the speed, which is the detail that matters most in real life: doubling your speed does not double the account, it quadruples it. A car at 120 kilometres per hour is holding four times the kinetic energy it had at 60.

Now the brakes. Braking is friction making a withdrawal: a backward force acting over the stopping distance, doing negative work until the account hits zero. And because the account quadruples when speed doubles, the withdrawal must quadruple too — same brakes, same force, four times the stopping distance. That is not a road-safety slogan; it is the work-energy theorem doing arithmetic, and it is why following distances matter so much on the N1.

The theorem also explains the crate dragged at constant speed: the worker deposits, friction withdraws, the two cancel, and the balance never moves. Zero net work does not mean nothing happened — it means the account ended where it started.

One habit to take into calculations: when a question gives you a DISTANCE, think bank account — work and energy. When it gives you a TIME, think back to impulse from Term 1. Distance pays in joules; time pays in momentum.

Questions on the account are coming to you now — every force is a teller, and the balance is a half m v squared.

## Subtopic: Why Ramps Feel Easier

Nobody lifts a fridge straight onto a bakkie. You find a plank, make a ramp, and roll it up — and everyone agrees it feels easier. But here is the honest physics: the ramp does NOT reduce the work. It reduces the FORCE, and charges you distance for the privilege.

Lifting the fridge straight up one metre takes its full weight of force over one metre. Rolling it up a gentle five-metre ramp to the same height takes roughly a fifth of the force — but over five metres of pushing. Force down by five, distance up by five: the work, force multiplied by distance, comes out identical. The ramp is a payment plan, not a discount: same total, smaller instalments.

That is why the slope's steepness only decides how the bill is split. On a gentle ramp, gravity's pull along the slope is small and the pushing is light; on a steep one, you fight most of the weight directly. But arrival at the same height always costs the same energy — weight multiplied by vertical height gained — and in the exam that vertical height, not the length of the ramp, is what sets gravity's share of the bill.

Friction, though, plays dirtier on a ramp. Friction charges per metre TRAVELLED, not per metre of height — so the long gentle ramp, with its five metres of surface, actually collects more friction toll than a short steep one. Real ramp choices are a compromise between a force you can manage and a friction bill you can tolerate.

And the constant-speed check works up here too: pushed steadily up the ramp, the fridge's kinetic energy never changes, so all your work went into height and heat — nothing wasted into speed. Deposits into lifting, withdrawals by friction, balance unchanged.

The last questions of the lesson are yours now — smaller force, longer path, same work, and friction billed by the metre.
