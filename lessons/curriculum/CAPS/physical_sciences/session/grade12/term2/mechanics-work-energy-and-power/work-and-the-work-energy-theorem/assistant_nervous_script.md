Hey, it's Mandy — work and energy is where the physics paper starts feeling abstract, and I know the cos theta in the formula is doing most of the intimidating. Let me shrink it for you: cos theta only ever takes three values in this topic, and you can read them straight off the picture.

Force along the motion: cos theta is one, work is positive, energy flows in. Force against the motion: cos theta is negative one, work is negative, energy drains out — that is friction, always. Force at right angles to the motion: cos theta is zero, no work at all — that is the normal force, always, and gravity on level ground. Look at the arrow, ask "with, against, or sideways?", and the cosine is done.

The method never changes. Draw the free-body diagram — same dot and arrows as Newton's laws, nothing new. Then every arrow owes you one work term, even if it is zero. Add them with their signs: that is the net work. Then one sentence of theorem: net work equals change in kinetic energy, a half m v squared final minus a half m v squared initial. Solve for whatever is missing.

Inclines are the same recipe plus one split: break the weight into m g sine theta along the slope and m g cosine theta into the slope. Only the sine part does work. Or skip the trigonometry entirely: gravity's work is always m g times the VERTICAL height, negative going up, positive coming down. Height, not slope length — write that in the margin.

Two checks keep you safe. Constant velocity means net work zero — if your terms do not cancel, hunt the missing force. And distance in the question means work-energy; time in the question means impulse. The data always points at the tool.

Three cosines, one diagram, one theorem, one split. Do five past-paper questions with that checklist and this topic turns into your calmest marks. You've got this!
