**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First: gravity pulls down on the crate the whole time it slides across the level floor — so why do we say gravity does zero work on it? A force that never stops acting doing nothing feels wrong.

**Tutor:** It feels wrong because everyday language treats "doing something" and "doing work" as the same thing, and physics deliberately splits them. Work has a precise meaning: force multiplied by displacement multiplied by the cosine of the angle between them. On level ground, gravity points straight down and the crate's displacement is horizontal — ninety degrees apart, and the cosine of ninety degrees is zero. So gravity does zero work, not because it is absent or weak, but because none of the crate's motion happens along gravity's direction. Here is the physical meaning behind the mathematics: work measures energy transfer. Ask yourself — did gravity make the crate speed up or slow down as it slid? It did not; the crate's speed would be identical on the Moon's gravity or Jupiter's, as long as the applied force and friction stayed the same. Since gravity transferred no energy into or out of the crate's motion, its work is genuinely zero, and the definition is just honest accounting. The moment the floor tilts, that changes: on a slope, part of the displacement now runs along gravity's direction, gravity acquires a component along the motion — m g sine theta — and it starts doing real work, negative on the way up, positive on the way down. So the sentence to remember: a force does work only on the part of the motion that runs along it.

**Mandy:** Next: we now have two theorems with almost the same shape — force times time gives change in momentum, and force times distance gives change in kinetic energy. How do we know which one a question wants?

**Tutor:** Let the given quantities choose for you, because the two theorems are two different multiplications of the same force and they answer different questions. The impulse-momentum theorem multiplies force by TIME and pays out in momentum: use it when the question mentions seconds of contact, a collision duration, or asks about momentum change. The work-energy theorem multiplies force by DISPLACEMENT and pays out in kinetic energy: use it when the question gives metres — a stopping distance, a length of rough floor, a drop height — or asks about speed changes. A braking question shows the fork perfectly. "The car stops in 5 seconds — find the force" is impulse territory: force equals change in momentum divided by time. "The car stops in 50 metres — find the force" is work-energy territory: force equals change in kinetic energy divided by distance. Both give the same physics; the data decides the route. And there is a deeper habit hiding here that full-marks candidates develop: when BOTH tools could work, energy is usually faster because it is a scalar — no directions to track, no vector diagrams — while momentum keeps its vector nature and demands signs. But energy's price is that it cannot tell you about time at all, and momentum's price is that it cannot see how far anything travelled. Two theorems, two currencies: seconds buy momentum, metres buy energy.

**Mandy:** Here is the incline one: on the ramp question, some learners computed gravity's work as weight times the 4 metres along the slope and got negative 784. Where exactly does that go wrong?

**Tutor:** It goes wrong at the cosine, and seeing why fixes inclines permanently. The definition is force multiplied by displacement multiplied by the cosine of the angle BETWEEN them — and on a 30 degree slope, the weight vector and the displacement vector are not opposite each other. The displacement points up along the slope; the weight points straight down; the angle between those two directions is 120 degrees, not 180. So the correct work is 196 newtons of weight, multiplied by 4 metres, multiplied by the cosine of 120 degrees, which is negative a half — giving negative 392 joules, half of the wrong answer. Multiplying the full weight by the slope distance with cosine 180 silently pretends the crate fell straight down 4 metres, which it did not. There are two clean ways to organise this so it never bites. Route one: resolve first — split the weight into its component along the slope, m g sine 30, which is 98 newtons, and then multiply that component by the 4 metres it directly opposes: 98 multiplied by 4 is 392, negative because it fights the motion. Route two: think vertically — gravity only cares about height, and the crate rose 4 multiplied by sine 30, which is 2 metres, so gravity's work is minus m g h: minus 20 multiplied by 9,8 multiplied by 2, again negative 392 joules. Same answer three ways, and the third way — m g h with the vertical height — is the fastest and the hardest to get wrong.

**Mandy:** Last one: if the net work on the crate dragged at constant speed is zero, the worker seems to be achieving nothing — yet he is clearly burning energy and the floor is warming up. Where does his energy go?

**Tutor:** The zero belongs to the CRATE's kinetic energy account, not to the universe, and separating those two ledgers is the mature insight of this topic. Track the flows. The worker's muscles do positive work on the crate: over 5 metres at 50 newtons, he feeds 250 joules into it. Friction simultaneously does negative work on the crate: it drains 100 joules per 5 metres in our example — though for genuine constant velocity his force would match friction exactly, so let us say he applies 20 newtons and deposits 100 joules while friction withdraws 100. The crate's balance never moves, which is exactly what constant speed means. But the energy did not vanish. The joules the worker deposited flowed straight through the crate and out the friction pipe, becoming heat at the sliding surfaces — that is the warm floor — and a little sound. Meanwhile his body burned considerably more than the work he delivered, because muscles are inefficient engines; the difference left him as body heat, which is why he is sweating. So three ledgers ran at once: the crate's kinetic energy, unchanged; the mechanical work ledger, energy in from muscles and out through friction; and the biological ledger, chemical energy from food degraded mostly to heat. The exam phrase "net work is zero" makes a claim only about the first ledger. Physics never says the worker achieved nothing — it says the crate is moving no faster, and every joule he spent is accounted for elsewhere. Energy is never lost; it is only ever relocated, and the next lesson makes that principle the star of the show.
