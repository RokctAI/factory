# Part 1 — Expert

The work-energy theorem tracked one account — kinetic energy. This session opens the full ledger. Mechanical energy, the sum of kinetic and gravitational potential energy, is conserved in a frictionless world; in the real world, friction and other non-conservative forces move energy in and out, and the equation W n c equals delta E k plus delta E p tells you exactly how much. That equation, plus power — the rate of doing work — carries some of the most reliably repeated questions in Paper 1.

## Subtopic: Conservative and Non-Conservative Forces

A CONSERVATIVE force is a force for which the work done in moving an object between two points is independent of the path taken. The gravitational force is the syllabus's standard example: lift a crate straight up 2 metres, or push it up any winding ramp to the same height, and gravity's work is identical — negative m g h either way, depending only on the height difference. Whatever gravity takes on the way up, it repays in full on the way down; nothing leaks.

A NON-CONSERVATIVE force is one whose work DOES depend on the path. Friction is the great example: drag a crate to the far wall and back, and friction charges you for every metre of the round trip — a longer path means more work done against it, and none of it is ever repaid. Applied forces, air resistance and tension are treated as non-conservative too.

Why the distinction matters: for conservative forces we can define a potential energy. Gravitational potential energy, E p equals m g h, is a stored account — the work gravity will repay if the object descends. Mechanical energy is the sum of the two accounts: E mech equals E k plus E p.

The PRINCIPLE OF CONSERVATION OF MECHANICAL ENERGY: in the absence of air resistance or any external force, the total mechanical energy of an object remains constant. Only conservative forces acting — no friction, no air resistance — means the sum of kinetic and potential energy at any point equals the sum at any other.

Use it once. A ball is dropped from 10 metres, air resistance ignored. At the top: all potential, no kinetic. At the ground: all kinetic. Set them equal: m g h equals a half m v squared. The mass cancels — every mass falls alike — and v squared equals 2 multiplied by 9,8 multiplied by 10, which is 196. The ball lands at 14 metres per second, no equations of motion required.

This section's questions are with you now: both definitions word for word, the principle with its conditions stated, and mass cancelling when it may.

## Subtopic: The Equation W n c Equals Delta E k Plus Delta E p

When friction or another non-conservative force joins the party, mechanical energy is no longer constant — but it changes by an exactly known amount. The bookkeeping identity: the work done by non-conservative forces equals the change in kinetic energy plus the change in potential energy. In symbols, W n c equals delta E k plus delta E p. If W n c is zero, the equation collapses back to conservation of mechanical energy; if not, W n c IS the leak, measured in joules.

The method has four steps. One: choose a reference level for height, usually the lowest point. Two: write the kinetic and potential energies at the start and at the end — four numbers. Three: form the two changes, each one final minus initial. Four: their sum is the non-conservative work, and its sign tells the story — negative means energy drained, almost always by friction; positive means energy pumped in, by an engine or a person.

Work the classic. A 30 kilogram child starts from rest at the top of a slide 3 metres high and reaches the bottom at 6 metres per second. Reference level: the bottom. Initial kinetic energy: zero. Initial potential energy: 30 multiplied by 9,8 multiplied by 3, which is 882 joules. Final kinetic energy: a half multiplied by 30 multiplied by 36, which is 540 joules. Final potential energy: zero. Delta E k: 540 minus zero — positive 540. Delta E p: zero minus 882 — negative 882. Sum: negative 342 joules. That is W n c: friction drained 342 joules from the ride and turned it into heat on the slide's surface — which is why slides are warm on a busy afternoon.

Sanity checks the examiner rewards: in a frictionless world the child would have arrived at v squared equals 2 g h — about 7,7 metres per second — so arriving slower at 6 confirms energy left the system. Slower than frictionless, negative W n c: the signs and the story must agree.

Questions on this section are in front of you now — four numbers, two changes, one signed sum, and a sentence saying where the energy went.

## Subtopic: Working the Equation on Slopes and Rough Ground

The same identity solves for any single unknown: an arrival speed, a starting height, or the friction force itself. One full example, solved to the friction force.

A 2 kilogram box is released from rest at the top of a rough ramp of length 4 metres, whose top sits 2,5 metres above the ground. It arrives at the bottom moving at 5 metres per second. Reference level: the ground. Initial energies: kinetic zero, potential 2 multiplied by 9,8 multiplied by 2,5, which is 49 joules. Final energies: kinetic a half multiplied by 2 multiplied by 25, which is 25 joules; potential zero. Delta E k: positive 25. Delta E p: negative 49. W n c: 25 minus 49, which is negative 24 joules.

Now convert the work to a force. Friction acted along the full 4 metres of ramp, opposite to the motion: W friction equals negative f multiplied by 4, so negative 24 equals negative 4 f, and f equals 6 newtons. Note carefully which distance divides: the 4 metres ALONG the surface, because friction charges by path length — never the 2,5 metres of height, which belongs only to gravity.

The same machinery runs uphill. If a motor drags that box back up the ramp at constant speed, delta E k is zero, delta E p is positive 49, and friction still drains 24 joules along the path, so the motor's work must supply W n c equals 49 plus 24 — 73 joules of positive non-conservative work. On the way down friction fought gravity; on the way up EVERYTHING fights the motor.

And the exam's favourite twist — a projectile with air resistance: a ball thrown upward that returns to the hand moving SLOWER than it left. No surface, but air resistance is non-conservative, its work is negative on both halves of the trip, and the missing kinetic energy at the catch equals the total work done against the air. Same equation, no ramp in sight.

This section's questions arrive now — divide by the path length for friction, the height for gravity, and check the sign against the story.

## Subtopic: Power — the Rate of Doing Work

Two motors lift identical water tanks to the same roof. Same work — but the one that finishes in half the time is the more powerful machine. POWER is the rate at which work is done, or equivalently the rate at which energy is transferred: P equals W divided by delta t. The unit is the watt: one watt is one joule per second. A 2 000 watt geyser element moves 2 000 joules into water every second.

First calculation, straight from the SBA practical: a 60 kilogram learner runs up a flight of stairs 3 metres high in 5 seconds. Work against gravity: 60 multiplied by 9,8 multiplied by 3, which is 1 764 joules. Power: 1 764 divided by 5 — 352,8 watts. Run it in 2,5 seconds and the work is unchanged but the power doubles: power is about speed of delivery, not size of the job.

Second, the borehole question the syllabus names explicitly. A pump raises 120 kilograms of water per minute from a depth of 30 metres, at steady speed. Work per minute: 120 multiplied by 9,8 multiplied by 30, which is 35 280 joules. Minimum power: 35 280 divided by 60 seconds — 588 watts. "Minimum" because a real pump also loses energy to friction in the pipe and to the kinetic energy of the moving water; the true motor must beat 588 watts, never meet it.

Third, the constant-velocity form. For an object moving at constant speed against a resisting force, the average power is P equals F multiplied by v average — force multiplied by speed. A truck holding 25 metres per second on a level road against total resistive forces of 1 800 newtons delivers 1 800 multiplied by 25, which is 45 000 watts — 45 kilowatts just to stand still against friction and air. The formula comes straight from the definitions: work is force times distance, and distance per time is speed.

The final expert questions are with you now — joules first, then divide by seconds, and F v for anything cruising at constant speed.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the whole energy story again as money: a toll road, a receipt, and the difference between rich and fast.

## Subtopic: The Energy Toll Road

Think of a skateboarder in an empty swimming pool. Standing on the rim, she is rich in height-money — potential energy. Rolling down, she converts it to speed-money — kinetic energy. Up the far side, speed converts back to height. In a perfect, frictionless pool, she would swing back and forth forever, the two accounts trading the same fixed total, cent for cent. That fixed total is mechanical energy, and the perfect pool is what physicists mean by conservation of mechanical energy.

Why does gravity allow this perfect trade? Because gravity is an honest banker: whatever it charges you to climb, it repays in full when you come down — and it does not care what route you took, lift or ladder or winding ramp. Same height, same charge, same repayment. Forces with that honest-banker property are called conservative.

Friction runs a different business. Friction is a toll collector who charges per metre of road, keeps the money, and burns it — literally, as heat. Take the long way and you pay more; come back the same way and you pay AGAIN. No refunds, ever. That is what non-conservative means.

So the real skateboarder rises a little less on each swing. Her total mechanical energy shrinks, and the missing amount is exactly what the toll collector took. Nothing disappeared from the universe — the pool's surface is a fraction warmer — but it is gone from HER accounts for good.

One habit before the numbers: ask of every force in a problem, "banker or toll collector?" Gravity: banker. Friction, air resistance, engines, muscles: toll collectors — or donors, when they pay energy in.

Your first questions are with you now — perfect pool first, then let the toll collector in.

## Subtopic: Friction's Receipt

Here is the beautiful thing: even when energy leaks, the books still balance — you just have to read the receipt. The receipt is the equation W n c equals delta E k plus delta E p, and it works like checking your wallet after a taxi ride.

The child on the slide: she started 3 metres up with 882 joules of height-money and no speed-money. At the bottom she has 540 joules of speed-money and no height. Count the wallet: 882 went in, 540 came out. The receipt says minus 342 joules — and that is friction's toll, converted to warmth on the slide. The number is not a mystery or an error; it is a measurement of the toll collector's take.

Read the sign like a bank statement. Negative W n c: money left your accounts — friction or air resistance was collecting. Positive W n c: someone paid money IN — an engine, a pump, someone pushing. Zero: the perfect frictionless pool, accounts merely trading.

The receipt also predicts. If the frictionless slide would deliver the child at nearly 7,7 metres per second and she actually arrives at 6, energy left — no measurement of the slide needed, the speeds alone convict the toll collector. And if a question says a ball thrown upward comes back to the hand SLOWER than it left, that slowness is the air's receipt: negative work on the way up AND on the way down, both halves charged.

One warning that saves marks: friction charges along the PATH — every metre of ramp — while gravity charges only for HEIGHT. When you convert friction's toll into a force, divide by the slope length. When you compute height-money, use the vertical rise. Mixing those two distances is the topic's most common error, and now you will never make it.

Questions on the receipt are coming to you now — wallet in, wallet out, sign tells the story.

## Subtopic: Power — Rich Against Fast

Two learners carry identical boxes up the same staircase. Same boxes, same height: exactly the same work done, joule for joule. But Thabo sprints it in 3 seconds and Sipho strolls it in 30. Who did more work? Neither — the work is identical. Who produced more POWER? Thabo, ten times more, because power is not how much you do; it is how FAST you do it. Work is the size of the bill; power is how quickly you pay it.

The unit makes it concrete. One watt is one joule delivered per second. Your phone charger trickles at about 20 watts. A kettle pours in 2 000 — which is why the kettle dims the lights when the geyser is also on: together they are demanding thousands of joules every single second. When Eskom talks about megawatts, it is counting joules per second across the whole country.

Two formulas, chosen by what the question gives. Given work and time: power is work divided by time. The stairs practical is exactly this — your weight times the height of the stairs, divided by your time, and a stopwatch turns you into a machine rated in watts; most learners rate a few hundred, which is to say, half a kettle.

Given a constant speed against a resisting force: power is force times speed. A truck cruising at constant speed on the N3 is not accelerating, so every watt goes to fighting friction and air — force times speed, minute after minute, which is why cruising faster burns disproportionately more fuel: the force AND the speed both climb.

And the borehole's "minimum power": the pump must at least pay gravity's bill on schedule — mass per second, times 9,8, times the depth. Any real pump pays extra for pipe friction. Minimum means gravity's share alone.

The last questions of the lesson are yours now — same bill, different speed of paying, and watts are just joules in a hurry.
