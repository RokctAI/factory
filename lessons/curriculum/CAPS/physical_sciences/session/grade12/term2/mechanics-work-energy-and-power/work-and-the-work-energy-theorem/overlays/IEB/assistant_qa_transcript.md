**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First one: the normal force did zero work in every single example today. Is that a law — can the normal force EVER do work on anything?

**Tutor:** It can, and seeing when rescues you from memorising the wrong rule. The correct rule is never "the normal force does no work" — it is "a force perpendicular to the displacement does no work." In every example today, the object slid ALONG the surface, so the surface pushed at right angles to the motion and earned zero joules. But put the object in a lift. As the lift accelerates upward, the floor's normal force points up and the displacement points up — zero degrees between them, cosine one — and the normal force does honest positive work on you. That work is exactly where your extra kinetic energy comes from as the lift speeds up. On the way down, the floor still pushes up while you move down, and the normal force does negative work, draining your kinetic energy as the lift slows. So the normal force is not a special zero-work force; it simply happens to be perpendicular to the motion whenever something slides along a stationary surface, which is most of the questions you will meet. The habit worth keeping: never classify a force by its name. Classify it by the angle it makes with the displacement in THIS problem. Names are not physics; angles are.

**Mandy:** Next: work can be positive or negative, but you kept insisting work is a scalar with no direction. Positive and negative feel like directions to me. What is the difference?

**Tutor:** Beautiful question, because the distinction is exactly what separates energy from momentum. A vector's direction lives in space — north, south, thirty degrees above the horizontal — and you need trigonometry to add vectors because they can partly cancel at odd angles. Work's plus and minus are not directions in space at all; they are the two sides of a ledger. Positive work means energy flowed INTO the object's motion; negative work means energy flowed OUT. In from the world or out to the world — that is a one-dimensional bookkeeping sign, like a deposit against a withdrawal, not a compass bearing. The proof is in how work adds: to find net work you just add the numbers, signs included, no components, no angle diagrams, no resultant to construct. Compare temperature: minus five degrees is not a temperature pointing backwards; it is a reading below a chosen zero. Work behaves the same way. Now feel the contrast with momentum, which really is a vector: two equal momenta pointing opposite ways sum to zero by direction, and you must respect the geometry. Two works of plus fifty and minus fifty joules sum to zero by accounting. That is also why energy methods are often quicker in calculations — scalars carry no geometry, so the arithmetic is flat addition. The signs tell a story of flow, never of direction.

**Mandy:** Here is the ramp one: you showed gravity's work coming out as minus m g h, using only the vertical height. Why does the length of the ramp not matter to gravity when it clearly matters to my legs?

**Tutor:** Because gravity and your legs charge for different things. Take the crate: 6 metres along a 30 degree ramp. Gravity's work is force times displacement times cosine of the angle between them, and only the part of the journey that runs along gravity's direction — straight down — counts. Slide up any slope you like, zigzag if you want: the along-gravity content of the whole journey is just the vertical height climbed, 3 metres in our example. That is why minus m g sine theta times the slope length and minus m g times the height give the identical answer — 122,5 newtons times 6 metres is 735 joules, and 25 times 9,8 times 3 is also 735. The sine of the angle and the length of the slope always multiply together to make the height, whatever the slope. Forces with this property are called conservative, and the next lesson builds a whole method on them. Your legs, though, are not gravity — they also fight friction, and friction charges per metre of surface travelled, not per metre of height. A longer ramp means more rubbing, more heat, more total effort from you, even though gravity's bill is unchanged. So the ramp length matters to the friction part of your effort and to how the gravity bill is split into instalments — but gravity itself reads only the altimeter, never the odometer.

**Mandy:** Last one: the work-energy theorem never mentions time. Two learners could drag identical bags the same distance, one in ten seconds and one in a minute, and the theorem treats them the same. Is it missing something?

**Tutor:** The theorem is not missing anything — it is answering a question in which time genuinely does not appear. If both learners apply the same net force over the same 8 metres, both bags receive the same 280 joules and arrive at the same final speed. That is a real physical fact: the energy delivered depends on force and distance, full stop. What differs between your two learners is the RATE of delivery — 280 joules spread over ten seconds against 280 joules spread over sixty — and physics gives that rate its own name and its own symbol: power, work divided by time, measured in watts. The faster learner develops six times the power for the same work. So the honest picture is a division of labour among three tools. Force times distance is work, and it pays out in kinetic energy — use it when a question gives metres. Force times time is impulse, and it pays out in momentum — use it when a question gives seconds of contact. And work divided by time is power — use it when a question asks how quickly energy is being delivered, which is where engines, pumps and electricity bills enter the story. The theorem deliberately stays silent about time so that it can be perfectly precise about energy. The next lesson picks up exactly this thread and makes power the headline.
