Hey, it's Mandy — energy conservation is the topic where the formula sheet suddenly looks like alphabet soup: E k, E p, W n c, deltas everywhere. Deep breath. Underneath the symbols, this is the friendliest topic in mechanics, because it is just accounting, and you already know how money works.

Every problem is the same wallet check. Two accounts: height-money, m g h, and speed-money, a half m v squared. Count both accounts at the start. Count both at the end. If the totals match, mechanical energy was conserved — that is the frictionless case. If money is missing, friction took it; if money appeared, an engine paid it in. The equation W n c equals delta E k plus delta E p is nothing more than that sentence written in symbols.

Four lines, every time. One: pick your zero level — the lowest point — and say so. Two: write the four energies as plain numbers. Three: two changes, always final minus initial — let the subtraction make the signs, never your feelings. Four: add them; that sum is W n c, and finish with one sentence about where the energy went.

One distance trap, one sentence to disarm it: gravity charges for HEIGHT, friction charges for PATH. When you turn friction's joules into a force, divide by the slope length, never the height.

Power is even kinder: it is just work divided by time — joules in a hurry. Same stairs, faster legs, more watts. If something cruises at constant speed, use force times velocity. And when they say "minimum power", add the free-mark sentence: the real value is bigger because friction and the water's kinetic energy also need paying.

Two accounts, four lines, two distances, joules per second. Nothing here is new — it is Term 1's forces wearing an accountant's jacket. Do three past papers with the four-line routine and you will wonder why this ever looked hard. You've got this!
