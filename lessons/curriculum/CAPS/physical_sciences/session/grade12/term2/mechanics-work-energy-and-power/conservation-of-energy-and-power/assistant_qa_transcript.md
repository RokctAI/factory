**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First: why is friction called non-conservative when the energy it takes is not destroyed — it just becomes heat? If the energy still exists somewhere, what exactly failed to be conserved?

**Tutor:** Beautifully asked, and the answer sharpens what the word conservative actually means. Total energy — all forms counted, heat included — is always conserved; that is the first law of the universe and friction cannot touch it. What friction fails to conserve is MECHANICAL energy: the useful, ordered sum of kinetic and potential energy that a moving object can spend. The distinction is about recoverability. When gravity takes kinetic energy from a rising ball, it stores it as potential energy, and the ball can withdraw every joule on the way down — the transaction is reversible, which is why we can define a potential energy for gravity at all. When friction takes kinetic energy, it scatters it as random jiggling of billions of molecules — heat spread through the slide and the child's clothes. No process will spontaneously gather that scattered jiggling back into the ordered motion of the child. The energy exists, but it has left the mechanical ledger permanently. That is why no "frictional potential energy" appears in any formula sheet: potential energy can only be defined for forces that repay path-independently, and friction, charging per metre and never refunding, breaks the requirement. So say it precisely in the exam: friction transfers mechanical energy to internal or heat energy, so mechanical energy is not conserved, while total energy always is.

**Mandy:** Next: learners freeze at the signs in W n c equals delta E k plus delta E p — final minus initial, negative potential changes, negative work. What is a reliable routine so the signs come out right every time?

**Tutor:** Use a fixed four-line routine and never improvise. Line one: choose the reference level — say it, write it — normally the lowest point in the problem, so heights are never negative. Line two: write four energies, not two — kinetic at start, potential at start, kinetic at end, potential at end — each as a plain positive number or zero. Numbers at this stage, no deltas yet. Line three: form the two changes mechanically, always final minus initial, and let the subtraction produce the sign — do not predict it, compute it. A falling object gives a negative delta E p because the final height is lower; a rising one gives positive; you never decide, the arithmetic does. Line four: add the two changes; the sum IS W n c, sign included, and then interpret it in one sentence — negative means the system leaked energy to friction or air, positive means an engine or a person pumped energy in, zero means mechanical energy was conserved. The routine's power is that every sign has exactly one source: reference level fixes the heights, final-minus-initial fixes the deltas, and the sum fixes the interpretation. The classic failure is deciding signs by feel — "friction is bad, so make something negative" — and then double-negating. Feel has no place in lines one to four; the story only enters at the interpretation sentence, where you check the sign AGAINST the story: slower than the frictionless prediction should mean a negative W n c, and if your sign disagrees with your story, one of them is wrong and you get to find out before the marker does.

**Mandy:** Here is the one that decides problem choice: when should we solve a mechanics problem with energy, and when do we still need Newton's laws and the equations of motion?

**Tutor:** Ask two questions of the problem: does it involve TIME, and does it involve DIRECTION at an instant? If either answer is yes, energy alone cannot finish the job. Energy methods are scalar bookkeeping between two snapshots — a start and an end. They are magnificent when the question connects speeds, heights and distances: how fast at the bottom, how high before stopping, how much energy did friction take over this rough patch. They shine brightest on curved paths, like a skateboarder in a pool, where forces change direction constantly and Newton's second law would demand calculus the syllabus does not expect of you: conservation of mechanical energy leaps from rim to bottom in one line, and never asks what happened in between. But energy is blind to time — work and energy contain no seconds — so "how long did the slide take" cannot be answered by any energy equation; that belongs to the equations of motion or to impulse. And energy is blind to instantaneous force: "find the normal force at the bottom of the dip" needs Newton's second law at that point. The mature exam pattern uses both in relay: energy carries you from the release point to the bottom of the slope, delivering the speed there; Newton or momentum takes over for the collision or the force question that follows. So plan your route before calculating: snapshots and distances — energy; seconds or instantaneous forces — Newton and kinematics; and the best candidates switch tools mid-question without ceremony.

**Mandy:** Last one: in the borehole and stairs questions, the marking guideline says "minimum power". What is the examiner actually testing with that word, and how do we make sure we earn it?

**Tutor:** The word minimum is testing whether you know what your calculation left out, and it is earned with one honest sentence. When you compute the pump's power as mass per second, multiplied by 9,8, multiplied by the depth, you have priced exactly one thing: the gravitational bill — lifting the water at constant speed with nothing wasted. A real installation pays more on three fronts. Friction in the pipe drains energy from the flow, and that toll rises with the flow rate. The water arrives at the top MOVING, so the pump has also bought kinetic energy for it, which your m g h ignored. And no motor is perfectly efficient — some electrical energy becomes heat in the windings before any water moves. Since every one of those is an extra cost, the true power demand can only sit above your number: your answer is a floor, not an estimate. Hence the phrasing that earns the mark: "the minimum power is 588 watts; the actual power must be greater because energy is also needed against friction in the pipe and to give the water kinetic energy." The same logic runs the stairs practical: weight times height over time prices only the lift against gravity — your muscles also spent energy on moving limbs, on heat, on the horizontal shuffle down the corridor, so your measured wattage understates your body's true output. Examiners love the word because it separates candidates who computed a formula from candidates who know what the formula models. Be the second kind: compute, then state what was excluded and which way that pushes the real answer.
