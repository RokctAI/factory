# Part 1 — Expert

An ambulance races past and its siren seems to drop in pitch the instant it passes you — yet the siren itself never changed. That everyday observation, the Doppler effect, earns a dedicated question in Paper 1 every year, runs the ultrasound machines in every hospital, and provides the evidence that the universe is expanding. This session defines the effect, masters the one equation and its signs, works the standard calculations, and follows the physics from the maternity ward to the edge of the cosmos.

## Subtopic: What the Doppler Effect Is

The definition, in the syllabus's wording: the Doppler effect is the change in frequency of the sound detected by a listener, because the sound source and the listener have different velocities relative to the medium of sound propagation. The key word is DETECTED: the source vibrates at its own fixed frequency throughout; what changes is the frequency arriving at the listener's ear.

Build the picture with wavefronts. A stationary siren sends out sound waves as expanding spheres, one crest after another, evenly spaced in all directions — like ripples around a stone dropped in still water. Now let the siren move. Each new crest is emitted from a position slightly ahead of the last one, so ahead of the moving source the crests bunch together — shorter wavelength — and behind it they stretch apart — longer wavelength. The speed of sound in the air does not change; it is a property of the medium, about 340 metres per second in air. Speed fixed, wavelength shortened ahead: since speed equals frequency multiplied by wavelength, the frequency detected ahead must be HIGHER, and behind, LOWER.

That is why the pitch drops as the ambulance passes: you stand ahead of the source, hearing bunched crests, until the moment it passes, and then you stand behind it, hearing stretched ones. Note carefully what changes and what does not. Pitch is the ear's name for frequency, and pitch changes. Loudness is about amplitude and distance, not Doppler — the siren also gets louder as it approaches, but that is a separate effect, and confusing the two costs the explanation mark.

A moving listener produces the same phenomenon by a different mechanism: running toward a stationary siren, you meet crests more often than you would standing still, so you detect a higher frequency; running away, crests struggle to catch you, and the frequency drops.

This section's questions are with you now — the definition word for word, and the wavefront picture ready to sketch.

## Subtopic: The Equation and Its Signs

One equation covers every case. The frequency detected by the listener equals the speed of sound plus or minus the speed of the listener, divided by the speed of sound plus or minus the speed of the source, all multiplied by the frequency of the source. In symbols: f L equals v plus or minus v L, over v plus or minus v s, times f s — where v is the speed of sound in the medium, v L the listener's speed, and v s the source's speed.

The signs are chosen by one physical principle: approach raises the detected frequency, recession lowers it. The listener's speed lives in the NUMERATOR: a listener moving TOWARD the source needs the numerator larger, so add v L; moving away, subtract. The source's speed lives in the DENOMINATOR: a source moving TOWARD the listener needs the denominator smaller, so subtract v s; moving away, add. Decide the physics first — should this answer come out higher or lower than the source frequency? — then pick the signs to match, and your final answer checks itself.

Work the standard case. An ambulance siren emits 900 hertz while the vehicle approaches a stationary listener at 30 metres per second; take the speed of sound as 340 metres per second. The listener is stationary: numerator is just 340. The source approaches: denominator is 340 minus 30, which is 310. Formula: f L equals v over v minus v s, times f s. Substitution: 340 divided by 310, multiplied by 900. Answer: 987,10 hertz — higher than 900, as approach demands.

The moment the ambulance passes and recedes, the denominator flips to 340 plus 30, which is 370: f L equals 340 divided by 370, multiplied by 900 — 827,03 hertz. The listener hears the pitch fall from 987 to 827 as the vehicle sweeps past, a drop of 160 hertz, and the siren itself never wavered from 900.

Questions on the equation are in front of you now — physics first, signs second, then substitute and check the direction of your answer.

## Subtopic: Moving Listeners and Reverse Problems

Now the listener moves. A car travels at 20 metres per second toward a stationary factory siren of 500 hertz. The listener approaches, so the numerator grows: 340 plus 20, which is 360; the source is at rest, so the denominator is 340. Formula: f L equals v plus v L, over v, times f s. Substitution: 360 divided by 340, multiplied by 500. Answer: 529,41 hertz. Driving away at the same speed would give 340 minus 20 over 340, times 500 — 470,59 hertz.

The examiner's favourite twist runs the equation backwards: given the detected frequency, find the speed. A stationary listener hears a train hooter of 800 hertz as 850 hertz. Approaching or receding? Higher than the source, so approaching. Formula: 850 equals 340, over 340 minus v s, times 800. Rearrange: 340 minus v s equals 340 multiplied by 800 divided by 850, which is 320. So v s equals 20 metres per second. Always begin these by stating the direction of motion from the comparison of frequencies — that sentence is usually a mark on its own.

Now the medical applications the syllabus names. Ultrasound — sound above 20 kilohertz, beyond human hearing — is beamed into the body, and it reflects off moving blood cells. The moving cell acts as a moving receiver and then as a moving re-transmitter, so the reflected ultrasound returns with a shifted frequency, and the size of the shift measures the speed of the blood flow. This is Doppler flow measurement: it detects narrowed arteries, where blood must speed up through the constriction, without a single incision. The same principle listens to a foetal heartbeat: the moving heart wall shifts the reflected frequency rhythmically, and the machine converts those shifts into the sound parents hear in the clinic. Non-invasive, painless, and pure Grade 12 physics.

This section's questions arrive now — state the direction first in reverse problems, and let approach-raises, recede-lowers audit every answer.

## Subtopic: Red Shifts and the Expanding Universe

The Doppler effect is not confined to sound. Light is also a wave, and relative motion between a light source and an observer shifts its detected frequency too: a source receding from the observer has its light shifted toward LOWER frequency — toward the red end of the visible spectrum, a RED SHIFT — while an approaching source shifts toward higher frequency, toward the blue, a blue shift.

How could anyone measure such a shift in starlight? With a built-in barcode. Each chemical element absorbs and emits light at its own precise set of frequencies — its spectral lines, learned in the atomic spectra section. Hydrogen's pattern of lines, for instance, is as distinctive as a fingerprint. When astronomers examine the light of a distant galaxy, they recognise hydrogen's pattern — but the entire pattern sits at lower frequencies than the same lines produced in a laboratory on Earth. The fingerprint is intact; it has simply slid toward the red. The galaxy's light is red-shifted, and the size of the slide reveals the speed of recession.

The observation that changed cosmology: the light from almost EVERY distant galaxy is red-shifted, and the farther the galaxy, the greater its red shift. Fainter, more distant galaxies recede faster. The conclusion is inescapable and is the one the syllabus wants stated: the galaxies are moving away from us, and away from each other, in every direction — the universe is EXPANDING. Run the film backwards and all that receding matter converges, which is the core of the Big Bang picture of an early, hot, dense universe.

Keep the exam claim precise. Red shift does not say Earth sits at some privileged centre — in an expanding universe, every observer in every galaxy sees the same recession pattern, like raisins in a rising loaf of bread all moving apart from each other. And it is evidence, reasoned from the Doppler effect, not a mere assumption: shifted fingerprints, measured recession, expanding universe — three steps the marker wants in that order.

The final expert questions are with you now — red means receding, and the whole sky is receding.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same physics from a street corner: one siren, one taxi rank, and a story the whole sky is telling.

## Subtopic: The Siren That Lies to You

Stand at a street corner as an ambulance screams past, and pay attention to what your ears report. Coming toward you: a high, urgent wail. The instant it passes: the note sags, suddenly deeper, as though the driver flicked a switch. No switch was flicked. Ride IN the ambulance and the siren holds one steady note the whole way. The siren never lies to the paramedic — only to you.

Here is the honest explanation. Sound is a parade of pressure crests marching out of the siren, evenly spaced. When the ambulance drives toward you, each new crest is launched from a bit closer to you than the last, so the crests arrive at your ear bunched together — more crests per second — and more per second IS a higher pitch. Once it passes, each crest launches from farther away than the one before: the parade stretches out, fewer crests per second reach you, and the pitch drops.

Two things never changed: the siren's own note, and the speed of sound through the air. Only the SPACING of what reaches you changed, because the launcher was moving. That is the whole Doppler effect: motion re-spaces the waves between source and listener.

Keep one distinction crisp, because the exam probes it: the siren also gets LOUDER as it approaches — but that is just closeness, the way a nearer drum is louder. Loudness is about distance; PITCH is about frequency, and only the pitch change is the Doppler effect.

Your first questions are with you now — the note sags because the spacing stretched, not because the siren changed.

## Subtopic: Taxis Leaving Every Ten Minutes

Forget sound for a moment; think about taxis. A rank dispatches a taxi down the same road every ten minutes, like clockwork. You stand at a stop down that road. Standing still, one taxi passes you every ten minutes — you receive them at exactly the rate the rank sends them.

Now start WALKING toward the rank. You meet the taxis head-on, each one a little sooner than you would have — one passes you every eight or nine minutes. The rank never changed its schedule; your motion raised your personal arrival rate. Walk away from the rank instead, and each taxi must chase you down before passing: eleven, twelve minutes between them. Schedule unchanged, arrival rate down.

That is a moving LISTENER, and it is half the Doppler equation. The other half is a moving RANK. Imagine the whole rank rolling slowly down the road toward you while still dispatching every ten minutes: each taxi starts its journey from closer than the last, so the gaps between them on the road shrink, and everyone standing downstream receives taxis more often. The rank retreating stretches the gaps instead.

Swap taxis for wave crests and the schedule for the source's frequency, and you have the physics exactly: approach packs the arrivals closer — higher frequency; retreat spreads them out — lower. And the equation's signs stop being a memory task: motion toward each other must always push the detected rate UP, so choose whichever plus or minus makes that happen. Decide the answer's direction first, and the equation can never betray you.

Questions on this section are coming to you now — you cannot change the schedule, only how often the schedule reaches you.

## Subtopic: From the Maternity Ward to the Whole Sky

Why does the syllabus care so much about this pitch trick? Because bouncing it off moving things measures their speed without touching them — and that one idea works from inside a womb to the far side of the universe.

In the clinic, the machine beams ultrasound — sound pitched far too high for ears — into the body. It bounces off whatever moves: flowing blood cells, a tiny beating heart. Reflected off a mover, the sound comes back with its pitch shifted, and the size of the shift betrays the speed of the mover. That is how a clinic measures blood flow through a narrowed artery — blood forced through a squeeze must speed up, and the machine hears it — and it is how parents hear a foetal heartbeat: the machine translates the rhythmic shifts from the moving heart wall into sound. No needles, no cutting. Speed, measured by echo.

Now aim the same idea at the night sky. Light is a wave too, so motion shifts it: a light source rushing away has its waves stretched toward the red end of the spectrum — a red shift. Every element leaves a fingerprint pattern in light, so astronomers can spot hydrogen's pattern in a galaxy's glow and see that the whole fingerprint has slid toward the red. Slid red means moving away. And here is the discovery that rewrote everything: almost every galaxy in the sky is red-shifted, and the farther away it lies, the harder it is receding. Everything is leaving. Not fleeing some centre — space itself is stretching, like raisins drifting apart in rising bread dough, every raisin watching all the others recede.

So one street-corner observation scales all the way up: the sagging siren, the racing blood cell, the retreating galaxy — one effect, three sizes. The last questions of the lesson are yours now — bounce a wave off it, read the shift, and the universe tells you its speed.
