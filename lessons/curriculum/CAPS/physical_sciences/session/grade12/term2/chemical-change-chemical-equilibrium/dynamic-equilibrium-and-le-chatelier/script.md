# Part 1 — Expert

Some reactions refuse to finish. Seal nitrogen and hydrogen in a vessel to make ammonia, and the mixture settles into a stalemate: ammonia forming and decomposing at exactly equal rates, forever. Chemical equilibrium is the chemistry of that stalemate — how it arises, how Le Chatelier's principle predicts its response to disturbance, and how the equilibrium constant K c measures it. Paper 2 devotes a full question to this topic nearly every year, and it rewards precise definitions above all.

## Subtopic: Open, Closed and Reversible

Three definitions build the foundation, and each must be exam-exact.

An OPEN system continuously exchanges matter and energy with its surroundings — a fizzing beaker on a bench, losing gas to the room. A CLOSED system exchanges energy but NOT matter — a stoppered flask: heat can pass through the glass, but nothing material escapes. Equilibrium can only establish itself in a closed system, because if a product drifts away, the reverse reaction is robbed of its ingredients.

A REVERSIBLE reaction is one in which products can be converted back to reactants — written with double arrows pointing both ways. Hydrogen and iodine combine to form hydrogen iodide, and hydrogen iodide simultaneously decomposes back into hydrogen and iodine.

Now the centrepiece. CHEMICAL EQUILIBRIUM is the state of a reversible reaction, in a closed system, in which the rate of the forward reaction equals the rate of the reverse reaction, so that the concentrations of reactants and products remain constant. It is called DYNAMIC equilibrium because both reactions continue at full tilt — molecules react in both directions every instant — while the totals stand still.

Two precision points the marker probes. First: constant concentrations do not mean EQUAL concentrations. At equilibrium there might be far more reactant than product or the reverse; what matters is that neither is changing. Second: from the outside, nothing appears to happen — colour, pressure and concentration all hold steady — but at the particle level the traffic never stops. An answer saying "the reaction has stopped" is wrong in exactly the way the examiner is fishing for.

How does the stalemate arise? At the start, reactant concentrations are high, so the forward rate is high; no products exist, so the reverse rate is zero. As products accumulate, the reverse rate climbs while the forward rate falls — until the two rates meet. From that moment, every forward event is balanced by a reverse one.

This section's questions are with you now: three definitions word for word, and the word dynamic defended.

## Subtopic: Le Chatelier's Principle

The principle: when the equilibrium in a closed system is disturbed, the system will re-instate a new equilibrium by favouring the reaction that opposes the disturbance. Learn the statement, then drive it through the three standard disturbances using the industrial reaction of the syllabus: nitrogen plus three hydrogen in equilibrium with two ammonia, with the forward reaction exothermic.

CONCENTRATION. Add more nitrogen, and the system opposes the addition by consuming nitrogen: the forward reaction is favoured, and the equilibrium shifts toward ammonia. Remove ammonia as it forms, and the system opposes the removal by making more: forward again. This is why industrial plants continuously draw off product — a permanently frustrated equilibrium keeps producing.

TEMPERATURE. Heat is a product of the exothermic forward reaction, so think of the equation as carrying "plus heat" on the product side. Raise the temperature and the system opposes the addition of heat by favouring the reaction that ABSORBS heat — the endothermic reverse reaction — so ammonia decomposes and the yield falls. Cool the system and the exothermic forward reaction is favoured: more ammonia. Temperature is the one disturbance that also changes the value of K c itself.

PRESSURE, for gases only. Count gas moles on each side: four on the left — one nitrogen plus three hydrogen — and two on the right. Increase the pressure by shrinking the volume, and the system opposes the crowding by favouring the side with FEWER gas moles: forward, toward ammonia. Decrease the pressure and the many-mole side is favoured. If both sides carry equal gas moles, pressure changes shift nothing.

The CATALYST, deliberately last: a catalyst speeds forward and reverse reactions EQUALLY, so it shifts nothing and changes no yield — it only brings the system to equilibrium sooner. Industrially that is still gold: the Haber process runs with an iron catalyst, moderate temperature as a compromise between yield and speed, and high pressure — every choice a Le Chatelier argument, and a favourite extended question.

Questions on the principle are in front of you now — name the disturbance, name the opposing reaction, state the shift.

## Subtopic: Reading Equilibrium Graphs

Equilibrium questions love concentration-time graphs, and they are read with three eyes: flat sections, jumps, and curves.

FLAT sections mean equilibrium: every species holding constant concentration. The system may sit at equilibrium for minutes of graph time, all lines level, however unequal their heights.

A vertical JUMP in one line is an imposed disturbance at that instant. Someone injected more nitrogen: its line leaps upward instantly, while the other lines have not yet responded. A sudden removal shows as an instant drop. Only the disturbed species jumps; everything else still sits at its old value at that first moment.

Then the CURVES tell the response. After nitrogen is added, the system consumes it: nitrogen's line curves back downward — not all the way to its old value — while hydrogen's line falls too, being consumed alongside, and ammonia's line climbs. All three settle onto new flat levels: a NEW equilibrium, at different concentrations from before. Le Chatelier in ink: the disturbance is partially, never completely, undone.

Volume and pressure changes mark themselves differently: change the volume and EVERY gaseous concentration jumps at once — halving the volume doubles them all instantly — and then the curves relax as the shift proceeds. A temperature change produces no instant jump at all, only curves bending away from the old levels toward new ones, because heating changes no concentration directly.

Rate-time graphs tell the same stories in another dialect: at equilibrium the forward and reverse rate lines lie on top of each other. Add a reactant and the forward rate spikes, then decays, while the reverse rate climbs, until the two meet again — at a level different from before. A catalyst makes both rate lines jump upward together and meet sooner, with no change in final concentrations.

The examiner's standard question — "identify the disturbance at time t" — is answered by the jump pattern: one line jumping is concentration; all lines jumping is volume or pressure; no jump but bending curves is temperature; both rates leaping together is a catalyst.

This section's questions arrive now — jumps for disturbances, curves for responses, flats for equilibrium.

## Subtopic: The Equilibrium Constant K c

Equilibrium has a number. For a reaction written a A plus b B in equilibrium with c C plus d D, the equilibrium constant K c equals the concentration of C raised to the power c, times the concentration of D raised to the power d, divided by the concentration of A raised to the power a, times the concentration of B raised to the power b. Products over reactants, each concentration in moles per cubic decimetre, each raised to its balancing coefficient. Two exclusions: pure solids and pure liquids never appear in the expression — their concentrations are constant — so only gases and dissolved species are written.

Work the standard calculation. Hydrogen plus iodine in equilibrium with two hydrogen iodide, in a 2 cubic decimetre sealed vessel. Initially 1,0 mole of hydrogen and 1,0 mole of iodine are injected; at equilibrium the vessel holds 1,6 moles of hydrogen iodide. Build the table. Forming 1,6 moles of hydrogen iodide consumed 0,8 moles of hydrogen and 0,8 moles of iodine — the two-to-one ratio read from the equation. Remaining: 0,2 moles of each reactant. Now divide every amount by the 2 cubic decimetre volume for concentrations: hydrogen 0,1, iodine 0,1, hydrogen iodide 0,8 moles per cubic decimetre. The expression: K c equals the hydrogen iodide concentration squared, divided by the product of the hydrogen and iodine concentrations. Substitute: 0,8 squared over 0,1 times 0,1 — 0,64 over 0,01. K c equals 64.

Interpretation carries marks of its own. A LARGE K c — much greater than one — means the equilibrium lies far toward the products: the forward reaction has gone nearly to completion. A SMALL K c — much less than one — means mostly reactants remain. Our 64 says the mixture strongly favours hydrogen iodide.

Final fact, tested constantly: K c is constant at a given TEMPERATURE, and temperature is the ONLY thing that changes it. Adding reactant, changing pressure, adding a catalyst — concentrations shift, the ratio recovers, K c stands. Raise the temperature of an exothermic reaction and K c genuinely falls; for an endothermic one it rises.

The final expert questions are with you now — RICE table, concentrations not moles, coefficients as exponents, and temperature alone moves K c.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same chemistry in a shopping mall: a busy shop, a stubborn see-saw, and a referee with a scoreboard.

## Subtopic: The Shop Where the Crowd Never Changes

Picture a popular shop on a Saturday morning. People stream in through the door; people stream out. Now suppose, every minute, exactly forty walk in and exactly forty walk out. Count the crowd inside at any moment: two hundred people. Count again ten minutes later: two hundred. Has the shop gone quiet? Not remotely — the doorway is churning. The COUNT is constant while the TRAFFIC never stops.

That is dynamic equilibrium, exactly. The forward reaction is the door in; the reverse reaction is the door out; and when the two rates match, the concentrations — the crowd inside — hold steady, even though individual molecules are reacting both ways every instant. Anyone who says "the reaction has stopped" is standing across the street counting heads through the window, fooled by the steady total.

Two details complete the picture. First, the steady crowd does not have to be a LARGE crowd — the shop can hold steady at twenty people or at two thousand; equally, an equilibrium can sit heavily on the reactant side or the product side. Constant does not mean equal. Second, the shop needs its doors — but the MALL needs walls. If shoppers could wander out of a missing back wall and never return, the in-and-out balance could never establish. That missing wall is an open system; equilibrium demands a closed one, where nothing material leaks away.

And how does the balance first arise? Opening time: the empty shop fills fast — everyone streams in, nobody is inside to leave. As the crowd builds, more people finish browsing and exit. In-rate falling, out-rate rising — until they meet. From then on, the crowd is frozen while the doorway churns.

Your first questions are with you now — steady count, busy doors, closed walls.

## Subtopic: The Stubborn See-Saw

Le Chatelier's principle sounds grand, but it describes something you already know: push a stubborn thing, and it leans back against you.

The equilibrium is a see-saw in balance. Every disturbance is someone jumping onto one end — and the system always responds by working to lift that end back. It never fully succeeds; the see-saw settles at a NEW balance point, tilted somewhat, the push partly absorbed. Partly undone, never fully undone — that is the fingerprint of every equilibrium response.

Run the three pushes. Dump extra reactant onto the left seat: the system leans right, consuming the addition, making more product. That is why factories keep syphoning product away — every removal from the right seat drags the see-saw rightward again, and the frustrated equilibrium keeps producing.

Heat is a passenger too, and here is the trick that simplifies every temperature question: write heat into the equation as if it were a chemical. Exothermic forward reaction? Then heat sits on the PRODUCT side. Warm the vessel and you have added a passenger to the product end — the system leans back toward reactants, and yield falls. Cool it, removing heat from the product side, and the system makes more product, heat included. One mental trick, no memorising of cases.

Pressure squeezes gases. Crowd the vessel and the system escapes toward the side with FEWER gas molecules — less crowding, pressure partly relieved. Count the moles on each side of the arrows; the smaller number is where a squeeze sends you.

And the catalyst never sits on the see-saw at all. It greases the pivot: both directions speed up equally, balance arrives sooner, and the final tilt is untouched. Faster to equilibrium, never more product — the distinction the exam tests every single year.

Questions on the see-saw are coming to you now — find the push, lean against it, settle part-way back.

## Subtopic: The Referee with the Scoreboard

After all the leaning and shifting, chemistry appoints a referee with one number on the scoreboard: K c. The number answers a single question — when the dust settles, who is winning, products or reactants?

The score is a fraction: products on top, reactants on the bottom, concentrations each raised to the power of their number in the balanced equation. A HUGE score — thousands — means the top dwarfs the bottom: the vessel is nearly all products; the forward reaction almost ran to completion. A TINY score — a small decimal — means the reaction barely left the starting line: mostly reactants. Our hydrogen iodide example scored 64: comfortably product-side.

The calculation itself is bookkeeping in four lines, and the exam rewards the layout as much as the answer. Line one: what went IN — the initial moles. Line two: the CHANGE — how much reacted, in the ratio of the balanced equation; if 1,6 moles of hydrogen iodide formed, then 0,8 of each reactant died to make it, because the equation says two from one-plus-one. Line three: what REMAINS at equilibrium. Line four: divide everything by the vessel's volume, because the referee only accepts concentrations — moles per cubic decimetre — never raw moles. Skipping that division is the most expensive shortcut in the topic.

Now the referee's one loyalty: temperature. Add reactants, squeeze the vessel, stir in catalyst — the concentrations dance, but they always dance back into the SAME score. Only changing the temperature changes the number itself: heat an exothermic reaction and its score genuinely drops — the products lose ground at the new temperature; an endothermic one scores higher when heated.

So the full picture in one breath: the shop crowd holds steady, the see-saw leans against every push, and the referee keeps the score — one number, loyal only to temperature.

The last questions of the lesson are yours now — in, change, remain, divide — and read the score for who is winning.
