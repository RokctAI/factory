Hey, it's Mandy — equilibrium is the topic where chemistry starts talking in riddles: reactions that never finish, systems that push back, a constant that is not always constant. Take heart: underneath the riddles are three pictures, and you already understand all three.

Picture one, the busy shop: people in equals people out, so the crowd inside never changes. That is dynamic equilibrium — both reactions running full speed, concentrations frozen. Two phrases to bank for the definition marks: "closed system" and "forward rate equals reverse rate". And never, ever write "the reaction has stopped" — that is the trap the definition question is built around.

Picture two, the stubborn see-saw: push it, and it leans back — partly. Every Le Chatelier answer is the same three sentences: name the disturbance, name the reaction that opposes it, state the shift. For temperature, write "plus heat" on the exothermic side and treat it like a chemical — then heating is just adding a product, and the logic runs itself. For pressure, count gas moles and move toward fewer when squeezed. And the catalyst? It touches nothing but the clock: same equilibrium, reached sooner. Say "no effect on the position, faster to reach it" and collect the mark.

Picture three, the referee's scoreboard: K c, products over reactants, coefficients as powers. The routine is five lines: write the expression first, build the moles table — initial, change, equilibrium — divide everything by the volume, substitute, then interpret: big K c, products winning; small, reactants. The one mark everyone drops is the volume division. Circle the vessel's volume the moment you read the question.

And the fact that decides at least one multiple choice every year: only TEMPERATURE changes K c. Nothing else. Not concentration, not pressure, not catalysts.

Three pictures, three sentences, five lines, one loyal fact. Equilibrium stops being a riddle the moment you see the shop, the see-saw and the scoreboard. You've got this!
