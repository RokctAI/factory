**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First, the one that starts arguments: if adding more reactant shifts the equilibrium to make more product, why does the concentration of the added reactant still end up HIGHER than before the addition? Surely the system should use it all up?

**Tutor:** This is Le Chatelier's fine print, and the phrase to hold onto is PARTIALLY opposed, never fully undone. Walk the numbers in your head. Before the addition, the system sat at equilibrium — the ratio of concentrations exactly matched K c. Inject extra nitrogen and its concentration leaps; the ratio is now wrong, too reactant-heavy, and the forward reaction outpaces the reverse to correct it. Nitrogen is consumed, hydrogen is consumed with it, ammonia is produced — but here is the crux: the correction only needs to continue until the RATIO satisfies K c again, not until the nitrogen returns to its old concentration. And because consuming nitrogen also consumes hydrogen and raises ammonia, the ratio recovers well before the added nitrogen is gone. The new equilibrium therefore holds more nitrogen than the old one — most of the addition still present — alongside less hydrogen and more ammonia. If the system fully undid every disturbance, equilibria would be useless to industry: adding reactant would achieve nothing. It is precisely because the response is partial that pushing works. So in any exam answer, write the sequence with care: the disturbance raises the concentration; the system opposes it by favouring the consuming reaction; the final concentration is lower than immediately after the addition, but higher than before it. Three positions of that concentration, in order — that is the full-marks picture, and the concentration-time graph shows all three: the jump, the partial fall, the new plateau above the old one.

**Mandy:** Next: learners lose marks by saying a catalyst increases the yield of the Haber process. Why is that wrong, and why does industry pay for catalysts anyway?

**Tutor:** It is wrong because a catalyst is scrupulously even-handed: it lowers the activation energy of the forward and reverse reactions by exactly the same amount, so both speed up in the same proportion, the rates re-balance at the same equilibrium position, and the final concentrations — the yield — are untouched. On the concentration-time graph, the catalysed run and the uncatalysed run flatten at identical levels; the catalysed one simply gets there sooner. So why does industry pay handsomely for iron in the Haber process and platinum in catalytic converters? Because TIME is money in a way the equilibrium position never captures. An ammonia plant that reaches equilibrium in minutes rather than hours produces vastly more ammonia per day from the same vessel — the yield per BATCH is unchanged, but the number of batches per day multiplies. There is a second, subtler gift: speed buys temperature freedom. The forward Haber reaction is exothermic, so Le Chatelier says cold favours yield — but cold also means slow, cripplingly slow without help. With a catalyst accelerating everything, the plant can afford a moderate temperature — around 450 degrees Celsius — collecting a workable rate without giving away too much yield. The catalyst does not shift the compromise; it makes the compromise affordable. Examiners phrase this as "explain why a catalyst is used although it does not affect the yield", and the two-part answer is: equilibrium is reached sooner, so more product per unit time; and an acceptable rate becomes possible at a lower, yield-friendlier temperature.

**Mandy:** Here is the graph one: given a concentration-time graph with a disturbance at some time t, what is the fastest reliable way to identify what was done to the system?

**Tutor:** Interrogate the instant of the disturbance — the jump pattern — before you look at anything else, because each disturbance leaves a distinct fingerprint at that first moment. Fingerprint one: exactly ONE line jumps vertically, up or down, while the others continue from their old values. That is an addition or removal of that single species — nothing else can move one concentration instantaneously without touching the rest. Fingerprint two: ALL the gaseous lines jump at the same instant, in the same direction proportionally — concentrations all up means the volume was decreased, all down means it was increased. Volume is the only lever that rescales every concentration simultaneously, because concentration is moles over volume and the volume sits under every species at once. Fingerprint three: NO line jumps, yet all the lines begin bending away from their plateaus. That is a temperature change — heating or cooling alters no concentration directly, only the rates, so the response appears as curves without a step. And on a rate-time graph, a fourth fingerprint: both the forward and reverse rate lines leap upward together and converge sooner — that is a catalyst, which no concentration-time graph can reveal at all, since it changes no concentrations. After the fingerprint, read the DIRECTION of the response to finish the answer: which lines rise and which fall tells you which reaction was favoured, and Le Chatelier tells you why. Jump pattern first, direction second, principle third — three sentences, full marks, no guessing.

**Mandy:** Last one: for the calculation — learners set up the whole table correctly and still get K c wrong by forgetting the volume, or they square the wrong thing. What is the bulletproof routine?

**Tutor:** Five lines, spoken to yourself in the same order every time, and two of the lines exist purely to catch the classic errors. Line one: write the balanced equation and, directly beneath it, the K c expression with every species raised to its coefficient — hydrogen iodide squared over hydrogen times iodine, in our example. Writing the expression FIRST, before any arithmetic, earns its own mark and forces the coefficients into view, which is what prevents squaring the wrong species. Check at this line that no solid or pure liquid crept into the expression — they are excluded because their concentrations do not change. Line two: the moles table — initial, change, equilibrium — with the change row obeying the equation's ratios: if 1,6 moles of hydrogen iodide formed, the two-to-one-to-one ratio says 0,8 moles of each reactant was consumed. The change row is where stoichiometry lives; every entry in it must be a multiple of the coefficients. Line three, the error-catcher: divide EVERY equilibrium amount by the vessel volume, and say the units out loud — moles per cubic decimetre. K c is built from concentrations; raw moles are only acceptable in the accidental case of a one cubic decimetre vessel, and relying on that accident is how the mark is lost in every other question. Line four: substitute the concentrations into the expression written in line one and compute — 0,8 squared over 0,1 times 0,1 gives 64. Line five: interpret and audit. Sixty-four is much bigger than one, so products dominate — does that agree with the story of the question? If a question says "the reaction barely proceeds" and your K c comes out large, one of the rows is wrong, and you have caught it while it is still cheap. Expression, table, divide, substitute, interpret — the marks are distributed across all five lines, which is exactly why the routine collects them.
