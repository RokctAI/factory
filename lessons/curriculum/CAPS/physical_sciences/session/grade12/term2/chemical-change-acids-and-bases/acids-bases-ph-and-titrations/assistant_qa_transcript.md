**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First, the perennial one: a 0,01 molar solution of hydrochloric acid and a 0,01 molar solution of ethanoic acid — same concentration, so why on earth do they have different pH values?

**Tutor:** Because pH does not measure how much acid you dissolved; it measures how many hydronium ions are actually loose in the solution, and the two acids release their protons with completely different willingness. Hydrochloric acid is strong: ionisation is complete, every single molecule donates its proton, so 0,01 moles per cubic decimetre of acid delivers the full 0,01 moles per cubic decimetre of hydronium — pH exactly 2. Ethanoic acid is weak: its ionisation is an equilibrium lying far to the intact side, with a K a around ten to the minus five, so at any instant only a small fraction of the molecules — a few percent — have actually handed over a proton. The hydronium concentration might be around four ten-thousandths rather than one hundredth, putting the pH near 3,4. Same label on the bottle, ten times fewer free hydronium ions or more. The examinable consequences follow in a chain: the weak acid's solution conducts electricity more poorly, because fewer ions carry the current; it reacts more slowly with magnesium or carbonate, because fewer hydronium collisions occur per second; and yet — here is the twist worth a mark — it will neutralise exactly as much base in a titration, because as the equilibrium's free protons are consumed, the intact molecules ionise to replace them, and eventually every proton is surrendered. Strength shows in pH, conductivity and rate; it does NOT show in the titration total. Keeping those two categories apart is precisely what this comparison question is built to test.

**Mandy:** Next: learners find conjugate pairs confusing when water appears on both teams — sometimes an acid, sometimes a base. How do we identify the pairs reliably in any equation?

**Tutor:** Use a bookkeeping method that never appeals to intuition: track single protons across the arrow. Take any acid-base equation and pick one species on the left. Find the species on the right that is identical to it except for exactly one proton — one hydrogen more or one hydrogen fewer, with the charge adjusted by one to match. Those two are a conjugate pair, and the direction of the difference tells the roles: the heavier one is the acid of the pair, the lighter one the base. Repeat for the other left-hand species and you have the second pair; every Lowry-Brønsted equation contains exactly two such pairs, no more, no fewer. Run it on ammonia in water. Ammonia on the left; on the right stands ammonium — same nitrogen, one proton heavier. Pair one: ammonium is the acid, ammonia its conjugate base. Water on the left; on the right stands hydroxide — one proton lighter. Pair two: water played the acid, hydroxide is its conjugate base. Now run hydrochloric acid in water and the same procedure gives water the OTHER role: it gains a proton to become hydronium, so water was the base of that pair. Nothing contradictory happened — water is an ampholyte, and its role is decided fresh in each reaction by whoever it partners with: facing a stronger donor it takes; facing a taker it gives. The exam phrase "identify the two conjugate acid-base pairs" is therefore a counting exercise, not a judgement call: one-proton differences, twice per equation, roles read from which side is heavier.

**Mandy:** Here is the calculation one: in pH problems, when do we multiply by two for sulfuric acid, and why does nobody multiply by two for the pH of a diprotic WEAK acid like carbonic acid?

**Tutor:** The multiplication by two is bought with a specific licence: complete ionisation of both protons, and only the strong diprotic acid of the syllabus — sulfuric acid — holds that licence. For sulfuric acid at 0,05 moles per cubic decimetre, the syllabus treatment says both protons are surrendered fully: each dissolved molecule contributes two hydronium ions, the hydronium concentration is 2 times 0,05, which is 0,1, and the pH is 1. Forgetting the two gives 1,3 and forfeits the mark; that is the standard trap. But the licence is what justifies the arithmetic, and a weak diprotic acid never qualifies. Carbonic acid releases its first proton only partially — a small equilibrium fraction — and its SECOND proton far more reluctantly still, because it must be pulled away from an ion that is already negatively charged, which holds protons tighter. So the true hydronium concentration of a weak diprotic acid is a small fraction of the first proton's supply plus a nearly negligible sliver of the second — nowhere near two times the nominal concentration, and calculating it properly needs the K a equilibrium mathematics that Grade 12 does not require of you. What Grade 12 DOES require is the conceptual sentence: a weak acid's hydronium concentration cannot be read off the bottle's concentration, because ionisation is partial. So the operational rule for the exam: strong monoprotic — hydronium equals the concentration; strong diprotic, meaning sulfuric — hydronium equals twice the concentration; any weak acid — state that the pH is higher than the strong-acid calculation would give, and expect a qualitative comparison rather than a computed value. Three cases, one licence, and the factor of two never wanders where it does not belong.

**Mandy:** Last one: why does the equivalence point of a titration not always sit at pH 7 — surely when the acid and base have exactly cancelled, the solution must be neutral?

**Tutor:** The intuition assumes that "exactly reacted" leaves behind nothing active, and that is the step that fails: it leaves behind the SALT, and salts are not always innocent bystanders. At equivalence, every mole of acid has met its stoichiometric match of base — but the products, the salt's ions, are themselves the conjugate partners of the parents, and conjugates of WEAK parents still play the proton game with water. Titrate ethanoic acid with sodium hydroxide and the equivalence beaker holds sodium ethanoate. The sodium ion is a spectator, but the ethanoate ion is the conjugate base of a weak acid — a genuine, if mild, proton taker. It accepts protons from water molecules, leaving a small surplus of hydroxide ions, and the solution sits slightly basic — equivalence above pH 7. Titrate ammonia with hydrochloric acid and the beaker holds ammonium chloride: chloride, conjugate of a strong acid, is far too weak a taker to bother water, but ammonium is the conjugate acid of a weak base and donates protons to water, tipping the solution slightly acidic — equivalence below 7. Only the salt of two strong parents, like sodium chloride, has two spectator ions and a genuinely neutral equivalence. This is hydrolysis, and it dictates the indicator: the endpoint must be announced AT the equivalence pH, so phenolphthalein — flipping in the basic range — serves the weak-acid-strong-base titration, and methyl orange — flipping in the acidic range — serves the strong-acid-weak-base one. Choose the wrong dye and it shouts too early or too late, and every calculation downstream inherits the error. The compact rule for revision: the salt takes after its stronger parent, and the indicator must change colour where the salt says the equivalence lies.
