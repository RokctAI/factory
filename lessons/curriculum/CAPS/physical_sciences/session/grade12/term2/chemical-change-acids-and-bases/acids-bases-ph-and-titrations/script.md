# Part 1 — Expert

Acids and bases close the Term 2 chemistry block and open Paper 2's most calculation-rich territory: pH, the ionisation constants K a and K b, and the titration — the one experiment every matric must be able to run on paper and at the bench. This session sets the definitions, separates strong from concentrated with the precision the examiner demands, builds the pH toolkit, and finishes with titration calculations, indicator choice and the hydrolysis of salts.

## Subtopic: Definitions and Conjugate Pairs

Two generations of definition, both examinable. ARRHENIUS: an acid is a substance that produces hydrogen ions — strictly, hydronium ions — when it dissolves in water, and a base is a substance that produces hydroxide ions in water. Serviceable, but water-bound. The broader definition the syllabus prefers is LOWRY-BRØNSTED: an acid is a PROTON DONOR, and a base is a PROTON ACCEPTOR — a proton meaning a hydrogen ion.

The power of Lowry-Brønsted is that it turns every acid-base reaction into a hand-over of one proton, and creates pairs. When hydrochloric acid donates a proton to water, the water acts as a base and becomes the hydronium ion; the chloride ion left behind is the CONJUGATE BASE of the acid. A conjugate acid-base pair is two species that differ by exactly one proton — hydrochloric acid and chloride; ammonium and ammonia; hydronium and water. In any such reaction, identify the two pairs by asking of each species: did it lose a proton, or gain one?

Water deserves its own sentence: it is an AMPHOLYTE — a substance that can act as either an acid or a base — donating a proton to ammonia in one beaker and accepting one from hydrochloric acid in another. The hydrogen carbonate ion and the hydrogen sulfate ion are ampholytes too, and spotting them is a favourite short question.

Acids in the syllabus come in two strengths of supply: monoprotic acids like hydrochloric and nitric acid donate one proton per molecule; diprotic acids like sulfuric acid can donate two. That factor of two will matter in every calculation ahead.

This section's questions are with you now: both definitions, pairs differing by one proton, and the ampholytes named.

## Subtopic: Strong, Weak, Concentrated, Dilute — and K a

Now the distinction that separates the top candidates: STRENGTH is not CONCENTRATION.

A STRONG acid ionises COMPLETELY in water: every molecule of hydrochloric acid donates its proton; none remains intact. The strong acids to know: hydrochloric acid, nitric acid, sulfuric acid. A WEAK acid ionises only PARTIALLY: in a bottle of ethanoic acid — the acid of vinegar — only a small fraction of molecules have donated protons at any instant, the rest sitting whole, in equilibrium with their ions. Weak acids of the syllabus: ethanoic acid, oxalic acid, phosphoric acid and carbonic acid. Among bases: sodium hydroxide and potassium hydroxide are strong, ionising completely; ammonia is weak, grabbing protons from only a fraction of its encounters with water.

CONCENTRATED and DILUTE speak about something entirely different: how much acid or base is dissolved per volume of solution — moles per cubic decimetre — regardless of what fraction ionises. So all four combinations exist: a concentrated weak acid, like the ethanoic acid in strong vinegar; a dilute strong acid, like the trace of hydrochloric acid in a swimming pool test; and the other two besides.

Because a weak acid's ionisation is an equilibrium, it carries an equilibrium constant: K a, the acid ionisation constant — and for bases, K b. A LARGE K a means the equilibrium lies far toward ionisation: a strong acid. A SMALL K a — ethanoic acid's is about ten to the minus five — means barely ionised: weak. K a is the strength written as a number, and comparing two acids' K a values ranks them instantly.

How would you TELL a strong from a weak acid of equal concentration at the bench? Three measurable differences, all flowing from the ion count. The strong acid has the LOWER pH — more hydronium ions. It conducts electricity BETTER — more charge carriers. And it reacts FASTER with magnesium or carbonates — more effective collisions with hydronium. Same concentration, different strength, three visible symptoms.

Questions on this section are in front of you now — strength is the fraction ionised, concentration is the amount per volume, and K a is strength quantified.

## Subtopic: pH, K w and the Calculations

The pH scale compresses the enormous range of hydronium concentrations into a friendly number line: pH equals the negative logarithm of the hydronium ion concentration. Each pH step is a factor of TEN in concentration: pH 3 holds ten times the hydronium of pH 4. Low pH, acidic; 7, neutral at 25 degrees Celsius; high, alkaline.

Underneath sits water's own equilibrium: water auto-ionises, a tiny fraction of molecules exchanging protons to form hydronium and hydroxide. The ionic product K w equals the hydronium concentration times the hydroxide concentration, which is 1,0 times ten to the minus fourteen at 25 degrees. In pure water each ion sits at ten to the minus seven — hence pH 7. K w is a see-saw: raise the hydronium and the hydroxide must fall so the product holds.

Calculation one: strong monoprotic acid. Hydrochloric acid at 0,01 moles per cubic decimetre ionises completely, so the hydronium concentration is 0,01 — ten to the minus two. pH: negative log of ten to the minus two, which is 2.

Calculation two: the diprotic trap. Sulfuric acid at 0,05 moles per cubic decimetre donates TWO protons per molecule, so the hydronium concentration is 2 times 0,05, which is 0,1 — and the pH is 1, not 1,3. The factor of two before the logarithm is the difference between full marks and a lost one.

Calculation three: a strong base, through K w. Sodium hydroxide at 0,001 moles per cubic decimetre gives a hydroxide concentration of ten to the minus three. Hydronium: K w divided by that — ten to the minus fourteen over ten to the minus three, which is ten to the minus eleven. pH: 11. Alkaline, as it must be.

And the weak-acid caveat, conceptual but examinable: ethanoic acid at 0,01 moles per cubic decimetre does NOT have pH 2. Only a fraction of its molecules ionise, so the hydronium concentration sits well below 0,01 and the pH lands noticeably higher — around 3,4 — closer to neutral than the strong acid of identical concentration. Concentration alone never fixes pH; strength stands between them.

This section's questions arrive now — count the protons, mind the powers of ten, and route bases through K w.

## Subtopic: Titrations, Indicators and the pH of Salts

A TITRATION determines the unknown concentration of an acid or base by reacting it exactly with a standard solution — one of precisely known concentration. The equivalence point is the moment the acid and base have reacted in the exact mole ratio of the balanced equation, and the calculation is four lines long.

Worked: 25 cubic centimetres of sodium hydroxide solution is exactly neutralised by 20 cubic centimetres of hydrochloric acid of concentration 0,1 moles per cubic decimetre. The equation: one mole of acid per mole of base. Moles of acid used: concentration times volume in cubic decimetres — 0,1 times 0,020, which is 0,002 moles. The one-to-one ratio makes the base also 0,002 moles. Its concentration: 0,002 divided by 0,025 cubic decimetres, which is 0,08 moles per cubic decimetre. Formula, substitution, ratio, answer — with volumes converted to cubic decimetres before anything else. Had the acid been sulfuric, the ratio line would carry the factor two: one mole of acid neutralises two of base.

The INDICATOR announces the endpoint by changing colour, and choosing it is a syllabus skill built on hydrolysis — because the salt left at equivalence sets the pH there. A salt of a strong acid and a strong base — sodium chloride — leaves the solution NEUTRAL at equivalence: either bromothymol blue or careful technique with any sharp indicator serves. A salt of a WEAK acid and a strong base — sodium ethanoate — hydrolyses BASIC: the ethanoate ion accepts protons from water, releasing hydroxide, so equivalence sits above 7 and the indicator must change there: phenolphthalein, turning from colourless to pink in exactly that range. A salt of a strong acid and a WEAK base — ammonium chloride — hydrolyses ACIDIC: the ammonium ion donates protons to water, equivalence sits below 7, and methyl orange, changing around that range, is the fit. The rule compresses to one sentence: the STRONG parent wins the salt's pH, and the indicator must change colour at the equivalence pH.

Auto-ionisation, hydrolysis, equilibrium constants — notice this whole topic is last fortnight's equilibrium chemistry wearing acid-base clothing, and the exam builds its hardest questions on exactly that connection.

The final expert questions are with you now — four-line titrations, volumes in cubic decimetres, and the strong parent naming the salt's pH.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same chemistry with a rugby ball, a bottle of cordial, and a ladder of tens.

## Subtopic: Givers and Takers

Strip away the vocabulary and acid-base chemistry is a game of pass-the-parcel with a single tiny parcel: the proton — a hydrogen atom that has lost its electron, the smallest package in chemistry.

An acid is a GIVER: a molecule holding a proton it is willing to pass on. A base is a TAKER: a molecule with room to receive one. Every acid-base reaction, no exceptions, is one hand-off: giver passes proton to taker, done. Hydrochloric acid meets water; the acid gives, the water takes, and what remains are the chloride ion — the acid minus its parcel — and the hydronium ion — water holding an extra parcel.

That after-picture explains the word CONJUGATE, which frightens more learners than it should. Once the acid has given away its proton, what is left could, in principle, take one back — a giver minus its parcel is a potential taker. So every acid has a shadow partner, its conjugate base: same species, one proton lighter. Ammonium and ammonia. Hydronium and water. Spot pairs by counting hydrogens: differ by exactly one, with the same everything else, and you have a conjugate pair.

Some players can play both positions. Water takes a proton from hydrochloric acid, but GIVES one to ammonia. A substance that can give or take depending on the company is an ampholyte — chemistry's utility player — and water is the most famous one on the team sheet.

Your first questions are with you now — find the giver, find the taker, and pair each player with its one-proton-lighter shadow.

## Subtopic: Strong Is Not Concentrated

Here is the sentence that upgrades your answers all year: strong is about WILLINGNESS, concentrated is about AMOUNT. They sound like synonyms in everyday speech; in chemistry they are different axes entirely, and the exam checks yearly whether you know it.

Think of cordial. How much syrup you pour into the glass is CONCENTRATION — a lot of syrup, concentrated; a splash, dilute. Now imagine two kinds of syrup: one dissolves instantly and completely the moment it touches water; the other barely mixes, most of it sitting stubbornly as syrup no matter how you stir. That eagerness to split up is STRENGTH. A strong acid is the instant syrup: drop it in water and EVERY molecule splits, handing its proton over. A weak acid is the stubborn one: only a small fraction splits; the rest floats intact, waiting.

So all four combinations exist, and you can name them. Concentrated strong: lots of acid, all of it split — battery acid. Dilute strong: a little acid, but all of it split — pool-test drops. Concentrated weak: lots of acid, barely split — the vinegar in your kitchen. Dilute weak: a splash and barely split — vinegar in salad dressing.

Chemists give the willingness a number: K a. Big K a, eager splitter, strong. Tiny K a, reluctant, weak. And at the bench, three symptoms betray the strong acid even at equal concentration: lower pH, brighter conductivity — more ions carrying charge — and faster fizzing on a piece of magnesium. Same amount in the bottle; completely different behaviour in the glass.

Questions on this section are coming to you now — one axis for amount, one for willingness, and never let the two blur.

## Subtopic: The Ladder of Tens and the Exact Balance

The pH scale is a ladder, and every rung is a factor of TEN. Step down one rung — pH 4 to pH 3 — and the solution holds ten times more hydronium. Step down three rungs, a thousand times more. That is why pH differences that sound small are chemically enormous, and why fish die when a river drops two rungs: the water became a hundred times more acidic.

The ladder's middle rung, 7, is where pure water stands — not because water is empty of ions, but because it holds exactly equal, tiny crowds of hydronium and hydroxide, each at one part in ten million. Acids climb the hydronium crowd up and the pH down; bases do the reverse, and the two crowds balance on a see-saw: multiply them together and you always get the same fixed number, so when one crowd grows, the other must shrink.

Now the exact balance: the titration, chemistry's most careful piece of pouring. The question it answers: how strong is this mystery bottle of base? The method: add acid of KNOWN concentration, drop by drop, until giver and taker have exactly matched — every proton delivered to a receiver, none spare. That perfect-match moment is the equivalence point, and from the volumes poured and the one known concentration, the unknown falls out of four lines of arithmetic. The only physical sign of the match is a puff of colour: the indicator, a dye that flips shade at a chosen pH, planted to shout at exactly the moment of balance.

One elegant twist to finish: the "neutral" endpoint is not always at 7. The salt left behind can itself lean acidic or basic — the child takes after the STRONGER parent. Vinegar neutralised by caustic soda leaves a slightly basic solution, so the indicator must flip above 7; ammonia neutralised by hydrochloric acid leaves slightly acidic, flipping below. Match the indicator to the salt's lean, and the shout comes at the true moment of balance.

The last questions of the lesson are yours now — ten times per rung, four lines per titration, and the salt takes after its stronger parent.
