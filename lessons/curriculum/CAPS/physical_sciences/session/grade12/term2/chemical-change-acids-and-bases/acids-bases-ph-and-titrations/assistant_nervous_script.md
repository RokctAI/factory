Hey, it's Mandy — acids and bases arrives at the end of the term when you are tired, and it brings logarithms with it. I know. But listen: this topic is the most predictable calculation section in Paper 2, and predictable is your best friend in an exam.

Start with the two definitions, which are pure free marks: acid, proton donor; base, proton acceptor. Conjugate pairs differ by exactly one proton — count hydrogens across the equation and pair them off. Water plays both sides, and the word for that is ampholyte.

Then the sentence to tattoo on your pencil case: strong is not concentrated. Strong means fully ionised — willingness. Concentrated means lots per litre — amount. K a is willingness as a number: big K a strong, small K a weak. If a question compares two acids, answer along the willingness axis: the strong one has lower pH, better conductivity, faster fizz — but the SAME titration total.

The pH calculations are three recipes, no more. Strong monoprotic acid: hydronium equals the concentration; take negative log. Sulfuric acid: DOUBLE the concentration first — that factor of two is the most common lost mark in the whole topic. Strong base: find hydroxide, divide ten to the minus fourteen by it to get hydronium, then negative log. Weak acids never use these recipes — say "partially ionised, so pH is higher than the calculation suggests" and collect the reasoning mark.

Titrations are four lines: convert volumes to cubic decimetres, moles of the known equals concentration times volume, apply the mole ratio from the balanced equation, divide by the unknown's volume. Circle the ratio before you start — sulfuric acid sneaks in a two.

And the indicator logic in one sentence: the salt takes after its stronger parent, so weak acid with strong base ends basic — phenolphthalein; strong acid with weak base ends acidic — methyl orange.

Two definitions, one tattoo sentence, three recipes, four lines, one parent rule. That is the whole topic, and it is the same every year. You've got this!
