Hey, it's Mandy — rates of reaction looks like a topic with five factors, four experiments, three graphs and a scary curve with a Greek-sounding name. Let me hand you the one sentence that answers eighty percent of it: more effective collisions per second means a faster reaction. That sentence, finished properly, IS the explanation mark, every time.

Every factor plugs into it the same way. Concentration up: more particles in the space, more collisions per second. Surface area up: more particles exposed, more collisions per second. Temperature up: bigger fraction of collisions has enough energy — say "fraction", it is the money word. Catalyst: lower activation energy, so more collisions clear the bar. Start from the factor, end at effective collisions per second. Never stop halfway.

For the graphs, three readings and no more. Steepness is speed. The bend is the reaction tiring as reactants run out. The plateau height is the amount made — the extent. And the rule that defuses the trap question: faster never means more. Same amounts of chemicals always means same plateau, no matter how steep the start. Only changing the amount of reactant moves the plateau.

The distribution curve has exactly three moves, one per factor. Temperature: the curve shifts right and flattens, same area, bigger tail past the activation line. Concentration: the curve grows taller, same position. Catalyst: the curve stays put and the LINE moves left. Learn which piece moves and the sketch marks are yours.

Calculations are one division: change in moles, or grams, or volume, divided by time. And in the disappearing cross, flip it: short time means fast rate — one over time, always.

One sentence, three graph words, three curve moves, one division. Sprint, tire, stop — that is every reaction, and now it is also your revision plan for tonight. You've got this!
