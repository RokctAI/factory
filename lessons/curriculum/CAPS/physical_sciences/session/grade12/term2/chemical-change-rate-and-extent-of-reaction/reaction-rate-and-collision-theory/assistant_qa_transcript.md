**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First, a subtle one that costs marks every year: why does raising the temperature increase the rate so much more powerfully than raising the concentration, when both give more collisions?

**Tutor:** Because the two factors act on different parts of the collision story, and one part is dramatically more sensitive than the other. Concentration is a numbers play: pack twice as many particles into the flask and you roughly double the collisions per second, so the rate roughly doubles — a solid, linear, predictable gain. But the FRACTION of those collisions that succeeds is untouched; you have more attempts at the same success rate. Temperature attacks the success rate itself. The energies of particles follow the distribution curve, and the particles able to react are only those in the thin tail beyond the activation energy. That tail is extraordinarily sensitive to warming: shift the whole curve modestly toward higher energies and the tail can double or triple, because you are moving the population of a steep, thin region of the graph. That is why the old laboratory rule of thumb says a rise of about ten degrees can roughly double the rate of many reactions — not because collisions become slightly more frequent, which they do, but because vastly more of the existing collisions now clear the energy bar. So in an explanation question, match the mechanism to the factor: concentration means more collisions per second at the same success fraction; temperature means a much larger fraction of collisions exceeding the activation energy, plus slightly more frequent collisions. Writing "more collisions" for temperature is half an answer, and the marking guideline treats it as such.

**Mandy:** Next: the definition says a catalyst is not consumed by the reaction. Learners then ask — if it never gets used, how can it possibly be doing anything? What is it actually doing in there?

**Tutor:** It is participating intimately and then walking away clean, and the trick is that it takes part in the MECHANISM without appearing in the overall equation. A catalyst offers the reactants a different route from reactants to products — often by binding a reactant to its surface, or by forming a short-lived intermediate compound — and that alternative route has a lower activation energy than the direct one. Somewhere in the middle of the journey, the catalyst is genuinely bonded into the chemistry; by the end of the journey, the products have left and the catalyst stands restored, identical to how it began, ready for the next set of reactants. That is why "not consumed" does not mean "not involved" — it means the involvement is perfectly recycled. Two consequences follow, and both are examinable. First, a small amount of catalyst can convert an enormous amount of reactant, because the same catalyst particles cycle endlessly — the platinum coating in one car's catalytic converter cleans exhaust for the vehicle's whole lifetime. Second, the catalyst changes only the SPEED of reaching the destination, never the destination itself: on the potential energy diagram it lowers the hump, but the energies of reactants and products — and therefore delta H — are exactly as before, and the eventual amounts of product are unchanged too. The image to carry: a mountain pass. The catalyst does not move your home or your destination; it opens a lower pass through the mountain, and the guide who showed you the pass is standing at the trailhead afterwards, unchanged, ready to lead the next travellers.

**Mandy:** Here is the graph one: when a question asks learners to sketch the curve for the SAME reaction but with a catalyst, or warmer acid, they never know what to keep and what to change. What is the checklist?

**Tutor:** Two questions, asked in order, settle every sketch. Question one: did the AMOUNTS of reactants change? If not, the final plateau — the extent — must not move a millimetre. Catalyst, temperature, surface area, stirring: none of these creates one extra molecule of product, so your new curve must flatten at exactly the same height as the original. Only changing the moles of a reactant — or which reactant is limiting — moves the plateau. Question two: did the RATE change, and which way? Faster conditions — powder, heat, higher concentration, catalyst — give a steeper initial gradient AND an earlier arrival at the plateau; slower conditions give a lazier climb and a later arrival. So the standard sketch for "same amounts, faster conditions" is: start at the same origin, climb more steeply, bend earlier, and merge into the same horizontal line. The failure modes the marker sees constantly: a faster curve drawn to a HIGHER plateau, which claims faster made more, wrong; and two curves crossing mid-graph, which is impossible for the same amounts, because the faster experiment is ahead at every moment — its curve sits above the slower one throughout and they only meet at the shared plateau. One more precision point: label the axes and mark the plateau value if it is given, because a sketch question is marked on shape, start, steepness comparison, and shared plateau — four features, four ticks, and now you know exactly where each tick lives.

**Mandy:** Last one: in the disappearing cross experiment, we measure the time for the cross to vanish — but time is not rate. How do we use those times correctly when the question asks about rate?

**Tutor:** Treat the time as an INVERSE messenger: rate behaves as one divided by the time, and every comparison must pass through that inversion. The logic first. The cross disappears when a fixed amount of sulfur has formed — enough cloudiness to block the view. So every run of the experiment measures how long the reaction needs to produce the SAME amount of product. If the acid is warmer and the cross vanishes in 30 seconds instead of 90, the same chemical distance was covered in one third of the time — the reaction ran three times faster. Rate is amount over time; with the amount fixed, rate is proportional to one over time. Practically, that gives you three rules. Rule one, for comparisons: shortest time equals fastest rate, always — say it explicitly in your answer, because the examiner is testing exactly that inversion. Rule two, for graphs: if you are asked to plot something that represents rate against temperature or concentration, plot one over time on the vertical axis, not time itself; a rising one-over-time graph correctly shows rate increasing. Rule three, for language: never write "the rate took longer" — rates are fast or slow, times are long or short, and keeping the vocabulary straight keeps the thinking straight. And one experimental honesty point worth a mark in evaluation questions: the human judgement of "the cross has vanished" adds uncertainty, which is why the same person should judge every run, from the same angle, in the same light — fair testing applied to your own eyes.
