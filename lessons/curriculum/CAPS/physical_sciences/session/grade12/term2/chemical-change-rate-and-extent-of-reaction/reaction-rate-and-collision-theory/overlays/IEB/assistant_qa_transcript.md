**Mandy:** Question time before we finish! First: if particles are colliding billions of times a second, why do most reactions take minutes or hours instead of finishing instantly?

**Tutor:** Because almost every one of those billions of collisions is a dud, and the arithmetic of duds is brutal. Two filters stand between a collision and a reaction. Filter one is energy: a colliding pair must arrive with kinetic energy at least equal to the activation energy, and on the energy distribution curve the qualifying tail is often a vanishingly small slice of the whole population — for many reactions at room temperature, fewer than one collision in millions carries enough energy. Filter two is orientation: even an energetic pair must meet with the right atoms facing each other; a hydrogen ion striking the wrong end of a molecule bounces off exactly as if it had never tried. Multiply two small probabilities and you get the observed leisurely pace: billions of attempts a second, a tiny yield of successes, and a reaction that ambles along for an hour. This is also why the rate factors work the way they do — none of them changes the chemistry of a single successful collision; they just rig the lottery. Concentration and surface area buy more tickets per second. Temperature improves the odds on every ticket by pushing more of the population past the energy filter. And the catalyst quietly lowers the winning threshold itself. So the honest picture of a beaker is not steady progress; it is an enormous number of failures per second, salted with just enough wins to move the chemistry forward.

**Mandy:** Next: you said temperature mostly works by increasing the fraction of effective collisions, not the number of collisions. How would an answer that only mentions "particles move faster so they collide more often" lose out?

**Tutor:** It would lose out because it names the minor effect and skips the major one, and the numbers show the gap dramatically. Warm a mixture by ten degrees and the particles' average speed rises only a few percent — so collision frequency creeps up by a few percent too. If frequency were the whole story, a ten-degree rise should quicken a reaction by a factor of about one point zero three. What we actually observe for many reactions is a DOUBLING of rate for roughly every ten-degree rise. A few percent cannot explain a hundred percent; something else must be doing the lifting. That something is the energy distribution. The qualifying tail beyond the activation energy line is the far, thin end of the curve, and when the whole curve slides even slightly toward higher energies, that tail does not grow slightly — it can double or triple, because you are adding area to a region that had almost none. The fraction of collisions that clear the bar explodes even though the total collision count barely moves. So the full explanation runs: heating raises the particles' average kinetic energy, so a much larger fraction of collisions now possesses at least the activation energy, giving many more effective collisions per second; the small rise in collision frequency helps, but it is the supporting act. Say the fraction sentence first and the frequency sentence second, and your explanation matches what the measurements demand.

**Mandy:** Here is the graph one: in the vanishing-cross experiment, why do we only get one time per run instead of a nice curve, and how do we compare runs fairly?

**Tutor:** Good eye — the vanishing cross is the odd one out among the techniques, because the flask hides its own progress. In the gas-syringe or balance experiments, the instrument reports continuously, so you harvest a whole curve from one run. The thiosulfate reaction clouds gradually, and the eye cannot grade cloudiness on a scale — but it CAN judge one crisp, repeatable event: the moment the cross under the flask disappears completely. So the design converts a continuous process into a single stopwatch reading — the time for a FIXED amount of sulfur to form. That fixed-amount idea is what makes comparison fair, and it imposes three disciplines. First, identical crosses, identical flasks, identical volumes, and the same eye at the same viewing position, run after run — the endpoint must mean the same amount of precipitate every time. Second, change only one variable between runs — concentration in one investigation, temperature in another — with everything else clamped. Third, convert before you compare: rate behaves as one over time, so a run of twenty-five seconds is ten times faster than a run of two hundred and fifty, and if you plot one-over-time against concentration you get a graph whose upward slope actually reads as "more concentrated, faster" — plotting raw time gives a downward curve that trips careless readers. And one honest limitation to mention whenever asked to evaluate the method: the human-judged endpoint adds uncertainty, which is why repeats and averages belong in the design.

**Mandy:** Last one: the catalytic converter in a car works for years without topping up. If the catalyst attends every reaction, how does it survive every reaction?

**Tutor:** Because attending is not the same as being spent — the catalyst is a participant that always gets returned, and the mechanism explains the magic. A catalyst works by offering the reaction an alternative pathway with a lower activation energy, and that pathway usually runs through temporary partnerships. In the converter, exhaust molecules land on the metal coating and stick briefly — that landing weakens their internal bonds, which is precisely why the energy barrier drops. The weakened molecules rearrange into the products, and here is the crucial step: the products let go. They leave the surface, freeing that patch of metal for the next molecules in the exhaust stream. Bind, transform, release, repeat — the metal atoms end every cycle exactly as they began it, chemically unchanged, which is why the definition insists a catalyst increases the rate without being consumed BY the reaction. One gram of surface can chaperone its own mass in exhaust gases millions of times over; that is why a thin coating on a honeycomb lasts the life of the car. But notice the definition's careful wording — not consumed by the REACTION. The real world can still ruin a catalyst from outside: certain impurities bind to the surface and never let go, blocking the sites permanently. That is called poisoning, and it is why leaded fuel destroyed converters — the lead squatted on the metal and the exhaust molecules could no longer land. So the catalyst is less like a fuel and more like a key: used every single time, consumed never, but very much capable of being jammed.
