# Part 1 — Expert

Why does a fire lick through dry grass in seconds while an iron gate takes years to rust, when both are reactions with oxygen? Rate of reaction is the chemistry of HOW FAST, and it is one of the most graph-heavy, experiment-heavy sections of Paper 2. This session defines rate and calculates it from data, explains every rate factor through collision theory, tours the standard measurement techniques, and finishes with the molecular energy distribution curves that carry the topic's hardest marks.

## Subtopic: What Reaction Rate Means

The definition: reaction rate is the change in the concentration or the amount of reactants or products per unit time. In symbols, rate equals delta concentration divided by delta time, or delta amount in moles divided by delta time. The units follow: moles per second, or, when concentrations are used, moles per cubic decimetre per second. Rate is always reported as a positive quantity, whether you track a reactant disappearing or a product appearing.

Calculate one from real data. Dilute hydrochloric acid is poured onto calcium carbonate chips on a balance, producing carbon dioxide gas that escapes, so the flask loses mass. In the first 50 seconds the mass drops by 2,2 grams. Moles of carbon dioxide: 2,2 divided by the molar mass of 44 grams per mole, which is 0,05 moles. Average rate: 0,05 divided by 50, which is 0,001 moles per second — one thousandth of a mole of gas per second, in that interval.

The word AVERAGE matters. Reactions are fastest at the start, when reactant concentrations are highest, and they slow continuously as reactants are consumed. So the rate over the first 50 seconds is higher than over the next 50, and a single reaction has many average rates depending on the interval chosen. The instantaneous rate at one moment is the gradient of the amount-time graph at that point — steep gradient, fast reaction; zero gradient, reaction finished.

Distinguish rate from EXTENT. Extent asks how much product forms in total by the end; rate asks how quickly it forms along the way. Two experiments with the same amounts of reactants reach the same extent — the same final mass of product — even if one runs a hundred times faster. Keep the two questions separate and half the trick questions in this topic disarm themselves.

This section's questions are with you now: definition, a moles-per-time calculation, and average kept apart from instantaneous.

## Subtopic: Collision Theory and the Factors That Change Rate

Collision theory is the explanation engine for the entire topic, and every explanation mark flows through it. Particles react only when they COLLIDE, and only when the collision is EFFECTIVE — meaning two conditions are met: the particles collide with the correct orientation, and they collide with kinetic energy equal to or greater than the ACTIVATION ENERGY, the minimum energy needed for a reaction to occur. Reaction rate is therefore decided by the number of effective collisions per second, and every factor that changes the rate changes either how often particles collide or what fraction of collisions are effective.

Run the factors. NATURE OF THE REACTANTS: some substances are simply more reactive than others — magnesium reacts with acid far faster than copper does — and reactions between ions in solution are typically faster than those needing strong covalent bonds broken.

SURFACE AREA: only particles at a surface can be struck. Grind a solid into powder and vastly more particles are exposed, collisions per second multiply, and the rate climbs — the same reason fine maize dust can explode in a silo while a solid cob merely burns.

CONCENTRATION, and for gases, PRESSURE: more particles packed into the same volume means more collisions per second, so more effective collisions per second. Doubling the acid concentration on the same chips visibly quickens the fizz.

TEMPERATURE, the powerful one: heating particles raises their average kinetic energy, so a much larger FRACTION of collisions now carries at least the activation energy. Collisions also become slightly more frequent, but the fraction argument is the one the marker wants — temperature works mainly by making each collision more likely to succeed, not just more common.

CATALYST: a substance that increases the rate of a reaction without being consumed by the reaction, held for the next section.

Questions on the factors are in front of you now — every answer must end at effective collisions per second, or it is not an explanation.

## Subtopic: Measuring Rate in the Laboratory

Rate experiments all share one design: find something measurable that changes as the reaction runs, and log it against time.

Technique one: GAS VOLUME. Collect the gas in a syringe or over water, reading volume against time. The volume-time graph rises steeply at first, flattens as reactants deplete, and goes horizontal when the reaction is complete — the plateau height is the extent, the gradient is the rate.

Technique two: MASS LOSS. Run the reaction in an open flask on a balance as a gas escapes; the mass-time reading falls quickly, then levels. Our calcium carbonate calculation came from exactly this set-up.

Technique three: TURBIDITY — the disappearing cross. React sodium thiosulfate with hydrochloric acid and the mixture clouds with a fine precipitate of sulfur. Stand the flask on a paper cross and time how long until the cross vanishes from view. A shorter time means a faster reaction — the measurement is one over time, so do not confuse a short time with a slow rate. This is the standard SBA investigation for temperature or concentration effects.

Technique four: COLOUR CHANGE, timed by eye or measured by instrument, for reactions with a coloured reactant or product.

Now the graph-reading skill the exam loves: same reaction, two curves. Equal masses of powdered and chipped calcium carbonate with the same excess acid give two volume-time curves that START differently but END at the same plateau. The powder's curve climbs steeply — more surface, faster rate; the chips climb lazily. But the same moles of carbonate must yield the same moles of gas, so both curves flatten at the SAME final volume. Steeper start, same finish: rate changed, extent unchanged. If a curve flattens LOWER, the amounts must have differed — less reactant, or a limiting reagent swapped.

This section's questions arrive now — match each technique to its graph, and read rate from gradients, extent from plateaus.

## Subtopic: Energy Distribution Curves and Catalysts

The deepest explanations in this topic ride on one graph: the Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution — the syllabus calls it the molecular energy distribution. Plot the number of particles against kinetic energy at a fixed temperature: few particles are very slow, few are very fast, most sit in a hump around the average. Mark the activation energy as a vertical line on the energy axis: only the particles to the RIGHT of that line — the shaded tail — can collide effectively. The area of that tail, relative to the whole, sets the fraction of successful collisions.

Now redraw for each factor. TEMPERATURE RAISED: the whole curve shifts toward higher energies and flattens — same total area, because the number of particles is unchanged — but the tail beyond the activation energy line grows dramatically. A modest temperature rise can multiply the reacting fraction several times over, which is why temperature is the most powerful lever on rate.

CONCENTRATION RAISED: the curve keeps its shape and position but sits taller — more particles at every energy, same average. The tail beyond the line grows in absolute number, though not in proportion. More collisions per second, same success fraction per collision.

CATALYST ADDED: the curve itself does not move at all. Instead, the activation energy LINE moves left. A catalyst provides an alternative reaction pathway with a lower activation energy, so the existing energy distribution suddenly finds far more of its particles beyond the (new, lower) threshold. The particles never changed; the barrier did. And because the catalyst is not consumed, a small amount serves the whole reaction — the catalytic converter in a car's exhaust works for years on the same coating.

On the potential-energy diagram from Grade 11, the catalyst lowers the hump between reactants and products while leaving the start and end energies — and therefore delta H — completely unchanged. A catalyst changes the journey, never the destination.

The final expert questions are with you now — say which feature of the graph moved: the curve, its height, or the line.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same chemistry at a party: how fast, not how far, and why hard bumps are the only bumps that count.

## Subtopic: How Fast Is Not How Far

Two questions about any journey: how far are you going, and how fast are you driving? Chemistry asks the same two about every reaction, and mixing them up is where the marks leak.

Drop one sugar cube into cold tea and one spoon of loose sugar into hot tea. The loose sugar in hot tea vanishes in seconds; the cube in cold tea takes minutes. But once both are fully dissolved, each cup is EXACTLY as sweet as the other — same sugar, same final sweetness. Speed changed; the destination did not. In chemistry language: the RATE differed, the EXTENT was identical.

That single idea decodes the classic exam graph. Two curves of gas volume against time: one shoots up steeply, the other climbs slowly, and both flatten at the same height. Translation: same amounts of chemicals — same destination — but one trip was driven faster, maybe with powder instead of chips, or warmer acid, or stronger acid. If instead one curve flattens at a LOWER height, that experiment carried less reactant: a shorter journey, regardless of driving speed.

And a speed detail worth owning: reactions sprint at the start and limp at the end. Early on, reactant particles are everywhere, bumping constantly; as they get used up, encounters become rare and the reaction wheezes toward its plateau. That is why the graph is steep first, flat later — and why "the rate" of a reaction usually means "the rate right now", read from the steepness at that moment.

Your first questions are with you now — steepness is speed, plateau height is distance, and never let one masquerade as the other.

## Subtopic: Bumper Cars That Only Count When They Hit Hard

Picture reactant particles as bumper cars at a night market. Collisions everywhere — but here is the rule of the game: a collision only COUNTS if the cars hit hard enough, and hit at the right angle. Soft bumps and glancing scrapes achieve nothing; the cars just bounce apart unchanged. Chemists call the counting bumps effective collisions, and the hard-enough threshold has a name: activation energy.

Once you see the game, every rate factor becomes obvious.

Add more cars to the same floor — that is raising concentration. More cars, more bumps per minute, more of the hard kind too. The fizz quickens.

Smash the big car into pieces — that is surface area. A boulder of calcium carbonate hides almost all its particles inside, where no acid can touch them. Grind it to powder and suddenly every particle is on the outside, available for bumping. Same amount of stone, a floor full of little cars instead of one big one.

Turn up every car's motor — that is temperature. Faster cars bump more often, yes, but the real magic is that far MORE of the bumps now cross the hard-enough threshold. That is why warmth is the strongest lever chemistry has: it upgrades ordinary bumps into counting bumps, floor-wide.

And the catalyst? The catalyst does not touch the cars at all. It lowers the BAR — it opens a side gate through which a gentler bump is enough. Same cars, same speeds, easier target, and the catalyst itself never gets used up; it just holds the gate open all night.

Questions on the bumper cars are coming to you now — every explanation ends the same way: more counting bumps per second.

## Subtopic: Reading the Story off the Graph

Every rate experiment ends as a graph, and the graph is a story you can read aloud if you know its three words: steepness, bend, and plateau.

STEEPNESS is speed. Where the curve climbs steeply, gas is pouring out, mass is plummeting, the reaction is sprinting. Compare two curves at the same moment: the steeper one is the faster experiment — the powder, the warmer acid, the stronger acid.

The BEND is the slowdown. As reactants are eaten up, encounters get scarce and the curve leans over. The story is always the same shape: sprint, tire, stop. No rate graph in this topic speeds UP later on its own — if a question shows that, something was added mid-race.

The PLATEAU is the finish line, and its HEIGHT tells you how much product was ever made — the extent. Here is the exam's favourite trick, so take it slowly: powdered chalk and chunky chalk, same mass, same acid. The powder sprints — steeper curve — but both plateaus sit at exactly the same height, because the same amount of chalk can only ever make the same amount of gas. Faster never means more. Change the AMOUNT of a reactant, though, and the plateau itself moves: half the chalk, half the final gas, a lower shelf.

One more reading skill: the disappearing cross experiment hands you a TIME, not a graph — how long until the cross vanishes. Short time means fast reaction, so when you plot or compare, think "one over time": thirty seconds is fast, three hundred is slow. Learners who treat a big time as a big rate walk straight into the trap.

The last questions of the lesson are yours now — read steepness for speed, plateau for amount, and let the story be sprint, tire, stop.
