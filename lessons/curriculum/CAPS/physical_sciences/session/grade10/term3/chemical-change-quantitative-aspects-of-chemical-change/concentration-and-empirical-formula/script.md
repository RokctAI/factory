# Part 1 — Expert

This session builds two of the sharpest tools in quantitative chemistry: concentration — how much substance is dissolved in each cubic decimetre of solution — and the empirical formula, the recipe of a compound recovered from nothing but percentages. Two exam problems drive everything. First: 8 grams of sodium hydroxide is dissolved in 500 cubic centimetres of solution; calculate the concentration. Second: a compound contains 40,0 percent carbon, 6,7 percent hydrogen and 53,3 percent oxygen by mass; determine its empirical formula. Both problems reward the same habit — moles first, everything else after.

## Subtopic: Concentration — Moles per Cubic Decimetre

Concentration answers the question: how crowded is this solution? The formal definition: concentration, symbol c, equals the number of moles of solute, n, divided by the volume of solution, V, in cubic decimetres. As a sentence: c equals n over V — the concentration equals the moles dissolved, divided by the volume they are dissolved in. The unit is moles per cubic decimetre, written mol per dm cubed, and a cubic decimetre is the same thing as a litre.

Two conversions guard the formula. Volume almost always arrives in cubic centimetres, and the formula speaks cubic decimetres: divide cubic centimetres by one thousand — 500 cubic centimetres is 0,5 cubic decimetres. And the substance almost always arrives in grams, while the formula speaks moles: divide the mass by the molar mass — n equals m over M, the tool you already own.

Now the first problem. Sodium hydroxide is NaOH. Molar mass, atom by atom from the periodic table: sodium 23, oxygen 16, hydrogen 1 — 23 plus 16 plus 1 is 40 grams per mole. Moles: n equals m over M — 8 grams divided by 40 grams per mole is 0,2 moles. Volume: 500 cubic centimetres divided by one thousand is 0,5 cubic decimetres. Concentration: c equals n over V — 0,2 moles divided by 0,5 cubic decimetres — 0,4 moles per cubic decimetre.

Sense-check it: 0,2 moles sitting in half a cubic decimetre means a full cubic decimetre would hold twice as much — 0,4 moles. The division simply scaled the crowd up to a standard container.

The questions for this section are with you now. They examine the formula read as a sentence, the two conversions — grams to moles, cubic centimetres to cubic decimetres — and the 0,4 moles per cubic decimetre answer with its reasoning.

## Subtopic: Percentage Composition — the Compound's Mass Recipe

Before empirical formulas, the reverse skill: given a formula, what percentage of the compound's mass does each element contribute?

The rule: percentage of an element equals the mass of that element in one mole of the compound, divided by the molar mass of the compound, times one hundred.

Take water, H2O. Molar mass: two hydrogens at 1 plus one oxygen at 16 — 18 grams per mole. Hydrogen's share: 2 grams out of 18 — 2 divided by 18 times 100 is 11,1 percent. Oxygen's share: 16 out of 18 — 88,9 percent. Check: 11,1 plus 88,9 is one hundred. The check is not decoration; percentages that refuse to total one hundred are announcing an arithmetic error.

One more: carbon dioxide, CO2, molar mass 44. Carbon's share: 12 divided by 44 times 100 — 27,3 percent. Both oxygens together: 32 divided by 44 — 72,7 percent. Notice the subscript discipline again: the two oxygens contribute as a pair, 32 grams, never 16.

Why this matters beyond the marks: percentage composition is measurable in a laboratory. A chemist with an unknown white powder can burn it, weigh the products, and recover these percentages without ever seeing a formula. Which sets up the next move — running the calculation backwards.

Work through this section's questions now. They examine the rule as a fraction of the molar mass, the water percentages of 11,1 and 88,9, and the totalling check that catches errors before the memo does.

## Subtopic: Empirical Formula — the Recipe Recovered from Percentages

Now the second exam problem: 40,0 percent carbon, 6,7 percent hydrogen, 53,3 percent oxygen. Find the empirical formula — the simplest whole-number ratio of atoms in the compound.

The method has four steps, and step one is a gift: assume a sample of exactly 100 grams. Then every percentage becomes a mass — 40,0 grams of carbon, 6,7 grams of hydrogen, 53,3 grams of oxygen. The assumption is legitimate because a ratio does not care how much sample you imagine; one hundred grams simply makes the arithmetic immediate.

Step two: convert each mass to moles, because atoms combine in mole ratios, never in mass ratios. Carbon: 40,0 divided by 12 is 3,33 moles. Hydrogen: 6,7 divided by 1 is 6,7 moles. Oxygen: 53,3 divided by 16 is 3,33 moles.

Step three: divide every answer by the smallest of them, 3,33. Carbon: 3,33 over 3,33 is 1. Hydrogen: 6,7 over 3,33 is 2,01 — call it 2. Oxygen: 3,33 over 3,33 is 1.

Step four: read off the whole-number ratio — one carbon to two hydrogens to one oxygen. The empirical formula is C H 2 O, written CH2O.

One refinement completes the method. If step three leaves something like 1,5 or 1,33, do not round it away — multiply every number in the ratio by 2 or by 3 until all are whole. A ratio of 1 to 1,5 is honestly 2 to 3; rounding 1,5 down to 1 invents a different compound.

Take this section's questions now. They run the method in order: the 100 gram assumption and why it is allowed, the division by atomic mass, the division by the smallest, and the multiply-up rule for ratios that arrive as fractions.

## Subtopic: Water of Crystallisation, the Full Method and the Traps

Some crystals lock water molecules into their structure in a fixed ratio — water of crystallisation. Blue copper sulfate crystals are CuSO4 dot 5H2O: every formula unit carries exactly five water molecules. Heat the crystals and the water leaves; the powder turns white and loses mass — and that mass loss is percentage composition working in the laboratory.

Compute the water's share. Molar mass of CuSO4: copper 63,5 plus sulfur 32 plus four oxygens at 64 — 159,5 grams per mole. The five waters: 5 times 18 is 90 grams. Total: 249,5 grams per mole. Water's percentage: 90 divided by 249,5 times 100 — 36,1 percent. Just over a third of the mass of blue copper sulfate is water, sitting inside apparently dry crystals.

Now the traps that fill marking reports on this whole lesson. Trap one: volume left in cubic centimetres — a concentration computed with 500 instead of 0,5 shrinks the answer a thousandfold; convert at the data-listing stage. Trap two: mass ratios treated as atom ratios — 40 percent carbon does not mean 40 percent of the atoms are carbon; only moles count atoms. Trap three: rounding a 1,5 in the ratio instead of doubling everything. Trap four: subscripts ignored in molar masses — the two in H2O and the five in 5H2O are multiplication instructions.

Where this leads: concentration is the working language of titrations and acid-base chemistry in Grade 11 and matric, and the empirical formula method returns in organic chemistry, where percentages from combustion identify unknown compounds. The moles-first habit you drilled today is the entire foundation.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the 36,1 percent of water locked in copper sulfate, the four traps, and the method steps in order. Answer them as method rather than memory.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same two tools from the kitchen and the spaza counter — cooldrink strength, and a recipe recovered from its label.

## Subtopic: How Strong Is the Cooldrink

Think about mixing concentrate with water. Everybody already understands concentration; nobody confuses strong cooldrink with weak. Strong means plenty of concentrate in the jug; weak means the same concentrate lost in a bigger jug. Chemistry's concentration is exactly that idea with the fuzziness removed: count what is dissolved in moles, measure the jug in cubic decimetres — a cubic decimetre is just a litre — and divide. The concentration is the moles divided by the volume: c equals n over V.

Our problem in jug language: 8 grams of sodium hydroxide in half a litre of solution. First count the packs, because chemistry counts in moles. One mole of NaOH weighs 40 grams — sodium 23, oxygen 16, hydrogen 1, read straight off the periodic table. So 8 grams is 8 divided by 40 — 0,2 moles. The jug: 500 cubic centimetres is half a cubic decimetre; divide cubic centimetres by a thousand, always. Now divide: 0,2 moles in 0,5 cubic decimetres is 0,4 moles per cubic decimetre.

Say it as cooldrink: this solution holds 0,4 moles in every full litre. Two jugs of different sizes can hold the same total sugar but taste completely different — concentration is about the crowding, not the total. That is why the volume must sit under the division line.

Your questions for this section are ready now. Hold two things: moles first, grams are never divided by litres directly; and cubic centimetres shrink by a thousand before they enter the formula. Get those two conversions and the formula is one division.

## Subtopic: Reading the Recipe Backwards

A recipe tells you the ingredients and you produce the dish. The empirical formula problem is the reverse: you are holding the finished dish and the label only says what percentage of the mass is flour, what percentage sugar, what percentage butter — and you must recover the recipe.

Here is the trick that makes it easy: pretend you have exactly 100 grams of the dish. Nothing is lost by pretending, because a recipe is a ratio — double the dish, same recipe. With 100 grams, the label's percentages turn straight into masses: 40,0 grams of carbon, 6,7 grams of hydrogen, 53,3 grams of oxygen.

But a recipe for atoms is counted in atoms, not weighed in grams — a bag of flour and a teaspoon of yeast can matter equally. So convert each mass into moles, chemistry's counting unit: carbon, 40,0 divided by 12, gives 3,33. Hydrogen, 6,7 divided by 1, gives 6,7. Oxygen, 53,3 divided by 16, gives 3,33.

Now simplify the ratio the way you simplify any recipe — divide everything by the smallest number, 3,33. Carbon becomes 1, hydrogen becomes 2, oxygen becomes 1. One carbon, two hydrogens, one oxygen: CH2O. The recipe is recovered.

The questions for this section are up now. Work them in the same three moves: pretend 100 grams, weigh out each ingredient in moles, then divide by the smallest. And if the ratio hands you one and a half of something, double the whole recipe — half atoms are not on the menu.

## Subtopic: The Water Hiding in Dry Crystals

Last piece: a fact that surprises almost everyone. Blue copper sulfate crystals — dry to the touch, pourable, stored in a jar — are more than a third water by mass. The water is built into the crystal itself, five water molecules escorting every CuSO4, and the formula says so: CuSO4 dot 5H2O. Heat the crystals gently and you can watch the truth: the blue fades to white powder, the test tube mists up, and the sample gets lighter on the balance by exactly the water's share.

How big is that share? Price the formula like an invoice. CuSO4: copper 63,5, sulfur 32, four oxygens 64 — total 159,5 grams per mole. The five waters: five times 18 is 90 grams. Whole crystal: 249,5 grams per mole. Water's slice: 90 out of 249,5, times 100 — 36,1 percent. The balance in the laboratory agrees with the arithmetic on paper, and that agreement is the whole point of percentage composition: formulas make predictions a scale can check.

Keep the two habits that protect everything in this lesson. Moles before anything — grams never enter a ratio and never sit over a volume without becoming moles first. And subscripts are multipliers — the two in H2O, the four in CuSO4, the five in 5H2O each command a multiplication, and skipping one poisons every later line.

The last questions of the lesson are with you now, and you are ready for them. Answer with the invoice picture in mind: read the formula atom by atom, price each atom from the periodic table, add carefully, and let the percentages confess what the compound is made of.
