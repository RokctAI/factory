**Thandi:** The questions that come up most on this topic, and the first is pure disbelief: how can 44 grams of carbon dioxide and 2 grams of hydrogen truly fill the same space? It feels impossible.

**Tutor:** It feels impossible because we imagine gases as packed crowds, and they are nothing of the sort. A gas is overwhelmingly empty space — the molecules themselves occupy a vanishingly small fraction of the volume, something like a few flies in a school hall. What fixes the volume is not the size of the flies but the size of the hall: how far apart the molecules sit, which temperature and pressure control. At the same temperature, heavy and light molecules carry the same average energy of motion and batter the walls to the same pressure — the heavy ones move slower, the light ones faster, and the effects balance exactly. So the carbon dioxide molecule being twenty-two times the mass of the hydrogen molecule is simply irrelevant to the space question: same count of molecules, same conditions, same spacing, same volume. That is Avogadro's insight, and it was radical precisely because it ignores everything about a gas except how many particles it has. Once the identity drops out, one constant serves every gas — and at STP that constant is 22,4 cubic decimetres per mole.

***

**Thandi:** Next, the licence question: people keep using 22,4 the moment they see a gas, anywhere in any problem. When exactly is it allowed?

**Tutor:** Two stamps must both be on the licence, and it expires the moment either is missing. Stamp one: the substance must actually be a gas in the reaction as written — look for the g in brackets. The constant describes molecules separated by huge empty gaps; in liquids and solids the particles touch, so each substance has its own cramped volume, and one mole of liquid water occupies about eighteen cubic centimetres — more than a thousand times less than 22,4 cubic decimetres. Apply the gas constant to a liquid and the answer is not slightly off; it is absurd. Stamp two: the conditions must be STP — zero degrees Celsius and 101,3 kilopascals — because 22,4 is the measured spacing at exactly those conditions. Warm the gas and it spreads; compress it and it shrinks; Grade 11 gives you the ideal gas equation to price any conditions you like. Until then, the discipline is to read the question for the words at STP or at standard temperature and pressure, and if they are absent, either the data for another route is present or the question expects you to state the STP assumption. What is never licensed is silent use.

***

**Thandi:** Then the trick question that catches someone every year: forty litres of gas went into the ammonia reaction and only twenty came out. Nothing leaked — so what happened to the missing volume?

**Tutor:** Nothing is missing, because volume was never the thing being conserved. Count the molecules instead. One nitrogen molecule and three hydrogen molecules — four particles — bond into two ammonia molecules: every atom present and accounted for, but packaged into half as many particles. Now remember what sets a gas's volume: the number of particles at a given spacing. Same conditions, half the particles, half the volume — forty litres becomes twenty with no leak and no loss. Check the mass and the books balance perfectly: twenty-eight grams of nitrogen plus six grams of hydrogen make thirty-four grams of ammonia, gram for gram. So keep the two ledgers separate in your head. The mass ledger always balances, because atoms are indestructible. The volume ledger follows the molecule count, and reactions are free to change that count — combining molecules shrinks the gas volume, splitting them grows it. When an exam asks where the volume went, the answer is one sentence: into fewer molecules.

***

**Thandi:** Last one, very practical: a question gives gas volumes on both sides and someone converts everything to moles, through 22,4, and back again. It worked, but is all that labour necessary?

**Tutor:** It is not, and seeing why is a small piece of elegance. Suppose the question says ten cubic decimetres of nitrogen and asks for the hydrogen required. The long road: divide ten by 22,4 to get 0,446 moles of nitrogen, multiply by three for the ratio to get 1,339 moles of hydrogen, multiply by 22,4 to get thirty cubic decimetres. Watch what the 22,4 did — it entered as a division and left as a multiplication, cancelling itself completely. It always will: when volumes are given and volumes are wanted, at shared conditions, the crate size cancels out of the ratio, and only the coefficients survive. So read the recipe directly: one to three means ten cubic decimetres wants thirty. The long road is not wrong — it lands on the same answer, and that agreement is a fine check when you have doubts — but the direct volume ratio saves three calculations and two rounding hazards. The skill being examined is recognising which situation you are in: grams anywhere in the problem force the journey through moles; volumes end to end, at the same conditions, let the coefficients answer immediately.
