**Thandi:** The questions that come up again and again on this topic, starting with the big one: why all the fuss about moles? Fifty grams went in — why can we not just use grams the whole way?

**Tutor:** Because the equation's promise is a count, and grams are not a count. The coefficients say one PARTICLE of calcium carbonate yields one PARTICLE of calcium oxide — scaled up, one mole for one mole. But a mole of carbonate weighs 100 grams and a mole of oxide weighs 56, so equal counts weigh differently, and a gram-for-gram reading is simply false: 50 grams in gives 28 grams out, not 50. Try the gram shortcut on a reaction with mixed coefficients and it collapses even faster — in 2H2 plus O2 gives 2H2O, four grams of hydrogen consume thirty-two grams of oxygen; read the coefficients as masses and you would expect two grams of hydrogen per one of oxygen, wrong by a factor of sixteen. Moles are the only currency the equation accepts. So the journey is fixed: change grams into moles at the border, cross using the coefficient ratio, change back on the far side. Two currency exchanges, one crossing — and the exchange rate at each border is that substance's own molar mass, never the other one's.

***

**Thandi:** Next: several learners always divide the wrong way in percentage yield and end up above one hundred. Is there a way to remember which number goes on top?

**Tutor:** Anchor it to the meaning rather than the formula: percentage yield asks how much of the promise was kept. The promise — the theoretical yield — is the standard you measure against, and the standard always sits underneath, the way a test mark puts your score over the total, never the total over your score. Here the laboratory kept 25,2 grams of a 28 gram promise: 25,2 over 28 is 0,9 — ninety percent of the promise kept. Invert it and you get 111 percent, and that number itself is the built-in alarm: a real yield can approach one hundred percent but never legitimately exceed it, because the atoms in 50 grams of carbonate can build 28 grams of oxide and not one gram more. So make the check part of the method: after computing any yield, ask whether it sits between zero and one hundred. Above one hundred means either the fraction is upside down, or — if the arithmetic is genuinely right — the weighed product is carrying stowaways: moisture it absorbed, or unreacted carbonate mixed through the powder.

***

**Thandi:** Then a sharp one: if the theoretical yield is never achieved, why calculate it at all? It seems like a number about a world that does not exist.

**Tutor:** It is a ceiling, and ceilings are among the most useful numbers in science precisely because reality lives underneath them. Three jobs, all real. First, it is the honest ruler for judging technique: 25,2 grams means nothing on its own, but against the 28 gram ceiling it becomes ninety percent — a good day's work. The same 25,2 against a 50 gram ceiling would be a disastrous fifty percent; the ceiling is what gives the actual number its meaning. Second, it is the fraud detector: any claim above the ceiling is instantly known to be error or contamination, no investigation needed, because the atoms cannot do it. Third, it is the planning tool: an industrial plant that needs a tonne of product a day, running at a known ninety percent yield, must load the reactors for a ceiling of one comma one tonnes — you divide the target by the yield to size the batch. Chemists calculate the perfect world not because they expect it, but because every real measurement only makes sense as a fraction of it.

***

**Thandi:** Last one, asked every single year: the CO2 just disappears into the air, so how can we claim the mass still balances? Nobody weighed the gas.

**Tutor:** Nobody weighed it directly — but the balanced equation weighs it for us, and history is on the equation's side. The reaction promises 0,5 moles of carbon dioxide alongside the 0,5 moles of calcium oxide, and 0,5 moles at 44 grams per mole is 22 grams. Add the products: 28 grams of solid plus 22 grams of gas is 50 grams — precisely the chalk we started with. The gas looks like disappearance only because air is invisible; run the same decomposition in a sealed container on a balance and the reading never moves, which is essentially the experiment that convinced chemists of conservation of mass in the first place. Early chemists burning and heating things in open vessels kept finding masses that would not balance, until careful sealed-vessel work showed the missing mass had simply left as gas. That is worth keeping as an exam instinct: whenever a reaction seems to lose mass, hunt for the gaseous product — the equation names it, its molar mass prices it, and the books always balance once the gas is counted.
