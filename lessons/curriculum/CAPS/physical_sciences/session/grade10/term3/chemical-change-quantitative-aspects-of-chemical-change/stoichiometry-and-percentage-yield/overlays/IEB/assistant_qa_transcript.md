**Thandi:** The lesson kept saying grams can never cross the equation directly, but the ratio here is one to one. If one mole gives one mole, why doesn't 42 grams just give 42 grams?

**Tutor:** Because the two substances charge different prices per mole. One mole of magnesium carbonate weighs 84 grams, but one mole of magnesium oxide weighs only 40 grams. The equation counts particles, not mass — so equal counts on each side still means unequal masses. That's why 42 grams in becomes 0,5 moles, crosses as 0,5 moles, and exits as 0,5 times 40, which is 20 grams. The one-to-one promise held perfectly — in moles. If a one-to-one mole ratio also meant one-to-one in grams, mass conservation would actually break, because the carbon dioxide leaving would then come from nowhere.

***

**Thandi:** Suppose I heated 21 grams of magnesium carbonate instead of 42. Can I just halve everything, or do I have to run the whole three-leg journey again?

**Tutor:** Both work, and it's worth seeing why. The full journey: 21 divided by 84 is 0,25 moles; one-to-one gives 0,25 moles of oxide; 0,25 times 40 is 10 grams. The shortcut: every leg of the journey is proportional, so half the input mass gives half the output — half of 20 grams is 10 grams. Same answer. The shortcut is safe only when nothing else changes: same reaction, same substances, only the amount scaled. The moment a question changes the substance or asks for a different product, go back to the three legs. In your working, show the legs anyway — the method earns the marks even when a proportion would find the number.

***

**Thandi:** My friend got 118 percent yield in a practice problem and thought she'd done brilliantly. I know it's wrong, but how do I tell whether it's an inverted fraction or a real experimental fault?

**Tutor:** Look at where the number came from. If it appeared on paper only — a calculation from given data — check the fraction first: dividing the theoretical 20 by the actual 17 gives exactly 118 percent, so an answer of 118 percent from those numbers is almost certainly the fraction upside down. Actual goes on top, always. But if the number came from a real balance in a real experiment, the fault is physical: the product was still wet, or undecomposed carbonate was mixed through it, so the balance weighed extra material that isn't magnesium oxide. Either way the diagnosis starts the same: more product than the atoms allow is impossible, so something else is inflating the number.

***

**Thandi:** The conservation check felt like magic — 20 plus 22 equalling 42 exactly. Will it always work out that neatly, and what should I do if my numbers don't add up?

**Tutor:** It will always work out exactly, and that's the point — it's a law, not a coincidence. Every atom in the 42 grams of carbonate ends up either in the solid oxide or in the escaping gas, so the two product masses must rebuild the starting mass to the last decimal. If your totals disagree, trust the law and hunt the arithmetic: the usual culprits are a molar mass with a subscript ignored — the three oxygens contributing 48 grams, not 16 — or a slip in one of the three legs. Run the check on every decomposition problem; it costs one addition and catches errors before they cost you anything.
