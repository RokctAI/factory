**Thandi:** Class questions before we close! The first one is pure disbelief: how can 2 grams of hydrogen and 44 grams of carbon dioxide really fill the same space? It sounds like a trick.

**Tutor:** It sounds like a trick because we picture gases as if they were liquids — substance filling space wall to wall. They are nothing like that. In a gas at ordinary conditions, the molecules themselves occupy a vanishing fraction of the volume; the rest is emptiness. Picture a school hall with a dozen flies in it: whether the flies are houseflies or fat bumblebees, the hall is the same size, because the hall's size was never about the flies — it is about the space between them. Temperature and pressure set that spacing: temperature fixes how energetically the molecules zip about, pressure fixes how tightly the outside world squeezes them, and the truce between those two dictates the average gap. The molecules' own mass and girth are irrelevant to the gap. That is Avogadro's insight: equal volumes at the same temperature and pressure hold equal counts. So a mole of anything gaseous — feathery hydrogen, hefty carbon dioxide — takes 22,4 cubic decimetres at STP. The masses differ by a factor of twenty-two; the count, and therefore the crate, is identical.

***

**Thandi:** Next: why does the 22,4 only work at STP? What actually breaks if the lab is at 25 degrees?

**Tutor:** Nothing breaks violently — the number simply stops being 22,4, and that matters when marks ride on it. The 22,4 is not a law of nature; it is a price quoted under specific trading conditions: 273 kelvin and 101,3 kilopascals. Warm the gas and the molecules push apart — at 25 degrees Celsius, one mole occupies roughly 24 and a half cubic decimetres, about ten percent more. Squeeze it at higher pressure and the volume shrinks below 22,4. So using 22,4 in a question set at room conditions builds a ten percent error into your answer before you have made a single arithmetic slip. The discipline is to read the question's conditions the way you read the word excess: if it says STP, the 22,4 licence is granted; if it names other conditions, Grade 11's ideal gas equation will be the tool, and at Grade 10 the question will supply whatever conversion you need; if it says nothing, state your assumption in writing — "assuming STP" — and continue. One more comfort: the volume RATIO shortcut needs no licence at all, because comparing two gases at the same shared conditions cancels the crate size out completely.

***

**Thandi:** Then the practical one: in the zinc problem, how do we actually know the acid is in excess — and what would change if the question flipped it around and made the zinc the excess one?

**Tutor:** In Grade 10 the question tells you, with the word excess or the word plenty, and your job is to hear the instruction inside it: the excess substance is off the books, and the OTHER reactant — the one that will run out — controls every amount. That runner-out has a name you will use formally next year: the limiting reagent. It limits because chemistry stops the instant one ingredient is finished, exactly as pancake production stops at the last egg no matter how much flour remains. Now flip the problem as you suggest: zinc in excess, and, say, 0,3 moles of hydrochloric acid supplied. Then the acid runs the books, and the equation's coefficients matter freshly: two HCl are consumed for every one H2 produced, so 0,3 moles of acid delivers 0,15 moles of hydrogen — 3,36 cubic decimetres at STP — and some zinc lies unreacted at the bottom of the flask. Notice the ratio changed from the one-to-one we used for zinc: crossing the equation always uses the coefficients of the two species involved. So the drill is unchanged in shape: identify who runs out, convert THAT one to moles, cross with the right coefficients, and exit through volume or mass as asked.

***

**Thandi:** Last one: someone answered a volume question with "44,8 cubic decimetres of water vapour condensed into 36 cubic centimetres" and is now thoroughly confused about when water counts as a gas.

**Tutor:** That confusion is actually the lesson working — they have discovered that state symbols carry real cargo. Water is the shape-shifter of chemistry equations: as steam, marked with a g, it is a genuine gas and claims its full place in gas arithmetic — two moles of steam at STP-like conditions would indeed be sized in tens of cubic decimetres. As liquid water, marked with an l, it abandons all gas privileges: the molecules touch, the emptiness is gone, and one mole huddles into about 18 cubic centimetres — a thousand-fold collapse. So the arithmetic your classmate did is not wrong physics; it is two different states priced correctly, and the confusion is only about WHICH state the equation declares. The rule to write on your hand: read the state symbols before touching any volume tool. Species marked g may use 22,4 at STP and may join volume ratios; anything marked l or s contributes no gas volume at all and simply sits out of that part of the calculation. In combustion equations especially, check whether the water is written as gas or liquid — the question decides, and your volume total must follow its decision, not your assumption.
