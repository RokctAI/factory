**Thandi:** Time for the questions that come up over and over on this topic. The first one every year: why divide cubic centimetres by one thousand? Where does the thousand come from?

**Tutor:** Picture the two containers. A cubic decimetre is a cube ten centimetres along each edge — the size of a small juice carton, and exactly one litre. A cubic centimetre is a cube one centimetre along each edge — a dice. How many dice pack into the carton? Ten rows of ten in each layer is one hundred, and ten layers of those is one thousand. So one cubic decimetre holds exactly one thousand cubic centimetres, and converting is just repackaging: 500 cubic centimetres is 500 thousandths of a cubic decimetre — 0,5. The formula c equals n over V is written for cubic decimetres, so the conversion must happen before the division. And feel the size of the error if it does not: dividing 0,2 moles by 500 instead of by 0,5 gives 0,0004 — a thousand times too small, an absurdly weak solution. That absurdity is your alarm bell. A concentration far below anything sensible usually means the volume walked into the formula still wearing cubic centimetres.

***

**Thandi:** Next, and this one causes real confusion: what is the difference between an empirical formula and a molecular formula? Are they not the same thing?

**Tutor:** They answer two different questions about the same compound. The empirical formula gives the simplest whole-number ratio of atoms — the recipe reduced as far as it will reduce. The molecular formula gives the actual number of each atom in one molecule. Sometimes they coincide: water really is H2O. But take our compound from the lesson, CH2O, with a formula mass of 12 plus 2 plus 16 — 30 grams per mole. Formaldehyde is CH2O exactly. Glucose, though, has the molecular formula C6H12O6 — six carbons, twelve hydrogens, six oxygens — and its ratio 6 to 12 to 6 simplifies to 1 to 2 to 1, the very same empirical formula. Percentage composition alone cannot tell formaldehyde from glucose, because both are built on the same ratio. To find the molecular formula you need one extra fact: the molar mass. Glucose weighs 180 grams per mole, and 180 divided by the empirical mass of 30 is 6 — so multiply every subscript in CH2O by six. Ratio from percentages, scale from molar mass: two facts, two formulas.

**Thandi:** Then this one, which trips people in the exam: the ratio came out as 1 to 1,51 and someone rounded it to 1 to 1 and a half marks vanished. What was the right move?

**Tutor:** The right move is to respect the half. A ratio of 1 to 1,5 is the fraction three halves in disguise, and atoms only combine in whole numbers — so multiply both sides by two and the true ratio is 2 to 3. Here is a real case. Iron oxide: 69,9 percent iron, 30,1 percent oxygen. Moles: 69,9 divided by 56 is 1,25 for iron; 30,1 divided by 16 is 1,88 for oxygen. Divide by the smaller: iron 1, oxygen 1,5. Round that 1,5 down and you invent FeO — a genuinely different compound that happens to exist, which is why the error is so dangerous. Double instead: 2 to 3, giving Fe2O3, the rust-red oxide the percentages actually describe. The discipline is this: after dividing by the smallest, treat anything near a simple fraction — 1,5, 1,33, 1,25 — as that fraction, and multiply the whole ratio by 2, 3 or 4 until everything is whole. Only genuine near-integers, like the 2,01 in our lesson, may be rounded, because they carry only rounding dust from earlier steps.

**Thandi:** Last one: pretending the sample is 100 grams feels like cheating. Why is chemistry allowed to just invent a sample size?

**Tutor:** Because the question being asked is a ratio, and ratios are immune to sample size. Think of a recipe: two cups of flour to one cup of sugar makes the same biscuits whether you bake one tray or ten — the amounts change, the proportions do not. A compound is the strictest recipe in nature: every sample of pure CH2O, from a milligram to a tonne, carries carbon, hydrogen and oxygen in exactly the same mass proportions. That is the law of constant composition from earlier this year. So when the analysis says 40 percent carbon, any imagined sample tells the same story — but 100 grams tells it with no arithmetic: 40 percent of 100 grams is simply 40 grams. Choose 250 grams instead and you get 100 grams of carbon, 16,75 of hydrogen, 133,25 of oxygen — every mole value scales by two and a half, and the moment you divide by the smallest, the scaling cancels and the same 1 to 2 to 1 emerges. The 100 grams is not an assumption about the world; it is a choice of convenience the mathematics immediately forgets.
