# Part 1 — Expert

This session builds stoichiometry — the art of predicting exactly how much product a reaction can deliver — and percentage yield, the honest comparison between that prediction and what the laboratory actually collects. One exam problem carries everything: 50 grams of calcium carbonate is heated strongly and decomposes into calcium oxide and carbon dioxide. Calculate the maximum mass of calcium oxide that can form; then, given that a learner actually collects 25,2 grams, calculate the percentage yield. Prediction first, honesty second.

## Subtopic: The Balanced Equation Is a Mole Recipe

Write the reaction: CaCO3, heated, gives CaO plus CO2 — calcium carbonate decomposes into calcium oxide and carbon dioxide. Check the balance: one calcium on each side, one carbon on each side, three oxygens on each side. Balanced as written, with every coefficient equal to one.

Now the idea the whole session stands on: the coefficients of a balanced equation are a MOLE ratio. This equation says one mole of calcium carbonate produces one mole of calcium oxide and one mole of carbon dioxide. It does not say one gram gives one gram — mass ratios are never read from coefficients, because a mole of each substance weighs a different amount. The equation is a recipe written in moles, and every stoichiometric calculation is a journey through that recipe.

The journey always has the same three legs. Leg one: convert the given mass to moles — n equals m over M. Leg two: cross the equation using the mole ratio — multiply by the ratio of coefficients. Leg three: convert the answer back from moles to whatever the question wants — mass, particles, or gas volume. Mass to moles, ratio across, moles to mass. Grams never jump the equation directly; they must change into moles at the border and change back on the other side.

The questions for this section are with you now. They examine what the coefficients do and do not promise, the three legs of the journey in order, and why a mass may never cross the equation without becoming moles first.

## Subtopic: The Prediction — 50 Grams In, How Much Out

Leg one: the given mass to moles. Molar mass of CaCO3, atom by atom: calcium 40, carbon 12, three oxygens at 16 makes 48 — total 100 grams per mole. A convenient number, and the arithmetic thanks us: n equals m over M — 50 grams divided by 100 grams per mole is 0,5 moles of calcium carbonate.

Leg two: cross the equation. The ratio of CaCO3 to CaO is one to one — so 0,5 moles of calcium carbonate yields 0,5 moles of calcium oxide. This leg looks trivial today precisely because the coefficients are ones; tomorrow's equations will multiply by two thirds or three halves here, and the habit of writing the leg out will earn its keep.

Leg three: moles back to mass. Molar mass of CaO: calcium 40 plus oxygen 16 — 56 grams per mole. Mass equals n times M: 0,5 moles times 56 grams per mole — 28 grams. The maximum possible mass of calcium oxide from 50 grams of calcium carbonate is 28 grams. This is the theoretical yield.

Now the elegant check, and it costs nothing: conservation of mass. The carbon dioxide escapes as gas — 0,5 moles at 44 grams per mole is 22 grams. Products together: 28 plus 22 is 50 grams — exactly the mass we started with. Atoms are never created or destroyed; if your product masses refuse to add up to the reactant mass in a decomposition, an error is hiding somewhere upstream.

Take this section's questions now. They follow the three legs: the 0,5 moles at the border, the one-to-one crossing, the 28 grams of theoretical yield, and the conservation check that ties 28 plus 22 back to 50.

## Subtopic: Percentage Yield — the Honest Number

The learner heats the 50 grams, collects the white solid, weighs it: 25,2 grams. Not 28. The laboratory has fallen short of the prediction — and it almost always does.

Percentage yield measures the shortfall: the actual yield divided by the theoretical yield, times one hundred. Substitute: 25,2 divided by 28, times 100. Compute: 25,2 over 28 is 0,9. The percentage yield is 90 percent.

Why is it not one hundred? For honest, physical reasons. The reaction may be incomplete — some calcium carbonate in the middle of the lumps never got hot enough to decompose. Product gets lost in transfers — dust left in the crucible, powder clinging to the spatula. Side reactions may consume reactant. And sometimes the reverse reaction claws product back. Every laboratory chemist plans around yields below one hundred percent; industrial chemists fight for every percentage point, because at factory scale one percent is tonnes.

Now the two directions of suspicion. A yield of 90 percent is credible. A yield of 40 percent says the technique leaked product everywhere. And a yield ABOVE one hundred percent is not good news — it is an announcement that something is wrong: the product is wet, or contaminated with unreacted material, or the weighing is faulty. More out than the atoms allow is impossible; the balance is measuring something extra, not the reaction overachieving.

This section's questions are in front of you. They examine the formula — actual over theoretical, times one hundred — the 90 percent answer, the physical reasons yields fall short, and the correct diagnosis of a yield over one hundred.

## Subtopic: The Full Method, the Classic Traps, and Where This Leads

The complete method for every stoichiometry problem, five steps. Step one: write and balance the equation — an unbalanced equation poisons every number after it. Step two: convert the given quantity to moles. Step three: cross the equation with the mole ratio, coefficients of wanted over given. Step four: convert to the units the question demands. Step five: if an actual yield is given, close with percentage yield — actual over theoretical, times one hundred — and sense-check that it sits below one hundred.

The traps, in the order markers meet them. Trap one: skipping the balance check — with a wrongly balanced equation the mole ratio is fiction. Trap two: reading coefficients as masses — one to one in moles is 100 to 56 in grams for this reaction; the two ratios agree only by pure accident. Trap three: molar masses with ignored subscripts — the three oxygens in CaCO3 contribute 48 grams, not 16. Trap four: dividing theoretical by actual — a yield of 111 percent from that inversion should trigger the over-one-hundred alarm instantly. Trap five: forgetting that the theoretical yield is a ceiling, not a promise — exam phrases like maximum mass and at most are stoichiometry's fingerprints.

Where this leads: everywhere. Grade 11 adds the limiting reagent — two given quantities racing to run out first — and volume relationships for gases. Matric runs titrations, electrolysis and industrial chemistry on this exact three-leg journey. The Chemical Change section is the largest block of marks in the matric paper, and this method is its engine room. Master the journey now and every later topic is this lesson wearing new substances.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the five steps in order, the traps — especially coefficients misread as masses — and the meaning of theoretical yield as a ceiling. Answer them as method, not memory.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same calculation from a bakery — a recipe, a batch, and the rolls that actually reach the shelf.

## Subtopic: A Recipe Promises, in Its Own Units

A bread recipe says: one batch of dough gives one tray of rolls. That sentence is a ratio — batches to trays — and notice what it does not say: it does not promise that a kilogram of dough gives a kilogram of rolls. Dough loses steam in the oven; the units of the promise are batches and trays, never kilograms.

A balanced chemical equation is exactly that kind of promise. CaCO3 heated gives CaO plus CO2 reads: one mole of calcium carbonate delivers one mole of calcium oxide and releases one mole of carbon dioxide gas. The promise is written in moles — chemistry's batches — and never directly in grams. One mole of the carbonate weighs 100 grams while one mole of the oxide weighs 56, the way one batch of dough and one tray of rolls weigh different amounts. Same count, different masses.

So every question of the type how much comes out follows the same route through the recipe. Turn your grams into moles at the door — divide by the molar mass. Walk across the equation using the promised ratio. Turn the moles back into grams on the way out — multiply by the new substance's molar mass. In through one door, across the recipe, out through the other door — and the two doors have different exchange rates, because the two substances weigh differently per mole.

Your questions for this section are ready. Keep one sentence in your pocket: the equation promises in moles, the balance speaks in grams, and the molar mass translates at each door.

## Subtopic: Fifty Grams of Chalk Through the Bakery

Run the actual problem in bakery language. You carry 50 grams of calcium carbonate — chalk, in effect — to the door. One mole of it costs 100 grams: calcium 40, carbon 12, and three oxygens totalling 48. So 50 grams is 50 over 100 — half a mole. Half a batch of raw material.

Cross the recipe: the equation promises one mole of calcium oxide for every mole of carbonate — one tray per batch. Half a batch in, half a tray out: 0,5 moles of calcium oxide.

Out through the far door: one mole of calcium oxide weighs 56 grams — calcium 40 plus oxygen 16. Half a mole is 28 grams. The oven can deliver at most 28 grams of calcium oxide. That word — most — matters: 28 grams is the ceiling the atoms allow, the theoretical yield.

And where did the other 22 grams go? Not lost — carried away. The recipe also promised half a mole of carbon dioxide, and half of 44 grams is 22 grams of gas that left for the air. Add the two products: 28 plus 22 is 50, every gram of the original chalk accounted for. Atoms never vanish in a reaction; they change address. When your products refuse to add up in a decomposition like this, the mistake is in your arithmetic, not in the atoms.

The questions for this section are up now. Walk each one through the doors in order — grams to moles, ratio across, moles to grams — and let the mass balance be your free final check.

## Subtopic: What Actually Reaches the Shelf

The recipe promised a full tray, and every baker knows what happens next: a roll sticks to the pan, one burns at the edge, the corner of the tray never rose properly. The shelf receives less than the promise. Chemistry is the same: our learner weighed the collected calcium oxide and found 25,2 grams against a promised 28.

Percentage yield is the shelf count: what you actually got, divided by what the recipe promised, times one hundred. Here: 25,2 over 28 is 0,9 — a 90 percent yield. Nine tenths of the promise reached the shelf, and in a school laboratory that is genuinely good baking.

The shortfall has ordinary causes, worth naming because exams ask for them. Some chalk in the middle of the lumps never got hot enough — an unfinished bake. Some powder stayed behind on the crucible and spatula — rolls stuck to the pan. And in other reactions, side reactions nibble at the ingredients — dough pinched before it reaches the oven.

Now the diagnosis that catches the most people: a yield over one hundred percent. It sounds like triumph — more product than promised! It is always a fault. The atoms in 50 grams of chalk can build 28 grams of calcium oxide and not one gram more; if the balance claims more, the balance is weighing something extra — water the product soaked up, or chalk that never decomposed sitting mixed into the powder. Extra mass on the shelf means a contaminated tray, never a generous oven.

The last questions of the lesson are with you now, and you are ready for them. Answer with the bakery in your head: the recipe promises in batches, the ceiling is set by the atoms, and the shelf count over the promise — times one hundred — is the honest measure of the day's work.
