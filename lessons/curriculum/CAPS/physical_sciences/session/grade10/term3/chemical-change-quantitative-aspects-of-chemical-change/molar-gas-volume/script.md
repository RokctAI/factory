# Part 1 — Expert

This session puts the molar gas volume to work inside stoichiometry — measuring chemistry not with a balance but with the space a gas fills. One exam problem drives everything: 10 grams of calcium carbonate reacts completely with excess hydrochloric acid, and we must calculate the volume of carbon dioxide gas produced at STP. Alongside it runs a second skill the examiners love because it needs almost no arithmetic at all: reading gas volumes straight off a balanced equation.

## Subtopic: Avogadro's Law and the 22,4 That Fits Every Gas

Start with the astonishing fact underneath the whole session. Take one mole of carbon dioxide — 44 grams. Take one mole of hydrogen — just 2 grams. Hold both at the same temperature and pressure. They occupy exactly the same volume.

That is Avogadro's law: equal volumes of gases, at the same temperature and pressure, contain equal numbers of molecules. The identity of the gas is irrelevant. The reason lies in emptiness: a gas is almost entirely space, its molecules separated by distances vastly larger than the molecules themselves. What sets the volume is how far apart the molecules sit — and temperature and pressure decide that, not the size or mass of each molecule. Heavy or light, fat or thin, the spacing is the same.

Fix the conditions and the shared volume becomes a usable constant. At STP — standard temperature and pressure, zero degrees Celsius, which is 273 kelvin, and 101,3 kilopascals — one mole of ANY gas occupies 22,4 cubic decimetres. That is the molar gas volume, symbol V m, and a cubic decimetre is the same thing as a litre. The working formula, read as a sentence: V equals n times V m — the volume of a gas equals the number of moles times 22,4 cubic decimetres per mole, at STP. And inward: n equals V over 22,4 — from litres of gas to moles in one division.

Guard the licence conditions from the start. The 22,4 belongs to gases only — never liquids or solids, whose particles touch. And it belongs to STP only — warm the gas or squeeze it and the spacing changes, which is Grade 11's business.

The questions for this section are with you now. They examine Avogadro's law and its emptiness argument, the STP conditions by name and number, and the formula V equals n times V m read in both directions.

## Subtopic: From 10 Grams of Solid to Litres of Gas

Now the exam problem. The reaction: CaCO3 plus 2HCl gives CaCl2 plus H2O plus CO2 — calcium carbonate plus hydrochloric acid gives calcium chloride, water and carbon dioxide. Check the balance: one calcium each side; one carbon each side; three oxygens each side; two hydrogens each side; two chlorines each side. Balanced.

The word excess matters before any arithmetic: the acid is supplied in excess, meaning more than enough — so ALL 10 grams of the carbonate reacts, and the carbonate alone controls the amounts. When one reactant is in excess, base every calculation on the other one.

Now the familiar three-leg journey, with a new final leg. Leg one, mass to moles: molar mass of CaCO3 is 40 plus 12 plus 48 — 100 grams per mole; n equals 10 over 100 — 0,1 moles of calcium carbonate. Leg two, cross the equation: the ratio of CaCO3 to CO2 is one to one, so 0,1 moles of carbon dioxide. Leg three — and here is the new move — moles to VOLUME, not to mass: V equals n times V m — 0,1 moles times 22,4 cubic decimetres per mole — 2,24 cubic decimetres of carbon dioxide at STP.

Sense-check the size: 2,24 litres is about one large cooldrink bottle of gas, fizzing out of just 10 grams of solid — two teaspoons of powder. Gases are spectacularly spacious; roomy answers are normal. If the question had wanted the mass instead, 0,1 moles times 44 grams per mole gives 4,4 grams — same moles, different exit door.

Take this section's questions now. They walk the legs in order: the role of the word excess, the 0,1 moles at the border, the one-to-one crossing, and the choice of exit — 2,24 cubic decimetres by volume or 4,4 grams by mass.

## Subtopic: Volume Ratios — Stoichiometry Without a Balance

Here is the beautiful shortcut Avogadro's law hands us: for gases at the same temperature and pressure, the mole ratio IS the volume ratio. Equal volumes hold equal molecule counts — so counting volumes counts molecules.

Take the industrial synthesis of ammonia: N2 plus 3H2 gives 2NH3 — one nitrogen plus three hydrogens gives two ammonias. Read the coefficients directly as volumes: one volume of nitrogen consumes three volumes of hydrogen and delivers two volumes of ammonia. So 10 cubic decimetres of nitrogen requires 30 cubic decimetres of hydrogen, and produces 20 cubic decimetres of ammonia — no molar masses, no moles, no 22,4, just the coefficients wearing litres.

Notice something the numbers are quietly saying: 10 plus 30 is 40 cubic decimetres in, but only 20 come out. Gas volumes need not be conserved — molecules are conserved, and four molecules of reactant become two molecules of product here, so the volume halves. Mass is always conserved; volume is not. Keep those two conservation rules separate and a favourite trick question loses its sting.

Two conditions guard the shortcut. All gases compared must be at the SAME temperature and pressure — though not necessarily at STP; the ratio works at any shared conditions, which is exactly why it needs no 22,4. And it applies only to the gases in the equation — a solid or liquid in the reaction has no volume claim; only species marked with a little g in brackets join the ratio.

This section's questions are in front of you. They examine the ratio read straight from coefficients, the 30 and 20 cubic decimetres of the ammonia example, why total gas volume may shrink while mass cannot, and which species are allowed into a volume ratio.

## Subtopic: The Gas Toolkit, the Traps, and Where This Leads

Assemble the toolkit. Tool one: V equals n times V m — moles to litres at STP, and back again by division. Tool two: the three-leg journey with a volume door — mass to moles, ratio across, moles to volume. Tool three: coefficients read directly as volume ratios for gases at shared conditions. Choose by what the question gives: grams given, go through moles; litres of one gas given and litres of another wanted, ride the volume ratio straight across.

Now the traps. Trap one: using 22,4 when the question never said STP — no standard conditions, no licence; state the assumption or use the data given. Trap two: applying 22,4 to a liquid or solid — one mole of liquid water is 18 cubic centimetres, nowhere near 22,4 cubic decimetres; the constant is a gas-only privilege. Trap three: unit slippage between cubic centimetres and cubic decimetres — one thousand cubic centimetres make one cubic decimetre, so 250 cubic centimetres is 0,25 cubic decimetres before it meets any formula. Trap four: ignoring the word excess and building the calculation on the wrong reactant. Trap five: expecting gas volumes to balance like masses — they answer to molecule counts, not to conservation.

Where this leads: directly into Grade 11, where the ideal gas equation replaces the STP shortcut and lets you price a gas at ANY temperature and pressure — 22,4 is the special case you will then understand from the inside. Volume relationships power the industrial chemistry of matric: ammonia plants, sulfuric acid plants, all designed around gas ratios. And in the laboratory next week, collecting gas over water and measuring it in a syringe, this is the arithmetic that tells you whether your experiment worked.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the three tools matched to question types, the five traps — STP licence, gas-only, units, excess, and false volume conservation — and the 18 cubic centimetres that expose the liquid-water error. Answer them as a toolkit inspection, tool by tool.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same chemistry with crates and taxis — same-size crates for every cargo, and a fizzing spoonful of chalk.

## Subtopic: Same Crate, Whatever the Cargo

Picture a courier company with one strict rule: every crate is the same size, no matter what goes inside. A crate of feathers, a crate of bricks — wildly different masses, identical crates, because the company packs by count: one thousand items per crate, always.

Gases run on exactly that rule. At the same temperature and pressure, equal volumes of any gases hold the same number of molecules — Avogadro's law. The molecules are so far apart that their own size is irrelevant; a gas is mostly nothing, and the nothing is what you measure. Light hydrogen, heavy carbon dioxide: same count, same space.

Fix the depot conditions — zero degrees Celsius and 101,3 kilopascals, the pair called STP — and the crate gets a number: one mole of any gas fills 22,4 cubic decimetres, and a cubic decimetre is just a litre. That is the molar gas volume. One mole of hydrogen: 22,4 litres. One mole of carbon dioxide, twenty-two times heavier: still 22,4 litres. The crate does not care about the cargo.

And the rule works in both directions, like any good packing system. Moles to litres: multiply by 22,4. Litres back to moles: divide by 22,4. Someone tells you they collected 11,2 litres of gas at STP and you know instantly — half a crate — that they hold half a mole, whatever gas it is.

Your questions for this section are ready now. Hold the crate picture: same size for every gas, the size is 22,4 at STP only, and multiplying or dividing by 22,4 is just packing or unpacking crates.

## Subtopic: A Spoonful of Chalk, a Bottle of Gas

Now the exam problem in kitchen terms. Drop 10 grams of chalk — about two teaspoons of calcium carbonate — into plenty of hydrochloric acid, and it fizzes furiously. The question: how much gas is that fizz, measured in litres at STP?

The word plenty is doing real work. The acid is in excess — more than enough — so every crumb of chalk reacts and the chalk alone decides the amounts. Always build the calculation on the ingredient that runs out, not the one swimming in surplus.

Walk the journey. Chalk to moles: one mole of calcium carbonate weighs 100 grams — calcium 40, carbon 12, three oxygens 48 — so 10 grams is one tenth of a mole. Cross the recipe: the balanced equation promises one carbon dioxide for every calcium carbonate — one to one — so one tenth of a mole of gas. Pack the crates: one tenth of 22,4 cubic decimetres is 2,24 cubic decimetres. From two teaspoons of powder, a full two-litre-bottle's worth of gas, and a bit more.

That surprise is worth keeping: solids are cramped, gases are roomy. The same molecules that sat quietly inside 10 grams of powder spread out into more than two litres the moment they become gas. When a gas answer comes out looking spacious, it is usually right; when a gas answer comes out tiny — a few cubic centimetres from grams of solid — suspect the arithmetic.

The questions for this section are up now. Follow the same order every time: check who is in excess, get the solid into moles, cross the equation, and only then bring in the 22,4 — it enters at the last step, never the first.

## Subtopic: Recipes Measured in Litres

Last tool, and it is the laziest one in the best way. When every substance in the question is a gas, and all at the same temperature and pressure, you can skip moles entirely: the equation's coefficients ARE the volume recipe.

Ammonia synthesis: one nitrogen plus three hydrogens gives two ammonias. Read it as a cooldrink mix: one cup of this, three cups of that, makes two cups of the blend. So 10 litres of nitrogen wants 30 litres of hydrogen, and the reward is 20 litres of ammonia. No molar masses, no 22,4, no calculator worth mentioning — the coefficients were the answer all along.

But notice the strange arithmetic on the totals: 40 litres of ingredients became 20 litres of product. Nothing leaked. Molecules are conserved — four reactant molecules really do become two product molecules, and fewer molecules at the same spacing means less volume. Mass always balances; volume follows the molecule count. Hold both sentences and the favourite trick question — where did the volume go? — answers itself: into fewer, bigger molecules.

Two small print lines close the deal. The gases need the SAME conditions as each other, but not necessarily STP — the ratio is about comparing crates, and any shared crate size cancels out. And only gases ride: if the equation includes a solid or a liquid, it contributes no volume to the recipe — read the state symbols and count only the letter g.

The last questions of the lesson are with you now, and you are ready for them. Ask one thing of each question first: is everything here a gas at shared conditions? If yes, read the recipe straight off the coefficients. If no, take the long road through moles — and let 22,4 wait at the exit where it belongs.
