# Part 1 — Expert

This session builds the equations of motion — the four-equation toolkit that describes anything moving with constant acceleration in a straight line — and the rule that selects the right one first time. One exam problem carries the hour: a car accelerates uniformly from 10 metres per second to 25 metres per second over a distance of 300 metres. We must choose the correct equation, calculate the acceleration, and then find the time taken. The choosing is the real skill — and by the end, you will choose in seconds.

## Subtopic: The Cast of Symbols and the Four Equations

Every motion problem speaks in five quantities. Learn them as characters. The initial velocity, v i — how fast the object moves when the clock starts. The final velocity, v f — how fast at the end. The acceleration, a — the rate at which velocity changes, in metres per second squared. The time, delta t — how long the motion lasts. And the displacement, delta x — the straight-line distance covered, direction included.

One condition governs everything today: the acceleration must be UNIFORM — constant. The problem says "accelerates uniformly"; that word is your licence to use these equations at all. No uniform acceleration, no equations of motion — that is why examiners plant the word, and why you should underline it.

Now the four equations. Equation one: v f equals v i plus a delta t — the final velocity equals the initial velocity plus the acceleration times the time. No displacement in it. Equation two: delta x equals v i delta t plus a half a delta t squared — the displacement equals the initial velocity times the time, plus one half times the acceleration times the time squared. No final velocity. Equation three: v f squared equals v i squared plus two a delta x — the final velocity squared equals the initial velocity squared plus twice the acceleration times the displacement. No time. Equation four: delta x equals the sum of v i and v f, divided by two, times delta t — the displacement equals the average of the two velocities times the time. No acceleration.

Notice what I did as I read each one: I named the MISSING character. Each equation is missing exactly one of the five quantities — and that missing character is the entire secret of choosing. Because every problem gives you three quantities and asks for a fourth: the fifth one, the one neither given nor wanted, is the one your equation must NOT contain. Find the absent character; pick the equation that excludes it.

The questions for this section are with you now. They examine the five quantities by name and unit, the licence word — uniform — and the absent-character pattern itself: which single quantity each of the four equations leaves out.

## Subtopic: Choosing the Equation and Finding the Acceleration

Back to the car. Extract the data first, formally — in the exam, this listing earns method marks and prevents chaos.

Given: v i is 10 metres per second. V f is 25 metres per second. Delta x is 300 metres. Wanted: the acceleration, a. Absent — neither given nor wanted: the time, delta t.

Now the choosing rule does its work: we need the equation with no time in it. That is equation three: v f squared equals v i squared plus two a delta x. Chosen not by luck, not by trying each — by the absence of time.

Substitute, carefully, units in place: 25 squared equals 10 squared plus two times a times 300.

Compute each piece: 25 squared is 625. 10 squared is 100. Two times 300 is 600. So: 625 equals 100 plus 600 a.

Solve for a: subtract 100 from both sides — 525 equals 600 a. Divide by 600: a equals 525 over 600, which is 0,875.

The acceleration is 0,875 metres per second squared, in the direction of motion — direction stated, because acceleration is a vector.

Sense-check before moving on, always. Is the answer's sign right? The car sped up, so a positive acceleration in the direction of travel: yes. Is the size believable? A modest car acceleration — just under one metre per second of extra speed each second: entirely reasonable. An answer of 87 or 0,008 would deserve suspicion; 0,875 passes.

Take this section's questions now. They run in the order the method does: identify the absent quantity, select the equation that excludes it, substitute with units, and judge 0,875 metres per second squared as a physically sensible acceleration rather than merely a calculator output.

## Subtopic: Finding the Time — Two Roads to One Answer

Second question: the time taken. Fresh data audit — now we know four quantities: v i is 10 metres per second, v f is 25 metres per second, delta x is 300 metres, and a is 0,875 metres per second squared. Wanted: delta t. With four knowns, several equations can carry us — a luxury we will exploit for verification.

Road one: equation one, v f equals v i plus a delta t — the simplest containing time. Substitute: 25 equals 10 plus 0,875 times delta t. Subtract 10: 15 equals 0,875 delta t. Divide: delta t equals 15 over 0,875 — which is 17,14 seconds, to two decimal places.

Road two — the check: equation four, which never touched our calculated acceleration: delta x equals the average of v i and v f, times delta t. The average of 10 and 25 metres per second is 17,5 metres per second. So 300 equals 17,5 times delta t, giving delta t equals 300 over 17,5 — 17,14 seconds again.

Pause on the power of what just happened. Road two used only ORIGINAL data — if our acceleration had been wrong, road one would have produced a different time from road two, and the disagreement would have exposed the error. Two independent roads agreeing is the strongest verification physics offers you in an exam. When time allows, travel both.

A strategic note: prefer equations built from original given data over ones using your own calculated values, whenever there is a choice. Errors cannot propagate through numbers you never calculated. Here, equation four was available from the start — using it first for time, and equation one as the check, would have been equally valid. Either order; the agreement is what matters.

This section's questions are in front of you. They examine both roads to the same answer for the time, the average velocity of 17,5 metres per second, and the reason a check built from original given data is stronger than one built on your own calculated values.

## Subtopic: The Full Method, the Classic Traps, and Where This Leads

The complete method, five steps, for every equations-of-motion problem. Step one: confirm uniform acceleration — the licence. Step two: list given, wanted, and absent quantities, converting units as you list — kilometres per hour must become metres per second before anything else, by dividing by 3,6. Step three: choose the equation missing the absent quantity. Step four: substitute with units, solve algebraically, state the answer with units AND direction where the quantity is a vector. Step five: sense-check size and sign; verify by a second road if time allows.

Now the traps that fill marking reports. Trap one: units — a car at 90 kilometres per hour entered as 90 metres per second inflates every answer; 90 divided by 3,6 is 25 metres per second. Convert at the data-listing stage, not later. Trap two: squaring only part of equation three — v f squared means 625, not 25; write the squares out immediately. Trap three: sign errors in deceleration — if the car had been braking, a would be negative, and dropping that sign reverses the physics. Choose a positive direction, declare it, and keep every vector's sign consistent. Trap four: grabbing the first equation you remember instead of the one the ABSENT character selects — that habit works only on the luckiest days; the absent-character rule works every day.

Where does this toolkit lead? Directly into the heart of matric physics. Grade 11 uses these equations inside Newton's laws problems — forces cause the accelerations these equations then track. Grade 12 lifts them into vertical projectile motion, where gravity supplies a constant 9,8 metres per second squared downward. Mechanics is the largest section of the matric paper, and these four equations run through all of it. The habits you drilled today — list the data, find the absent character, convert the units, verify by a second road — are the exact habits that matric rewards.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the five steps in order, the conversion of kilometres per hour by dividing by 3,6, the full squaring in equation three, and the sign a deceleration must carry. Answer them as method rather than as memory.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same four equations from the passenger seat of a taxi on a straight road — five facts about a trip, and the one you can ignore.

## Subtopic: Five Facts About Any Trip

Put the symbols aside for a moment. Picture a minibus taxi pulling away from a robot and building up speed along a straight stretch of road. If you had to describe that piece of the trip completely to somebody on the phone, how many facts would you need?

Five — and you already know all five. How fast it was going when you started watching: physics calls that the initial velocity, v i. How fast it was going when you stopped watching: the final velocity, v f. How quickly it was gaining speed: that is the acceleration, a, measured in metres per second squared, which simply means how many metres per second of extra speed it picks up in each second. How long that stretch took: the time, delta t. And how far it travelled: the displacement, delta x.

That is the whole cast — two speeds, a rate of gaining speed, a time and a distance. Every straight-line motion question in Grade 10 is about those five and nothing else.

One condition comes attached, and it is not negotiable: the gaining of speed must be steady, the same extra speed every second. When a question says the car accelerates "uniformly", that word is your permission slip. Crawling in stop-start traffic does not qualify, and neither do these equations.

Now the genuinely useful part. There are four equations, and here is the pattern: each one is missing exactly one of the five facts. Listen to them as sentences. The final speed equals the starting speed plus the acceleration times the time — no distance anywhere in it. The distance equals the starting speed times the time, plus one half times the acceleration times the time squared — no final speed. The final speed squared equals the starting speed squared plus two times the acceleration times the distance — no time. And the distance equals the average of the two speeds times the time — no acceleration.

Your questions for this section are with you now, and they are friendlier than they look. Match each equation to the fact it leaves out, and hold on to the permission word, uniform. Get the five characters straight and everything that follows in this lesson is ordinary arithmetic.

## Subtopic: The One Fact You Don't Need

Here is the trick that makes choosing easy, and it is the opposite of what most people do. Most people hunt for an equation containing what they want. Instead, hunt for the fact that nobody mentioned.

Our problem: a car speeds up smoothly from 10 metres per second to 25 metres per second while covering 300 metres, and we want the acceleration.

Write down what you have, the way you empty your pockets at the door. Starting speed: 10 metres per second. Final speed: 25 metres per second. Distance: 300 metres. Wanted: the acceleration. Now run through the five characters — starting speed, final speed, distance, acceleration, and time. Time is the one nobody mentioned and nobody asked for. So choose the equation with no time in it: the final speed squared equals the starting speed squared plus two times the acceleration times the distance.

Put the numbers in with their units. Twenty-five squared equals ten squared plus two times the acceleration times 300 metres. Twenty-five squared is 625; ten squared is 100; two times 300 is 600. So 625 equals 100 plus 600 times the acceleration. Take 100 off both sides and 525 equals 600 times the acceleration. Divide both sides by 600, and the acceleration is 525 over 600 — 0,875 metres per second squared, pointing the way the car is travelling.

Now say what that number means out loud, because it turns an answer into a picture: every second, the car gains just under one metre per second of speed. A perfectly ordinary pull-away. And that is your free sense check — 87 or 0,008 would have needed investigating, while 0,875 sits comfortably in the real world.

That was a complete exam calculation, chosen and solved, so take the win. The questions for this section are up now: find the fact nobody mentioned, take the equation that leaves it out, substitute with units and solve. And say the direction along with the acceleration when you answer, because acceleration is a vector.

## Subtopic: Checking the Fare Two Ways

Last piece: how long the 300 metres took, and how to know your answer is right before a marker tells you.

The first road uses the acceleration we just found. The final speed equals the starting speed plus the acceleration times the time: 25 equals 10 plus 0,875 times the time. Subtract 10 from both sides: 15 equals 0,875 times the time. Divide by 0,875, and the time is 17,14 seconds, to two decimals.

The second road never touches that calculated acceleration. The car went smoothly from 10 to 25 metres per second, so its average speed for the trip is halfway between the two: 10 plus 25 is 35, and half of 35 is 17,5 metres per second. Distance equals average speed times time, so 300 metres equals 17,5 metres per second times the time, and the time is 300 divided by 17,5 — 17,14 seconds. The same answer, built only from numbers that were in the question from the beginning.

That is the entire point of the second road. If the acceleration had come out wrong, road one would have carried the mistake forward and road two would not, so the two times would have disagreed — a warning you can still act on while you are sitting in the exam. It is the same instinct as counting your change twice: once by adding up what you bought, once by subtracting from the note you handed over. Two counts that agree are worth far more than one count made confidently.

Two habits protect everything else. First, units at the door: a speed arriving in kilometres per hour must become metres per second before it goes anywhere near an equation, and you get there by dividing by 3,6 — 90 kilometres per hour is 90 000 metres in 3 600 seconds, which is 25 metres per second. Feed 90 in as it stands and every answer afterwards is wrong by that same factor. Second, signs: choose the direction of travel as positive, and if the car is braking rather than speeding up, its acceleration is negative — losing that minus sign turns a stopping car into a racing one.

These are the last questions of the lesson, and you are ready for them. Work each one the way you count your change: list what you were given, choose by the fact that is missing, substitute with units, then check by a second road wherever one exists. That is the habit that turns a good mark into a mark you can rely on.
