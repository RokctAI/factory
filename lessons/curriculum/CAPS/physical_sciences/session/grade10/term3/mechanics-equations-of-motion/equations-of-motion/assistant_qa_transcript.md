**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! The first is a groan more than a question: how is anybody supposed to memorise four whole equations? They blur into each other.

**Tutor:** Do not memorise them as four strangers — identify each one by the character it is missing, and they stop blurring immediately. Equation one, the final velocity equals the initial velocity plus the acceleration times the time, has no displacement. Equation two, the displacement equals the initial velocity times the time plus one half the acceleration times the time squared, has no final velocity. Equation three, the final velocity squared equals the initial velocity squared plus twice the acceleration times the displacement, has no time. Equation four, the displacement equals the average of the two velocities times the time, has no acceleration. Learn them by their holes and two things happen at once: each acquires a distinguishing feature, and the choosing becomes automatic, because you match the hole in the equation to the quantity absent from the problem. There is a second comfort: these equations are supplied on the data sheet in the examination. Your marks do not depend on recall — they depend on recognising which equation the question has chosen for you, and on substituting cleanly with units. Recognition is a far smaller job than memory, and it is the one worth practising.

***

**Thandi:** Next: someone wants to know why they could not simply start the car problem with the final velocity equals the initial velocity plus the acceleration times the time. It looks like the simplest equation there.

**Tutor:** Try it honestly and watch where it stops. Substituting the given values, 25 equals 10 plus the acceleration times the time. That is one equation carrying two unknowns — the acceleration and the time — and one equation can surrender only one unknown. You are stuck, not because the equation is wrong, but because it is not the one the problem selected. Now run the rule instead. The problem gave the initial velocity, the final velocity and the displacement, and asked for the acceleration; the quantity neither given nor wanted is the time, so the correct equation is the one with no time in it: the final velocity squared equals the initial velocity squared plus twice the acceleration times the displacement. Substituting there, 625 equals 100 plus 600 times the acceleration — one equation, one unknown, solved in two lines to 0,875 metres per second squared. Simplest-looking is not the test. The test is whether the equation leaves you with exactly one unknown, and the absent-character rule guarantees that every time.

***

**Thandi:** Then this, and it hurt: a learner got the acceleration right, then got the time wrong, and lost nearly everything that followed. Is there any way to catch that inside the exam?

**Tutor:** There is, and it costs less than a minute. After computing the time by one route, recompute it by a route that never used any number you calculated yourself. Here, road one gave the time from the final velocity equals the initial velocity plus the acceleration times the time: 25 equals 10 plus 0,875 times the time, so the time is 15 divided by 0,875, which is 17,14 seconds. Road two uses only the original data: the car accelerated uniformly from 10 to 25 metres per second, so the average velocity is 17,5 metres per second, and the displacement equals the average velocity times the time, so 300 equals 17,5 times the time, giving 17,14 seconds. Agreement confirms both the time AND the earlier acceleration, because a wrong acceleration would have shifted road one and left road two untouched. Disagreement is equally valuable — it tells you an error exists while you can still repair it. Independence is what gives the check its power: never verify a result using the very number you are trying to test.

***

**Thandi:** Last one, and it feels harsh to the class: why does 90 kilometres per hour have to become 25 metres per second? The answer was only out by a factor.

**Tutor:** That factor is 3,6, and it does not stay in one place — it contaminates everything downstream. An acceleration computed from an inflated velocity is wrong by that factor, the time that follows is wrong, and any later part of the question inherits both errors. The conversion itself is only unit arithmetic, so it is worth feeling rather than memorising: 90 kilometres is 90 000 metres, and one hour is 3 600 seconds, so 90 kilometres per hour is 90 000 metres divided by 3 600 seconds, which is 25 metres per second. The 3,6 is simply 3 600 divided by 1 000. The equations of motion are built for SI units — metres, seconds, metres per second, metres per second squared — and they have no way of knowing you fed them something else; they will return a confident, wrong number. So make the conversion part of listing the data: the moment you write "initial velocity equals", the kilometres per hour are already gone. Convert at the door, and the trap never gets inside the problem.
