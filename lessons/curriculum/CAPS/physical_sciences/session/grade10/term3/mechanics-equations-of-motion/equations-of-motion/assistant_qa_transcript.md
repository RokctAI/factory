**Student:** How am I supposed to memorise four whole equations? They all blur together.

**Thandi:** Don't memorise them as four strangers — meet them as a family with one missing member each! Each equation is defined by who's NOT in it: equation one has no displacement, equation two has no final velocity, equation three has no time, equation four has no acceleration. Learn them by their absences and two things happen: they stop blurring (each has a unique "hole"), and choosing becomes automatic (match the hole to your problem's absent quantity). Also — they're usually on the formula sheet! Your real job isn't memory; it's recognition.

***

**Student:** In the car problem, why couldn't I start with $v_f = v_i + a\Delta t$? It's the simplest one.

**Thandi:** Try it and watch what happens: $25 = 10 + a \times \Delta t$. Two unknowns — both $a$ and $\Delta t$ — in one equation. Stuck! One equation can only surrender one unknown. That's exactly why the absent-character rule exists: the problem gave vi, vf and Δx, and wanted a, so the quantity you must AVOID is time. Equation three has no time in it — one equation, one unknown, solvable immediately. Simplest-looking isn't the test; solvable-right-now is.

***

**Student:** I got the acceleration right but then the time wrong, and I lost almost everything. Is there a way to catch that in the exam?

**Thandi:** Yes — the two-roads trick, and it's gold. After finding time with $v_f = v_i + a\Delta t$, spend forty seconds on the independent road: $\Delta x = \frac{(v_i + v_f)}{2} \times \Delta t$. It uses only ORIGINAL given numbers — never your calculated acceleration — so it can't inherit your mistake. Both roads give 17,14 s? Everything's right, including the earlier acceleration. Different answers? You've caught an error while it's still fixable. Two independent witnesses agreeing — that's how physicists sleep at night.

***

**Student:** Why does 90 km/h have to become 25 m/s? My answer was only wrong by "a factor" and it feels harsh.

**Thandi:** That "factor" is 3,6 — and it poisons *everything* downstream: acceleration wrong by 3,6, time wrong by 3,6, and any further parts wrong too. The equations are built for SI units — metres and seconds — so speeds must arrive in m/s. Feel why: 90 km/h means 90 000 metres in 3 600 seconds, and 90 000 ÷ 3 600 = 25 m/s. That ÷3,6 isn't a random rule; it's just the unit arithmetic compressed. Make conversion part of your data-listing ritual: the moment you write "vi = ", the km/h dies and m/s is born. Convert at the door, and the trap never gets inside.
