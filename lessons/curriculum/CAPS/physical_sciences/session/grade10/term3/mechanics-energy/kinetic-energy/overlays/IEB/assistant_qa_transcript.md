**Thandi:** Class questions before we close! First: a few people are suspicious of the half. Where does the half in half m v squared actually come from — why not just m v squared?

**Tutor:** Fair suspicion, and the answer is beautiful: the half is the price of building speed up gradually. Kinetic energy equals the work done to accelerate the object from rest, and while that acceleration happens, the object is NOT yet at full speed — it spends the whole journey somewhere between zero and v. On average, under steady acceleration, it travels at half its final speed. So the work done — force times distance — comes out at half of what you would charge if the object had moved at full speed throughout. That averaging is where the half lives. You will meet the same half in other uniform-growth formulas — it is the triangle-versus-rectangle story from the motion graphs: the speeding-up triangle holds half the area of the full-speed rectangle. What matters for your marks is simpler: the half is not decoration, and dropping it doubles every kinetic energy you ever calculate. Write the formula in full, every time — a half, times m, times v squared — and let the mathematics keep its own books.

***

**Thandi:** Next: someone calculated the bakkie's energy using 54 instead of 15 and got a monstrous number. They know they should convert, but why does the mistake get so big so fast here?

**Tutor:** Because this formula squares its velocity, and squaring squares the mistake. In a linear formula, feeding in kilometres per hour instead of metres per second inflates the answer by the conversion factor, 3,6 — bad, but survivable to spot. Here, the wrong number gets multiplied by itself: 54 squared is 2 916 against the correct 225, and 2 916 over 225 is about 13 — the square of 3,6. So the answer balloons thirteenfold, not fourfold. Run it: half of 800 times 2 916 gives roughly 1 166 000 joules, when the truth is 90 000. That is why the conversion must happen at the data-listing stage, before the squaring line, never after. And this generalises into a habit worth keeping for all of physics: whenever a formula raises a quantity to a power, unit errors in that quantity are amplified by that power. The sense check is your safety net — 1,1 million joules for a bakkie at suburban speed should feel wrong, because it is the wallet of a vehicle doing well over 180 kilometres per hour.

***

**Thandi:** Then the comparison question: yesterday's paint tin on the platform held about a hundred joules, and today's bakkie holds ninety thousand. Are potential and kinetic energy really the same kind of thing?

**Tutor:** The same currency exactly — that is the deepest fact in this chapter. Both are measured in joules, and both are defined by the same test: the capacity to do work. The tin's energy is stored in position; the bakkie's is stored in motion; and nature exchanges the one for the other at par, with no commission. Drop the tin and its potential energy converts to kinetic joule for joule as it falls — height draining, speed filling. Fire a ball upward and the exchange runs the other way. Tomorrow that becomes the conservation of mechanical energy, the single most elegant accounting rule in mechanics: while only gravity acts, E p plus E k stays constant, so what leaves one account arrives in the other, to the joule. The size gap you noticed is real but incidental — vehicles at speed simply hold more joules than tins on platforms, which is why a runaway bakkie is a bigger emergency than a falling tin. Same currency, different balances. Once you truly believe the two accounts hold the same money, the energy chapter stops being formulas and becomes bookkeeping.

***

**Thandi:** Last one, the practical one: how does knowing about the square actually change how someone should drive or cross a road?

**Tutor:** It rewrites your intuition about small speed differences, which is exactly where human judgement fails. Instinct says 120 is "a bit faster" than 100; the square says the energy — and so the destructive capacity and much of the stopping distance — has grown by 44 percent, because 1,2 squared is 1,44. Instinct says a following gap that worked at 60 will roughly do at 120; the square says the braking part of your stopping distance is FOUR times longer, before you even add reaction time. For a pedestrian, the same arithmetic decides survival odds: the energy delivered in an impact at 60 is more than double that at 40 — 2,25 times, to be exact — which is why urban limits sit where they do. Even the choice to slow just 10 kilometres per hour matters more than it feels: from 120 to 110 sheds about 16 percent of the energy. So the practical rule is this: never judge speed differences with subtraction — judge them with squares. Your instincts speak linearly; the road charges quadratically; and this one lesson is the exchange rate between the two.
