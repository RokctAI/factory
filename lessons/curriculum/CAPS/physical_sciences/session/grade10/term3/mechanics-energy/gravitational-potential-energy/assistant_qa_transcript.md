**Thandi:** The questions that come up most on this topic. First: mass and weight keep swapping places in people's formulas. What is the clean way to keep them apart?

**Tutor:** Give each a job title and the swapping stops. Mass is the amount of matter — measured in kilograms, the same on Earth, on the Moon, or floating between them. Weight is a FORCE — gravity's pull on that mass, measured in newtons, computed as m times g, and different wherever g differs. The formula E p equals m g h wants the mass, and then multiplies by g itself — so the formula manufactures the weight internally as m times g before multiplying by height. That is exactly why feeding it a weight is a disaster: give it 19,6 newtons in the m slot and it multiplies by 9,8 again, delivering answers nearly ten times too large. The checkpoint is the unit: kilograms go into the m slot, newtons never do. If a question hands you a weight, divide by 9,8 first — 19,6 newtons is 2 kilograms of mass — and then proceed. And the reverse discipline holds in force problems: when a diagram wants the gravitational force, that is the weight in newtons, not the mass. Kilograms for amount, newtons for pull; the slot decides which one it wants, and the unit is the passport it checks.

**Thandi:** Next, the philosophical one that arrives every year: if everyone can choose a different zero and get a different energy, is potential energy even real?

**Tutor:** It is real in exactly the way that matters: the differences are real, and differences are all nature ever spends. The situation is precisely a bank switching account displays: whether the app shows your balance measured against zero or against your overdraft limit, the amount any purchase costs you is identical. The brick on the platform has 98 joules by the ground's books and 156,8 by the basement's — but ask the physical question, how much energy arrives when it falls to the ground, and both books answer 98 joules, because the fall is a difference and the zero subtracts out. No experiment can ever measure an absolute potential energy; every measurable thing — the speed gained falling, the height reached rising, the dent left on landing — is a difference between two positions. So the freedom to choose zero is not a flaw in the concept; it is a signature of what kind of quantity this is. Physics keeps such freedoms deliberately, because they cost nothing and buy convenience: choose the zero that makes your particular problem's arithmetic simplest, declare it, and every legitimate observer will agree with your physical predictions even while their ledgers differ.

***

**Thandi:** Then the slope question, because it always appears in the exam: a worker carries a brick up a long zigzag staircase instead of a ladder. Does the longer path bank more energy?

**Tutor:** Not one joule more. Gravitational potential energy is position property, not path history: E p equals m g h asks only how high the brick ENDS UP, never how it got there. The zigzag staircase, the straight ladder, a crane, a spiral ramp — if each delivers the 2 kilogram brick to the 5 metre platform, each banks exactly 98 joules. The vertical height is the only distance gravity charges for, because gravity pulls only vertically; the horizontal wandering of the staircase is movement gravity does not oppose, so it adds nothing to the gravitational bill. Now, the worker's legs certainly feel the longer path — extra effort went into friction, into muscles, into heat — but that energy was spent elsewhere, not banked in the brick. The exam version of this idea is the ramp trap: a trolley pushed 10 metres along a ramp that rises 3 metres has h equal to 3, and the 10 is a distractor planted for anyone who grabs the first number wearing metres. The defence is one written line: h equals vertical height equals 3 metres. State it, then substitute — and the distractor dies on the page.

***

**Thandi:** Last one, very practical: how does anyone remember whether to use 235,2, or 98, or 137,2 when a problem has two platforms? People drown in their own numbers.

**Tutor:** They drown because they compute first and ask questions second; reverse the order and the numbers organise themselves. Before any arithmetic, write a three-line energy table: ground, zero joules; first platform, 98; second platform, 235,2 — each level priced once from the declared zero. Now every question the examiner can ask is a straight read or a single subtraction. Energy at the top? Read the line: 235,2. Energy gained in the full lift from ground? Top minus bottom: 235,2 minus zero. Gained between platforms? 235,2 minus 98 — 137,2. Released falling from the first platform? 98 minus zero. The table converts a wordy multi-part question into ledger lookups, and it also self-checks: balances must grow with height, and any jump that disagrees with m g times the height difference exposes an arithmetic slip immediately. This habit scales beautifully — next lesson, when kinetic energy joins, the table grows one column and becomes the energy audit that carries conservation of mechanical energy, the tool that answers matric questions in three lines. Learn the table now while it is small.
