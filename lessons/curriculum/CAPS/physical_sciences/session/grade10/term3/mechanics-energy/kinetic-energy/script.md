# Part 1 — Expert

This session builds kinetic energy — the energy an object carries because it is moving — and the formula that prices it: E k equals a half m v squared. One exam problem carries everything: a 1 000 kilogram car travels at 20 metres per second; calculate its kinetic energy, and then determine what happens to that energy when the driver doubles the speed to 40 metres per second. The second answer is the most important road-safety fact in the whole physics course, and the square in the formula is entirely responsible for it.

## Subtopic: Energy Carried by Motion

Kinetic energy is the energy an object possesses because of its MOTION. Anything moving can do work on whatever it hits — a moving hammer drives a nail, moving water spins a turbine, moving air bends a tree. Bring the object to rest and the energy must go somewhere: into the nail, the turbine, the crumpled metal. That capacity-to-do-work-by-moving is kinetic energy.

The formula: E k equals one half, times the mass, times the velocity squared — a half m v squared. Mass in kilograms, velocity in metres per second, answer in joules — the same joule as yesterday's potential energy, because energy is one currency with many accounts.

Look at the formula's two dials. Mass sits in the formula once, plainly: double the mass, double the energy — a loaded truck carries twice the kinetic energy of the empty one at the same speed. Velocity sits in the formula SQUARED: double the speed and the energy multiplies by four; triple it, nine. The two dials are not symmetrical, and the square dial is the one with consequences.

One more property before the numbers: kinetic energy is a scalar. The velocity is squared, and squaring erases direction — a car doing 20 metres per second east and one doing 20 west carry identical kinetic energy. No direction is ever attached to an energy answer, and E k is never negative: mass is positive, and a square cannot go below zero. Zero — for anything at rest — is the floor.

The questions for this section are with you now. They examine the definition, the two dials of the formula — one plain, one squared — the scalar nature of E k, and why kinetic energy can never be negative.

## Subtopic: Pricing the Motion — a Tonne at Twenty

Now the problem. Data first: mass 1 000 kilograms — one tonne, a small car. Velocity: 20 metres per second — and feel that speed properly: 20 times 3,6 is 72 kilometres per hour, an ordinary main-road speed.

Substitute into E k equals a half m v squared. First the square, always: 20 squared is 400. Then assemble: a half times 1 000 is 500, and 500 times 400 — 200 000 joules. Two hundred thousand joules — 200 kilojoules — riding along in an ordinary car at an ordinary speed.

Give that number a body: 200 000 joules is a 2 kilogram brick raised more than 10 kilometres — the cruising height of an airliner. Every joule of it must be removed by the brakes to stop the car, converted to heat in the discs. Brakes are energy furnaces; that is the entire job.

Now watch the commonest error die. A learner who forgets to square computes a half times 1 000 times 20 — 10 000 joules, twenty times too small. The defence is mechanical: write the square out as its own line — v squared equals 400 — before touching the rest. Squares handled first never get forgotten.

Units close the loop: kilograms times metres squared per second squared is exactly the joule. And if a speed arrives in kilometres per hour, divide by 3,6 BEFORE squaring — 72 kilometres per hour is 20 metres per second; square the wrong unit and the error squares itself.

Take this section's questions now. They walk the calculation: the 400 of the square, the 200 000 joules, the brick-at-altitude comparison, and the twenty-fold penalty for a forgotten square.

## Subtopic: The Square — Why Doubling Speed Quadruples the Danger

Now the driver doubles the speed: 40 metres per second — 144 kilometres per hour. Recompute: 40 squared is 1 600; a half times 1 000 times 1 600 — 800 000 joules.

Compare: 800 000 against 200 000. Double the speed, FOUR times the energy. Not double — four times, because the velocity enters squared, and 2 squared is 4. Triple the speed and the factor is 9. The relationship between speed and energy is not a straight line; it is a curve that steepens viciously.

Translate the mathematics into the road. Those 800 000 joules must ALL be dissipated to stop — by brakes in the good story, by crumpling metal in the bad one. Four times the energy means, for the same braking force, roughly four times the braking distance — the stopping gap grows with the SQUARE of the speed, which is why following distances that feel generous at 60 become fatal at 120. And in a collision, four times the energy is four times the destruction to be absorbed. A crash at 80 kilometres per hour is not twice as severe as at 40 — it is four times as severe. Speed limits are not linear caution; they are quadratic arithmetic.

The square also explains an asymmetry worth noticing: going from 100 to 120 kilometres per hour adds more kinetic energy than going from 0 to 60. Check it in round terms with a 1 000 kilogram car, dividing each speed by 3,6: at 60 kilometres per hour — 16,7 metres per second — the energy is about 139 000 joules; at 100 — 27,8 metres per second — about 386 000; at 120 — 33,3 metres per second — about 556 000. The last 20 kilometres per hour of speed cost about 170 000 joules; the first 60 cost 139 000. The top of the speed range is where the energy lives.

This section's questions are in front of you. They examine the four-times result, the square behind it, the braking-distance consequence, and the top-heavy way energy piles up at high speed.

## Subtopic: The Full Method, the Classic Traps, and Where This Leads

The method, four steps. Step one: list mass and velocity, converting as you list — grams to kilograms, kilometres per hour to metres per second by dividing by 3,6. Step two: square the velocity as its own written line. Step three: assemble a half times m times v squared, carrying units. Step four: sense-check — the answer must be positive, carry no direction, and sit at a believable scale: hundreds of joules for balls and bricks, hundreds of thousands for vehicles.

The traps. Trap one: the forgotten square — the twenty-fold error you have already watched die; keep it dead with the separate line. Trap two: squaring the wrong unit — convert to metres per second first, then square; 72 squared and 20 squared differ by a factor of 3,6 squared, which is about 13. Trap three: dropping the half — E k is HALF m v squared; the factor comes from the mathematics of building speed up gradually from rest, and omitting it doubles every answer. Trap four: attaching a direction or a negative sign — kinetic energy is a scalar with a floor at zero; an object moving in the negative direction still has positive E k, because the square destroyed the sign. Trap five: comparing energies by comparing speeds linearly — always square first, then compare.

Where this leads: tomorrow, conservation of mechanical energy joins E p and E k into a single budget — height converting to speed and back, with the total constant when only gravity acts. That principle answers falling, swinging and rolling problems in three lines each. Grade 12 sharpens all of it into the work-energy theorem and collision analysis, where kinetic energy decides what survives. And every road-safety statistic you will ever read is this lesson's square, wearing data.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the four steps, the five traps — the square, the units, the half — and the quadratic heart of road safety. Answer them as method, not memory.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same energy from the taxi rank and the toll road — motion as money, and the square as the surcharge nobody expects.

## Subtopic: The Moving Wallet

Yesterday's picture was a bank account in the sky: lift something and the height holds your deposit. Today's picture is a wallet that only exists while you move: kinetic energy, the cash an object carries because it is in motion.

Everything moving carries this wallet. A rolling soccer ball carries a little — enough to rattle the net. A minibus at highway speed carries a fortune. And the wallet's whole meaning is what happens at the moment of stopping: the cash MUST be spent, all of it, on whatever does the stopping. Caught softly by hands, it is spent gently over a metre of give. Stopped by a wall, it is spent instantly on bending metal. The wallet never just disappears; stopping is spending.

The price list is E k equals a half m v squared — half the mass, times the speed times itself. Two dials set the balance. The mass dial is honest and boring: twice the load, twice the cash — a full taxi carries double the empty one's energy at the same speed. The speed dial is the interesting one, because the speed multiplies by ITSELF: twice the speed is not twice the cash — it is four times. Hold that thought; the whole second half of the lesson lives inside it.

One friendly note: the wallet has no colour of direction. North at 20 or south at 20, the cash is identical — squaring a number wipes out its sign, so energy never points and never goes negative. Standing still is simply an empty wallet.

Your questions for this section are ready now. Keep the wallet: motion is carrying cash, stopping is spending it all, and the two dials — plain mass, squared speed — set how much is in there.

## Subtopic: Counting the Taxi's Cash

Count a real wallet. A small car — 1 000 kilograms, one tonne — cruising at 20 metres per second, which is 72 kilometres per hour on the speedometer: an ordinary trip to town.

The price list says: square the speed first. Twenty times twenty — 400. Then half the mass: half of 1 000 is 500. Multiply: 500 times 400 — 200 000 joules. Two hundred thousand joules of motion-cash in an ordinary car doing an ordinary speed.

Numbers that big need a picture. Two hundred thousand joules is yesterday's 2 kilogram brick lifted TEN KILOMETRES into the sky — up where the planes cruise. That is what the brakes must swallow, every single stop, turning the whole wallet into heat in the brake discs. It is also what the crumple zones must swallow in an accident — which is why modern cars are designed to fold like cardboard at the front: folding metal is the least bad way to spend a fortune fast.

And here is the twenty-times warning, cheap to learn now and expensive later: forget the square — compute half of 1 000 times a plain 20 — and you get 10 000 joules, a wallet twenty times too thin. The habit that saves you is one written line before anything else: v squared equals 400. Square first, on its own line, every time. And if the speedometer gave you kilometres per hour, divide by 3,6 before that line — the square must be taken in metres per second.

The questions for this section are up now. Count carefully: square first as its own step, half the mass, multiply — and give each big answer a picture to check it against.

## Subtopic: The Quadratic Surcharge

Now the lesson inside the lesson. The driver doubles the speed: 40 metres per second — 144 on the speedometer. The wallet: 40 squared is 1 600; half of 1 000 is 500; multiply — 800 000 joules.

Double the speed. FOUR times the cash. That is the quadratic surcharge, and it is not an accounting quirk — it is the reason speed kills. Every extra bit of speed costs energy at a growing rate, like a toll road where the fee per kilometre keeps rising the further you drive: the step from 100 to 120 kilometres per hour adds MORE energy than the entire climb from 0 to 60. The top of the speed range is where the fortune piles up.

Spend the surcharge on the road and it reads like this. Four times the energy needs roughly four times the stopping distance for the same brakes — the safe gap at 60 is nowhere near safe at 120, not by a bit but by a factor. Hit something, and four times the energy is four times the wreckage; a collision at 80 is four collisions at 40 happening at once. Speed limits are drawn from this square, not from caution for its own sake: the difference between 60 and 120 in a crash is not twice as bad — it is four times as bad, every time, by arithmetic no argument can soften.

So carry two sentences out of this lesson. Motion is a wallet, and stopping spends every cent of it — through brakes as heat, or through metal as wreckage. And the wallet grows with the SQUARE of the speed — double the pace, four times the fortune to spend.

The last questions of the lesson are with you now, and you are ready for them. Square first, half the mass, multiply — and whenever two speeds are compared, compare their squares, because that is how the road will compare them for you.
