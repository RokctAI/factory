# Part 1 — Expert

This session builds gravitational potential energy — the energy an object stores purely because of where it sits in a gravitational field — and the formula that prices it: E p equals m g h. One exam problem carries everything: a construction worker lifts a 2 kilogram brick from the ground up to a scaffold platform 5 metres high; calculate the gravitational potential energy the brick gains, and then the further gain when the brick is raised to a second platform at 12 metres. Small numbers, but every idea in the energy chapter starts here.

## Subtopic: Energy, the Joule, and Energy Stored by Position

Energy is the capacity to do work — to make things move, deform, heat or break. Its unit is the joule, symbol J. One joule is roughly the effort of lifting a small apple through one metre; a joule is a small coin, and real situations deal in hundreds and thousands of them.

Gravitational potential energy is energy stored by POSITION in the gravitational field. Lift an object and you work against gravity's pull; that work does not vanish — it is banked in the object's raised position, ready to be paid back the moment the object falls. A brick on a high scaffold is dangerous precisely because of this stored energy; a hydroelectric dam is valuable for exactly the same reason. Stored, waiting, recoverable: that is what potential means.

The formula prices the storage: E p equals m g h — the gravitational potential energy equals the mass, times the gravitational acceleration, times the height. Mass m in kilograms; g at 9,8 metres per second squared on Earth's surface; height h in metres, measured from a chosen reference level. Read the logic inside the multiplication: m times g is the object's weight — the force gravity exerts on it — and weight times the height lifted is the work done against gravity. The formula is not arbitrary; it is the lifting bill, itemised.

Note what E p is: a scalar. Energy has size and no direction — joules never point anywhere. No direction is ever attached to an energy answer.

The questions for this section are with you now. They examine the joule, the storage-by-position idea, the formula read as weight times height, and the scalar nature of every energy quantity.

## Subtopic: Pricing the Lift — 2 Kilograms to 5 Metres

Now the problem. Data first, formally. Mass: 2 kilograms. Gravitational acceleration: 9,8 metres per second squared. Height: 5 metres above the ground, and we choose the ground as the reference level — height zero, energy zero.

Substitute into E p equals m g h: 2 times 9,8 times 5. Compute in stages: 2 times 9,8 is 19,6. Then 19,6 times 5 — 98. The brick gains 98 joules of gravitational potential energy.

Check the units earn their name. Kilograms times metres per second squared gives newtons — the 19,6 is the brick's weight, 19,6 newtons. Newtons times metres gives newton-metres, and a newton-metre is precisely a joule. The units assemble themselves into joules, which is the quiet confirmation the substitution was sound.

Now the second platform at 12 metres. Total energy there: 2 times 9,8 times 12 — 235,2 joules. But the question asks the FURTHER gain from 5 metres to 12: 235,2 minus 98 — 137,2 joules. Alternatively, price only the extra lift: the height difference is 7 metres, so 2 times 9,8 times 7 is 137,2 joules — the same answer by a shorter road. Energy differences care only about height differences; that observation is about to become a principle.

Sense-check the sizes: roughly a hundred joules for a brick raised head-high — believable; thousandfold answers would mean a unit slipped somewhere.

Take this section's questions now. They follow the calculation: the 98 joules, the units assembling into joules through the weight, and the two roads to the 137,2 joule further gain.

## Subtopic: The Reference Level — Where Zero Lives

A subtlety with real marks attached: the height h is measured from a reference level that YOU choose — and the choice changes the numbers without changing the physics.

Take the brick on the 5 metre platform. Measured from the ground: h is 5, E p is 98 joules. Now let a second worker stand in a basement 3 metres below ground and measure from there: h is 8 metres, E p is 2 times 9,8 times 8 — 156,8 joules. Same brick, same platform, two different energies. Which is correct? Both — each is correct relative to its own declared zero.

The resolution is that nature only ever cashes in energy DIFFERENCES. When the brick falls from the platform to the ground, it releases 98 joules — and both bookkeepers agree: the ground observer computes 98 minus zero; the basement observer computes 156,8 minus 58,8 — the basement value of ground level being 2 times 9,8 times 3 — and gets the same 98. The zero is a bookkeeping convention; the differences are physics.

Practical consequences, three of them. One: declare the reference level in every solution — usually the ground, or the lowest point in the problem, chosen so heights stay positive and simple. Two: an object BELOW your reference level has negative potential energy — legitimate and meaningful, less energy than at zero. Three: never mix reference levels inside one problem; the commonest error in this subtopic is measuring one height from the ground and another from a platform, and the mixture is meaningless.

This section's questions are in front of you. They examine the free choice of zero, the 156,8 joules of the basement bookkeeper, the agreement on differences, and the one forbidden move — mixing levels mid-problem.

## Subtopic: The Full Method, the Classic Traps, and Where This Leads

The method for every potential energy problem, four steps. Step one: list mass, g, and height — converting as you list: grams to kilograms, centimetres to metres. Step two: declare the reference level and measure every height from it. Step three: substitute into E p equals m g h and compute, carrying units. Step four: sense-check the size, and if the question involves a change, work with the height difference directly.

The traps. Trap one: using weight where mass belongs — if a question supplies a weight of 19,6 newtons, the formula's m is 2 kilograms, weight divided by g; multiplying 19,6 by g again buys a wrong answer nearly ten times too large. Trap two: height measured along a slope — h is VERTICAL height only; a trolley pushed 10 metres up a ramp that rises 3 metres has gained height 3, and gravity charges only for the 3. Trap three: unit slips — a 500 gram object is 0,5 kilograms; 250 centimetres is 2,5 metres. Trap four: attaching a direction to an energy answer — energy is a scalar; 98 joules downward is not a thing. Trap five: g rounded to 10 — CAPS uses 9,8 metres per second squared unless a question explicitly says otherwise.

Where this leads: next in this energy chapter, kinetic energy prices motion the way E p prices position — and then conservation of mechanical energy links the two, letting stored E p convert into speed and back, which is how physics predicts the speed of falling objects without touching the equations of motion. Grade 12 rebuilds all of it as the work-energy theorem, the single most powerful tool in matric mechanics. Every one of those topics quotes today's formula.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the four steps, the five traps — the weight-versus-mass swap and the slope's vertical height above all — and the road ahead through kinetic energy. Answer them as method, not memory.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same energy from a water tower and a savings account — height as money in the bank.

## Subtopic: The Bank Account in the Sky

Every township water tower is a savings account. The pump spends electricity all night pushing water up into the tank — hard work, paid in full. But that spending is not waste: it is a DEPOSIT. The water sits up there holding the energy, and when you open a tap in the morning, the height pays it back as rushing water pressure. Work in, storage, work out.

That is gravitational potential energy: energy banked by lifting something against gravity. Lift a brick to a scaffold and the effort of the lift is deposited in the brick's position. It waits there — an hour, a year — and the moment the brick falls, the account pays out in speed. Stored because of where it is: that is the whole idea.

The price of the deposit is the lifting bill: E p equals m g h — mass times gravitational acceleration times height. And the bill makes sense read in two halves. Mass times g is the object's WEIGHT — how hard gravity pulls it down, in newtons; that is the force you must beat to lift it. Weight times the height is force times the distance lifted — the total effort of carrying it all the way up. Heavier object, bigger bill; higher lift, bigger bill; both at once, biggest of all.

The currency is the joule — about the effort of lifting an apple through one metre. Everyday deposits run in the hundreds: keep that scale in your pocket for sense-checking.

Your questions for this section are ready now. Hold the water tower: lifting is depositing, sitting high is savings, falling is the withdrawal — and the bill for the deposit is weight times height.

## Subtopic: The Brick's Bank Balance

Run the actual problem as a bank statement. The brick: 2 kilograms. Gravity's pull on it: 2 times 9,8 — 19,6 newtons of weight. The lift: 5 metres straight up to the platform.

The deposit: weight times height — 19,6 times 5 — 98 joules. The brick sits on the scaffold with 98 joules in its account, measured from the ground where we set the balance to zero.

Now the foreman wants it higher: a second platform at 12 metres. Two ways to price the top-up, and they must agree. Road one: full balance at the top minus full balance below — 2 times 9,8 times 12 is 235,2 joules, minus the 98 already banked — 137,2 joules. Road two: charge only for the extra climb — 7 more metres at 19,6 newtons is 19,6 times 7 — 137,2 joules. Same number, and the agreement teaches the lesson: gravity charges per metre climbed, so only the CHANGE in height ever matters to a change in energy.

Feel the scale of the danger too, because this is why building sites use hard hats: those 235 joules come back out in full if the brick falls from 12 metres — every joule of deposit converts to speed on the way down. Stored energy is real energy; height is a loaded account.

The questions for this section are up now. Work them like statements: weight first, then weight times height for any deposit, and height DIFFERENCE times weight for any top-up. The account arithmetic never changes.

## Subtopic: Choosing Where Zero Is

Last piece — the one that looks like a paradox and turns out to be freedom. Where exactly is the account balance zero?

Your bank measures your balance from zero rand. But heights come with no built-in zero: the worker measures the platform from the ground and says 5 metres; a colleague in the basement, 3 metres lower, measures the same platform and says 8. Their energy claims disagree — 98 joules against 156,8 — and both are right, each from its own zero. It is like two people describing the same room as first floor or second floor because one counts from the street and one from the parking garage: the room never moved.

Physics escapes the paradox because nature only ever pays out DIFFERENCES. The brick falling from platform to ground releases 98 joules — and both bookkeepers compute exactly that, because their zero-point disagreement subtracts away. The zero is your free choice; the payouts are fixed by the fall.

So use the freedom well, with three habits. Declare your zero in the first line — reference level: the ground — the way you declare east positive in motion problems. Choose the LOWEST point in the problem as zero when you can, so every height is positive and the arithmetic stays friendly. And never, ever switch zeros halfway — one account, one zero, from the first line to the final answer. An object below your chosen zero simply has a negative balance — overdrawn, meaningfully so, and the mathematics handles it without complaint.

The last questions of the lesson are with you now, and you are ready for them. Answer them like an accountant with one iron rule: any zero you like, declared once, used throughout — and let the differences, which are the physics, take care of themselves.
