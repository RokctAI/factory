**Thandi:** Class questions before we close! The big one first: mass and weight keep swapping places in people's formulas. What is the clean way to keep them apart in this topic?

**Tutor:** Give each a home and never let them visit. Mass lives in kilograms and answers "how much stuff?" — it is the m that belongs inside E p equals m g h. Weight lives in newtons and answers "how hard does gravity pull that stuff?" — it is m times g, already containing the g. The swap becomes dangerous because the formula quietly computes the weight on its way to the answer: 4 times 9,8 is 39,2 newtons, and then times the height. So when a question hands you a WEIGHT — say 39,2 newtons — the g has already been applied, and your first move is to divide by 9,8 to recover the 4 kilograms before using the formula. Multiplying 39,2 by g again charges gravity twice and inflates the answer nearly tenfold — an error a marker spots instantly because the units collapse. Here is the two-second test: kilograms in the data means you have mass, proceed; newtons in the data means you have weight, divide by 9,8 first. Units are the passport — check them at the border and the swap can never happen.

**Thandi:** Next: someone worked a ramp problem and used the 8 metres of ramp as the height. Why does gravity only charge for the 2 metres of rise?

**Tutor:** Because gravity pulls vertically, and it only does work against motion that opposes that pull — the vertical part. Push a wheelbarrow 8 metres up a gentle ramp that climbs 2 metres, and only 2 of those metres were spent fighting gravity; the other distance was spent moving sideways, which gravity does not tax. The formula's h means vertical height gained, full stop. Here is the thought experiment that settles it: carry a tin up a spiral staircase to a balcony 3 metres up, or haul it straight up on a rope — the rope route is shorter and the staircase winds for 20 metres, but the potential energy at the top is identical, 4 times 9,8 times 3, because the tin ends at the same height. Potential energy cares about where you END UP vertically, not the path you took. That is also why ramps exist at all: they let you pay the same energy bill in smaller instalments of force over a longer path. In any slope problem, draw the right-angled triangle, label the slope length and the vertical rise separately, and hand the formula only the rise.

***

**Thandi:** Then the philosophical one that half the class argued about at break: if the cellar observer and the ground observer disagree about the tin's energy, does the tin ACTUALLY have a definite amount of energy or not?

**Tutor:** Wonderful argument to have, and the resolution is precise: the tin has a definite energy DIFFERENCE between any two positions, and no absolute energy at all — because there is no absolute zero of height built into the world. The situation is exactly like asking for the "true" altitude of your school: measured from sea level it is one number, from the valley floor another, and neither the sea nor the valley has a cosmic claim to being zero. What is absolute is the drop — the tin falling from the platform to the ground releases 117,6 joules, and every observer, from the cellar to the roof, computes the same figure, because their zero choices cancel in the subtraction: 196 minus 78,4 equals 117,6, just as 117,6 minus 0 does. Physics is built so that only those differences ever appear in a measurable prediction — the speed of the falling tin, the height of a bounce. So declare a zero for convenience, compute with it consistently, and remember that the number on any single line is bookkeeping, while the difference between lines is the world.

***

**Thandi:** Last one, in two parts: can potential energy really be negative, and why do we use 9,8 instead of just rounding to 10?

**Tutor:** Negative first. Yes — and it is bookkeeping, not exotic physics. If you declare the ground as zero and lower the tin into a cellar 2 metres down, its potential energy is 4 times 9,8 times minus 2: minus 78,4 joules. The minus simply says the tin sits below your zero — it holds 78,4 joules LESS than it would at ground level, and you would have to deposit 78,4 joules of lifting work just to bring the balance back to zero. Overdrawn, in the banking picture — meaningful and perfectly legal. What the minus never means is "negative energy direction"; energy is a scalar, and the sign is position relative to your chosen zero, nothing more. Now the 9,8. That is the measured strength of Earth's surface gravity in metres per second squared, and using it keeps your answers honest to about one percent — rounding to 10 injects a two percent error into every energy figure and, worse, into every answer built on top of it later, like the speeds in conservation problems. Unless a question explicitly instructs you to take g as 10, work with 9,8 — and keep your calculator's value consistent through every line of one solution.
