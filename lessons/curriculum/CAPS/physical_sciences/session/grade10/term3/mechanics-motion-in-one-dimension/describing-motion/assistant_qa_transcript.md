**Thandi:** The questions that come up over and over on this topic. First: if both frames in the taxi example are correct, how do we ever know which frame an exam question wants us to use?

**Tutor:** The question almost always tells you, quietly, and when it does not, the ground is the default. Phrases like a car moving at 30 metres per second, a learner walks east, a ball is dropped from a bridge — all of these measure motion relative to the Earth's surface, and that is the frame the examiner intends unless another is named. When a question deliberately plays with frames — a passenger walking forward inside a moving train, say — it will name both explicitly, and then the discipline is to answer in the frame the question specifies: the passenger's velocity relative to the train is one number, relative to the ground another, and both can be marks in the same question. What you must never do is drift between frames without noticing. Choose, write the choice down — let east be positive, positions measured from the starting point — and every sign in your solution now has a fixed meaning. The declaration is one line, and it is the cheapest insurance in mechanics: markers can follow a wrong-but-consistent solution and award method marks, but a solution that silently swaps frames contradicts itself, and contradictions earn nothing.

***

**Thandi:** Next, the classic: a learner computed the displacement for the 40 east, 30 west walk as 70 metres, because 40 plus 30 is 70. What went wrong in their head?

**Tutor:** They added path lengths, and displacement refuses to be built that way — it is defined by endpoints alone: final position minus initial position, route ignored. The mental fix is to see the two legs as a tug in opposite directions rather than two contributions to a pile. In the declared frame, east positive, the first leg is plus 40 and the second is minus 30 — the sign carries the direction — and adding SIGNED quantities gives plus 10: ten metres east. The unsigned addition, 40 plus 30, answers a different and perfectly respectable question — how much ground was covered — which is the distance, 70 metres. So both numbers live in the problem; the error is handing one in when the other was requested. A quick self-test catches it every time: displacement can never exceed distance, because the straight line from start to finish is the shortest possible route. If your displacement magnitude comes out bigger than your distance — or equal to it after the walker visibly turned around — one of the two has been miscomputed. Ten against seventy passes; seventy against seventy, after a U-turn, fails instantly.

***

**Thandi:** Then the one that produces disbelief every year: how can a sprinter running a whole lap have an average velocity of zero? It sounds like physics calling the race pointless.

**Tutor:** It sounds that way only until you hear what velocity is paid to measure. Average velocity is net change of position over time — it answers where did you end up, relative to where you began, and how quickly did that net change happen. The lap runner ends exactly at the start: change of position zero, so average velocity zero, regardless of effort. Nothing about the effort is denied — it is simply booked under a different account. Average speed, distance over time, credits every metre: 400 metres in 80 seconds is 5 metres per second, a very respectable pace. Physics is not judging the race; it is keeping two ledgers that answer two different questions. And the distinction is anything but pedantic once you see where each ledger is used. A taxi's fuel consumption follows the odometer ledger — every metre burns petrol. Whether you actually got to Durban follows the displacement ledger. The exam's favourite trick is asking for one while your finger is on the other, so train the reflex: velocity and displacement travel together, speed and distance travel together, and no member of one pair may answer for the other.

***

**Thandi:** Last one, practical: a journey has three legs with a rest stop in the middle, and everybody divides by the wrong time. What is the rule?

**Tutor:** The rule is short: an average for a journey uses the TOTAL time of that journey, first instant to last, rest stops included. The reasoning sits inside the word average — it smears the whole journey, movement and pauses alike, into one steady equivalent rate. Suppose our walker covers the 70 metres of ground in 50 seconds of walking but also rests for 20 seconds midway. The average speed for the journey is 70 divided by 70 — one metre per second, not 1,4 — because a bystander watching from start to finish saw 70 metres covered in 70 seconds of real time. The 1,4 answers a narrower question, the average during the walking portions, and an exam that wants it will say so explicitly. Unless the question carves out a portion, divide by the full clock. The same applies to velocity: the pigeon's 10 metres east over the full 70 seconds gives 0,14 metres per second east. Practical habit: build a tiny timeline before calculating — leg one, rest, leg two, with times attached — then read total displacement, total distance and total time straight off it. The timeline costs ten seconds and makes the wrong-time error almost impossible.
