# Part 1 — Expert

This session builds the vocabulary of motion — frame of reference, position, distance, displacement, speed and velocity — the six ideas every mechanics question assumes you own. One exam problem carries everything: a learner walks 40 metres east along a straight road, then turns and walks 30 metres west, taking 50 seconds in total. We must find the distance travelled, the displacement, the average speed and the average velocity — four answers from one short walk, and two of them surprise almost everyone the first time.

## Subtopic: Frame of Reference and Position

Before anything can move, physics must answer a quieter question: where is it now? Position is location relative to a chosen reference point — an agreed zero. Without the reference point, position means nothing: sitting in a moving taxi, you are stationary relative to the seat and flying relative to the road. Both descriptions are correct; they simply use different frames of reference.

A frame of reference is the combination of a reference point and a chosen positive direction. For our problem: let the starting point be zero, and let east be positive. Now every location on the road gets a number. Twenty metres east of the start: position plus 20 metres. Ten metres west of it: minus 10 metres. The sign is direction; the size is how far from zero.

Watch our walker's position evolve. Start: zero. After the eastward leg: plus 40 metres. Then 30 metres west — position slides backwards to plus 10 metres. Final position: 10 metres east of the start. Notice position is a vector: magnitude and direction, packaged as a signed number once the frame is declared.

Two disciplines from the first line of every solution. Declare the frame — a sentence like let east be positive costs five seconds and governs every sign after it. And never change frames midway: east positive in line one and west positive in line three produces nonsense that no marker can untangle.

The questions for this section are with you now. They examine the reference point, the sign-as-direction idea, the taxi seat versus the road, and the walker's final position of plus 10 metres.

## Subtopic: Distance versus Displacement

Now the pair that splits every motion problem in two. Distance: how much ground was covered — the full length of the actual path, direction ignored. A scalar. Displacement: the straight-line change in position, from start to finish, direction included. A vector. Same walk, two different questions.

Compute both for our walker. Distance: 40 metres out plus 30 metres back — every metre counts — 70 metres. Displacement: final position minus initial position — plus 10 minus zero — 10 metres east. Say both answers aloud and feel how different they are: seventy metres of walking achieved a net change of only ten.

The symbols: distance is D; displacement is delta x — delta always meaning change in, so delta x reads change in position. The unit for both is the metre; the difference is not the unit but the information carried — displacement must be reported with a direction, and an answer of 10 metres with no direction is incomplete and loses the mark.

Push the contrast to its extreme, because exams do. A full lap of a 400 metre athletics track: distance 400 metres; displacement zero — finish and start are the same point, so the change in position is nothing. Real effort, zero displacement. The two quantities agree only when motion never changes direction: walk 40 metres east and stop, and distance and displacement magnitude are both 40.

Take this section's questions now. They examine the definitions as questions — ground covered versus change of position — the 70 and the 10 east, the lap that scores zero, and the one situation where the two agree.

## Subtopic: Average Speed versus Average Velocity

Divide each of our two answers by the time, and the pair splits again — this time into speed and velocity.

Average speed: distance divided by total time. A scalar, like the distance it comes from. Substitute: 70 metres over 50 seconds — 1,4 metres per second. That is the number a pedometer would report: ground covered per second of the trip, direction ignored.

Average velocity: displacement divided by total time — delta x over delta t. A vector, like the displacement it comes from, inheriting its direction. Substitute: 10 metres east over 50 seconds — 0,2 metres per second, east. Seven times smaller than the speed, because the two legs of the walk mostly cancelled each other's progress.

Both answers are honest; they answer different questions. How fast was the ground covered? One comma four. How fast did the position change, net? Nought comma two, east. An examiner who writes calculate the average velocity has chosen the second question deliberately, and answering with 1,4 — the speed — earns nothing, even though the arithmetic was perfect. Read the question's noun before touching the numbers.

One more consequence worth pinning down: average velocity can be zero while average speed is large. The full lap of the track in 80 seconds — average speed 400 over 80, which is 5 metres per second; average velocity zero over 80 — zero. Whenever a path returns to its start, velocity's verdict is that nothing moved, net. Speed keeps the effort; velocity keeps the outcome.

This section's questions are in front of you. They pair each formula with its parent quantity — speed from distance, velocity from displacement — and examine the 1,4, the 0,2 east, and the lap where five metres per second of speed carries zero velocity.

## Subtopic: The Full Method, the Classic Traps, and Where This Leads

The method for every describing-motion problem, four steps. Step one: declare the frame — reference point and positive direction, in writing. Step two: track positions as signed numbers, leg by leg. Step three: compute distance along the path, displacement from endpoints only — final position minus initial. Step four: divide by TOTAL time — speed from distance, velocity from displacement — and attach a direction to every vector answer.

The traps, in the order they claim marks. Trap one: answering speed when velocity was asked, or the reverse — the scalar-vector pair is the single most examined distinction in this topic. Trap two: computing displacement by adding path lengths — displacement never cares about the route, only the endpoints; 40 plus 30 is the distance, 40 minus 30 the displacement magnitude. Trap three: dropping the direction from a vector answer — 0,2 metres per second is incomplete; 0,2 metres per second east is the answer. Trap four: dividing by the wrong time — average quantities use the total time for the whole journey, including any rest stops; a walker who pauses still burns clock. Trap five: sign chaos from an undeclared or switched frame.

Where this leads: everywhere in mechanics. Next in this term, acceleration measures how velocity changes — impossible to grasp if velocity itself is fuzzy. The equations of motion speak entirely in displacement and velocity. Grade 11 momentum is mass times velocity — the vector, not the speed. And matric's vertical projectile motion lives and dies on signs declared exactly the way you practised today. This vocabulary is the alphabet; everything after it is spelling.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the four steps, the five traps — especially the scalar-vector swaps — and the reason total time includes the rest stop. Answer them as method, not memory.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same walk with a taxi rank, an odometer and a pigeon — the whole topic in three everyday pictures.

## Subtopic: Where Are You, Relative to What

Give directions to your house and listen to what you actually say: from the taxi rank, two streets up. Nobody ever gives a location from nowhere — there is always a from, an agreed starting marker. Physics calls that marker the reference point, and position is simply where you are, measured from it.

Add one more agreement — which way counts as forward — and you have a full frame of reference. Choose the rank as zero and up-the-hill as positive, and every spot in the neighbourhood becomes a number: the shop two streets up is plus two; the school one street down is minus one. The minus is not a punishment; it is just the other direction, written small.

Our exam problem does exactly this. The walker's starting point is zero; east is positive. Walk 40 metres east: you are standing at plus 40. Turn, walk 30 metres west: you slide back to plus 10. That final number is a complete description — ten metres from the start, on the east side — one signed number holding both the how far and the which way.

And the moving-taxi puzzle stops being a puzzle: relative to the seat you are still; relative to the road you are moving. Neither answer is wrong — they come from different reference points, and both are legitimate frames. Physics does not ask which is true; it asks you to declare which one you are using, then stay loyal to it.

Your questions for this section are ready now. Keep the taxi rank in your head: every position needs its from, every sign is a direction, and the declaration in line one is what keeps all the later arithmetic honest.

## Subtopic: The Odometer and the Pigeon

Two ways to measure a journey, and you know both already.

The odometer in a car counts every metre the wheels roll — to the shop, around the block, back home — the total only ever grows. That is distance: ground covered, route and all, no interest in direction. Seventy metres of walking is seventy on the odometer, full stop.

The pigeon does not care about your route. Released at the finish, it flies straight back to the start — and the length and direction of that flight measure how far you ended up from where you began. That is displacement: start to finish in a straight line, direction included. Our walker's pigeon flies just 10 metres, pointing west back to the start — so the displacement was 10 metres east.

Same walk, two honest numbers: odometer 70, pigeon 10 east. The gap between them is the doubling back — every metre of the return leg grew the odometer while shrinking the pigeon's flight. And the famous extreme: run a full lap of the track, and the odometer proudly shows 400 metres while the pigeon does not even take off — start and finish are the same spot, displacement zero. Effort is real; net change is nothing. The only time the two numbers match is a journey that never turns around.

The questions for this section are up now. Sort each situation with the two instruments: what does the odometer read, and what does the pigeon fly? Get those two, and distance versus displacement is settled for good.

## Subtopic: Two Speedometers for One Walk

Divide each instrument's reading by the time, and you get the two averages the exam loves to swap.

Odometer over time: the walker covered 70 metres of ground in 50 seconds — 70 over 50 is 1,4 metres per second. That is average speed: how briskly the ground was eaten, direction ignored. It is the number that matches how tired your legs feel.

Pigeon over time: the net change of position was 10 metres east in those same 50 seconds — 10 over 50 is 0,2 metres per second, east. That is average velocity: how fast the position changed, net, with the direction attached. It is the number that matches how far you actually got.

One walk, two speedometers, answers seven times apart — and both correct. The gap tells its own story: most of the walking was spent undoing earlier walking. A delivery scooter zigzagging through a township all afternoon can log a big average speed and a tiny average velocity; the lap runner manages five metres per second of speed with a velocity of exactly zero, because the pigeon never flies.

So when the question arrives, read its noun like a price tag. Speed asked? Odometer over time. Velocity asked? Pigeon over time — and say the direction, always, because velocity without direction is half an answer. One more habit completes the set: divide by the whole time of the journey, tea breaks included; the clock does not pause because you did.

The last questions of the lesson are with you now, and you are ready for them. Two instruments, two divisions, one declared direction — sort each question into odometer or pigeon before you touch the calculator, and this topic becomes the easiest marks on the paper.
