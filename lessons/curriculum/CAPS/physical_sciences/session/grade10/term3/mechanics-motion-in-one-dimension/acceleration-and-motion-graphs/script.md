# Part 1 — Expert

This session builds acceleration — the rate at which velocity changes — and the three motion graphs that turn any journey into a picture you can read marks off. One exam problem carries everything: a car starts from rest and speeds up uniformly to 20 metres per second in 10 seconds, cruises at 20 metres per second for 20 seconds, then brakes uniformly to rest in 5 seconds. We must find both accelerations, and then use the velocity-time graph to find the total displacement — no equations of motion, just a gradient and an area.

## Subtopic: Acceleration — the Rate Velocity Changes

The definition first, as a sentence: acceleration equals the change in velocity divided by the time taken for that change — a equals delta v over delta t. Delta v itself is final velocity minus initial velocity. The unit: metres per second, per second — written metres per second squared. An acceleration of 2 metres per second squared means the velocity grows by 2 metres per second during every second.

Compute the car's first phase: from rest — initial velocity zero — to 20 metres per second, in 10 seconds. Delta v is 20 minus zero — 20 metres per second. Divide by 10 seconds: a equals 2 metres per second squared, in the direction of motion. Every second of that phase, the speedometer climbed by the same 2 metres per second: zero, 2, 4, 6, up to 20. That evenness is what uniform acceleration means.

Phase two, the cruise: velocity constant at 20. Delta v is zero, so the acceleration is zero. Constant velocity is zero acceleration — motion continues, change does not.

Phase three, the braking: from 20 metres per second to rest in 5 seconds. Delta v is zero minus 20 — negative 20. Divide by 5: negative 4 metres per second squared. The sign is doing honest work: with the direction of motion positive, a negative acceleration means the velocity is being eaten — 4 metres per second of speed lost each second. Watch the language carefully: negative acceleration here means slowing down, and everyday speech calls it deceleration — but in physics the sign belongs to the frame, and later problems will show a negative acceleration speeding an object up in the negative direction. Read signs against the declared frame, always.

The questions for this section are with you now. They examine the definition read as a sentence, the 2 and the negative 4, the zero of the cruise, and what an acceleration number promises about each second of motion.

## Subtopic: The Position-Time Graph — Gradient Is Velocity

Now the pictures. First: the position-time graph — position on the vertical axis, time on the horizontal.

The central reading rule: the GRADIENT of a position-time graph is the velocity. Gradient is rise over run — change in position over change in time — and that quotient is the definition of velocity itself. Steep means fast; shallow means slow; flat means stationary — a horizontal line is an object going nowhere while time passes.

So the three basic shapes tell three stories. A straight sloped line: constant gradient, constant velocity — equal ground in equal seconds. A horizontal line: zero velocity, parked. A CURVE: changing gradient, changing velocity — acceleration. During our car's first phase the position-time graph curves upward, steepening — each second covers more ground than the last, the gradient growing from zero towards 20. During the cruise it is a straight sloped line. During braking it curves again, flattening towards horizontal — the gradient dying to zero as the car stops.

One more shape completes the kit: a straight line sloping DOWNWARD. Position decreasing — the object moving backwards, towards and possibly past the reference point. Negative gradient, negative velocity. The graph does not need arrows; the slope carries the direction.

Take this section's questions now. They examine the gradient rule, the four shapes — sloped line, flat line, steepening curve, flattening curve — and the downward slope that announces motion in the negative direction.

## Subtopic: The Velocity-Time Graph — Gradient and Area, Both Paying

The velocity-time graph is the workhorse of Grade 10 motion, because it pays out twice.

First payment: the GRADIENT of a velocity-time graph is the acceleration — change in velocity over change in time, exactly the definition. Draw our car's journey: from the origin, a straight line rising to 20 metres per second at 10 seconds — gradient 20 over 10, which is 2: the acceleration we calculated. Then a horizontal line at 20 for 20 seconds — gradient zero, acceleration zero. Then a straight line falling from 20 to zero in 5 seconds — gradient negative 20 over 5, negative 4. The three phases read straight off the slopes.

Second payment: the AREA between the graph and the time axis is the displacement. Velocity times time gives displacement, and area is exactly that multiplication done piece by piece. Compute the three areas. Phase one: a triangle — half base times height — half times 10 seconds times 20 metres per second: 100 metres. Phase two: a rectangle — 20 seconds times 20 metres per second: 400 metres. Phase three: another triangle — half times 5 times 20: 50 metres. Total displacement: 100 plus 400 plus 50 — 550 metres.

Pause on what just happened: a two-part question — acceleration and displacement — answered entirely by reading one picture. Gradient for acceleration, area for displacement. No equations of motion, no memorised formulas beyond the graph rules themselves. That is why examiners love velocity-time graphs, and why the fastest students draw one even when the question does not ask for it.

This section's questions are in front of you. They examine the two payment rules, the three gradients — 2, zero, negative 4 — the triangle-rectangle-triangle areas, and the 550 metres they total.

## Subtopic: Reading All Three Graphs Together, and the Classic Traps

The third graph: acceleration-time. For uniformly accelerated motion it is the dullest picture and the easiest marks — horizontal steps. Our car: a flat line at 2 for ten seconds, a flat line at zero for twenty, a flat line at negative 4 for five. Constant values, no slopes to read.

Now the family as a family. The three graphs are one motion wearing three outfits, linked by two operations: gradients travel downward through the family — the gradient of position-time gives velocity, the gradient of velocity-time gives acceleration — and areas travel upward — the area under acceleration-time gives the change in velocity, the area under velocity-time gives the displacement. Exams hand you one graph and demand another; the two links are the entire translation dictionary.

The traps, in the order they harvest marks. Trap one: reading a velocity-time graph as if it were position-time — a rising line means speeding up, not moving away; a falling line means slowing down, not coming back. It is the single commonest graph error in the paper. Trap two: confusing a curve with a slope — curves on position-time mean acceleration; straight lines on velocity-time mean acceleration; the same physics draws differently on different axes. Trap three: forgetting that a horizontal velocity-time line at some height is motion, not rest — rest is the line lying ON the time axis. Trap four: computing area only up to the graph line instead of down to the axis, or missing a piece of the total. Trap five: gradient units — a velocity-time gradient of 2 is 2 metres per second squared, not 2 metres per second; the axes' units divide.

Where this leads: instantaneous velocity arrives via the tangent to a curved position-time graph — the gradient at a single point, in the topic just after this one. The equations of motion are the velocity-time graph translated into algebra — the displacement equations are literally this triangle and rectangle added. Grade 11 and matric read forces off these same graphs through Newton's second law. Learn to read the pictures now and half of mechanics becomes looking, not calculating.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the flat steps of acceleration-time, the gradient-down area-up family links, and the five traps — especially the rising-line misreading. Answer them with the pictures in your head.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same journey through a taxi's windscreen and a bank statement — speeding up, cruising, braking, and a graph that pays interest.

## Subtopic: The Speedometer's Diary

Sit behind the taxi driver and watch only the speedometer needle. Acceleration is nothing more than the needle's diary: how fast is the needle itself moving?

Pulling away from the rank, the needle climbs — zero, 2, 4, 6 — gaining 2 metres per second with every second that passes. That steady climb is an acceleration of 2 metres per second squared. Say the unit the friendly way: two metres per second, per second — every second, two more metres per second. The formula is just the diary entry formalised: acceleration equals the change in velocity divided by the time it took — 20 metres per second gained in 10 seconds is 2 per second, per second.

On the open road the needle rests at 20. The taxi is moving — quickly — but the NEEDLE is still. No change in velocity, no acceleration. Zero acceleration never means parked; it means the speed is not changing. This single sentence, properly absorbed, prevents years of confusion.

Then the robot ahead turns red and the driver brakes: the needle falls from 20 to zero in 5 seconds — losing 4 metres per second every second. Change in velocity: minus 20. Acceleration: minus 4 metres per second squared. The minus sign simply records which way the needle moved — down instead of up. Passengers feel each phase in their bodies: pressed back into the seat while the needle climbs, relaxed while it rests, tipped forward while it falls. Acceleration is the phase you can feel.

Your questions for this section are ready now. Keep the needle in mind: acceleration is about the needle moving, not the taxi moving — climbing needle positive, resting needle zero, falling needle negative.

## Subtopic: The Journey as a Picture

Now draw the whole trip as a velocity-time graph — time along the bottom, speedometer reading up the side — and watch the journey become a shape.

Phase one: the needle climbed steadily from zero to 20 in 10 seconds. On the graph, a straight ramp rising from the corner. Phase two: twenty seconds of cruising at 20 — a flat plateau, high above the axis. Phase three: braking to rest in 5 seconds — a steep ramp falling back to the floor. Ramp up, plateau, ramp down: the trip's whole story in one silhouette, like a mountain seen from the road.

Reading the graph is two skills, and here is the first: STEEPNESS is acceleration. The climbing ramp rises 20 in 10 — steepness 2, our pull-away acceleration. The plateau is level — steepness zero, the cruise. The falling ramp drops 20 in 5 — steepness minus 4, the braking. Steeper means harder acceleration; the sign says climbing or falling. You can read the driver's foot straight off the silhouette: accelerator, cruising, brake.

And learn to catch the classic misreading before it catches you: this is a picture of the SPEEDOMETER, not a map of the road. The falling ramp does not mean the taxi rolled backwards down a hill — it means the needle fell while the taxi still moved forward, slowing. Height is speed; the taxi only moves backwards if the graph crosses below the axis.

The questions for this section are up now. Match each piece of silhouette to the driver's foot — ramp, plateau, ramp — and hold on to the golden correction: height means how fast, never how far.

## Subtopic: The Area That Pays Out in Metres

The second reading skill is the one that feels like a magic trick: the AREA under the velocity-time graph is the distance the taxi covered.

Feel why before calculating. Cruise at 20 metres per second for 20 seconds and you cover 20 times 20 — 400 metres. But look at the graph: that multiplication is exactly the plateau's rectangle — height 20, width 20, area 400. Velocity times time IS area; the graph was doing the multiplication all along, in picture form.

The ramps work the same way, just with triangle arithmetic. Pull-away: a triangle 10 wide and 20 tall — half of 10 times 20 — 100 metres. And the triangle makes physical sense: speeding up from rest, the taxi averaged only half its top speed, so it covered half of what a full-speed rectangle would have given. Braking: a triangle 5 wide and 20 tall — half of 5 times 20 — 50 metres of stopping distance.

Add the pieces: 100 plus 400 plus 50 — 550 metres for the whole trip, read off one drawing with primary-school geometry. No equations of motion, no memorised formulas: a triangle, a rectangle, a triangle.

That is the deal the velocity-time graph offers, and it is the best deal in Grade 10 physics: draw the silhouette once, and it pays twice — steepness hands you every acceleration, area hands you every distance. When a motion problem arrives wordy and confusing, the strongest first move is always the same: sketch the velocity-time graph and let the shape do the thinking.

The last questions of the lesson are with you now, and you are ready for them. Work each one on the drawing: label the heights and widths, read steepness for acceleration, cut the area into triangles and rectangles for distance — and let the picture carry the arithmetic.
