# Part 1 — Expert

This session builds the first stone of all mechanics: the difference between vectors and scalars, and the arithmetic each kind obeys. Two exam tasks organise the work — classify displacement, speed, force, energy and velocity as vectors or scalars; then find the resultant of two forces, 30 newtons east and 50 newtons west, acting on the same object. Small questions on paper; enormous ideas underneath, because every mechanics and electricity topic through matric is built on getting this distinction right.

## Subtopic: Two Kinds of Quantities

Physics measures the world with quantities — and they come in exactly two kinds.

A scalar is a quantity described completely by a magnitude — a size with a unit. Say "the temperature is 25 degrees Celsius" and you have said everything; asking "25 degrees in which direction?" is nonsense. Mass, time, temperature, distance, speed, energy: state the number and the unit, and the description is complete.

A vector is a quantity that is only fully described by BOTH a magnitude AND a direction. Say "a force of 30 newtons acts on the box" and a physicist immediately asks: in which direction? Pushing east and pushing west are utterly different events with the same magnitude. Force, displacement, velocity, acceleration: without a direction, the description is incomplete — a sentence with a missing word.

The test you will use forever: ask "does a direction make sense — is it NEEDED to complete the meaning?" If yes, vector. If no, scalar.

Why does physics bother with the split? Because the two kinds obey different arithmetic. Scalars add like ordinary numbers: 3 kilograms plus 2 kilograms is always 5 kilograms, no exceptions. But vectors — as our forces problem will show — can add, partially cancel, or fully cancel each other, depending on their directions. Thirty newtons plus fifty newtons is NOT necessarily eighty newtons. Using scalar arithmetic on vector quantities is the deepest-rooted error in first-year mechanics, and today we pull that root out.

This section's questions are with you now. They probe the two definitions themselves — a magnitude alone against a magnitude with a direction — and the reason the split exists at all: that the two kinds obey different arithmetic. Answer with the direction test, not with a memorised list.

## Subtopic: Classifying the Five — and the Famous Pairs

Now the classification task, quantity by quantity, each with its reason — because exams award the reason, not the guess.

Displacement: the straight-line change in position, FROM somewhere TO somewhere. "I am 3 kilometres from home" — incomplete; 3 kilometres in which direction? Displacement needs direction: VECTOR.

Speed: how fast something moves — the rate at which distance is covered. A car's speedometer reads 60 kilometres per hour without caring where the car points. Direction adds nothing: SCALAR.

Force: a push or a pull — and a push east is not a push west. Direction essential: VECTOR.

Energy: the capacity to do work, measured in joules. A battery stores 500 joules — "in which direction?" has no meaning. SCALAR.

Velocity: the rate of change of POSITION — speed with its direction attached. Sixty kilometres per hour NORTH. VECTOR.

Notice the famous pairs hiding in that list, because examiners adore them. Distance and displacement: the scalar-vector twins of position. Walk 400 metres around a full athletics track lap: distance 400 metres — every metre of path counts; displacement ZERO — you ended where you began, and displacement only compares start to finish. Speed and velocity: the twins of motion. Same lap in 80 seconds: average speed is 400 metres divided by 80 seconds, 5 metres per second; average velocity is 0 metres divided by 80 seconds, zero — because displacement was zero. Two different questions about the same run, two different answers. The scalar tells you how much moving happened; the vector tells you the net effect of the moving.

Take this section's questions now. They ask for classification WITH reasons, and they examine the famous pairs on that same 400-metre lap — distance against displacement, average speed against average velocity. Give the reason each time, not merely the label; the reason is where the marks sit.

## Subtopic: The Resultant — 30 N East Meets 50 N West

Now the forces problem: 30 newtons east and 50 newtons west act on the same object. Find the resultant.

First, the word: the resultant is the SINGLE vector that has the same effect as all the original vectors acting together. Two ropes pulling the box; the resultant is the one imaginary rope that would do the identical job alone.

Vectors in one dimension are added with a sign convention — the tool that turns directions into algebra. Choose a positive direction, declare it, and stick to it. Let us choose: east is positive. Then 30 newtons east is plus 30 newtons. And 50 newtons west — the opposite way — is MINUS 50 newtons. The directions have become signs; the addition becomes arithmetic.

Add: plus 30 newtons plus negative 50 newtons equals negative 20 newtons.

Now translate the answer back from algebra into physics. Negative, in our convention, means westward. The resultant is 20 newtons WEST.

Read the physics of what happened: the two forces fought. Thirty newtons of eastward effort cancelled thirty of the fifty westward newtons, leaving twenty newtons of unbeaten westward force. The object experiences exactly what one 20-newton westward push would deliver. That is why "80 newtons" — the scalar reflex answer — is so wrong: it ignores the battle entirely, adding the armies of both sides as if they fought for the same general.

State the answer in full: magnitude AND direction — 20 newtons west. A vector answer without a direction is half an answer, and memos dock it every time. And the sanity checks: the resultant must point in the direction of the LARGER force — west, correct; and its magnitude must be the difference of opposing magnitudes — 50 newtons minus 30 newtons, twenty newtons, correct.

The questions for this section are in front of you. Watch three things as you work: the sign convention declared before any arithmetic, the translation of the negative answer back into a direction, and the two sanity checks — the resultant follows the larger force, and its magnitude is the difference of the opposing magnitudes.

## Subtopic: The Toolkit, the Special Cases, and the Road to Matric

Generalise the one-dimensional method into your permanent toolkit. Step one: declare a positive direction — any choice works; consistency is what matters. Step two: convert every vector into a signed number. Step three: add algebraically. Step four: translate the sign back into a direction, and state magnitude with direction. Four steps, any number of forces — five forces on a line are handled exactly like two.

Know the special cases as landmarks. Same direction: 30 newtons east and 50 newtons east — resultant 80 newtons east; magnitudes add when directions agree, and this is the ONLY case where the scalar reflex gives the right number. Opposite directions: subtract, resultant follows the larger — today's problem. Equal and opposite: 50 newtons east and 50 newtons west — resultant ZERO; the forces balance completely, and the object behaves as if untouched. That zero-resultant case has a future: it is the equilibrium condition at the heart of Newton's first law, which you will meet in the free-body diagram work and again all through Grade 11.

The error museum, four exhibits. Exhibit one: adding magnitudes while ignoring directions — the 80-newton disaster; directions first, always. Exhibit two: dropping the direction from the final answer — 20 newtons is incomplete; 20 newtons west is physics. Exhibit three: switching sign conventions midway — east positive in line one, west positive by line three; declare once, never wander. Exhibit four: classifying by habit instead of by test — "energy sounds like a vector because it is powerful"; sound is not the test; NEEDING a direction is the test.

Where does this foundation carry you? Everywhere. Grade 11 opens with vectors in two dimensions — forces at angles, resolved into components exactly like the rope at 30 degrees in your free-body diagram work. Newton's laws are vector laws; momentum in matric is a vector; electric fields in Grade 11 and 12 point — vectors again. Mechanics plus electricity and magnetism make up the overwhelming majority of the matric Paper 1 marks, and every one of those pages speaks the vector language you learned today. Classify by the direction test; add with a sign convention; answer with magnitude and direction. Three habits — a lifetime of physics stands on them.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the four-step method, the three special cases, and the four exhibits of the error museum. Answer each one in full — magnitude and direction together — exactly as the memo will demand of you.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same two kinds of quantity from the street and the sports field, where directions matter enough to argue about.

## Subtopic: Does It Need a "Which Way"?

Somebody phones and says, "I'm five kilometres away." What is your very first question? Which way — five kilometres in which direction? Now somebody says, "the loaf costs eighteen rand." Nobody alive answers, "eighteen rand in which direction?" That is the entire difference this lesson turns on, and you have been using it since you could talk.

Some measurements are finished the moment you say the number and the unit. Eighteen rand. Two kilograms of mince. Twenty-five degrees on a hot Polokwane afternoon. Forty minutes without electricity. Number, unit, done — physics calls those scalars.

Other measurements are only half said until a direction arrives. A push on a stalled bakkie: thirty newtons — forwards or backwards? Same number, opposite events. A trip: three kilometres — towards town or away from it? A speed on a road sign stands fine on its own; a velocity is a speed that has told you where it is heading. Physics calls those vectors, and the missing direction is not decoration, it is half the meaning.

So you never have to memorise a list. Run the sentence test out loud: attach a direction and hear whether the sentence stays sensible or falls over. "A force of thirty newtons eastward" — stands, and needed it. "Five hundred joules of energy northward"? It collapses; a battery does not point anywhere. Energy is a scalar, however powerful the word sounds — and "sounds powerful" was never the test. The test is whether the meaning needs a which-way.

Your questions for this section are with you now, and this is the friendliest set in the lesson. Run each one through the sentence test out loud instead of hunting for a memorised list: if the direction is needed, it is a vector; if the sentence falls over, it is a scalar. You had this instinct before today — you are only naming it now.

## Subtopic: The Lap Around the Block

Now the pair that catches more learners than any other, and one jog sorts it out for good.

You leave your gate, run once around the block, and stop back at the same gate. How far did you run? If the block is four hundred metres around, you ran four hundred metres. Your legs know it and your watch knows it, and that is the distance: every metre of the path added up, no direction attached anywhere. Scalar.

Now a different question about the same run: where are you, compared with where you started? Exactly where you started. So your displacement is zero metres — because displacement only ever compares the finish with the start, in a straight line, direction included. What happened in between is none of its business. Vector.

Two honest questions, two honest answers, and they disagree because they were never asking the same thing. Distance asks how much moving happened. Displacement asks what the moving achieved. The exam version is the athletics track: one full lap of four hundred metres gives a distance of four hundred metres and a displacement of zero.

Speed and velocity split the same way, and now you can predict it. Say the lap took eighty seconds. Average speed is distance divided by time: four hundred metres divided by eighty seconds is five metres per second — you were genuinely moving, and that number says so. Average velocity is displacement divided by time: zero metres divided by eighty seconds is zero metres per second. Not a trick and not a contradiction — you moved plenty and arrived nowhere. Run the same four hundred metres straight up the road instead, and the distance is still four hundred metres, but now the displacement is four hundred metres north and the velocity is five metres per second north.

Here are your questions for this section, and you are in good shape for them. Carry the lap with you: for each one, ask which question is really being asked — how much moving happened, or what the moving achieved. That single habit shuts the distance-and-displacement trap for the rest of your school career.

## Subtopic: Two Teams on One Rope

Last piece, and it is the one that looks like broken arithmetic until you watch it happen.

Thirty newtons pull a crate east; fifty newtons pull the same crate west. Total force? The reflex shouts eighty, and the reflex is wrong, because these two are not helping each other — they are fighting. Picture the tug-of-war at a school sports day: the left team pulls with thirty newtons, the right team with fifty. Thirty of the right team's newtons are fully occupied cancelling the left team; only twenty newtons are spare to drag anybody anywhere. The rope creeps to the right, and it creeps exactly as if one person were pulling it with twenty newtons. That leftover is what physics calls the resultant: the single pull that would do the same job as the whole struggle.

To get that on paper without drawing teams, turn the directions into plus and minus signs. Pick a direction, call it positive, and say so out loud — east, let us say. Then thirty newtons east is plus thirty newtons, and fifty newtons west is minus fifty newtons, because it goes the opposite way. Add them like ordinary numbers: plus thirty plus minus fifty gives minus twenty. Now read the sign back into words: minus meant west, so the answer is twenty newtons west.

Two checks you can run in your head before you write anything. The winner should be the bigger force — west, correct. And the size should be the difference between them: fifty newtons minus thirty newtons is twenty newtons, correct. If the two had pulled the same way — thirty east and fifty east — they would be teammates, and eighty newtons east would be right; that is the one situation where the reflex answer survives. And if they had been equal and opposite — fifty east against fifty west — the answer is zero newtons: the crate feels nothing at all, and both teams go home sweaty.

One last habit, worth marks in every vector question you will ever write: finish the sentence. "Twenty newtons" is half an answer. "Twenty newtons west" is physics. Magnitude and direction, every single time.

The last questions of the lesson are up now, and you have earned them. Declare your positive direction before you touch the numbers, keep it to the final line, and let every answer leave with its direction attached. Do that consistently and you already own the habit that carries you through Grade 11 forces and matric momentum.
