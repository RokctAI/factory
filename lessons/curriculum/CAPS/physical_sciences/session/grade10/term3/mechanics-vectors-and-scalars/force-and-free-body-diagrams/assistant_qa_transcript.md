**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! The first one is a proper knot: if the only forward pull is the rope, and all the forces balance, what keeps the box moving at all? Balanced forces sound like the box should just stop.

**Tutor:** That knot took humanity about two thousand years to untie, so the class is in respectable company. The instinct says that motion needs a continuing cause — that if you stop pushing, motion drains away. What actually drains motion away is friction, and on a rough floor there is plenty of it. Newton's first law states the corrected rule: an object keeps doing exactly what it is already doing — staying at rest, or moving in a straight line at a steady speed — unless a net force changes it. Forces are not fuel for motion; they are the cause of CHANGES in motion. So balanced forces do not mean "stop"; they mean "carry on unchanged". A parked box with balanced forces stays parked. A box already sliding at half a metre per second with balanced forces keeps sliding at half a metre per second. Our rope is not feeding the motion — it is exactly cancelling the friction that would otherwise slow the box down. Take the rope away and the forces stop balancing: friction wins, the box decelerates and stops. That is why the examiner's phrase "constant velocity" and the phrase "zero net force" mean the same thing.

***

**Thandi:** Next: people are unhappy that the normal force is not equal to the weight. In every example we had before, the normal force was the weight. What changed?

**Tutor:** What changed is that something else now pulls vertically. The normal force is not a partner of the weight — it is the surface's response, exactly as large as it needs to be to stop the object sinking in, and no larger. Read our vertical balance as a sentence: the floor's upward push, plus the upward part of the rope's pull, together equal the weight of 49 newtons. Rearranged, the floor's push equals 49 newtons minus the rope's upward part. Put numbers to it: if the rope pulls with 20 newtons at 30 degrees, its upward part is 20 newtons times the sine of 30 degrees, and the sine of 30 degrees is exactly one half, so that is 10 newtons of lift. The floor then supplies only 39 newtons, not 49. Push the box downwards at an angle instead and the arithmetic runs the other way: the downward part of your push adds to the weight, and the normal force grows LARGER than 49 newtons. So "normal force equals weight" is not a law — it is what happens in the special case where nothing else acts vertically. Always read the up-and-down balance for the situation in front of you.

***

**Thandi:** Then this: how does one rope become two forces? There is only one rope and one pull, so where does the second one come from?

**Tutor:** Nothing has been duplicated — one force is doing two jobs at once, and components are simply the bookkeeping that keeps score of each job. Pull a wheeled suitcase by its handle: the same single effort drags it along the floor AND takes a little weight off it. Physics measures the dragging job with the horizontal component and the lifting job with the vertical component. For a 20-newton pull at 30 degrees above the horizontal, the horizontal component is 20 newtons times the cosine of 30 degrees, about 17,3 newtons, and the vertical component is 20 newtons times the sine of 30 degrees, exactly 10 newtons. Notice they do not add up to 20 — 17,3 plus 10 is 27,3 — and that is correct, not a mistake: components are two perpendicular directions, not two slices of a cake. Split them apart and each direction can be balanced on its own, which is the whole reason we bother: sideways, the 17,3 newtons must match the friction; upwards, the 10 newtons helps hold the box, so the floor supplies less. One rope, one pull, two accounts.

***

**Thandi:** Last one, and it cost somebody a mark: does friction always point backwards? They drew it backwards on a question about a box on a ramp and lost it.

**Tutor:** Friction has no fixed compass direction — it has a fixed grudge. It always opposes the relative sliding between the two surfaces, or the sliding that is about to start. On our flat floor the box slides forwards, so friction acts backwards; the "backwards" is a consequence, not a rule. Put the same box on a slope and think again. A crate sliding DOWN a ramp has friction pointing UP the slope, because up-slope is the direction that fights the downward slide. A crate parked on a slope and not moving still has friction acting up the slope — static friction, holding it against the pull that would start it sliding. And here is the case that catches people: a box on the back of a bakkie that accelerates away from a robot is carried forwards by friction, which acts FORWARD on the box, because the box would otherwise slide backwards relative to the load bed. So never ask "which way is backwards?" Ask "which way is this surface sliding, or trying to slide, against the other?" — then set friction dead against that.
