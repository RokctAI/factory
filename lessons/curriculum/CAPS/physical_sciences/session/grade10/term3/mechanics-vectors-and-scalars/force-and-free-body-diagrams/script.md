Welcome, student. Today we learn the working tool of all mechanics: the force diagram and its sharper cousin, the free-body diagram. Our exam problem: a 5 kilogram box is pulled at constant velocity across a rough floor by a rope held at 30 degrees above the horizontal. Draw the free-body diagram, naming the weight, the normal force, the applied force and the friction. Every Newton's-laws question you will ever answer — through Grade 11 and into the biggest section of the matric paper — begins with this drawing done correctly.

## Subtopic: Forces Are Interactions — the Cast Around the Box

A force is a push or a pull — an interaction between two objects. That word "interaction" is your protection against invented forces: every real force on your diagram must have another object responsible for it. If you cannot name the object doing the pushing or pulling, the force does not exist.

Interrogate the box. Who is touching or otherwise acting on it?

First, the Earth — acting without touching. Gravity pulls the box straight down. We call this force the weight, symbol w or F g, and its size is the mass times the gravitational acceleration: 5 kilograms times 9,8 metres per second squared — 49 newtons, downward. Note carefully: mass and weight are different creatures. Mass, 5 kilograms, is the amount of matter — a scalar, the same on the Moon. Weight, 49 newtons, is a force — a vector, produced by gravity, different on the Moon. Exams love this distinction.

Second, the floor — touching. The floor pushes back on the box, perpendicular to its surface. That perpendicular push is the normal force, N — "normal" is the mathematician's word for perpendicular. Without it the box would sink through the floor.

Third, the rope — touching. The person pulls through the rope: the applied force, F applied, directed along the rope — at 30 degrees above the horizontal in our problem.

Fourth, the roughness of the floor — friction, f. Friction opposes the sliding: the box slides forward, so friction points backward, horizontally, against the motion.

Four forces, each with a responsible object: Earth, floor, rope, rough surface. No "force of motion", no "force of speed" — motion is not a force, and adding one is the oldest error in mechanics.

## Subtopic: Drawing the Free-Body Diagram

Now the drawing itself — and the difference between the two diagram types the syllabus names. A force diagram shows the forces drawn on a sketch of the actual situation — the box, the floor, the rope. A FREE-body diagram strips all of that away: the box becomes a single dot, and only the force arrows remain, all starting from that dot. Free of the scenery — that is the "free" in free-body.

Draw it with me, arrow by arrow.

Arrow one: weight, w — from the dot straight DOWN. Label it w or F g; 49 newtons if values are asked.

Arrow two: the normal force, N — from the dot straight UP, perpendicular to the floor. Its length: we will discover in the final subtopic that it is NOT simply equal to the weight here — a subtlety that separates the top answers.

Arrow three: the applied force, F applied — from the dot, angled at 30 degrees above the horizontal, pointing in the direction of pulling. Mark the 30-degree angle between the arrow and the horizontal — an unlabelled angle loses the mark.

Arrow four: friction, f — from the dot, horizontally BACKWARD, opposite to the sliding.

Quality rules the markers apply. Every arrow starts ON the dot and points away from it. Every arrow has an arrowhead showing direction. Every arrow carries a label — the accepted symbol or name. Rough lengths should reflect rough sizes where known. And nothing else appears: no floor, no rope, no speed arrows — a velocity arrow drawn as a force is an instant penalty. Four labelled arrows from one dot: that is full marks.

## Subtopic: Reading the Physics Off the Diagram — Constant Velocity

Why does the examiner tell us the velocity is CONSTANT? Because that phrase is physics in disguise. Newton's first law: an object moving at constant velocity has ZERO net force — all the forces balance. The diagram we drew is not just a picture; it is an equation waiting to be read.

Read it direction by direction. Horizontally: the forward pull must balance the backward friction. But careful — the rope pulls at 30 degrees, so only PART of the applied force acts horizontally: the horizontal component, F applied times cosine of 30 degrees. Balance: F applied cos 30 equals f. Grade 10 needs the idea of components qualitatively; the calculation sharpens in Grade 11 — but seeing that a slanted force splits into a horizontal part and a vertical part is the insight that unlocks everything later.

Vertically: three players. The normal force pushes up. The weight pulls down, 49 newtons. AND the rope helps lift — the vertical component of the applied force, F applied times sine of 30 degrees, points upward. Balance: N plus F applied sin 30 equals w.

Now the subtlety promised earlier: because the rope carries some of the box's weight, the floor pushes less than 49 newtons. The normal force is LESS than the weight: N equals w minus the upward pull of the rope. Learners who write "N always equals w" have memorised instead of read — and this problem is precisely where that memorisation fails. The normal force is whatever perpendicular push the surface must supply — read it off the vertical balance, never assume it.

One more payoff: since friction in later grades is proportional to the normal force, pulling at an upward angle genuinely makes the box easier to drag — less normal force, less friction. Your diagram just explained a piece of everyday experience.

## Subtopic: The Method, the Error Museum, and the Road Ahead

The complete method, for any free-body diagram. Step one: isolate the object — one object per diagram, always ask "forces ON the box", never forces by the box on other things. Step two: hunt systematically — gravity first (always present), then every touching object: surfaces give normal forces and possibly friction, ropes give tension along the rope, hands give applied forces. Step three: draw the dot and the arrows — from the dot, labelled, angles marked. Step four: read the motion state — at rest or constant velocity means everything balances; accelerating means the net force points with the acceleration. Step five: write the balance statements direction by direction.

The error museum, four exhibits. Exhibit one: the phantom "force of motion" pushing the box forward beyond the rope's pull — motion needs no force to continue; only CHANGES of motion need force. Exhibit two: normal force automatically equal to weight — refuted by our own problem; read the vertical balance. Exhibit three: friction drawn opposite to the applied force instead of opposite to the sliding — here they coincide, but on an object pushed downhill they differ; friction opposes relative sliding, always. Exhibit four: arrows floating near the dot, unlabelled, or without heads — free marks handed back to the marker.

Where does this drawing lead? Everywhere. Grade 11: Newton's second law — the unbalanced version of today's reading, where net force equals mass times acceleration; inclined planes, where weight itself gets split into components. Grade 12: work, energy and power — computed along the directions your diagram reveals. Mechanics is 65 marks of the matric physics paper, and every one of those questions opens with a dot and four or five honest arrows. Draw ten free-body diagrams this week — boxes pulled, boxes pushed, objects hanging from ropes — until the hunt for forces becomes instinct.

Class is dismissed.
