# Part 1 — Expert

This session builds the working tool of all mechanics: the force diagram, and its sharper cousin the free-body diagram. One exam problem carries the hour — a 5 kilogram box is pulled at constant velocity across a rough floor by a rope held at 30 degrees above the horizontal; draw the free-body diagram, naming the weight, the normal force, the applied force and the friction. Every Newton's-laws question you will ever answer — through Grade 11 and into the biggest section of the matric paper — begins with this drawing done correctly.

## Subtopic: Forces Are Interactions — the Cast Around the Box

A force is a push or a pull — an interaction between two objects. That word "interaction" is your protection against invented forces: every real force on your diagram must have another object responsible for it. If you cannot name the object doing the pushing or pulling, the force does not exist.

Interrogate the box. Who is touching or otherwise acting on it?

First, the Earth — acting without touching. Gravity pulls the box straight down. We call this force the weight, symbol w or F g, and its size is the mass times the gravitational acceleration: weight equals mass times g — 5 kilograms times 9,8 metres per second squared — 49 newtons, downward. Note carefully: mass and weight are different creatures. Mass, 5 kilograms, is the amount of matter — a scalar, the same on the Moon. Weight, 49 newtons, is a force — a vector, produced by gravity, different on the Moon. Exams love this distinction.

Second, the floor — touching. The floor pushes back on the box, perpendicular to its surface. That perpendicular push is the normal force, N — "normal" is the mathematician's word for perpendicular. Without it the box would sink through the floor.

Third, the rope — touching. The person pulls through the rope: the applied force, F applied, directed along the rope — at 30 degrees above the horizontal in our problem.

Fourth, the roughness of the floor — friction, f. Friction opposes the sliding: the box slides forward, so friction points backward, horizontally, against the motion.

Four forces, each with a responsible object: Earth, floor, rope, rough surface. No "force of motion", no "force of speed" — motion is not a force, and adding one is the oldest error in mechanics.

The questions for this section are with you now. Test every force in them against the interaction rule — name the object responsible, or discard the force — and keep mass and weight strictly apart: 5 kilograms and 49 newtons are different quantities in different units, and only one of them is a vector.

## Subtopic: Drawing the Free-Body Diagram

Now the drawing itself — and the difference between the two diagram types the syllabus names. A force diagram shows the forces drawn on a sketch of the actual situation — the box, the floor, the rope. A FREE-body diagram strips all of that away: the box becomes a single dot, and only the force arrows remain, all starting from that dot. Free of the scenery — that is the "free" in free-body.

Build it arrow by arrow.

Arrow one: weight, w — from the dot straight DOWN. Label it w or F g; 49 newtons if values are asked.

Arrow two: the normal force, N — from the dot straight UP, perpendicular to the floor. Its length: we will discover in the final subtopic that it is NOT simply equal to the weight here — a subtlety that separates the top answers.

Arrow three: the applied force, F applied — from the dot, angled at 30 degrees above the horizontal, pointing in the direction of pulling. Mark the 30-degree angle between the arrow and the horizontal — an unlabelled angle loses the mark.

Arrow four: friction, f — from the dot, horizontally BACKWARD, opposite to the sliding.

Quality rules the markers apply. Every arrow starts ON the dot and points away from it. Every arrow has an arrowhead showing direction. Every arrow carries a label — the accepted symbol or name. Rough lengths should reflect rough sizes where known. And nothing else appears: no floor, no rope, no speed arrows — a velocity arrow drawn as a force is an instant penalty. Four labelled arrows from one dot: that is full marks.

Work the questions on this section now. They examine the conventions exactly: one dot, four labelled arrows leaving that dot, the 30-degree angle marked against the horizontal, and nothing borrowed from the scenery — no floor, no rope, and above all no velocity arrow among the forces.

## Subtopic: Reading the Physics Off the Diagram — Constant Velocity

Why does the examiner tell us the velocity is CONSTANT? Because that phrase is physics in disguise. Newton's first law: an object moving at constant velocity has ZERO net force — all the forces balance. The diagram we drew is not just a picture; it is an equation waiting to be read.

Read it direction by direction. Horizontally: the forward pull must balance the backward friction. But careful — the rope pulls at 30 degrees, so only PART of the applied force acts horizontally: the horizontal component, F applied times cosine of 30 degrees. Balance: F applied cos 30 equals f — in words, the horizontal part of the rope's pull equals the friction force. Grade 10 needs the idea of components qualitatively; the calculation sharpens in Grade 11 — but seeing that a slanted force splits into a horizontal part and a vertical part is the insight that unlocks everything later.

Vertically: three players. The normal force pushes up. The weight pulls down, 49 newtons. AND the rope helps lift — the vertical component of the applied force, F applied times sine of 30 degrees, points upward. Balance: N plus F applied sin 30 equals w — in words, the floor's push plus the rope's upward part together equal the weight.

Now the subtlety promised earlier: because the rope carries some of the box's weight, the floor pushes less than 49 newtons. The normal force is LESS than the weight: N equals w minus the upward pull of the rope. Learners who write "N always equals w" have memorised instead of read — and this problem is precisely where that memorisation fails. The normal force is whatever perpendicular push the surface must supply — read it off the vertical balance, never assume it.

One more payoff: since friction in later grades is proportional to the normal force, pulling at an upward angle genuinely makes the box easier to drag — less normal force, less friction. Your diagram just explained a piece of everyday experience.

This section's questions are in front of you. Expect the constant-velocity condition to be interrogated, along with the two balance statements — horizontal and vertical — and the normal force read off the vertical balance rather than assumed equal to the weight.

## Subtopic: The Method, the Error Museum, and the Road Ahead

The complete method, for any free-body diagram. Step one: isolate the object — one object per diagram, always ask "forces ON the box", never forces by the box on other things. Step two: hunt systematically — gravity first (always present), then every touching object: surfaces give normal forces and possibly friction, ropes give tension along the rope, hands give applied forces. Step three: draw the dot and the arrows — from the dot, labelled, angles marked. Step four: read the motion state — at rest or constant velocity means everything balances; accelerating means the net force points with the acceleration. Step five: write the balance statements direction by direction.

The error museum, four exhibits. Exhibit one: the phantom "force of motion" pushing the box forward beyond the rope's pull — motion needs no force to continue; only CHANGES of motion need force. Exhibit two: normal force automatically equal to weight — refuted by our own problem; read the vertical balance. Exhibit three: friction drawn opposite to the applied force instead of opposite to the sliding — here they coincide, but on an object pushed downhill they differ; friction opposes relative sliding, always. Exhibit four: arrows floating near the dot, unlabelled, or without heads — free marks given straight back to the marker.

Where does this drawing lead? Everywhere. Grade 11: Newton's second law — the unbalanced version of today's reading, where net force equals mass times acceleration; inclined planes, where weight itself gets split into components. Grade 12: work, energy and power — computed along the directions your diagram reveals. Mechanics is 65 marks of the matric physics paper, and every one of those questions opens with a dot and four or five honest arrows. Draw ten free-body diagrams this week — boxes pulled, boxes pushed, objects hanging from ropes — until the hunt for forces becomes instinct.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the five steps of the method, the direction friction always takes, and the exhibits of the error museum. Answer them in the order you would draw — object isolated first, forces hunted systematically, motion state read last.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same box from the yard outside — a gas bottle, a rope over the shoulder, and four honest arrows.

## Subtopic: Who Is Actually Pulling on the Box?

Picture dragging a full gas bottle across a concrete yard with a rope over your shoulder. Before any physics, ask the ordinary question: who is doing something to that bottle right now? Not who is standing nearby — who is actually pushing or pulling it.

The Earth is, always, pulling it straight down. That downward pull is called the weight, and its size is easy: the mass, times how hard gravity pulls on each kilogram. Our box is 5 kilograms, and gravity pulls with 9,8 newtons on every kilogram, so 5 kilograms times 9,8 metres per second squared gives 49 newtons, straight down. Said as a sentence: weight equals mass times gravitational acceleration.

Two words get mixed up here, so separate them now. Mass is how much stuff there is — 5 kilograms of box, and it would still be 5 kilograms on the Moon. Weight is how hard gravity pulls on that stuff — 49 newtons on Earth, and far less on the Moon, because the Moon pulls more gently. Kilograms for mass, newtons for weight, and only weight is a force.

The concrete is doing something too. It holds the bottle up, pushing straight up, square to the ground — otherwise the bottle would sink into it. That upward push from any surface is the normal force, and "normal" is just the maths word for at right angles to the surface.

The rope is doing something: through it, you pull the bottle along the rope's direction — forward and slightly upward. That is the applied force.

And the rough ground is doing something: it drags back on the bottle, fighting the slide. That is friction, pointing backwards while the bottle slides forwards.

Four actions, four culprits — Earth, ground, rope, roughness. And here is the rule that keeps imaginary forces off your page: if you cannot name who is doing it, it is not a force. There is no "force of moving forward", because moving is not a person, a floor or a rope. Moving is something the box is doing, not something being done to the box.

The questions for this section are ready for you now, and you have everything you need. For each force in them, name the culprit in your head — who is pushing or pulling — and check whether mass and weight have been swapped. Two small habits, and these marks come easily.

## Subtopic: A Dot and Four Arrows

Now the drawing, which is far simpler than it sounds, because most of it is throwing things away.

There are two pictures in this topic. In the first, you sketch the real scene — the box, the floor beneath it, the rope going up to a hand — and lay the force arrows on top of that scene. That is a force diagram: the whole story with the forces added.

In the second, you delete the scene. The box shrinks to one dot in the middle of the page. The floor is gone, the rope is gone, the person is gone. Only the force arrows are left, every single one starting at that dot and pointing away from it. That is the free-body diagram, and the "free" means free of the scenery.

Hear ours being built, arrow by arrow. From the dot, an arrow straight down the page, labelled w — the weight, 49 newtons of Earth pulling. From the same dot, an arrow straight up the page, labelled N — the normal force, the floor's push. From the same dot, an arrow going up and to the right on a shallow slant, thirty degrees above the horizontal, labelled F applied — the rope's pull, with the thirty-degree angle written in the gap between that arrow and the horizontal. And from the same dot, an arrow straight to the left, flat and level, labelled f — the friction, set against the sliding, which is to the right. Four arrows leaving one dot, like four spokes of a wheel pointing different ways.

Markers check small things, so know them. Every arrow starts on the dot and points outwards. Every arrow ends in an arrowhead, or it has not shown a direction. Every arrow carries its label, because an unlabelled arrow tells the marker nothing at all. Where sizes are known, bigger forces get longer arrows. And nothing else may appear: no floor, no rope, no box outline, and above all no arrow for the speed — a velocity arrow sitting among the forces is an instant deduction, because speed is not a force.

Take your questions for this section now, and keep the dot in your mind while you answer: down for weight, up for the normal force, slanted forward and up for the rope, backwards for friction. If you can say those four arrows out loud in order, you can produce them under exam pressure — and that is the real win here.

## Subtopic: Why the Slant Helps — and What "Constant Speed" Is Telling You

Two pieces of gold are left in this problem, and both come free once the arrows are in place.

First: why does the examiner bother to say the box moves at constant velocity? Because that phrase is a fact in disguise. Anything moving at a steady speed in a straight line is not being pushed around on balance — the forces on it cancel out exactly. So "constant velocity" is the examiner quietly telling you that forward and backward are equal, and up and down are equal. Nothing is winning, so nothing changes.

Go one direction at a time. Sideways: the rope pulls the box forward, friction drags it back, and those two must match. But the rope is slanted, so only part of its pull works sideways. Think of pulling a wheeled suitcase by the handle — some of your effort drags it along the floor, and some of it lifts the handle end. One pull, two jobs. Physics calls the two jobs the horizontal component and the vertical component, and the sideways balance reads: the horizontal part of the rope's pull equals the friction. In symbols, F applied times cosine of thirty degrees equals f.

Up and down: three things are at work — the floor pushing up, the weight of 49 newtons pulling down, and the lifting part of the rope's pull helping upward. Balanced means the two upward effects together equal the one downward effect: the normal force plus the rope's upward part equals the weight. In symbols, N plus F applied times sine of thirty degrees equals w.

Now the sentence that separates the best answers from the rest. Because the rope is carrying a little of the load, the floor no longer has to carry the whole 49 newtons — so the normal force here is LESS than the weight. Anyone who learned "the normal force equals the weight" as a law has memorised a coincidence: it is only true when nothing else pushes or pulls vertically. Read the up-and-down balance every time instead of assuming.

And here is a bonus you have felt without naming. Friction grows and shrinks with the normal force, so easing the floor's push eases the friction too — which is exactly why dragging a heavy bag by a strap slanted up over your shoulder is easier than dragging it flat along the ground. Your physics has just explained your own back.

These are the last questions of the lesson, and they are with you now. Ask two things as you answer each one: what is balancing what, and has the rope's upward help been counted? If you can explain in your own words why the floor pushes with less than 49 newtons, you have got the sharpest idea in this whole topic — and you are ready for Newton's laws next year.
