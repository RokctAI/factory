**Student:** How can the answer be 20 N? There's 80 newtons of force in that problem — where did the other 60 go?

**Thandi:** Nothing vanished — the forces spent themselves fighting each other! Picture a tug-of-war: one team pulls east with 30 N, the other west with 50 N. Thirty newtons from each side lock together and cancel — that's 60 N busy neutralising itself — leaving 20 N of *unmatched* westward pull. The object only ever feels the leftover. The resultant isn't "all the force in the problem"; it's the net effect after the battle. Adding to 80 would only be right if both forces pulled the SAME way — teammates, not opponents.

**Student:** Why is distance a scalar but displacement a vector? They're both just "how far".

**Thandi:** They answer two different questions! Distance answers "how much ground did you cover?" — every step counts, direction irrelevant. Displacement answers "where did you END UP relative to where you started — and in which direction?" Run a full 400 m lap: distance 400 m (you definitely did the work — your legs know it!), but displacement zero, because you finished exactly where you began. Net position change: none. One number describes the journey; the other describes the net result. That's the scalar/vector split in a nutshell.

**Student:** Does it matter which direction I pick as positive? What if I choose west instead of east?

**Thandi:** Pick whichever you like — physics doesn't care, as long as you're loyal to your choice! Watch: with west positive, our forces become −30 (east) and +50 (west). Add: +20 — positive, which in THIS convention means west. Same answer: 20 N west. The convention is just a translation dictionary between directions and signs; any dictionary works if you use it consistently. The only crime is switching dictionaries mid-problem — east-positive on line one, west-positive by line three — that's how signs scramble. Declare once at the top, underline it, stay loyal.

**Student:** For the classification questions, is there a trick for remembering which quantities are vectors? I keep second-guessing energy.

**Thandi:** Drop the memorising and run the test live: try to attach a direction and see if the sentence survives. "A force of 30 N... eastward" — survives, needs it even. Vector. "500 joules of energy... northward"? The sentence collapses — energy in a direction is meaningless, a battery doesn't point. Scalar! Energy trips people up because it *feels* powerful and dynamic, but feelings aren't the test — needing a direction is. Same for speed (speedometer doesn't care where you're pointed — scalar) versus velocity (60 km/h NORTH — vector). When in doubt, build the sentence and listen for the nonsense.
