**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! The first one is the loudest: how can the answer be 20 newtons when there is 80 newtons of force in the problem? Where did the other 60 newtons go?

**Tutor:** Nothing went anywhere — the sixty newtons are spent fighting. Hold the tug-of-war in your mind: one team pulls east with thirty newtons, the other pulls west with fifty. Thirty of the westward newtons are fully occupied neutralising the thirty eastward ones, and that pairing accounts for sixty newtons of effort that produces no movement at all. Twenty westward newtons are left with nobody to fight, and those twenty are the only ones the crate ever feels. That leftover is the resultant: the single force that would produce exactly the same effect as the whole struggle acting together. So the resultant is not "all the force in the problem" — it is the net effect after the cancelling. Eighty newtons would be correct only if the two forces were teammates, both pulling the same way, because magnitudes add only when directions agree. And notice the arithmetic still balances perfectly: thirty of the fifty westward newtons are matched, twenty are unmatched, and fifty minus thirty is twenty newtons west. The energy of both teams is real; the net force is what survives the argument.

***

**Thandi:** Next: why is distance a scalar but displacement a vector? People keep saying both of them just mean "how far".

**Tutor:** They answer two different questions, and the words hide it. Distance answers "how much ground was covered?" — every metre of the path counts, whichever way it wandered, and no direction is attached, so it is a scalar. Displacement answers "where did the object end up relative to where it started, and in which direction?" — only the start and the finish are consulted, in a straight line, with the direction included, which makes it a vector. Take one full lap of a 400-metre track. Distance: 400 metres, every metre genuinely run. Displacement: zero metres, because the finish line and the start line are the same place. The runner did the work and achieved no net change of position. Because speed is distance divided by time and velocity is displacement divided by time, the same split runs through them: for an 80-second lap the average speed is 400 metres divided by 80 seconds, which is 5 metres per second, while the average velocity is 0 metres divided by 80 seconds, which is zero. Both statements are true about the same run. Read the question carefully and answer the one that was asked.

***

**Thandi:** Then this one, which worried a lot of people: does it actually matter which direction I choose as positive? What if I choose west instead of east?

**Tutor:** It does not matter at all, provided you stay loyal to the choice. The sign convention is only a translation between directions and signs; any consistent dictionary produces the same physics. Work our problem with west positive and watch. The fifty-newton westward force becomes plus fifty newtons; the thirty-newton eastward force becomes minus thirty newtons. Add them: plus fifty plus minus thirty is plus twenty newtons. Positive, in this convention, means west — so the resultant is twenty newtons west, exactly as before. Same magnitude, same direction, different bookkeeping. The one thing that must never happen is changing conventions inside a single solution: east positive in the first line and west positive by the third line scrambles every sign that follows, and it is impossible for a marker to award method marks to a solution that contradicts itself. So write the convention down as your first line — "take east as positive" — and treat it as binding until the answer is stated. Then translate the final sign back into a direction in words, because the memo wants "west", not "minus".

***

**Thandi:** Last one, in two parts: does a minus sign mean the force is somehow smaller? And why does energy keep getting classified as a vector?

**Tutor:** Take the minus sign first. In one dimension a sign is a direction label, never a size. Minus twenty newtons and plus twenty newtons are equally strong pushes that point opposite ways; the magnitude of a vector is its size only, always stated as a positive number with a unit, and the direction is carried separately in words. So minus twenty newtons, with east positive, is reported as twenty newtons west — and a magnitude written with a minus sign in a final answer will cost you. Now energy. It gets misclassified because the word feels forceful and dynamic, and because joules turn up in problems full of vectors. But run the test rather than the feeling: try to attach a direction and see whether the sentence survives. "Five hundred joules of energy, northward" means nothing — a charged battery holds five hundred joules whichever way it lies on the table, and nobody can point to where the energy is aimed. No direction is needed, so energy is a scalar. Apply the same test to speed and velocity: a speedometer reading of sixty kilometres per hour is complete on its own, so speed is a scalar, while sixty kilometres per hour north is a velocity, and the north is not optional.
