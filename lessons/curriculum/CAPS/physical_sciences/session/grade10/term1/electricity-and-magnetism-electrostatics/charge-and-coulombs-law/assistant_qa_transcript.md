**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! The first one is the one everybody asks: if protons carry positive charge, why can an object never become positive by gaining protons? Surely that would work just as well?

**Tutor:** It would, if protons were free to move, and they simply are not. A proton lives inside the nucleus of an atom, bound there by the strong nuclear force, which at that range is vastly more powerful than any electrical effect you can produce by rubbing a rod on a cloth. Moving a proton out of a nucleus means changing the element itself — turning one kind of atom into another — and that is nuclear physics, not electrostatics. Electrons, by contrast, sit on the outside of the atom, and the outermost ones are held relatively loosely, so friction, contact with another object, or a nearby charged body can move them without disturbing the nucleus at all. So the whole of electrostatics is written in one currency: electron movement. An object becomes negative because it gained electrons, and positive because it lost them — a positive object is one with a shortage of electrons, not a surplus of protons. This matters in the exam because explanation questions are marked on the mechanism, not the outcome. "The cloth became positive because it lost electrons to the rod" earns full marks; "the cloth gained protons" describes an event that never happened, and it will be marked wrong even where the sign is right.

***

**Thandi:** Next: why does the sharing rule divide by two? Some of us used it on spheres of different sizes and got it wrong, and we do not understand what the difference is.

**Tutor:** The rule divides by two because of symmetry, and the moment the symmetry breaks, the rule breaks with it. When two conducting spheres touch, they become a single conductor, and charge on a conductor spreads out until it is in equilibrium — until no piece of charge is being pushed towards anywhere better. If the two spheres are identical in size and shape, there is nothing to distinguish one from the other, so equilibrium must place exactly the same amount on each. Separate them and each carries half the total, which is what Q equals Q one plus Q two over two says. But if one sphere is larger, its surface offers more room, and the charges spread further apart on it and repel each other less, so equilibrium puts MORE charge on the larger sphere and less on the smaller one — not an equal split. Grade 10 does not ask you to calculate that unequal split; it asks you to know the rule's condition. So read every question for the word "identical", and if it is absent, do not halve. What always survives, whatever the sizes, is conservation: the total charge after contact equals the total before, and you can use that to check any answer you produce.

***

**Thandi:** This one confused a lot of us: how can a charged balloon stick to a neutral wall? If the wall has no charge, there should be no force at all.

**Tutor:** The wall has no NET charge, which is a very different thing from having no charge. Every wall is packed with positive nuclei and negative electrons in equal numbers, and normally they are so evenly mixed that their effects cancel from a distance. Bring a negatively charged balloon close, and that even mixing is disturbed. Within each molecule in the wall's surface, the electron cloud is repelled slightly away from the balloon while the positive nucleus is pulled slightly towards it. Each molecule stays neutral overall, but it becomes lopsided — one end a little positive, the other a little negative. That is polarisation. Now the surface of the wall facing the balloon is very slightly positive and the layer just behind it very slightly negative. The balloon's negative charge is attracted to the near positive layer and repelled by the further negative layer, and because electrostatic force weakens sharply with distance, the attraction to the nearer layer wins. The result is a small net attraction, and the balloon hangs there. The same reasoning explains a charged ruler picking up paper scraps, and a comb bending a thin stream of water from a tap. In an exam, the words worth writing are "the neutral object becomes polarised, the near side acquires the opposite charge, and since the opposite charge is closer, attraction results".

**Thandi:** Last one: in Coulomb's law, why does doubling the distance quarter the force instead of halving it? It feels like it should just be twice as weak.

**Tutor:** Because the distance appears squared in the denominator, and the reason it is squared is geometric rather than arbitrary. Picture the influence of a charge spreading outward equally in every direction, like paint sprayed from a single point. At any distance, all of that influence is spread over the surface of an imaginary sphere centred on the charge — and the surface area of a sphere grows as the square of its radius. Go twice as far out and the same influence is spread over four times the area, so the amount arriving at any one spot is a quarter. Go three times as far and it is spread over nine times the area, giving a ninth. That is where the square comes from, and the same geometry gives Newton's law of gravitation its inverse square as well; you will meet it again in Grade 11. Two practical consequences for your working. First, never forget to square r, because it is the single commonest error in the topic and it turns a correct method into a wrong answer. Second, convert to metres before squaring: substituting 30 instead of 0,30 changes the denominator by a factor of ten thousand, and an answer wrong by four orders of magnitude cannot be rescued by anything else on the page.
