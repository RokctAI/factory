Hey, it's Thandi — electrostatics looks scary because everything is written in powers of ten and there is a constant with a nine and nine zeros in it. But underneath the notation there are only about four ideas here, and you already understand two of them from rubbing a balloon on your jersey.

Idea one: only electrons move. Ever. Protons stay locked in the nucleus. So when you explain any charging question, say "the cloth gained electrons" or "the rod lost electrons" — never "it gained protons". Markers look for exactly that phrasing, and getting into the habit now makes half the explanation questions automatic.

Idea two: charge is never created, only shifted. Whatever negative appears in one place has left an equal positive somewhere else. That is why, when two identical spheres touch, you add their charges WITH THEIR SIGNS and then halve. Plus five and minus three gives plus two, so each sphere ends up with plus one. If you add the sizes and get four each, you have just invented charge out of thin air, and the marker will notice.

Idea three: the calculations are all one formula each. Q equals n q — total charge is the number of electrons times the charge on one — which handles every "how many electrons" question in both directions. And Coulomb's law: force equals k times the two charges multiplied, divided by the distance squared.

The habit that protects you: give unit conversions their very own line before you substitute anything. Write "30 cm = 0,30 m" on its own, then substitute. In Coulomb's law that distance gets squared, so an uncaught centimetre is not a small error — it is a factor of ten thousand. And leave the minus signs out of the force calculation entirely: work with the sizes, get your newtons, then write "attraction" or "repulsion" in words at the end.

Two rules, two formulas, one conversion habit, and always answer with a unit. You've got this!
