# Part 1 — Expert

Electricity begins with a single property of matter: charge. This session establishes what charge is, the two rules it never breaks — conservation and quantisation — the three ways an object becomes charged, and then the law that turns the vague statement "like charges repel" into a number in newtons. Every calculation here runs on prefixes and powers of ten, and the arithmetic punishes a missed conversion or an unsquared distance more severely than almost anything else in Paper 1.

## Subtopic: Charge, Its Unit, and Quantisation

There are exactly TWO kinds of electric charge, named positive and negative. Protons in the nucleus carry positive charge; electrons carry negative charge; neutrons carry none. An object with equal numbers of protons and electrons is NEUTRAL — not chargeless, but balanced.

The SI unit of charge is the COULOMB, symbol C. A coulomb is an enormous amount of charge in everyday terms, which is why electrostatics problems are full of microcoulombs and nanocoulombs: one microcoulomb, written μC, is 10⁻⁶ C; one nanocoulomb, nC, is 10⁻⁹ C.

Charge is QUANTISED. That means charge exists only in whole-number multiples of a fundamental unit — the magnitude of the charge on one electron, symbol e, with the value 1,6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C. You can have the charge of three electrons or of three million electrons, but never of two and a half. The relationship is Q = nq, read aloud as "Q equals n q — the total charge equals the number of charged particles times the charge on one of them".

Worked example. A small sphere carries a charge of −4,8 × 10⁻¹⁷ C. How many excess electrons does it hold? Formula: Q = nq, so n = Q ÷ q. Substitute the magnitudes: n = (4,8 × 10⁻¹⁷) ÷ (1,6 × 10⁻¹⁹). Answer: n = 300 electrons. The negative sign tells you they are EXCESS ELECTRONS, not missing ones, and the answer has no unit because it is a count.

Run it the other way too. If an object has lost 5 × 10¹⁰ electrons, its charge is Q = nq = (5 × 10¹⁰)(1,6 × 10⁻¹⁹) = 8 × 10⁻⁹ C, and the sign is POSITIVE, because losing electrons leaves a proton surplus.

The questions for this section are with you now: define quantisation in your own words, use Q = nq in both directions, and get the sign of the answer right by reasoning about electrons gained or lost.

## Subtopic: Three Ways to Charge an Object

Only ELECTRONS move. Protons are locked in nuclei and never transfer in these processes — so every charging explanation must be written in terms of electrons gained or electrons lost. Write "the cloth gained electrons", never "the rod gained protons".

CHARGING BY FRICTION, also called tribo-electric charging, works on insulators. Rub two different insulating materials together and one of them holds electrons more strongly than the other, so electrons transfer from the weaker holder to the stronger. Rub a plastic pen on your school jersey and the pen typically gains electrons and becomes negative while the jersey, having lost them, becomes positive. Which way round depends on the pair of materials, and a tribo-electric series lists materials in order of how strongly they hold electrons.

CHARGING BY CONTACT works on conductors. Touch a charged object to a neutral conductor and charge is shared between them, so both end up with the SAME SIGN of charge. Touch a negatively charged rod to a neutral metal sphere and electrons spread onto the sphere; the sphere becomes negative too.

CHARGING BY INDUCTION also works on conductors, and it produces the OPPOSITE sign without any contact. Bring a negatively charged rod near a neutral metal sphere without touching. Electrons in the sphere are repelled to the far side, leaving the near side positive — the sphere is polarised but still neutral overall. Now earth the far side briefly, by touching it with a finger, and the repelled electrons flow away to earth. Remove the finger, then remove the rod, and the sphere is left with an overall POSITIVE charge — the opposite of the rod.

Insulators cannot conduct charge away, but they still respond: their molecules become POLARISED, with the electron clouds shifting slightly so that the side nearest the charged object develops the opposite charge. That is why a charged ruler attracts small neutral scraps of paper, and why a balloon rubbed on your jersey sticks to a wall.

Take the questions for this section now, and state each explanation strictly in terms of electron movement, naming friction, contact or induction and the sign it produces.

## Subtopic: Conservation of Charge and the Sharing Rule

The PRINCIPLE OF CONSERVATION OF CHARGE states that charge cannot be created or destroyed — it can only be transferred from one object to another. In an isolated system the total charge before an interaction equals the total charge after it. Rubbing a rod on a cloth does not manufacture charge; it moves electrons, so whatever negative charge appears on one appears as an equal positive charge on the other.

That principle produces a rule the examination uses constantly. When two IDENTICAL conducting spheres touch and are then separated, the total charge is shared equally between them: Q = (Q₁ + Q₂) ÷ 2 — in words, the charge on each sphere afterwards equals the sum of the original charges divided by two. Note the two conditions: the spheres must be identical, and the charges are added WITH THEIR SIGNS.

Worked example. Sphere A carries +5 nC and sphere B carries −3 nC. They are brought into contact and separated. Find the final charge on each, and the number of electrons transferred. Final charge: Q = (Q₁ + Q₂) ÷ 2 = (+5 + (−3)) ÷ 2 = (+2) ÷ 2 = +1 nC on each sphere. Check conservation: +1 plus +1 gives +2 nC, exactly the total before. Now the electrons. Sphere A went from +5 nC to +1 nC, so it gained 4 nC of negative charge, which is 4 × 10⁻⁹ C. Number of electrons: n = Q ÷ q = (4 × 10⁻⁹) ÷ (1,6 × 10⁻¹⁹) = 2,5 × 10¹⁰ electrons, transferred from B to A.

Two traps live here. First, adding the magnitudes and ignoring the signs: 5 plus 3 over 2 gives 4 nC, which is wrong and breaks conservation. Second, applying the halving rule to spheres of different size — the rule holds only for identical spheres.

Work through the questions for this section now, keeping the signs inside the bracket and checking conservation on every answer before you write it down.

## Subtopic: Coulomb's Law and the Inverse Square

Like charges repel and unlike charges attract — that much is qualitative. COULOMB'S LAW makes it a number, and although it is examined formally in Grade 11, meeting it now makes sense of everything above.

The law states that the electrostatic force between two point charges is directly proportional to the product of their charges and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them. In symbols: F = kQ₁Q₂ ÷ r². In words: F equals k Q one Q two over r squared — the force equals Coulomb's constant times the product of the charges, divided by the distance squared. Coulomb's constant k has the value 9 × 10⁹ N·m²·C⁻², and r is measured in metres between the centres of the charges.

Worked example. A charge of +3 × 10⁻⁶ C is placed 0,30 m from a charge of −4 × 10⁻⁶ C. Find the magnitude of the force. Formula: F = kQ₁Q₂ ÷ r². Substitute magnitudes only: F = (9 × 10⁹)(3 × 10⁻⁶)(4 × 10⁻⁶) ÷ (0,30)². The numerator is 0,108; the denominator is 0,09. Answer: F = 1,2 N, and because the charges are unlike, the force is one of ATTRACTION.

Now the inverse square, which examiners love. Move the same charges to 0,60 m apart — double the distance. The denominator becomes 0,36, and F = 0,108 ÷ 0,36 = 0,3 N. Doubling the separation cut the force to a QUARTER, not to a half. Triple the distance and the force falls to a ninth.

The traps, named. One: forgetting to square r — the single commonest error in the topic. Two: leaving centimetres in the substitution; 30 cm must be written as 0,30 m, and since r is squared, that slip is a factor of ten thousand. Three: putting the negative signs into the formula, which yields a meaningless negative force; substitute magnitudes and then state attraction or repulsion in words. Four: dropping the prefix, since a microcoulomb is 10⁻⁶ C and a nanocoulomb is 10⁻⁹ C. Five: omitting the unit — the answer is in newtons.

The final questions of this part are in front of you now: quote the law, substitute magnitudes in SI units, square the distance, and finish with a direction stated in words.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same charge from a balloon on a jersey, a doorknob that bites in winter and two people sharing a taxi fare — same rules, same answers, plain words first.

## Subtopic: The Balloon and the School Jersey

Rub a balloon on your jersey, hold it against the classroom wall, and let go. It sticks. Nothing was glued, nothing was heated — so what happened?

Every object is built from bits that carry a tiny electrical property we call charge, and it comes in two flavours: positive and negative. Normally an object has exactly as much of one as the other, so the two cancel out and we call it neutral. Rubbing breaks that balance.

Here is the crucial fact, and the whole topic rests on it: only the negative bits — the electrons — ever move. The positive bits are locked deep inside the atoms and stay put, always. So when you rub the balloon on your jersey, some electrons are scraped off the jersey and stick onto the balloon. Now the balloon has extra negatives and is negatively charged; the jersey is short of negatives and is positively charged. Nothing was created. Something was simply moved from one place to the other — and that is the conservation rule in one sentence.

Then why does the balloon stick to a wall that was never rubbed at all? Because the balloon's extra negatives push the wall's negatives slightly away from the surface, leaving the face of the wall slightly positive. Opposite charges pull towards each other, and the balloon hangs there. The same trick makes a rubbed ruler pick up little scraps of paper, and it is why your hair follows a comb on a dry Highveld morning.

And because electrons are what move, charge always comes in whole electrons — never half of one. That is what the fancy word "quantised" means. Count in electrons the way you count coins: you can have three coins or thirty, but not two and a half.

Quick check before we go on — questions on the balloon are with you now, especially on which bits move and which never do.

## Subtopic: Three Ways to Get Charged, and Sharing the Total

Three ways to charge something up, and each has a picture.

RUBBING is the balloon story. Two insulators, one grabs electrons harder than the other, electrons move across, and the two end up with opposite charges. That is why winter is the season for shocks: dry air, jerseys, carpets, and a doorknob that bites when you touch it. You picked up extra electrons walking across the floor, and the metal knob is the first easy escape route they find.

TOUCHING is for metals. Bring a charged thing into contact with a neutral metal thing and the extra electrons simply spread out across both. They now share the charge, so they end up with the SAME sign — negative touches neutral, both come out negative.

BRINGING NEAR, without touching, is the clever one. Hold something negative close to a metal ball. The electrons in the ball are pushed to the far side, so the near side is left positive. Now touch the far side with your finger for a moment — the crowded electrons run off through you into the ground. Take your finger away first, then take the rod away, and the ball is left positive: the opposite of what you brought near it. That is charging by induction.

And when two identical metal balls touch, they simply split the total evenly, like two friends putting their taxi fare into one pocket and splitting it back out. If one has plus 5 and the other has minus 3, add them with their signs — plus 5 and minus 3 gives plus 2 — then share: plus 1 each. Notice you must add the signs, not the sizes. Adding 5 and 3 to get 4 each would mean charge appeared out of nowhere, and charge never does that.

Your questions on this part are up now: name the three methods, say what sign each produces, and add the signs before you halve.

## Subtopic: Close Is Strong

Last piece: how hard do two charges actually push or pull?

Two things decide it, and both are obvious once said. Bigger charges push harder — double one charge and you double the force. And distance matters enormously, but not in the way people expect. Move twice as far apart and the force does not halve. It drops to a QUARTER. Move three times as far and it drops to a ninth. Distance gets squared, so it bites twice.

Think of it like standing near a fire. Step back a little and the heat drops off far faster than you would guess, because the warmth spreads out over a bigger and bigger area as it travels. Electric force spreads the same way, which is why the tidy name for this is the inverse square rule.

Put the two ideas together and you have Coulomb's law: force equals a constant, times the two charges multiplied together, divided by the distance squared. The constant is nine times ten to the nine, and the answer comes out in newtons.

Run one. Two charges, three millionths of a coulomb and four millionths of a coulomb, sitting thirty centimetres apart. Thirty centimetres is 0,30 of a metre — turn it into metres BEFORE anything else, because that number is about to be squared, and a slip there is a slip of ten thousand times. Multiply the top: nine times ten to the nine, times three millionths, times four millionths, gives 0,108. Square the bottom: 0,30 squared is 0,09. Divide: 1,2 newtons. One charge is positive and the other negative, so they pull towards each other — attraction.

Now shift them to sixty centimetres, double the distance. The bottom becomes 0,36, and the answer becomes 0,3 newtons. Exactly a quarter, just as promised.

Two habits carry this: metres before you square, and never put the minus signs into the sum — work with the sizes, get your newtons, then say in words whether they pull or push. Last questions of the lesson are with you right now, and if you remember only one thing, let it be that squaring the distance is where the marks are won.
