**Thandi:** Before you close your books — the four questions that came up most. First: the lesson said sound is a wave of pressure, not moving air. But when a speaker plays loudly I can feel air on my hand right in front of it. Isn't that the air travelling with the sound?

**Tutor:** What you feel at close range is real, but it is not the sound — and separating the two is exactly the understanding the definition wants. Right against the cone there are small local air movements, and at high volume you can feel that flutter. But follow the sound across the room and ask what makes the journey. Each molecule near the speaker rocks forward a fraction of a millimetre, shoves its neighbours, and rocks back to where it started — that is its whole trip. What crosses the room is the pattern: a compression handed from layer to layer of air, like the squash travelling up the taxi queue while every person stays in their place. Here is the test that settles it. Sound covers about 340 metres every second. If sound were a wind of travelling air, every note from a loudspeaker would hit you as a 340-metre-per-second gale — faster than any storm ever recorded — and a whisper across a library would part your hair. It does not, because the air stays home and only the pressure pattern, carrying energy, arrives at your ear. So in an answer, write it in two halves: the particles vibrate about fixed positions, parallel to the direction of travel; the compressions and rarefactions — and the energy — are what actually propagate.

***

**Thandi:** Next one: we learned sound is fastest in solids. But a brick wall muffles sound, and a closed door makes everything quieter. If solids carry sound so well, why do walls block it?

**Tutor:** Because two different questions are hiding in one observation — how fast sound travels INSIDE a material, and how much sound gets INTO it at the surface. The ranking — solid fastest, liquid, then gas — answers the first question only: once vibrations are inside steel or brick, the tightly bound particles pass them on at thousands of metres per second. Press your ear against a steel gate while a friend taps the far end and you hear the tap sharp and early, through metres of solid — that is the classic proof. What a wall does to room noise is a different story, told at the boundary. When sound in air strikes a dense solid surface, the air's gentle pressure variations struggle to shake something so heavy, so most of the energy REFLECTS back into the room and only a small fraction enters the wall to be carried on and partly absorbed. The muffling happens at the crossing, not in the material. Keep the two sentences apart and use each where it belongs: within a medium, tighter binding means faster sound; between media, a large mismatch means strong reflection. That second sentence, by the way, is exactly why sonar works — the seabed and a shoal of fish both bounce sound back up to the ship.

***

**Thandi:** Then the one that half the class swapped: a singer holds one note and slowly gets louder. What changed in the wave, what stayed the same — and why does turning up a speaker not change the pitch?

**Tutor:** Run the two dials separately. The note the singer holds is the pitch, and pitch is set by frequency — how many compressions leave her throat each second. As long as she holds the same note, that count does not move. Getting louder means her vocal cords swing with a bigger amplitude: each compression is packed harder, each rarefaction stretched thinner, so more energy rides every cycle to your ear. So in the wave: amplitude grew, frequency, wavelength and speed all held still — the speed because the air is unchanged, the wavelength because v and f both stood firm in v equals f lambda. Now the loudspeaker: the volume knob is an amplitude knob. It makes the cone sweep further on every swing, but it does not change how many swings per second the recording tells the cone to make — that timing is baked into the signal. Bigger sweeps, same rhythm: louder, same pitch. If you want a one-line memory hook, use the drum: hit it harder and it is the same drum note, only bigger — how hard is loudness, how often is pitch. And keep quality in reserve for the third kind of question: two instruments on the same note at the same loudness still sound different, because each stacks its own faint extra tones on top, giving its waveform a recognisable shape.

**Thandi:** Last one, on the echo sums: people either forget to halve, or they halve when they should not. How do we know, quickly and safely, when the factor of two belongs in the answer?

**Tutor:** Ask one question of every problem before touching the calculator: whose journey does the clock measure? Draw the journey as an arrow diagram if you have any doubt. If the sound leaves the source, bounces, and returns to a detector at the SAME place — a clap coming back from a quarry wall, a sonar ping returning to the boat, a bat hearing its own squeak — then the clock timed a there-and-back journey, and the distance you are asked for is half of speed times time. Write the total first, then halve visibly: 340 times 0,9 gives 306 metres of travelling, wall at 153. The marker must see the division by two on paper. But if the sound makes a ONE-WAY trip — thunder rolling from a lightning strike to your ears, a starter's gun heard across a field — there was no bounce, the clock timed a single leg, and halving would wrongly shrink the answer. The arrow diagram makes it impossible to confuse: two arrowheads means divide by two, one arrowhead means leave it alone. Add the unit patrol — kilohertz into hertz, so 60 kHz enters as 60 000 — and the standard three lines, formula, substitution, answer with unit, and every echo, sonar and echolocation question in this topic follows the same safe road.
