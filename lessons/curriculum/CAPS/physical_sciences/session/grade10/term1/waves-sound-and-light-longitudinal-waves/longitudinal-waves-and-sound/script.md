# Part 1 — Expert

Transverse waves move the medium across the line of travel. This session takes the other family — the waves that move the medium along that same line — and follows them into the most familiar example there is. What a compression and a rarefaction are, why sound is longitudinal and why it dies in a vacuum, how the medium fixes its speed, what pitch and loudness actually measure, and how echo and sonar calculations are set out so the there-and-back factor is never lost.

## Subtopic: Compressions, Rarefactions and the Longitudinal Wave

A LONGITUDINAL WAVE is one in which the particles of the medium vibrate parallel to — in the same line as — the direction in which the wave travels. Compare that with the transverse case, where the vibration is perpendicular to the travel. That single sentence is the whole distinction, and it is the one examiners ask for.

Take a slinky stretched along a desk and push one end sharply forward, along the spring rather than across it. A bunched-up region races down the spring. Where the coils are pressed close together, particles are crowded and the pressure and density are HIGHER: that region is a COMPRESSION. Immediately behind it the coils are pulled further apart than normal, particles are spread out, and pressure and density are LOWER: that region is a RAREFACTION. Keep pushing and pulling rhythmically and you get compression, rarefaction, compression, rarefaction, marching away from your hand.

Every quantity from the transverse work carries over, redefined for this shape. The WAVELENGTH is the distance between two successive compressions, or equally between two successive rarefactions — the distance between successive points in phase — measured in metres. The AMPLITUDE is still the maximum displacement of a particle from its rest position, in metres; a bigger amplitude means the coils are driven harder and the compressions are more crowded. The PERIOD is the time for one complete wave to pass a point, in seconds. The FREQUENCY is the number of complete waves passing a point per second, in hertz.

And the two equations are untouched. f = 1/T — frequency is one divided by the period. And v = fλ — v equals f lambda, wave speed equals frequency times wavelength. Nothing about the geometry changes the mathematics; only the picture changes.

One more contrast worth banking: a longitudinal wave can travel through solids, liquids and gases alike, because every material resists being squeezed. Take the questions for this section now, holding the definitions of compression and rarefaction and the parallel-versus-perpendicular distinction exactly as stated.

## Subtopic: Sound as a Longitudinal Wave, and the Speed of the Medium

Sound is a longitudinal wave produced by a VIBRATING OBJECT. A guitar string, a speaker cone, a taxi hooter's diaphragm, your vocal cords — each pushes forward into the air, crowding the molecules in front of it into a compression, then pulls back, leaving a rarefaction. Those pressure variations travel outward, reach your eardrum, and drive it into the same vibration. Sound is a wave of pressure, not a wave of moving air: the molecules jostle their neighbours and return to where they began.

The immediate consequence is that sound REQUIRES A MEDIUM. In the classic bell-jar demonstration, an electric bell ringing inside a jar grows fainter and fainter as the air is pumped out, and in a good vacuum falls silent though the hammer is plainly still striking. No particles, no compressions, no sound. Space is silent, and any film that puts an explosion's roar in a vacuum is wrong.

The speed of sound is a property OF THE MEDIUM, not of the source. Shout softly or loudly, high or low: the sound leaves you at the same speed. What changes the speed is what the sound travels through, and the order is fixed. Sound travels FASTEST IN SOLIDS, more slowly in liquids, and slowest in gases — because the closer and more strongly bound the particles, the faster each one passes the disturbance to its neighbour. In air at ordinary room temperature the speed is about 340 m·s⁻¹; in water roughly 1 500 m·s⁻¹; in steel around 5 000 m·s⁻¹. In a gas, raising the temperature raises the speed, because the molecules already move faster and collide sooner.

Two numbers to keep. The AUDIBLE RANGE of human hearing runs from about 20 Hz to about 20 000 Hz; below that is infrasound, above it ultrasound. And the working value for the speed of sound in air, unless a question gives you another, is 340 m·s⁻¹.

The questions for this section are with you now: be ready to justify why sound cannot cross a vacuum, and to rank solids, liquids and gases by wave speed with the reason attached.

## Subtopic: Pitch, Loudness and Quality

Three things your ear can tell apart, and each is controlled by a different property of the wave. Keep them separate and this section is free marks.

PITCH is how high or low a note sounds, and it is determined by FREQUENCY. High frequency gives high pitch; low frequency gives low pitch. A thin, tight, short guitar string vibrates at high frequency and sounds high; a thick loose bass string vibrates at low frequency and sounds low. Notice that pitch has nothing to do with how hard you pluck.

LOUDNESS is how strong the sound seems, and it is determined by AMPLITUDE. A larger amplitude means the source pushed the air harder, the compressions are more crowded, more energy arrives at your ear, and the sound is louder. Pluck the same string harder and the pitch is identical while the loudness rises. Loudness also depends on the ear itself: human hearing is most sensitive to frequencies in the middle of the audible range, so two sounds of equal amplitude but very different frequency do not seem equally loud.

QUALITY, sometimes called tone or timbre, is what lets you recognise your mother's voice among a hundred, or tell a guitar from a marimba playing exactly the same note. It is determined by the WAVEFORM — the shape of the wave. Real sources never produce a single pure frequency; they add fainter higher frequencies on top of the main one, and the particular mix gives each source its own waveform and its own character.

Now a calculation joining pitch to spacing. A taxi hooter sounds a note of 850 Hz. Take the speed of sound in air as 340 m·s⁻¹ and find the wavelength. Formula: v = fλ, therefore λ = v ÷ f. Substitute: λ = 340 ÷ 850. Answer: λ = 0,4 m. Raise the pitch and that wavelength shortens, since the speed cannot change while the air is the same air.

Work through the questions for this section now, matching each of the three — pitch, loudness, quality — to frequency, amplitude and waveform without hesitation.

## Subtopic: Echoes, Ultrasound, and the Factor of Two

An ECHO is sound reflected from a hard surface and heard as a separate sound. Every echo calculation shares one structure, and one trap.

The trap first: the sound travels to the surface AND BACK. The distance covered is twice the distance to the surface. So v = 2d ÷ Δt, which rearranges to d = (v × Δt) ÷ 2 — the distance to the reflector is speed times time, divided by two. Learners who forget the two double every answer, and the memo has no mercy for it.

Worked example. A hiker in the Drakensberg shouts at a cliff face and hears the echo 1,2 s later. Take the speed of sound in air as 340 m·s⁻¹ and find the distance to the cliff. Formula: d = (v × Δt) ÷ 2. Substitute: d = (340 × 1,2) ÷ 2 = 408 ÷ 2. Answer: d = 204 m.

ULTRASOUND is sound of frequency above the upper limit of human hearing — above about 20 000 Hz, with the syllabus range running from 20 kHz to 100 kHz. It obeys exactly the same physics; we simply cannot hear it. Bats and dolphins emit ultrasound pulses and time the returning echoes to locate insects and fish in the dark: echolocation. Ships and trawlers do the same electronically with SONAR.

Worked example. A trawler off Saldanha Bay sends a sonar pulse straight down and detects the echo 0,4 s later. The speed of sound in seawater is 1 500 m·s⁻¹. Find the depth. Formula: d = (v × Δt) ÷ 2. Substitute: d = (1 500 × 0,4) ÷ 2 = 600 ÷ 2. Answer: d = 300 m.

Medically, different tissues reflect ultrasound differently, and a scanner turns those echoes into an image — the prenatal scan being the familiar case. Its advantage over X-rays is that it is NOT ionising radiation, so it does not damage cells. Ultrasound also breaks up kidney stones, cleans delicate instruments, and finds hidden cracks in metal castings.

Guard the units as fiercely as the factor of two: 40 kHz is 40 000 Hz, never 40. The final questions of this part are in front of you now — halve the round trip, convert the kilohertz, and name an ultrasound application with its reason.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same waves from a slinky on a desk, a queue at the taxi rank and a shout at a cliff — same definitions, same answers, plain words first.

## Subtopic: The Slinky and the Taxi Queue

Picture a long queue for a taxi at rank on a Friday afternoon. Someone at the back bumps the person in front of them. That person stumbles forward into the next one, who bumps the next, and a little squash travels all the way up the queue to the front. Nobody walked to the front. Everyone shuffled forward and settled back into their spot — but the squash ran the length of the line.

That squash is a compression: a place where people are packed tighter than usual. And just behind it, where everyone has been pushed forward and left a gap, the queue is stretched thin — that thin patch is a rarefaction. Bump the queue over and over, and squashes and gaps alternate all the way up: squash, gap, squash, gap.

Here is the important bit, and it is the whole definition. The people moved FORWARD AND BACK, along the same line the squash travelled. In the stadium wave, people moved up and down while the wave went sideways — across. In the queue, movement and travel are along the same line. That is what "longitudinal" means, and a slinky on a desk does exactly this if you push its end along instead of flicking it across.

Measuring it is no different from before. From one squash to the next squash is the wavelength. How far each person shuffles from their spot is the amplitude. How many squashes pass the front of the queue each second is the frequency, and how long one squash takes to pass is the period. Same four measurements, new picture.

Quick check before we move on — some questions on the queue are with you now, especially on which way the people move compared with which way the squash travels.

## Subtopic: Why Sound Is a Queue of Air

Now swap the people for air molecules and you have sound, exactly.

Something vibrates — a guitar string, a speaker cone, a hooter, your voice box. It pushes forward and squashes the air right in front of it. Then it pulls back and leaves a thin patch. Push, pull, push, pull, and squashes and thin patches run out through the room until one of them arrives at your eardrum and pushes it in. Your ear feels the squash, and you call it a sound.

So sound needs air — or water, or brick, or anything made of particles — because without particles there is nothing to squash. Put a ringing bell inside a jar and pump the air out and the ringing fades away to nothing while you can still see the hammer hitting. That is why space is silent. Movies get this wrong constantly.

And the stuff you send the sound through decides how fast it goes, not how loudly you shout. Think of the queue again. If people stand shoulder to shoulder, the squash reaches the front almost instantly. If they stand two metres apart, it takes ages. Solids are the tight queue, so sound moves fastest through them — about five thousand metres a second through steel. Water is the medium queue, about fifteen hundred. Air is the loose queue, about three hundred and forty metres a second. Same order every time: solid, then liquid, then gas.

One more familiar fact falls out of this. Lightning and thunder happen at the same instant, but light gets to you almost immediately while the thunder trudges through the air at 340 metres a second — which is why you count the gap.

Your questions on this part are up now: what sound actually is, why a vacuum is silent, and which medium carries sound fastest, with the reason.

## Subtopic: High, Loud, and the Round Trip

Three things your ear notices, and each has its own dial.

Turn a note HIGHER or LOWER and you are changing the frequency — how many squashes arrive per second. Lots of squashes per second sounds high; few sounds low. That is pitch, and it is set by frequency alone.

Turn a note LOUDER or SOFTER and you are changing the amplitude — how hard the source shoved the air. Big shove, crowded squashes, more energy at your ear, louder sound. Pluck a guitar string harder and it is the same note, just bigger. So: pitch is how fast, loudness is how hard. Mixing those two up is the most common slip in the whole topic.

And the third one is why your gogo's voice is recognisable in a crowded room even when she says nothing special. A real voice never makes one clean note — it adds faint extra tones on top, and that mixture gives the wave its own particular shape. That shape is the quality of the sound.

Now the sums, and there is only one thing to remember: the sound goes there AND comes back. Shout at a cliff in the Drakensberg and hear the echo after 1,2 seconds. In that time the sound covered 340 times 1,2 — that is 408 metres. But 408 metres is the whole round trip, so the cliff is half of that away: 204 metres. Same story under a boat off Saldanha. The sonar pulse returns after 0,4 seconds through seawater at 1 500 metres a second, so the total journey is 600 metres. Halve it: the sea floor is 300 metres down.

Bats do that trick in the dark with squeaks too high for us to hear — ultrasound, anything above about 20 000 hertz — and hospitals do it to see a baby before it is born, safely, because ultrasound is not the same kind of radiation as X-rays and does not harm cells.

Last questions of the lesson are with you right now: pitch on the frequency dial, loudness on the amplitude dial, and every echo answer halved before you write it down. Get the halving right and this whole topic pays you back.
