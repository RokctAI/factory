**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! The first one came up three times: if sound is air molecules bumping each other, why does my breath not blow across the room when I speak? Where does the air actually go?

**Tutor:** Nowhere at all, and that is the heart of what a wave is. When you speak, your vocal cords vibrate and your mouth pushes the layer of air right in front of it forward by a tiny amount. That layer crowds into the layer beyond it, which crowds into the next, and so on. But every one of those layers springs back to where it started, because air behaves like a set of tiny springs — squeeze it and it pushes back out again. So each molecule shuffles forward and back over a distance far smaller than a millimetre and stays in its own neighbourhood for the whole conversation. What travels across the room is the pattern of crowding, together with the energy needed to maintain it. Think of the taxi queue: the squash reaches the front of the line, but no person walked from the back to the front. If air really did travel from your mouth to your friend's ear, a long conversation would create a wind, and speaking would empty your lungs at the speed of sound. Instead sound is a pressure pattern moving through air that stays put — which is exactly why we call a wave a transfer of energy without a transfer of matter.

**Thandi:** Next: our class insisted that sound must travel faster in air than in water, because water is thick and heavy and hard to move through. The memo says the opposite. Why?

**Tutor:** The intuition comes from thinking about swimming, where water resists your body — but a sound wave is not swimming through the medium, it is being handed from particle to particle. What matters is two things: how close the particles are, and how strongly they are bound to each other. In air the molecules are far apart, so a disturbed molecule flies a relatively long way before it collides with the next one and passes the message on, and that travel time is dead time. In water the molecules are packed almost shoulder to shoulder and attract each other strongly, so the message is handed over almost immediately, and the wave moves at about 1 500 metres per second, roughly four to five times faster than the 340 metres per second in air. In a solid such as steel the particles are locked into a rigid structure and the speed rises again, to around 5 000 metres per second. So the order is solids fastest, then liquids, then gases. The rule of thumb to carry into the exam is: the stiffer and more closely packed the medium, the faster sound moves through it. And the practical test is one you can do yourself — press your ear to a long steel railing and have a friend tap the far end, and the tap through the metal reaches you noticeably before the tap through the air.

**Thandi:** This one caused real confusion in the calculations: why do we divide by two in echo questions but not in ordinary speed questions? Some of us divided by two in a question where we should not have.

**Tutor:** The division by two is not a rule about echoes; it is a consequence of the path the sound actually took, and that is how you should decide every time. The formula underneath is always the same: speed equals distance divided by time, where distance means the total path length travelled. In an ordinary question — a hooter sounds and you hear it 2 seconds later from 680 metres away — the sound made a one-way trip, so the total path is the distance between you and the source, and no halving is needed. In an echo question the sound left the source, travelled to a reflecting surface, and came back, so the total path is twice the distance to the surface. Multiply speed by time and you get that whole round trip; halve it to get the distance to the cliff or the sea floor. The safe method is to write one sentence before you calculate: "the sound travelled to the cliff and back". If that sentence is true, halve. If it is not, do not. Then work in the standard order — formula, substitution with units, answer with units — so that even if you slip on the halving, the method marks are safely on the page.

**Thandi:** Last one: if ultrasound is just sound we cannot hear, why is it so much more useful than ordinary sound for looking inside things?

**Tutor:** Because of its wavelength. Take the wave equation, v equals f lambda, and rearrange it: lambda equals v over f. The speed is fixed by the medium, so raising the frequency shortens the wavelength in exact proportion. A 340 hertz note in air has a wavelength of about a metre; an ultrasound pulse at 40 000 hertz has a wavelength of about 340 divided by 40 000, which is 0,0085 metres, or 8,5 millimetres — and in body tissue, where the speed is higher and the frequencies used are higher still, the wavelengths come down to a fraction of a millimetre. That matters because a wave can only resolve detail roughly as fine as its own wavelength. Long waves simply flow around small objects and tell you nothing about them; short waves reflect off them and carry back a usable echo. So ultrasound can pick out the outline of a foetus, a kidney stone or a crack inside a metal casting, where audible sound would give you nothing but a blur. The second advantage is safety: ultrasound is a mechanical pressure wave, not ionising radiation like X-rays, so it does not knock electrons off atoms and does not damage the cells it passes through. Fine detail plus safety is why hospitals reach for it.
