Hey, it's Thandi — if the words compression and rarefaction sound like something out of a different subject entirely, breathe. They are just posh names for "squashed together" and "spread apart". That is genuinely all they mean, and once you see them that way the whole topic gets much smaller.

Here is the shape of it. There is one new idea: in a longitudinal wave the particles move backwards and forwards along the same line the wave travels, instead of across it. Everything else you already learned with transverse waves comes across untouched — amplitude, wavelength, period, frequency, f equals one over T, and v equals f lambda. You are not learning a new set of formulas. You are learning a new picture for the ones you have.

Then there are three sound facts that carry most of the marks. Sound needs a medium, so a vacuum is silent. Sound goes fastest through solids, slower through liquids, slowest through gases, because tightly packed particles hand the disturbance on faster. And the speed belongs to the medium, not to you — shouting louder does not make it arrive sooner.

The habit that protects your marks: in every echo, sonar or bat question, write "there and back" at the top of your working before you do anything else, then divide by two. That single word "back" is worth more marks in this topic than any other. Total distance is speed times time; the distance to the wall or the sea floor is half of it.

And keep the three ear-words on separate shelves. Pitch is frequency — how many arrive per second. Loudness is amplitude — how hard the source pushed. Quality is the waveform — why a guitar and a marimba differ on the same note. If a question says "louder" and you reach for frequency, you have swapped shelves.

Two pictures, three sound facts, one halving habit. That is the topic. You've got this!
