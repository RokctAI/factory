**Thandi:** Before you move on — the questions that come up a lot with this topic. First: if electrons drift from negative to positive, why do we draw the current arrow the wrong way? It feels dishonest.

**Tutor:** It is not dishonest — it is a convention that predates the discovery, kept because it never breaks anything. When scientists first mapped circuits, nobody knew what actually moved in a wire; they guessed that something positive flowed, and drew arrows from the positive terminal around to the negative one. A century later the electron turned up, negatively charged, drifting the opposite way — and by then every law, every diagram and every instrument had been built around the original arrow. Here is the key insight: it makes no difference to the physics. A negative charge moving left is mathematically identical, in every circuit calculation, to a positive charge moving right — the energy delivered, the readings on the meters, the heat in the resistor all come out the same. So physics kept the old arrow and gave it a name, conventional current, and that is the arrow you draw in every diagram: out of the positive terminal, through the external circuit, into the negative terminal. The exam trap is a question that asks specifically about ELECTRON flow — then, and only then, you reverse: electrons drift from negative to positive. Read the question's exact word — current means the conventional arrow; electrons means the opposite direction — and both marks are safe.

***

**Thandi:** Next: people say a battery stores charge, and the lesson says that is wrong. What does a battery actually run out of?

**Tutor:** Energy — specifically, chemical energy — and getting that straight untangles half the beginner mistakes in circuits. The wire, the bulb and the battery are already full of charge before you connect anything: a metal is a lattice of ions swimming in free electrons, everywhere, always. The battery's job is not to supply those charges but to PUSH them — its internal chemical reactions do work on every coulomb that passes through, loading it with energy, joule by joule, according to the battery's voltage. A 1,5 volt cell loads 1,5 joules onto every coulomb; that is what the label means. The charge then circulates — never consumed, never diminished, arriving back at the battery in exactly the numbers it left — but the energy it carried has been spent in the bulb as light and heat. Eventually the chemicals inside the battery reach the end of their reaction: no more pushing, no more loading, and we say the battery is flat. Not one coulomb went missing in the process. The stadium picture holds it best: the supporters are never used up as they circulate through the gates; it is their ticket money that gets spent. When an exam asks why a battery goes flat, the full-marks sentence is: its store of chemical energy is depleted — the charge in the circuit is conserved throughout.

**Thandi:** Then the connection rules: in series for the ammeter, in parallel for the voltmeter. Is there a way to reason it out instead of memorising it?

**Tutor:** There is, and it starts from what each instrument claims to measure. An ammeter answers the question: how much charge passes THIS POINT per second? To count what passes a point you must stand at the point — a tollgate counts cars because every car drives through it. So the ammeter is spliced into the line, in series, and every coulomb it reports genuinely passed through its own works. That placement carries an obligation: sitting in the main road, the meter must not slow the traffic it counts, so it is built with the lowest possible resistance — effectively a plain piece of wire with a display. A voltmeter answers a completely different question: how much energy per coulomb is lost BETWEEN these two points? A between question needs two probes, one on each side of the component, which is exactly what an in-parallel connection is — measuring a waterfall's height with a mark at the top and a mark at the bottom. And that placement carries the opposite obligation: the voltmeter forms a side path around the component, so it must be almost impossible for current to take that shortcut — hence enormous resistance. Now the disasters explain themselves. Voltmeter in series: you have barricaded the main road with your high-resistance instrument, and the circuit dies. Ammeter in parallel: you have laid a copper shortcut across the component — nearly all the current floods through the meter, which is a short circuit. Reason from the question each meter asks, and the rules stop being memory work.

**Thandi:** Last one: why does a longer wire resist more but a thicker wire resist less? People keep flipping those two.

**Tutor:** Keep them straight by asking what each change does to the electrons' obstacle course. Resistance is born in collisions: electrons pushed along a metal keep striking the vibrating ions of the lattice, losing energy to them as heat. Lengthening the wire extends the obstacle course — every extra centimetre is extra lattice that every electron must fight through, so the collisions per journey rise in direct proportion, and so does the resistance. Double the length, double the resistance. Thickening the wire does something completely different: it does not shorten anyone's journey, it opens more parallel LANES. A wire twice as thick offers twice the cross-sectional area — twice as many side-by-side paths through the same length of lattice — so the flow divides itself among the lanes and passes with half the overall difficulty. More area, less resistance. The corridor at break time carries both rules at once: a longer corridor means more pushing to reach the end; a wider corridor lets the same crowd stream through easily. Then add the third dial, temperature: heat the corridor's occupants into wild motion and crossing gets harder — hot lattice ions vibrate more violently and collide with more electrons, so a metal's resistance climbs as it heats. The exam-day summary is six words long: longer — more; thicker — less; hotter — more. Say it against the corridor picture once and it never flips again.
