# Part 1 — Expert

Electrostatics dealt with charge standing still. This session sets it moving. The four foundation ideas of every circuit you will ever analyse: electric current and the coulomb-per-second that defines it, potential difference and the joule-per-coulomb that defines it, the two meters that measure them and the strict rules for connecting each, and resistance — what it is microscopically, and the three factors that control how much of it a conductor has.

## Subtopic: Current — Charge on the Move

ELECTRIC CURRENT is the rate of flow of charge — how much charge passes a point in a circuit per unit time. As an equation: I equals Q divided by delta t, where I is the current in amperes, Q is the charge in coulombs, and delta t is the time in seconds. One AMPERE is one coulomb of charge passing a point every second. The coulomb itself carries over from electrostatics: the SI unit of charge, and an enormous one — a single coulomb is the combined charge of about six billion billion electrons.

Work the definition both ways. If 30 C of charge passes through a torch bulb in 10 s, the current is I equals Q over delta t: 30 divided by 10, which is 3 A. Reversed: a cellphone charger delivering 0,5 A for 2 minutes moves charge Q equals I times delta t. Convert first — 2 minutes is 120 s. Then Q equals 0,5 times 120, which is 60 C.

Now the direction convention, which is pure history and pure marks. CONVENTIONAL CURRENT is taken to flow from the POSITIVE terminal of the battery, through the external circuit, to the NEGATIVE terminal. The electrons — the actual moving charges in a metal — drift the opposite way, from negative to positive, because they are negatively charged. The convention was fixed before the electron was discovered, and physics has kept it ever since. Unless a question explicitly asks about electron flow, every arrow you draw shows conventional current: positive to negative, outside the battery.

One more truth to bank now: current is not used up. The charge that leaves one terminal all arrives at the other; a bulb does not devour charge, it extracts energy from charge passing through. Take this section's questions now — the definition, the rearrangements, and the direction stated without hesitation.

## Subtopic: Potential Difference, Emf and the Volt

Charge moves because energy moves it, and POTENTIAL DIFFERENCE is the accounting of that energy. The potential difference between two points is the WORK DONE per unit charge — the energy transferred for every coulomb that moves between those points. As an equation: V equals W divided by Q, with W the work, or energy transferred, in joules, and Q the charge in coulombs. The unit is the VOLT: one volt is one joule per coulomb.

Calculate with it. If 24 J of work is done to move 8 C of charge through a resistor, the potential difference across the resistor is V equals W over Q: 24 divided by 8, which is 3 V. Read a label with it: a 1,5 V cell gives every coulomb 1,5 J of energy; a 12 V car battery gives every coulomb 12 J. And rearranged, W equals V times Q: pushing 5 C through a 12 V system transfers 60 J.

Now the distinction the syllabus insists on: EMF versus TERMINAL POTENTIAL DIFFERENCE. Both are measured in volts, both across the same battery — the difference is the circumstances. The emf is the voltmeter reading across the battery when NO current flows — the open-circuit reading, the battery's full energy promise per coulomb, from the chemical reactions inside it. The terminal potential difference is the reading across the same terminals while the battery DELIVERS current — and it is always slightly LESS than the emf, because the battery's own internal resistance swallows some energy as charge squeezes through the battery itself. Emf: the promise, measured at rest. Terminal pd: the delivery, measured under load. A battery marked 9 V that reads 9 V on the shelf might read 8,5 V while driving a motor; nothing is broken — the difference is being spent inside the battery.

The questions for this section are with you now: joules per coulomb, both rearrangements, and emf separated cleanly from terminal pd.

## Subtopic: Ammeters and Voltmeters — Reading the Circuit

Two instruments, two quantities, two completely different connection rules — and examiners test the rules every single year.

The AMMETER measures current. Current is a flow THROUGH a point, so the ammeter must be part of the path: it is connected IN SERIES, spliced into the circuit so that every coulomb it counts actually passes through it, like a tollgate on the road itself. And because the ammeter sits in the main path, it must interfere as little as possible: an ideal ammeter has a VERY LOW resistance — effectively zero — so that inserting it changes the current it is trying to measure as little as possible.

The VOLTMETER measures potential difference — the energy difference BETWEEN two points. So it is connected IN PARALLEL, across the component: one lead on each side of the bulb or resistor, comparing the energy per coulomb before and after, like measuring the height of a waterfall by standing beside it with one mark at the top and one at the bottom. And because a voltmeter offers an alternative route around the component, it must steal as little current as possible: an ideal voltmeter has a VERY HIGH resistance — effectively infinite.

Swap the rules and disaster follows. A voltmeter wired in series, with its enormous resistance, throttles the circuit's current to almost nothing — the circuit stops working. An ammeter wired in parallel, with its near-zero resistance, invites a huge current through itself — a short circuit that can blow the meter's fuse or damage the battery. So the drill for every circuit diagram: ammeter in the line, in series, low resistance; voltmeter across the component, in parallel, high resistance.

Reading practice belongs here too: note the scale a meter is set to, and read the needle or display in amperes or volts accordingly, quoting the unit every time. Take the questions for this section now — connection, resistance, and the reason for each.

## Subtopic: Resistance — the Friction of the Wire

RESISTANCE is a material's opposition to the flow of electric charge. Its symbol is R and its unit is the OHM. A conductor has a resistance of one ohm if a potential difference of one volt across it drives a current of one ampere through it.

The microscopic picture explains everything else. A metal is a lattice of fixed positive ions bathed in free electrons. When a potential difference drives those electrons along the wire, they do not glide — they repeatedly COLLIDE with the vibrating ions of the lattice. Each collision transfers energy from the electrons to the lattice, making the ions vibrate harder. That transferred energy appears as HEAT, which is why a working resistor warms up, why a kettle element glows, and why energy must be continually supplied to keep current flowing. Resistance is, microscopically, the story of those collisions.

From that picture, the three factors the syllabus names fall out logically. LENGTH: a longer wire means more lattice to cross and more collisions per journey — resistance increases with length; double the length, double the resistance. THICKNESS: a thicker wire offers more parallel lanes of travel, more cross-sectional area for the same flow — resistance DEcreases as thickness increases, the way a wide corridor passes a crowd more easily than a narrow one. TEMPERATURE: heating a metal makes its ions vibrate more violently, so the electrons collide more often — for metallic conductors, resistance increases as temperature rises. A bulb's filament, cold at the instant of being turned on, has far less resistance than it does glowing at working temperature.

Material matters too: copper resists far less than nichrome, which is why house wiring is copper and heating elements are nichrome — one chosen to waste little energy, the other chosen to convert energy to heat deliberately. Long, thin, hot: high resistance. Short, thick, cool: low. This part's final questions are in front of you now — the definition, the collision picture, and all three factors with their directions.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same four ideas with water on a roof, a gate at the stadium, and a crowded school corridor — same physics, home pictures first.

## Subtopic: The Water Pump on the Roof

Think of a garden water feature: a pump lifts water up to a tank on the roof, and the water runs back down through a pipe, turning a little wheel on the way. Around and around, the same water all afternoon.

The pump is the battery. Its whole job is to LIFT — to give every litre of water some height, some stored energy, before sending it around. The battery does exactly that to charge: it gives every coulomb a parcel of energy and pushes it out of the positive terminal. The voltage on the battery label is just the size of that parcel — a 1,5 V cell packs 1,5 joules into every coulomb; a 12 V battery packs 12. Volts are joules per coulomb: how much energy each unit of charge is carrying when it sets out.

The little wheel is the bulb or the resistor. Falling water spends its height turning the wheel; moving charge spends its energy parcel heating a filament until it glows. Note what is spent and what is not: the WATER is not used up — every litre returns to the pump to be lifted again. Same in the circuit: charge is never consumed, only the ENERGY it carries is dropped off. A battery goes flat not because charge ran out, but because the pump's chemical lifting power did.

One honest detail completes the picture. The pump's pipes have a bit of drag inside the pump itself, so the water arrives at the top with slightly less height than the pump's full effort — that is why a battery's terminal reading under load sits slightly below its open-shelf reading, the emf. The promise is the emf; the delivery is the terminal pd.

Questions on the pump picture are with you now — what the battery gives, and what never gets used up.

## Subtopic: Counting Heads at the Gate

Now current, with the easiest picture in the subject: a stadium gate on match day.

Current is simply COUNTING AT A POINT: how many supporters pass through the gate every second. Not how fast any one person walks, not how many are in the stadium — how many pass the turnstile per second. In the circuit, the supporters are coulombs of charge, and the count is the current: one coulomb passing per second is one ampere. Thirty coulombs through the bulb in ten seconds? Three coulombs a second — 3 A. A charger trickling half a coulomb a second for two minutes? Half times one hundred and twenty — 60 coulombs delivered.

The counting picture also fixes the biggest beginner's fear: the idea that current gets used up along the way. Walk the queue: every supporter who enters the gate comes out the other side; nobody vanishes inside the turnstile. Current is the same on both sides of a bulb. What the bulb takes is the ENERGY the charge carries — the ticket money, not the people.

And the direction business is just an old map with an old arrow on it. Scientists drew the current arrow — positive terminal around to negative — before anyone knew that in a metal wire it is the negative electrons that drift, the opposite way. The arrow stayed. So every diagram you draw uses the official arrow, conventional current, positive to negative around the outside; mention electrons only if the question asks for them by name.

Take the gate questions now — the per-second counting, and who actually moves versus which way the arrow points.

## Subtopic: The Narrow Corridor

Last idea, and the school corridor at break time teaches all of it: resistance.

Picture pushing through a corridor packed with learners. You bump, you slow, you weave — and every bump costs you a bit of energy and leaves the crowd slightly more jostled. That is exactly what electrons experience in a wire: the corridor is the metal's lattice of ions, the bumping is the collisions, and the jostling left behind is heat. That is why wires warm up when current flows, and why a kettle element — built to be bumped through — gets hot enough to boil water. Resistance is the corridor's difficulty rating.

Three things make the corridor harder, and each maps straight onto the exam's three factors. Make it LONGER: more corridor, more bumps — a long wire resists more than a short one, in direct proportion. Make it NARROWER: fewer lanes to pass through, more crowding — a thin wire resists more than a thick one, so thickening the wire lowers resistance. Make everyone in it MOVE about wildly: a corridor of milling, jittery learners is far harder to cross than one where everyone stands still — and that is temperature, because heating a metal sets its ions vibrating harder, so a hot wire resists more than a cold one.

Choose your corridor for the job. Copper is the wide, calm hallway — nearly effortless, so we wire houses with it. Nichrome is the deliberately awkward passage — chosen for kettles and heaters precisely because charge fighting through it converts electrical energy into heat on purpose.

Long, thin and hot: maximum resistance. Short, thick and cool: minimum. The last questions of the lesson are with you right now — the corridor's three dials, plus the meters from earlier: counter in the line, height-gauge across the ends. Get those five pictures straight and the whole of circuits, this year and next, is standing on solid ground.
