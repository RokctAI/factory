Hey, it's Thandi — if current, potential difference, emf and resistance are blurring into one grey cloud of electricity words, take a slow breath — this topic is four ideas, and you already own a picture for each one.

Current is counting at a gate: how many coulombs pass per second. That is the whole definition — I equals Q over delta t — and one ampere is just one coulomb per second. Thirty coulombs in ten seconds? Three amps. Done.

Potential difference is the energy parcel each coulomb carries: joules per coulomb. A 1,5 volt cell packs 1,5 joules into every coulomb. V equals W over Q. And emf versus terminal pd is only promise versus delivery — the battery's reading at rest, versus its slightly smaller reading while working, because a little energy is spent inside the battery itself.

The meters are a pair of opposites, and reasoning beats memory: the ammeter counts what passes a point, so it stands IN the line — series, low resistance. The voltmeter compares two points, so it stands ACROSS the component — parallel, high resistance. Ask what each meter measures and the wiring rule writes itself.

And resistance is the crowded corridor: electrons bumping through a lattice, leaving heat behind. Three dials only — longer means more, thicker means less, hotter means more.

The two guaranteed marks people drop: charge is never used up — a battery goes flat because its chemical ENERGY runs out; and the current arrow runs positive to negative even though electrons drift the other way.

Four pictures: gate, parcel, two meters, corridor. Run the practice questions with those in your pocket and this topic turns into steady, friendly marks. You've got this!
