If pulses, superposition and interference sound like three separate mountains, breathe out — this whole topic is one flick of a rope and one piece of adding.

Here is the shape of it. A pulse is a single bump travelling along a rope. The rope itself goes nowhere: each piece bobs up and down while the bump and its energy run past. That is the first idea, and it is one sentence: energy travels, the medium stays.

The second idea is even friendlier, because you already know how to do it — it is adding with signs, exactly like money. Up-bumps are money in, down-bumps are money out. When two pulses meet, the rope just adds the two numbers, signs included. Plus 30 and plus 20 make plus 50 — that is constructive. Plus 30 and minus 20 make plus 10 — that is destructive. Plus 25 and minus 25 make zero — a flat rope for one instant. No new maths anywhere. Grade 8 adding, wearing a lab coat.

The two traps, so you can step around them. Trap one: thinking a harder flick makes a faster pulse. It never does — speed belongs to the rope, and only tightening the rope changes it. Trap two: killing the pulses after they meet. They always pass through each other and come out unchanged, even after a perfect cancel. Flat is not gone.

So your exam recipe is three lines: write each displacement with its sign, add them for the overlap moment, then say both pulses continue unchanged. Same signs build, opposite signs cancel, everybody survives.

One flick, one addition, two traps avoided. Go and try the practice questions now — this is the gentlest topic in the whole course, and it is about to pay you full marks. You've got this!
