**Thandi:** Before you move on — the questions that come up a lot with this topic. The first one: if the rope itself never travels, what is actually moving down the rope? It feels like a trick.

**Tutor:** What moves is a pattern, and the energy that maintains it — and the best proof is the one you can do with your own eyes. Tie a small ribbon to the middle of a rope and send a pulse down it. The hump sweeps past; the ribbon jumps up and settles back exactly where it was. Do it a hundred times and the ribbon never migrates a single centimetre toward the far end. So the rope's material is doing short vertical round trips, each piece lifting the next piece slightly later, like a crowd doing a stadium wave where every person stands and sits in their own seat while the wave circles the whole stadium. What arrives at the far end is the disturbance — the instruction to move — and the energy your hand put in, which is why the far end can be made to jump, or even flick a light object off a hook, without any rope arriving there. That is the definition worth memorising: a pulse transfers energy through a medium without transferring the medium itself. Whenever an exam asks what is transferred by a pulse or a wave, the one-word core of the answer is energy — never matter, never particles, never the rope.

***

**Thandi:** Next one: why does flicking the rope harder not make the pulse arrive sooner? Half the class was sure a stronger flick means a faster pulse.

**Tutor:** Because the flick and the journey are two separate jobs done by two separate things. Your flick decides how much energy goes in, and that energy is stored in the size of the pulse — its amplitude. But once the pulse is on the rope, the journey is run entirely by the rope: how tightly it is stretched, and how heavy each metre of it is. Think about what carries the pulse forward — each piece of rope pulls the next piece into motion. How quickly that next piece responds depends on how hard the rope is being stretched, which is the tension, and on how much mass has to be accelerated, which is the thickness. A tighter rope snatches its neighbouring pieces into motion faster; a heavier rope responds sluggishly. Nowhere in that story does the size of the original flick appear. So a tall pulse and a tiny pulse on the same rope travel side by side at identical speed — 3 metres per second on our rope, whether the hump is 30 centimetres or 3. This is the first appearance of a rule that runs through the whole waves course: speed belongs to the medium. It comes back with sound, where shouting louder does not make the sound arrive sooner, and it comes back with light. Learn it here, on the rope, and you have it for three chapters.

***

**Thandi:** Then the one that always causes an argument: when a crest and an equal trough cancel and the rope is flat, where has the energy gone? Surely something got destroyed.

**Tutor:** Nothing was destroyed — the energy has simply moved into a form the photograph cannot show. A snapshot of the flat rope shows you displacement, and the displacement really is zero everywhere. But energy on a rope lives in two accounts, not one: the position of the particles, and the MOTION of the particles. At the instant of perfect cancellation, look at what the particles are doing rather than where they are. The particles ahead of the meeting point are being commanded upward by the arriving crest; the particles behind are being commanded downward by the arriving trough — the rope is flat but moving, the way a swing passing through its lowest point is at zero height but maximum speed. All the pulse energy is momentarily stored as kinetic energy of the rope's particles. That is exactly why, an instant later, the crest reappears on one side and the trough on the other, both full size: the motion rebuilds them. The exam picture of the flat rope is testing precisely this. The full-marks answer says: the displacements sum to zero at that instant, but both pulses still exist, the energy is stored in the motion of the medium, and the pulses then continue unchanged. Flat is not empty.

***

**Thandi:** Last one, on exam craft: in interference questions people keep adding the sizes when they should subtract, or subtracting when they should add. What is the reliable drill?

**Tutor:** Stop deciding whether to add or subtract — always add, but add signed numbers, and let the signs do the deciding. That is what algebraic sum means, and it removes the judgement call that causes the errors. The drill has three written steps. Step one: set a sign convention and write it down — upward displacements positive, downward negative. Step two: at the moment the question asks about, write each pulse's displacement at the overlap point with its sign attached: plus 0,3; minus 0,2. Step three: add them exactly as you would add money in and money out — plus 0,3 plus minus 0,2 gives plus 0,1, so a small hump of 0,1 metres. Notice you never chose to subtract; the minus sign performed the subtraction for you. Two crests come out as plus 0,3 plus 0,2, giving plus 0,5 — the method builds them up automatically too. Then finish with the naming sentence, because it is often a separate mark: same direction, resultant larger — constructive; opposite directions, resultant smaller — destructive. And if the question carries a follow-up about a moment later, end with the survival sentence: both pulses pass through and continue unchanged. Signed numbers in, one addition, two sentences out — that drill covers every interference question this topic can ask.
