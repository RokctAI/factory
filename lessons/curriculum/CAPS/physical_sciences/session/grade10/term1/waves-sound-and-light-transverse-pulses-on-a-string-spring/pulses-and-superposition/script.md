# Part 1 — Expert

Every wave in this subject — the ripple on a dam, the note from a guitar, the light from the sun — is built out of one smaller idea: the pulse. This session isolates that building block. What a pulse is and what actually moves when one travels, how amplitude is defined and measured, and then the rule that governs what happens when two pulses arrive at the same place at the same time: the principle of superposition, with its two famous faces, constructive and destructive interference.

## Subtopic: The Pulse — a Single Disturbance That Travels

A PULSE is a single disturbance that moves through a medium. Not a repeating pattern — one bump, one hump, travelling. Flick the end of a rope once, sharply, and a single hump races away from your hand toward the far end. That hump is a pulse. Flick the rope up and down over and over and you get a wave — a succession of pulses. The wave chapter to come is built entirely on what the single pulse does, which is why the syllabus puts this first.

Now the definition that carries the marks. A TRANSVERSE PULSE is a pulse in which the particles of the medium move at RIGHT ANGLES — perpendicular — to the direction in which the pulse travels. Watch one point on the rope, perhaps a knot tied in the middle. As the pulse arrives, the knot rises straight up; as the pulse passes, the knot drops straight back down to where it started. The knot moves vertically. The pulse moves horizontally. Movement of the medium across the line of travel: that crossing is what the word transverse means.

Here is the idea learners find strangest, and examiners love most: THE MEDIUM DOES NOT TRAVEL WITH THE PULSE. The knot never gets any closer to the far end of the rope. Every particle of rope does a short round trip — up, then down, back to rest — while the DISTURBANCE, and the ENERGY it carries, travels the full length of the rope. A pulse is a transfer of energy through a medium without any transfer of the medium itself. That sentence, or one like it, is the standard two-mark answer.

The speed of the pulse is set by the MEDIUM — how heavy the rope is, how tightly it is stretched — not by how hard you flicked. A pulse that covers 6 m of rope in 2 s is travelling at speed equal to distance divided by time: 6 divided by 2, which is 3 m·s⁻¹. On the same rope under the same tension, every pulse travels at that same 3 m·s⁻¹, big flick or small.

## Subtopic: Amplitude, and What the Medium Actually Does

The AMPLITUDE of a pulse is the MAXIMUM displacement of a particle of the medium from its REST position. Three words in that definition do the work, and each is examinable on its own.

Maximum: not the average height of the hump, not its width — the furthest any particle gets from where it started. Displacement: a change of position, measured in metres, and it carries direction — a hump above the rope's rest line is a positive displacement, a dip below it is negative. Rest position: the flat, undisturbed line the rope lies along before the pulse arrives, sometimes called the equilibrium position. Amplitude is always measured FROM that rest line to the top of the hump — never from a trough to a crest, and never along the rope.

So if the rope's rest line is flat and the highest point of the hump stands 0,2 m above it, the amplitude is 0,2 m. If a pulse is sent as a dip that reaches 0,15 m below the rest line, its amplitude is 0,15 m — amplitude itself is quoted as a size, while the displacement that produced it was negative. Direction matters enormously in the next two sections, so keep the sign language ready: up is positive, down is negative.

What does amplitude tell you physically? Energy. A bigger flick puts more energy into the rope, and that energy shows up as a larger maximum displacement. Amplitude is the medium's record of how much energy the pulse carries. What amplitude does NOT change is the speed: a tall pulse and a short pulse on the same stretched rope arrive together, because speed belongs to the medium. A pulse travelling at 3 m·s⁻¹ that needs to cover 4,5 m takes time equal to distance divided by speed: 4,5 divided by 3, which is 1,5 s — whatever its amplitude.

One more habit: as a pulse travels along a real rope, friction steadily drains its energy, so the amplitude gradually shrinks while the speed stays the same. Energy fades; the medium's speed rule does not.

The questions for this section are with you now — the full definition of amplitude, where it is measured from, and what it does and does not control.

## Subtopic: The Principle of Superposition

Send one pulse from each end of the same rope, toward each other. They meet. What happens in the metre where they overlap? The answer is the single most important rule of this topic.

THE PRINCIPLE OF SUPERPOSITION: when two or more pulses occupy the same place at the same time, the displacement of the medium at that point is the ALGEBRAIC SUM of the displacements of the individual pulses. Algebraic sum means: add the displacements WITH their signs. Up counts positive, down counts negative, and the rope at each point simply does the arithmetic.

Work one example in words. A pulse of displacement plus 0,3 m travels right; a pulse of displacement plus 0,2 m travels left. At the instant their peaks coincide, the rope at that point stands at plus 0,3 plus 0,2 — a displacement of 0,5 m. Now change the second pulse to a dip of minus 0,2 m. At the instant the peaks coincide, the rope stands at plus 0,3 minus 0,2 — just 0,1 m.

And now the part that separates the top answers from the rest: after the overlap, the pulses PASS THROUGH each other completely unchanged. Each pulse emerges on the far side with its own original shape, amplitude and direction of travel, exactly as if the other had never existed. Superposition is a momentary meeting, not a collision. Pulses do not bounce off each other, do not swallow each other, do not permanently cancel. Two torch beams crossing in the air come out the other side intact; pulses on a rope do precisely the same.

So every superposition question has two moments to describe: DURING the overlap, add the displacements with signs; AFTER the overlap, restore both original pulses and let them carry on. Learners who forget the second half lose easy marks by killing pulses that physics says survive.

## Subtopic: Constructive and Destructive Interference

The two headline cases of superposition have names, and definitions worth learning word for word.

CONSTRUCTIVE INTERFERENCE happens when the pulses displace the medium in the SAME direction — both up, or both down. The displacements add to something LARGER than either pulse alone. Two crests of 0,3 m and 0,2 m meet: the rope momentarily stands 0,5 m high, taller than either parent. Two troughs of minus 0,2 m each meet: the rope momentarily dips to minus 0,4 m — that is also constructive, because the disturbances reinforced each other. Constructive means built up, in either direction.

DESTRUCTIVE INTERFERENCE happens when the pulses displace the medium in OPPOSITE directions — one up, one down. The displacements partly or wholly cancel, and the result is SMALLER than the larger pulse. A crest of 0,3 m meets a trough of minus 0,2 m: the sum is plus 0,1 m, a stub of a hump. And the perfect case: a crest of 0,25 m meets a trough of minus 0,25 m, equal sizes, opposite signs. Sum: zero. For one instant the rope is COMPLETELY FLAT — while both pulses are still in there, hidden, their energy stored in the motion of the particles. A moment later they emerge on opposite sides, whole, and continue on their ways. The flat rope is the exam's favourite trick picture: flat does not mean gone.

The method for any interference question is fixed. Step one: at the moment asked about, write down each pulse's displacement at the overlap point, with its sign. Step two: add them algebraically — that sum is the displacement of the medium. Step three: if asked what happens next, redraw both original pulses past each other, unchanged. Practise until the signs feel automatic: same signs build, opposite signs cancel, and equal-and-opposite cancels to a momentary flat line.

This part's final questions are in front of you now — name each case, compute each sum, and defend the survival of both pulses.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same physics with a washing line, a skipping rope and two friends who flick at the same time — same rules, same answers, home language first.

## Subtopic: One Flick of the Washing Line

Monday morning, wet washing pegged out, and a hadeda lands dust on the far end of the line. You flick your end once, hard, to shake it loose. Watch what actually happens: a single hump runs along the line, under the pegged socks, all the way to the far end. That travelling hump is a pulse — one bump on the move.

Now watch one peg instead of the hump. As the hump arrives, the peg jumps straight up. As the hump passes, the peg drops straight back to where it hung. The peg never slides along the line toward the hadeda — it only bobs up and down while the hump slides past underneath it. Up and down for the peg, sideways for the hump. Movement across the line of travel — that is why this is called a transverse pulse, and it is the whole reason your washing does not end up at the neighbour's house: the line itself goes nowhere. Only the bump, and the energy of your flick, makes the journey.

And here is the thing you cannot change by trying harder: how fast the hump runs is decided by the LINE — how heavy it is and how tight it is strung — not by your wrist. Flick gently, flick furiously: on the same line, every hump runs at the same speed. If a hump covers 6 m of line in 2 s, it is doing 3 metres every second, and so is every other hump you send after it. Want a faster pulse? Tighten the line. There is no other way.

Some questions on the washing line are with you now — especially on what the peg does, and what the peg never does.

## Subtopic: When Two Bumps Meet in the Middle

Now take a skipping rope, and put a friend on the other end. You flick your end up; at the same moment your friend flicks their end up. Two humps set off toward each other, meet in the middle — and for one instant the rope throws up a single tall hump, higher than either of you made alone. Your 30 centimetres and their 20 centimetres stand on each other's shoulders: 50 centimetres of hump.

That is the whole rule, and it is just addition. Where two pulses share the same spot at the same moment, the rope adds their heights. Two ups make a bigger up. Two downs make a deeper down. The rope does not choose a winner — it takes both instructions at once and adds them.

But watch the middle of the rope a heartbeat later, because this is the part everyone gets wrong. The tall hump does not stay, and the pulses do not smash and die. Your hump comes out the other side, still your size, still heading for your friend. Their hump comes out your side, still their size, still heading for you. They passed THROUGH each other like two people walking opposite ways through the same doorway — squeezed together for a second, then on their separate ways, unchanged. Nothing bounced. Nothing broke.

So every meeting of pulses is a two-scene story. Scene one, the overlap: add the heights. Scene two, right after: both pulses walk on as if they never met. Tell both scenes and the marks are yours.

Quick check before the last stretch — your questions on the meeting in the middle are up now.

## Subtopic: Adding Heights With Signs

One last flick, and it is the sneaky one. This time you flick UP and, at the same moment, your friend flicks DOWN. A hump runs one way, a dip runs the other. What happens when an up-bump meets a down-bump?

They fight. And the fight is settled by ordinary adding with signs, the same arithmetic as money. Your hump is plus 30 centimetres — money in. Their dip is minus 20 centimetres — money out. When they meet: plus 30 minus 20 leaves plus 10. For an instant the rope shows only a little 10-centimetre bump, much smaller than either pulse. The up and the down mostly cancelled. That cancelling is called destructive interference, and the building-up from the previous section is called constructive interference. Same signs build; opposite signs cancel. That one line is the whole vocabulary.

Now make it perfect. You flick up 25 centimetres; your friend flicks down 25 centimetres. Equal sizes, opposite directions. When they meet: plus 25 minus 25 is zero. For one instant the rope is dead flat — as if nobody had flicked at all. But both pulses are still in there, like two dancers passing back to back, and a blink later the hump pops out one side and the dip pops out the other, both perfect, both still travelling. A flat rope in that instant is not an empty rope. The exam will show you the flat picture and ask where the pulses went. Answer: nowhere — they are passing through, and they will reappear unchanged.

Last questions of the lesson are with you right now: same signs build, opposite signs cancel, and after every meeting both pulses live on. Add with signs, tell both scenes, and this topic is banked.
