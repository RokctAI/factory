# Part 1 — Expert

Sound needs air; light does not. This session builds the family of waves that cross empty space: what an electromagnetic wave is made of, why every member of the family travels at exactly the same speed in a vacuum, the order of the spectrum from radio waves to gamma rays, and the two equations that convert between frequency, wavelength and photon energy. The calculations run entirely in scientific notation, so the discipline of prefixes and powers of ten is the difference between a full answer and a wasted one.

## Subtopic: What an Electromagnetic Wave Is

An ELECTROMAGNETIC WAVE is produced by an ACCELERATING ELECTRIC CHARGE. Shake an electron up and down in an aerial and its changing electric field creates a changing magnetic field, which in turn creates a changing electric field further out, and the pair regenerate each other outward through space. The wave is therefore made of an oscillating electric field and an oscillating magnetic field, at right angles to each other and both at right angles to the direction of travel. That last fact makes electromagnetic radiation TRANSVERSE.

The consequence that matters most: because the fields sustain each other, an electromagnetic wave needs NO MEDIUM. Sound dies in a vacuum; light crosses one hundred and fifty million kilometres of vacuum from the sun to your face. This is the sharpest difference between the two wave families, and it is examined constantly.

In a vacuum every electromagnetic wave travels at the same speed, the speed of light, symbol c, with the value 3 × 10⁸ m·s⁻¹ — three times ten to the eight metres per second. Radio waves are not slower than gamma rays; visible light is not faster than infrared. In a vacuum they are identical in speed and differ only in frequency and wavelength. In a material such as glass or water they all slow down, but that comes later in the syllabus.

Electromagnetic radiation also has a DUAL NATURE. The WAVE MODEL describes it as those travelling fields, and it explains reflection, refraction, diffraction and interference. The PARTICLE MODEL describes it as a stream of tiny packets of energy called PHOTONS, and it explains how radiation is emitted and absorbed in fixed amounts, and how ultraviolet light can eject electrons from a metal surface while bright red light cannot. Neither model alone is complete; radiation behaves as a wave in some experiments and as particles in others, and physics accepts both.

The questions for this section are with you now: state what produces an electromagnetic wave, name the two fields and their orientation, and justify why no medium is required.

## Subtopic: The Spectrum in Order

The ELECTROMAGNETIC SPECTRUM is the full range of electromagnetic radiation arranged in order of frequency, and it must be learned in sequence. From lowest frequency and longest wavelength to highest frequency and shortest wavelength: RADIO WAVES, MICROWAVES, INFRARED, VISIBLE LIGHT, ULTRAVIOLET, X-RAYS, GAMMA RAYS.

Read that order twice, because frequency and wavelength run in opposite directions along it. Radio waves have the longest wavelengths — metres, and for some broadcast bands hundreds of metres — and the lowest frequencies. Gamma rays have wavelengths smaller than an atomic nucleus and frequencies above ten to the twenty hertz. Every step to the right multiplies the frequency and divides the wavelength, because their product must stay equal to c.

VISIBLE LIGHT is a narrow band in the middle, roughly 400 to 700 nanometres, and it is the only part your eye detects. Within it the same rule holds: RED has the longest wavelength and lowest frequency; then orange, yellow, green, blue, indigo; VIOLET has the shortest wavelength and highest frequency. Red at one end, violet at the other, and the visible band sits between infrared — below red — and ultraviolet — beyond violet. The names themselves tell you where the neighbours sit.

Two further quantities increase steadily along the spectrum, and they explain everything in the uses-and-dangers section. First, ENERGY PER PHOTON rises with frequency. Second, PENETRATING ABILITY rises with energy: radio waves pass through walls but through your body harmlessly, while gamma rays pass through concrete and through cells, tearing electrons off atoms as they go. Radiation energetic enough to remove electrons from atoms is called IONISING RADIATION, and the ionising members of the family are ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays.

Take the questions for this section now — recite the seven bands in order, state which end carries long wavelengths, and place red and violet correctly within the visible band.

## Subtopic: The Two Equations — c = fλ and E = hf

Two formulae govern every calculation in this topic, and both must be quoted before substitution.

The first is the wave equation with the speed fixed: c = fλ. In words: c equals f lambda — the speed of light equals frequency times wavelength. Because c is a constant, frequency and wavelength are inversely proportional: double the frequency and the wavelength halves.

Worked example. South African FM broadcasts sit close to 100 MHz. Find the wavelength. Formula: c = fλ, so λ = c ÷ f. Convert: 100 MHz = 100 × 10⁶ Hz = 1 × 10⁸ Hz. Substitute: λ = (3 × 10⁸) ÷ (1 × 10⁸). Answer: λ = 3 m. Three metres — which is why an FM aerial is a metre or two of wire, while a microwave oven at 2,45 × 10⁹ Hz produces waves only about 0,12 m long, and its metal door grid, with holes far smaller than that, keeps them inside.

The second formula gives the energy of one photon: E = hf, where h is Planck's constant, 6,63 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s. In words: E equals h f — photon energy equals Planck's constant times frequency. Combining it with c = fλ gives the alternative form E = hc/λ — energy equals h c over lambda.

Worked example. Ultraviolet radiation has a wavelength of 250 nm. Find its frequency and the energy of one photon. Convert first: 250 nm = 250 × 10⁻⁹ m = 2,5 × 10⁻⁷ m. Frequency: f = c ÷ λ = (3 × 10⁸) ÷ (2,5 × 10⁻⁷) = 1,2 × 10¹⁵ Hz. Photon energy: E = hf = (6,63 × 10⁻³⁴)(1,2 × 10¹⁵) = 7,96 × 10⁻¹⁹ J.

Now compare. A photon from that 100 MHz radio wave carries E = hf = (6,63 × 10⁻³⁴)(1 × 10⁸) = 6,63 × 10⁻²⁶ J — about twelve million times less energy than the ultraviolet photon. That ratio, not any difference in speed, is why one gives you a radio programme and the other gives you sunburn.

Work through the questions for this section now, converting every prefix into standard form before you substitute, and quoting every answer with its unit.

## Subtopic: Uses, Dangers and the Traps

Each band earns its uses and its dangers from its photon energy and penetrating ability, so learn them as consequences rather than as a list.

RADIO WAVES: broadcasting, television and cellphone signals. Lowest photon energy, non-ionising, no established danger at broadcast intensities. MICROWAVES: cooking — water molecules absorb strongly and heat the food — plus cellphone links, satellite communication and radar. At high intensity they heat body tissue. INFRARED: heat lamps, remote controls, thermal imaging and night-vision cameras; excessive exposure burns skin. VISIBLE LIGHT: sight, photosynthesis, optical fibre communication; intense beams such as lasers damage the retina.

Then the ionising three. ULTRAVIOLET: sterilising water and instruments, producing vitamin D in skin, revealing security marks on banknotes. Its dangers are serious in this country, where the ultraviolet index runs high for much of the year: sunburn, premature ageing of skin, skin cancer and cataracts. X-RAYS: imaging bones and teeth, airport security scanning, inspecting welds; ionising, so they damage living cells and radiographers stand behind screens while patients wear lead shielding. GAMMA RAYS: radiotherapy to kill cancer cells, sterilising medical equipment, treating food to kill bacteria. The most penetrating and most ionising of all, causing severe cell damage and radiation sickness at high doses.

Now the traps. First, prefix conversion — nanometres, megahertz and gigahertz must all become metres and hertz before substitution: 250 nm is 2,5 × 10⁻⁷ m, not 250. Second, the speed error: all electromagnetic waves travel at the same speed in a vacuum, so gamma rays are not faster than radio waves, only more energetic. Third, calculator discipline with scientific notation — enter powers of ten using the exponent key, never by typing "times ten to the". Fourth, the wrong lambda in E = hc/λ; that formula needs metres. Fifth, a units answer of joules for photon energy, and never hertz.

The final questions of this part are in front of you now: pair each band with a use and a danger, explain both from photon energy, and convert every prefix before any substitution.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same family from the radio in the kitchen, the sun over the beach and the plate at the dentist — same order, same sums, everyday words first.

## Subtopic: One Family, Same Speed

Everything in this topic is one single family of waves. They are all made the same way, they all cross empty space without needing anything to travel through, and here is the part that surprises everyone: they all move at exactly the same speed. Three hundred million metres every second. The radio signal, the light from your phone screen, the X-ray at the clinic — same speed, no exceptions, once they are in empty space.

So if they all move at the same speed, what makes them different? Only how tightly packed they are. Some come at you long and lazy, stretched out over metres. Others come short and sharp, packed billions upon billions into every second. That is the entire difference between a radio broadcast and a gamma ray.

Line them up from the longest and laziest to the shortest and sharpest, and you get the family in order: radio, microwave, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, X-rays, gamma rays. Seven of them, and the order matters more than anything else in this topic. A trick for keeping it: you already know where visible light sits, because two of its neighbours are named after it. Infra-red means below red, and it is the warmth you feel from braai coals without seeing it. Ultra-violet means beyond violet, and it is what burns you at Durban beach on a cloudy day. Light sits in the middle of the family, with warmth on one side and sunburn on the other.

And inside that little visible strip, the same pattern repeats. Red is the long lazy one, violet is the short sharp one, and the rest of the rainbow sits in between in the order you already know.

Small check before we go on — questions on the family are with you now, especially the order of the seven and the fact that they all share one speed.

## Subtopic: The See-Saw and the Punch

Two ideas run this whole topic, and neither needs a formula to understand.

The first is a see-saw. Since the speed never changes, wavelength and frequency have to trade off against each other perfectly. Make the waves shorter and more of them must arrive each second. Make them longer and fewer arrive. Push one side down and the other goes up, always. That is all c equals f lambda is saying: speed of light equals frequency times wavelength, and since the speed is stuck, the other two must balance each other.

Try it on the radio. Local FM stations sit around a hundred million cycles a second. Share the light's three hundred million metres per second out among those hundred million waves and each wave is three metres long — which is exactly why a car aerial is a rod about that sort of size. Now try a microwave oven, buzzing away at nearly two and a half billion cycles a second. Far more waves per second, so each one is far shorter: about twelve centimetres. Look at the mesh in the oven door and you will see holes far smaller than that. The waves are simply too fat to fit through, so the heat stays in and your face stays out of it.

The second idea is the punch. The shorter and sharper the wave, the harder each little packet of it hits. Long lazy radio waves arrive like someone tapping you with a feather; you never notice. Ultraviolet arrives like a flick with a fingernail; enough of it and your skin is damaged. X-rays and gamma rays arrive like a hammer, straight through skin and into the cells underneath. Run the numbers and a single ultraviolet packet carries around twelve million times the punch of a single radio packet — same speed, wildly different force.

Your questions on this part are up now: the see-saw between long waves and high frequency, and why short waves hit harder.

## Subtopic: Sunscreen, the Remote and the Lead Apron

Now walk through an ordinary day and meet all seven, with their jobs and their risks.

Radio wakes you up — the station, the TV, and every call and message on your phone. Gentle, harmless, and it goes through walls, which is why you get signal indoors. Breakfast: the microwave heats last night's leftovers by shaking the water molecules inside the food until they get hot. Same waves carry your cellphone call to the tower and satellite TV to the dish on the roof.

You point the remote at the TV and infrared does the work — invisible, and the same thing you feel on your face from braai coals or a heater. Then visible light, the thin strip your eyes are actually built for, and the same light that plants use to grow and that carries internet down fibre cables.

Outside, ultraviolet is waiting. It is useful — it makes vitamin D in your skin, sterilises water, and shows up the hidden marks on a banknote — but in South Africa the sun is fierce for most of the year, and too much ultraviolet means sunburn, damaged eyes and skin cancer. That is what sunscreen and a hat are for, and cloud does not stop it.

At the clinic, X-rays go straight through soft tissue and get stopped by bone, which is how a broken arm shows up. But the same energy that gets through you can damage cells on the way, which is why the radiographer steps behind a screen and you get a lead apron. And at a hospital, gamma rays — the fiercest of all — are aimed deliberately at tumours to kill cancer cells, and used to sterilise surgical equipment.

Same family, same speed, all the way from harmless to hazardous, and the only thing that changed along the line was how tightly the waves are packed.
