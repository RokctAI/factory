**Thandi:** Before you pack up — the questions that filled my inbox on this one. First: the lesson insists every band travels at the same speed in a vacuum. But gamma rays feel so much more violent than radio waves. If not speed, what exactly makes them dangerous?

**Tutor:** Separate the delivery van from the parcel. Speed is the delivery van, and in a vacuum every band drives the identical van at three times ten to the eight metres per second — the wave equation never lets one band overtake another. What differs is the parcel each photon carries, and that is priced by E equals h f: energy per photon grows in direct proportion to frequency. A gamma photon oscillating at ten to the twenty hertz carries roughly a million million times the energy of a radio photon at ten to the eight hertz — same van, but one delivers an envelope and the other delivers a wrecking ball. Danger begins at a very specific threshold: when one photon carries enough energy to tear an electron off an atom, the radiation is ionising, and molecules in living cells — including DNA — get broken. Ultraviolet is the first band across that threshold, which is why the family splits into the harmless four below it and the dangerous three from it upward. Notice the subtle point: brightness does not rescue a weak photon. A stadium floodlight pours out astronomically more total energy than a faint ultraviolet lamp, yet cannot ionise a single atom, because ionisation is decided photon by photon, one collision at a time. That is exactly the observation the particle model was invented to explain. So in an answer, never write that high-frequency bands are faster — write that their photons are more energetic, and name the ionising three.

**Thandi:** Next: the nanometre conversions keep going wrong — someone wrote 300 nm as 3 times ten to the minus nine metres and lost the whole calculation. What is the safest way to handle the prefixes?

**Tutor:** Treat every prefix as a number you write down, never one you juggle mentally. The method has three steps. Step one: replace the prefix with its power of ten on paper before anything else — nano is ten to the minus nine, micro ten to the minus six, mega ten to the six, giga ten to the nine. So 300 nm becomes 300 times ten to the minus nine metres, written exactly like that. Step two: tidy into standard form, moving the comma and adjusting the exponent together: 300 is 3 times ten to the two, so 300 times ten to the minus nine is 3 times ten to the minus seven metres. The error you mention came from skipping step one — remembering "nano is minus nine" but forgetting the 300 also carries a power of ten. Step three: sanity-check against the spectrum you know. Visible light lives around 4 to 7 times ten to the minus seven metres; ultraviolet sits just below that. If your converted ultraviolet wavelength lands at ten to the minus nine, you have drifted into X-ray country, and the check catches it before the substitution does any damage. Same discipline on frequencies: 150 megahertz is 150 times ten to the six, tidied to 1,5 times ten to the eight hertz. And on the calculator, use the exponent key so the machine holds the power of ten as one object. Prefix to power, tidy to standard form, sanity-check the neighbourhood — three written steps, and the nanometres never bite again.

**Thandi:** Then the one that starts arguments: if microwaves are so weak per photon, why does the oven cook food? And is a cellphone slowly cooking my head?

**Tutor:** Two different mechanisms are hiding under one word, "radiation", and unpicking them answers both questions. Ionising damage is the photon-by-photon mechanism — one energetic packet breaking one molecule. Microwave photons are millions of times too feeble for that; no quantity of them can ionise an atom, just as no number of feather-taps becomes a knife cut. What the oven uses is heating, a bulk mechanism: water molecules are electrically lopsided, and the oscillating fields of the microwaves twist them back and forth billions of times a second. All that jiggling is heat, and the oven wins not through photon strength but through sheer concentrated power — hundreds of watts sealed inside a reflective metal box with your food, deliberately focused on it for minutes. The door mesh keeps that intensity where it belongs: the holes are far smaller than the twelve-centimetre waves, so the waves cannot pass. A phone is the same physics at a completely different scale: it whispers at a fraction of a watt, radiating in all directions, mostly toward the tower — the warmth you feel after a long call is overwhelmingly the battery and screen working, not tissue cooked by the signal. So the layered answer is: microwaves are non-ionising at any intensity; at very high intensity they heat tissue, which is why the oven is shielded; at phone intensities the heating is trivial. Intensity and photon energy are separate dials — most confusion in this topic comes from turning one while thinking about the other.

**Thandi:** Last one, for the test itself: the uses-and-dangers table feels like fourteen random facts. How do we answer those questions without pure memorising?

**Tutor:** Generate the table instead of memorising it, from the one number that orders the whole family: photon energy. Walk the spectrum and ask, at each band, what can a photon of this strength actually do? Radio photons are too weak to do anything to matter, which is itself the use — signals pass through walls and bodies unharmed, perfect for carrying information. Microwave photons manage one trick: twisting water molecules, so cooking and wet-tissue heating are the use and the danger in one. Infrared photons shake molecules generally — felt as warmth: heaters and remotes, burns in excess. Visible photons are tuned to trigger the chemistry of eyes and leaves: sight and photosynthesis, with only concentrated laser beams a threat. Then the threshold: ultraviolet photons can break chemical bonds — kill microbes and make vitamin D, or damage skin DNA. X-ray photons pass through soft tissue but not bone: imaging, with cell damage the toll. Gamma photons pass through nearly everything: deep radiotherapy and sterilising sealed equipment, and the severest cell damage of all. Notice each band's use and its danger are the same property wearing two hats — the structure to use in any answer: name the property, then give its good face and its bad face. State the photon's capability, and the use-danger pair follows without a single memorised fact. Practise deriving the table twice and you will never need to recall it — you will rebuild it.
