**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! First one, and it is the one nobody believes: how can a gamma ray be so much more dangerous than a radio wave if they travel at the same speed? Surely dangerous means fast?

**Tutor:** Speed and energy are two completely different quantities, and this topic separates them ruthlessly. Every electromagnetic wave crosses empty space at three times ten to the eight metres per second, so speed tells you nothing about danger at all. What differs is the energy carried by each individual photon, and that is set by frequency through E equals h f. A gamma photon oscillates something like ten to the twenty times a second; a radio photon oscillates perhaps a hundred million times a second. Put those into E equals h f and the gamma photon comes out carrying billions upon billions of times more energy. Now think about what happens on arrival. A radio photon reaches an atom in your body with far too little energy to disturb it, so it passes through and nothing happens. A gamma photon arrives carrying enough energy to knock an electron clean off an atom — that is ionisation — and an ionised atom inside a cell can damage the molecule it belongs to, including DNA. So the danger comes from the size of each individual hit, not from how quickly the hits arrive. A useful comparison: a ping-pong ball and a bullet can travel at the same speed, and only one of them goes through a wall.

**Thandi:** Next: if radio waves and light are the same kind of thing, why can radio signals reach my phone inside a brick house while sunlight cannot get through the wall?

**Tutor:** Because whether a wave passes through a material depends on the relationship between its wavelength and the structure of that material, and radio waves and visible light are enormously different in wavelength. An FM radio wave is roughly three metres long, which is far larger than the bricks, the gaps and the structural features of a wall, so the wall barely registers as an obstacle — the wave diffracts around and through it much as a long ocean swell rolls past a single pole. Visible light has a wavelength of a few hundred nanometres, hundreds of millions of times shorter, so from its point of view the wall is a vast solid landscape of atoms, and it is absorbed almost immediately. The same reasoning explains several everyday things. Your radio works in a tunnel far worse than in the open, because the surrounding rock is thick enough to matter even for metre-long waves. A microwave oven's door mesh stops twelve-centimetre waves with holes a few millimetres across, while visible light passes straight through those same holes so you can watch your food. And on a cellphone, the higher-frequency, shorter-wavelength bands carry more data but penetrate buildings less well than the lower-frequency bands, which is why signal quality changes as you walk indoors.

**Thandi:** This one tripped up half the class in the test: we did E equals h c over lambda for a wavelength given in nanometres and got a ridiculous answer. What went wrong?

**Tutor:** Almost certainly the nanometres went into the calculator unconverted, and it is worth understanding exactly how much damage that does. A nanometre is ten to the minus nine of a metre, so a wavelength of 250 nm is 2,5 times ten to the minus seven metres. If you substitute 250 instead, your lambda is wrong by a factor of a thousand million, and since lambda sits in the denominator your energy comes out a thousand million times too small — a number so tiny it should immediately look wrong. The cure is procedural, not mental. Give the conversion its own line before you write the formula's substitution: "250 nm = 250 × 10⁻⁹ m = 2,5 × 10⁻⁷ m", then substitute. Do the same for megahertz and gigahertz — one megahertz is ten to the six hertz, one gigahertz is ten to the nine hertz. And build a sense of what a reasonable answer looks like, because that is your safety net: photon energies for visible and ultraviolet light land in the region of ten to the minus nineteen joules. If your answer is ten to the minus twenty-eight or ten to the minus ten, you have a conversion error, not a physics error, and you can find it in ten seconds by checking that one line.

**Thandi:** Last one: the syllabus says radiation is both a wave and a particle. That sounds like physics refusing to make up its mind. Which is it really?

**Tutor:** It is genuinely both, and that is not a dodge — it is one of the great results of twentieth-century physics. The honest position is that electromagnetic radiation is not a little ripple and not a little bullet; it is something that has no everyday equivalent, and we describe it with two models, each of which works perfectly in its own domain. The wave model explains everything about how radiation travels: reflection off a mirror, refraction as light bends into water, diffraction as it spreads through a gap, and interference where two beams reinforce or cancel. None of that can be done with particles. The particle model explains everything about how radiation is given out and taken in: an atom emits a photon of one specific energy, a photon is absorbed whole or not at all, and — the decisive experiment — dim ultraviolet light ejects electrons from a metal surface while intensely bright red light ejects none, because what matters is the energy of each individual photon and not how many arrive. No wave picture can explain that. So for Grade 10 the position to hold is this: radiation exhibits a dual nature, behaving as a wave in propagation experiments and as particles in emission and absorption experiments, and the model you use is chosen by the situation in front of you.
