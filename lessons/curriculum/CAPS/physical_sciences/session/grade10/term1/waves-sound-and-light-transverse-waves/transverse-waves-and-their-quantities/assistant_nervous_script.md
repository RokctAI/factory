Hey, it's Thandi — I know this one looks like a pile of new words dropped on you all at once. Amplitude, wavelength, period, frequency, crest, trough, in phase, out of phase, and a Greek letter you have never written before. It feels like a language test disguised as physics. It isn't. There are only four things to measure, and two of them are lengths and two of them are times.

Sort them into those two boxes and the panic drops by half. The LENGTH box: amplitude, measured up and down from the rest line, and wavelength, measured along the wave from one crest to the next. Both in metres. The TIME box: period, how long one wave takes, in seconds, and frequency, how many waves fit into one second, in hertz. Two lengths, two times. That is the whole vocabulary.

The one habit that protects your marks more than anything else: write the unit next to every single number, every time, even in your rough working. If your answer for a frequency comes out in seconds, you know instantly that you flipped it the wrong way — the unit catches the mistake before the marker does. And do your conversions on the substitution line, not in your head: 3 cm becomes 0,03 m right there on the page where you can see it.

Two traps worth naming out loud, because they catch almost everybody. Amplitude is rest line to crest, never crest to trough — crest to trough is double. And before you measure anything off a graph, read the bottom axis: if it says distance, you are measuring a wavelength; if it says time, you are measuring a period. The pictures look identical, so let the axis tell you which one it is.

Then the sum. v equals f lambda — speed is frequency times wavelength — and you can rebuild it any time you doubt it. In one second the wave sends out f humps and each is lambda long, so it travels f times lambda in that second. That is a speed. No memorising needed; you can talk yourself back to it.

Four quantities, two boxes, one equation, and units written down every time. That's it. You've got this!
