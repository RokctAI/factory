**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! First one everybody argues about: if the rope's particles only go up and down, how does anything actually get to the far end? What is moving along the rope?

**Tutor:** Energy is moving, and it moves by being handed on. Think about what happens the instant you lift your end. That first piece of rope rises, and because it is physically joined to the piece beside it, it drags that neighbour upward a fraction of a second later. That neighbour drags the next one, and so on down the line. Each piece does the same small job: it rises, it pulls its neighbour up, it falls back to where it started. No piece of rope travels anywhere. What travels is the instruction to move, together with the energy needed to obey it — and that energy came from your arm. The proof is the ribbon test. Tie a ribbon anywhere on the rope, send a pulse, and afterwards the ribbon is exactly where it was. If matter had travelled, the ribbon would have moved along with it. This is not a rope curiosity either; it is the definition of a wave and it applies to every wave you will meet. When you shout across a soccer field, no air travels from your mouth to your friend's ear. Air molecules jostle their neighbours and return to where they started, and your voice's energy arrives. A wave is a transfer of energy through a medium without a transfer of matter, and that single sentence is worth writing out until it is automatic.

***

**Thandi:** Next: the equation says v equals f lambda, so surely if I shake the rope faster, the wave must travel faster? Everyone in the class expects that and the memo says no.

**Tutor:** The memo is right, and the reason is that the equation is a relationship between three quantities, not an instruction about which one causes which. The wave speed on a rope is fixed by the rope: how tight it is pulled, and how heavy the rope is per metre. A tighter rope carries waves faster; a heavy rope carries them slower. Your hand has no vote on that at all. What your hand controls is the frequency — how many humps you send off per second. So when you shake twice as fast, the speed cannot change, which means the wavelength has to halve so that f times lambda still gives the same v. Picture it physically: the wave front is still crawling along at the same rate, but you are now stuffing twice as many humps into the same length of rope, so each hump must be half as long. The general rule to carry with you is this: the source decides the frequency, the medium decides the speed, and the wavelength is the one that adjusts to keep the equation true. That rule will matter again with sound moving from air into water, and again with light entering glass — the frequency stays put and the wavelength changes.

***

**Thandi:** This one caused a real fight: two learners measured the same wave and got different wavelengths off two different graphs. How is that possible?

**Tutor:** Almost certainly one of them was not looking at a wavelength at all. There are two graphs in this topic and they look exactly alike — the same repeating wavy shape — but they answer different questions, and the horizontal axis is the only thing that tells them apart. A displacement-versus-position graph is a photograph of the whole wave along the rope at one frozen instant. On that graph the repeating distance along the bottom is the wavelength, measured in metres. A displacement-versus-time graph is completely different: it forgets the rest of the rope and follows one single particle as it bobs up and down. On that graph the repeating interval along the bottom is the period, measured in seconds. So a learner who measured the repeat on the time graph and called it a wavelength got the right number off the paper and attached the wrong name and the wrong unit to it. The discipline is simple and it never fails: before you measure anything, read the horizontal axis label out loud. Distance on the bottom means you are measuring wavelength. Time on the bottom means you are measuring period. And notice that the vertical axis is the same on both graphs, which is why you can always read the amplitude off either one.

**Thandi:** Last one: why does the syllabus make such a fuss about points being in phase? It feels like a definition invented purely to trip us up.

**Tutor:** It looks that way until you see where it leads. In phase means two points are doing the identical thing at the same moment — the same displacement, and moving in the same direction. Both at a crest counts. Both at a trough counts. Both passing through the rest line on the way up counts. But one at a crest and one at a trough does not, because although both are at maximum displacement they are at opposite ends of the motion; those points are out of phase. Now here is why it earns its place. Points in phase are always a whole number of wavelengths apart — one, two, three lambdas — and points exactly out of phase are an odd number of half wavelengths apart. That means phase is really a way of measuring distance in units of wavelength, and once you can do that you can predict what happens when two waves meet. Two waves arriving in phase reinforce each other; that is constructive interference and it is why some seats in a hall are loud. Two waves arriving exactly out of phase cancel; that is destructive interference and it is why other seats are strangely quiet. The definition you are being asked to memorise now is the tool you will use in Grade 11 and 12 for interference and for noise-cancelling headphones. Learn it as a distance idea, not a picture idea, and it stops feeling arbitrary.
