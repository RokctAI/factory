# Part 1 — Expert

Every wave in this year's physics — the ripple crossing a farm dam, the sound leaving a taxi hooter, the light arriving from the sun — is described by the same short list of quantities and tied together by one equation. This session builds that list precisely: what a pulse is, what turns a pulse into a wave, how amplitude, wavelength, period and frequency are defined and measured, and how the wave equation converts any two of them into the third. The arithmetic is light; the discipline — correct symbols, correct SI units, and never confusing a period with a frequency — is where the marks live.

## Subtopic: From a Single Pulse to a Travelling Wave

Begin with the smallest disturbance that physics names. A PULSE is a single disturbance that moves through a medium. Lay a long rope along the ground, hold one end, flick your wrist once, and a single hump runs down the rope to the far end. That hump is a pulse.

It is a TRANSVERSE pulse because of the geometry of the movement. The particles of the medium move at right angles — perpendicular — to the direction in which the pulse travels. Tie a ribbon to one fibre of the rope and watch it: the ribbon rises, falls, and settles back exactly where it started. The pulse travels; the medium does not. What is carried to the far end is ENERGY, handed on from particle to particle.

The AMPLITUDE of a pulse is the maximum displacement of a particle from its rest position — its equilibrium position — measured in metres. Flick harder and the amplitude grows, because you gave the rope more energy.

When two pulses meet they obey the PRINCIPLE OF SUPERPOSITION: at every point the resultant displacement is the algebraic sum of the displacements the two pulses would each produce alone. Two pulses on the same side of the rest line add — CONSTRUCTIVE interference, a taller combined hump. A pulse above meeting a pulse below subtract — DESTRUCTIVE interference, and equal pulses leave the rope momentarily flat. Then, crucially, both pulses continue on unchanged. Interference is a meeting, not a collision.

Now repeat the flick, steadily, over and over, and pulse follows pulse down the rope: that continuous train of transverse pulses is a TRANSVERSE WAVE. A wave is therefore a repeated disturbance that transfers energy through a medium without transferring matter — and every quantity that follows measures either the size of that disturbance or its timing.

The questions for this section are with you now: hold the definitions of pulse, transverse and amplitude exactly as they were stated, and be ready to say what travels along the rope and what does not.

## Subtopic: Crest, Trough, Amplitude and Wavelength

The picture of a transverse wave is a repeating shape rising above and dipping below a horizontal rest line, and every feature on it has a name the memo expects in full.

A CREST is a point at maximum positive displacement — the top of a hump. A TROUGH is a point at maximum negative displacement — the bottom of a dip. The horizontal rest line is the equilibrium position, where the medium sits when undisturbed.

AMPLITUDE, symbol A, is the maximum displacement of a particle of the medium from its equilibrium position. SI unit: the metre. Read that definition slowly, because the commonest single mark lost on this topic is measuring from a crest DOWN to a trough. That vertical distance is TWO amplitudes. If a crest stands 6 centimetres above the rest line and the trough 6 centimetres below it, the amplitude is 6 centimetres — 0,06 metres — and not twelve.

WAVELENGTH, symbol λ, the Greek letter lambda, is the distance between two successive points that are IN PHASE. SI unit: the metre. Crest to the very next crest is one wavelength; trough to the very next trough is one wavelength; so is the distance from any point to the next point doing exactly the same thing.

That phrase "in phase" carries its own definition, and examiners test it directly. Two points are IN PHASE when they have the same displacement AND are moving in the same direction — both at a crest, or both rising through the rest line together — and they sit a whole number of wavelengths apart. Two points are OUT OF PHASE when they do opposite things — one at a crest while the other is at a trough — and they sit an odd number of half wavelengths apart: half a lambda, one and a half, two and a half.

Amplitude and wavelength never trade places. Amplitude is measured up and down, at right angles to the direction of travel, and reports how much energy the wave carries. Wavelength is measured along the direction of travel, and reports how the wave is spaced.

Take the questions for this section now — expect to be asked for the amplitude from a sketch, for a wavelength measured between two named points, and for a pair of points that are in phase.

## Subtopic: Period, Frequency and the Wave Equation

Two further quantities describe the wave's TIMING, and they are reciprocals of each other.

The PERIOD, symbol T, is the time taken for one complete wave to pass a fixed point — equivalently, the time for one particle to complete one full oscillation. SI unit: the second.

The FREQUENCY, symbol f, is the number of complete waves passing a fixed point per second. SI unit: the hertz, written Hz, which means one per second — seconds to the power minus one.

In symbols, f = 1/T; in words, frequency is one divided by the period. Turned around, T = 1/f — the period is one divided by the frequency. If ten waves pass every second, each takes a tenth of a second. Same fact, read from either end.

A first calculation. Fifteen complete waves pass a fishing float on the Vaal in 5 seconds. Frequency is the number of waves divided by the time: f = 15 ÷ 5 = 3 Hz. Then the period: T = 1/f = 1 ÷ 3 = 0,33 s.

Now the equation that joins timing to spacing. In exactly one period the wave advances exactly one wavelength — that is what a period means. Speed is distance divided by time, so v = λ ÷ T; but one divided by T is the frequency, so speed equals frequency multiplied by wavelength. In symbols: v = fλ. In words: v equals f lambda — wave speed equals frequency times wavelength. The units agree, since hertz times metres gives metres per second, m·s⁻¹.

Worked example. In a ripple tank the vibrator runs at 20 Hz and the wavelength on the water surface is 3 cm. Find the speed. Formula: v = fλ. Convert first — 3 cm = 0,03 m. Substitute: v = (20)(0,03). Answer: v = 0,6 m·s⁻¹.

Worked example, rearranged. Waves on a dam travel at 1,5 m·s⁻¹ with a period of 0,5 s. Find the wavelength. First the frequency: f = 1/T = 1 ÷ 0,5 = 2 Hz. Then from v = fλ, λ = v ÷ f = 1,5 ÷ 2 = 0,75 m. Notice the order in both — formula, substitution, answer with its unit — because each of those lines carries marks of its own.

Work through the questions for this section now, and set out every calculation in the three-line form you have just seen.

## Subtopic: The Traps That Cost Marks

Four errors account for most of the marks lost on this topic, and each has a fixed cure.

The first is unit conversion. Wavelengths arrive in centimetres, periods in milliseconds, frequencies in kilohertz — and the formula demands metres, seconds and hertz. Convert on the line where you write the substitution, not in your head: 3 cm becomes 0,03 m; 25 ms becomes 0,025 s; 4 kHz becomes 4 000 Hz. A perfect method with a centimetre still inside it earns the method marks and loses the answer.

The second is amplitude read from crest to trough. Say the definition before you measure: maximum displacement FROM THE REST POSITION. Crest to trough is double.

The third is the period-frequency swap. They are reciprocals with different units answering opposite questions — the period asks how long one wave takes, the frequency asks how many waves fit into one second. A period of 0,25 s is a frequency of 4 Hz. Write the unit next to every number and the swap becomes impossible: an answer in seconds cannot be a frequency.

The fourth is the graph-axis error, and it is the most expensive. A displacement-versus-POSITION graph is a photograph of the wave along the rope, so its repeating distance is the WAVELENGTH. A displacement-versus-TIME graph watches one particle bobbing, so its repeating interval is the PERIOD. The two look identical. Read the horizontal axis label first, every time.

One conceptual trap remains. Because v = fλ, learners conclude that raising the frequency raises the speed. It does not. Wave speed is fixed by the medium — how taut the rope is, how deep the water is. Shake faster and the speed stays put while the wavelength shortens in exact proportion. The source sets the frequency; the medium sets the speed; the wavelength adjusts.

The final questions of this part are in front of you now: convert the units before you substitute, name the quantity each axis is measuring, and keep the period and the frequency strictly apart.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same waves from the stands at a stadium, a skipping rope in the street and a bucket of water — same definitions, same answers, and no symbols until we have earned them.

## Subtopic: The Wave in the Stands

Picture a packed stadium on a Saturday. One section stands up, throws their arms in the air, and sits down. The section next to them copies a moment later, then the next, then the next — and a wave runs right around the ground.

Now look at what actually happened. Nobody walked anywhere. Every single person moved straight UP and straight DOWN, then sat back into the same seat. But the wave moved SIDEWAYS around the stadium. People moving one way, wave moving the other, at right angles — that crossways relationship is exactly what the word "transverse" is pointing at, and it is what a rope does when you flick it.

One ripple of standing-and-sitting running once around the ground is a pulse. Everybody doing it over and over, so ripple follows ripple round the stands — that is a wave.

And here is the part worth keeping: what travelled round the stadium was not people. It was the energy of standing up, passed along seat to seat. That is what every wave does — it moves energy from place to place without carrying the stuff along with it. Your voice reaches a friend without any air travelling from your mouth to her ear.

Small check before we go on — some questions on the stadium are coming to you now, especially on what moves sideways and what only moves up and down.

## Subtopic: Measuring a Wave with a Skipping Rope

Take a skipping rope, tie one end to the gate, and shake the other end steadily up and down. Everything a wave question can ask, you can now measure with your own hands.

How HIGH you flick is the amplitude — measured from the flat, lazy line the rope makes when you stop, up to the top of a hump. Flick gently, small amplitude. Flick hard, big amplitude, and your arm tires faster because you are putting in more energy. Be careful of one thing only: amplitude is rest line to top, not top to bottom. Top to bottom is two of them, and that mistake costs more marks than anything else in this topic.

How far apart the humps sit is the wavelength — from the top of one hump to the top of the next. And you control it. Shake slowly and the humps come out long and lazy. Shake twice as fast and you fit twice as many humps into the same rope, so each one is half as long.

Now stand your friend beside the rope and count. How long does one whole hump take to pass her foot — one up, one down, back to the start? That time is the period. How many whole humps pass her foot in one second? That count is the frequency.

Those two are the same fact wearing different clothes. If four humps pass her foot every second, each hump takes a quarter of a second. Flip one and you get the other — which is all that little formula f equals one over T is saying: frequency is one divided by the period.

Your questions on measuring are up now: rest line to hump-top for the amplitude, hump-top to hump-top for the wavelength, and the flip between how-many-per-second and how-long-each-takes.

## Subtopic: One Sum That Does Everything

Here is the only real calculation in the topic, built from the rope rather than handed to you.

In one second, your hand sends off a certain number of humps — call it the frequency. Each hump is a certain length — call it the wavelength. So how far has the front of the wave travelled in that one second? Number of humps, times the length of each hump. That distance in one second IS the speed. And that is the whole wave equation: v equals f lambda — wave speed is frequency times wavelength. It is just counting humps and multiplying by their length.

Try it on the ripple tank. The vibrator taps the water 20 times a second and each ripple measures 3 centimetres. Change centimetres into metres first — 3 centimetres is 0,03 of a metre — then multiply: 20 times 0,03 gives 0,6. The ripples move at 0,6 metres per second. Twenty ripples a second, each three centimetres long, is sixty centimetres of water surface covered every second. It agrees, because it is the same sentence twice.

Run it backwards when the question hands you the speed instead. Dam waves move at 1,5 metres per second and one full wave takes half a second. Half a second each means two waves a second, so the frequency is 2 hertz. Now share the 1,5 metres between those two waves: 1,5 divided by 2 gives 0,75. Each wave is 0,75 metres long.

Two habits protect all of it. Turn every centimetre into metres and every millisecond into seconds BEFORE you multiply. And when a graph appears, read the bottom axis label out loud before measuring: distance on the bottom means you are measuring a wavelength, time on the bottom means a period. Same picture, different question. Get that right and this topic is genuinely yours.

Last questions of the lesson are with you right now: convert first, multiply second, and check the bottom axis before you trust any measurement you take off a graph.
