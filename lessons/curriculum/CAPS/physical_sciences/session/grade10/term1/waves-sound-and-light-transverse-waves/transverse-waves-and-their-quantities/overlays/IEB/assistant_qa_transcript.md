**Thandi:** Before you go — the questions that came up most on this topic. First: the lesson says two points a whole wavelength apart are in phase. But the definition also demands same displacement and same direction. Why are both conditions needed — surely same height is enough?

**Tutor:** Same height alone is a trap, and here is the picture that exposes it. Take a point climbing up through the rest line, and another point half a wavelength away sliding down through the rest line. Photograph them: identical displacement, both exactly zero. Same height. Yet they are doing precisely opposite jobs — one rising, one falling — and half a period later one sits at a crest while the other sits in a trough. Those two are perfectly OUT of phase despite matching displacements at that instant. That is why the definition carries two clauses: same displacement AND same direction of motion. Only when both hold do the points copy each other completely, and that happens exactly when they are separated by a whole number of wavelengths — one lambda, two lambda, three. The out-of-phase partners sit an odd number of half wavelengths apart: half, one and a half, two and a half. In an answer, quote both clauses and then give the spacing. If a question shows you two points and asks whether they are in phase, check the direction arrows before anything else — displacement alone has fooled half the class before, and it is designed to.

***

**Thandi:** Next: a friend measured the crest-to-trough height on a diagram, got 16 centimetres, halved it and wrote amplitude equals 8 centimetres — and still lost a mark for not showing the definition. What should the written answer actually look like?

**Tutor:** The halving instinct is fine physics but incomplete paperwork, and the fix costs one line. The definition — maximum displacement of a particle from its equilibrium position — is itself creditable, so open with it. Then show where your number comes from: the crest-to-trough distance on the diagram is 16 centimetres, and since that distance spans from maximum positive to maximum negative displacement, it equals two amplitudes. Write that middle step explicitly: two A equals 16 centimetres, so A equals 8 centimetres, which is 0,08 metres. Three short lines — definition, the two-amplitude statement, the value with SI unit — and every mark is collected. And know why the shortcut version is risky even when the arithmetic is right: without the two-amplitude line, whoever reads your work cannot tell whether you understood the geometry or divided by two on luck. Diagrams sometimes bait exactly this, by drawing the rest line off-centre so crest and trough heights differ; then the honest method — measure from the rest line — is the only one that survives. Measure from equilibrium every time, and let the crest-to-trough distance be a check, never the source.

***

**Thandi:** Then the calculation one. In the harbour-swell example we had to find the frequency first and then the wavelength. A lot of us tried to jam the period straight into v equals f lambda. Is there a fixed route through these two-step problems?

**Tutor:** There is, and it starts before any algebra: take inventory. The wave equation owns exactly three quantities — speed, frequency, wavelength. The period is not one of them; it is frequency's other face. So step one, list what the question gave you with units attached: v equals 2,4 metres per second, T equals 0,8 seconds. Step two, spot the imposter — a period sitting where the equation wants a frequency — and convert it immediately: f equals one over T, one divided by 0,8, which is 1,25 hertz. Do that on its own line, because that line carries credit. Step three, only now bring out v equals f lambda, rearrange for the unknown before substituting — lambda equals v over f — then substitute converted numbers: 2,4 divided by 1,25, giving 1,92 metres. Answer with unit, done. The whole discipline is: never let T touch the wave equation directly. If you see period in the question, your first written move is turning it into frequency — or, if you prefer, use v equals lambda over T, but pick one route and write it as a formula first. Students who substitute before rearranging, or rearrange in their heads, are the ones who end up dividing the wrong way and getting 3 metres. The inventory habit catches that before it happens.

***

**Thandi:** Last one: the two graphs. Displacement against position, displacement against time — identical curves. In a test, what is the fastest safe way to tell which quantity the repeat distance is giving me?

**Tutor:** Use a two-question drill, spoken in your head before your pencil moves. Question one: what is on the horizontal axis? Read the label and its unit — metres or seconds. That alone sorts the twins: metres along the bottom means the graph is a photograph of the whole rope at one frozen instant, so the repeat you measure is a distance between matching points — the wavelength. Seconds along the bottom means the camera stayed on one particle and recorded its bobbing, so the repeat is the duration of one full oscillation — the period. Question two, as a safety net: ask what one bump on the graph physically is. On the position graph a bump is a real hump you could walk along; on the time graph the bump is one up-and-down of a single point, which has no length in space at all — a common wrong answer is quoting a "wavelength in seconds", which should sound broken the moment you attach the unit. Then let the graphs feed the calculation: read amplitude off either one from the rest line, take lambda from the position graph, take T from the time graph, flip T into f, and multiply f by lambda for the speed. Given both graphs, that is the classic full-route question — axis label first, unit attached to every number, and the twins can never swap on you.
