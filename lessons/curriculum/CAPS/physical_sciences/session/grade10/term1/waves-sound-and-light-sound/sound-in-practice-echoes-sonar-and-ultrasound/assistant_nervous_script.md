Hey, it's Thandi — if sonar, echolocation and ultrasound feel like three new topics stacked on top of the sound work, relax — they are one equation and one question, wearing different uniforms.

The equation you already own: v equals f lambda. For sound it has a fixed cast. The medium owns the speed — 340 in air, 1 500 in water, no exceptions, no matter how loud. The source owns the frequency. The wavelength is just what is left over: v divided by f. Every wavelength in this lesson — the bat's 5 millimetres, the dolphin's 2 centimetres, the sonar's 5 centimetres — came from that one division.

The question that sorts every calculation: did the sound come back to where it started? Thunder — no, one-way trip, no halving; three seconds means about a kilometre. Sonar ping, bat squeak, hospital scanner — yes, out and back, so halve the round trip. Write "one-way" or "round trip" at the top of your working and the famous factor of two takes care of itself.

And the one idea that explains all the high squeaking: short waves see small things. Waves slide around anything smaller than themselves, so to bounce off a moth you need moth-sized waves, and moth-sized waves mean high frequency. That is the whole reason ultrasound exists — plus the gentle bonus that sound is not ionising radiation, which is why it is safe to look at a baby with it.

Keep the kilohertz honest — 30 kHz is 30 000 before it enters any formula — and this topic is four marks of division, two marks of halving, and a sentence about safety. Go collect them. You've got this!
