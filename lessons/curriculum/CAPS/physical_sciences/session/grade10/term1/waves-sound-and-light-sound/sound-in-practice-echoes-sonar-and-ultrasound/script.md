# Part 1 — Expert

Sound as a longitudinal wave — compressions, rarefactions, pitch and loudness — is established ground. This session puts that machinery to work. The wave equation v equals f lambda applied properly to sound, sonar calculations where echo timing and the wave equation join forces, the biological sonar of bats and dolphins, and the reason hospitals see unborn babies with sound: why the shortness of ultrasound's wavelength is exactly what makes it useful.

## Subtopic: The Wave Equation Working for Sound

Everything in this session runs on one equation: v equals f lambda — wave speed equals frequency times wavelength. For sound, the three symbols have very particular jobs, and knowing which one is fixed by what is where the marks live.

The speed v belongs to the MEDIUM. In air at everyday temperatures, use 340 m·s⁻¹ unless a question says otherwise; in water, about 1 500 m·s⁻¹; in steel, around 5 000 m·s⁻¹. In a gas, warmer means faster, because the molecules already move faster and pass the compression on sooner. The frequency f belongs to the SOURCE — a loudspeaker driven at 500 Hz makes 500 compressions every second, and nothing about the medium changes that count. Which leaves lambda, the wavelength, as the negotiated result: lambda equals v divided by f.

Run it. A loudspeaker at 500 Hz in air: lambda equals 340 divided by 500. That is 0,68 m — compressions spaced about two-thirds of a metre apart marching across the room.

Now the classic deep question: the same 500 Hz sound crosses from air into water. The frequency CANNOT change — every compression that arrives at the surface enters the water, 500 per second in, 500 per second on. But the speed jumps to 1 500 m·s⁻¹. So the wavelength must stretch: lambda equals 1 500 divided by 500, which is 3 m. Same note, same frequency, more than four times the wavelength. When sound changes medium, frequency stays, speed and wavelength change together. That sentence settles a whole family of exam questions.

Keep the unit discipline ready: kilohertz means thousands of hertz, so 68 kHz is 68 000 Hz before it touches the equation. Take the questions for this section now — who owns each symbol, and what survives a change of medium.

## Subtopic: Sonar — Timing Plus the Wave Equation

SONAR — sound navigation and ranging — is the technology of asking the sea a question and timing the answer. A ship's transducer fires a short pulse of sound straight down; the pulse reflects off the seabed; a detector times the returning echo. Two tools then work together: the echo rule for distance, and the wave equation for wavelength.

The echo rule first, with its famous factor: the pulse travels down AND back, so the depth is d equals v times delta t, divided by 2.

Worked example, complete. A survey ship off Durban sends a sonar pulse of frequency 30 kHz into seawater, where sound travels at 1 500 m·s⁻¹. The echo returns after 1,6 s. First the depth. Formula: d equals v times delta t divided by 2. Substitute: d equals 1 500 times 1,6, divided by 2 — that is 2 400 divided by 2. Answer: d equals 1 200 m. Second, the wavelength of the pulse in the water. Convert: 30 kHz is 30 000 Hz. Formula: lambda equals v divided by f. Substitute: lambda equals 1 500 divided by 30 000. Answer: lambda equals 0,05 m — five centimetres.

Notice how the two calculations use DIFFERENT logic. The depth uses the echo's travel time and needs the factor of two. The wavelength uses the wave equation and has no factor of two anywhere — halving belongs to round trips, not to wavelengths. Mixing the two logics is the common catastrophe, so label each line: this is the timing calculation; this is the wave-equation calculation.

Why 30 kHz and not a hooter's 300 Hz? A 300 Hz sound in seawater has a wavelength of 5 m — it would wash over a wreck or a shoal of sardines and return almost no useful reflection. The 0,05 m pulse reflects crisply off objects around its own size. Short wavelengths see small things; hold that thought, because nature discovered it first.

This section's questions are with you now: the two-step sonar problem, each step with its own formula and its own logic.

## Subtopic: Bats and Dolphins — Nature's Sonar

Long before ships, evolution built sonar twice — in the air and in the sea.

A bat hunting moths at dusk emits pulses of ULTRASOUND — sound above the human hearing limit of about 20 000 Hz; the syllabus band for ultrasound runs from 20 kHz to 100 kHz. Take a bat calling at 68 kHz in air. Wavelength: lambda equals v divided by f, which is 340 divided by 68 000. Answer: 0,005 m — five millimetres. That is moth-sized, and that is the entire point. A wave only reflects usefully off objects about as big as its own wavelength or bigger, so a five-millimetre wavelength turns a moth into a mirror. The bat's deep audible squeak would flow around the insect like a wave around a jetty pole and report nothing.

The bat also times its echoes, and the arithmetic is pure echo rule. A moth flies 1,7 m away. The pulse's round trip is 2 times 1,7, which is 3,4 m, at 340 m·s⁻¹: time equals distance divided by speed, 3,4 divided by 340, which is 0,01 s. One hundredth of a second — and the bat updates that reading many times a second as it closes in, steering by ears in total darkness. This is ECHOLOCATION: locating objects by emitted sound and returned echoes.

Dolphins run the same system underwater. A dolphin clicking at 75 kHz in seawater at 1 500 m·s⁻¹ produces wavelengths of 1 500 divided by 75 000, which is 0,02 m — two centimetres, fine enough to pick out a single fish, and even to read its size from the strength of the echo. Note what the dolphin's medium buys it: at 1 500 m·s⁻¹ its answers come back more than four times sooner than a bat's would over the same distance.

Same physics, three users — ship, bat, dolphin: emit high frequency for short wavelength, time the echo, halve the round trip. Take this section's questions now.

## Subtopic: The Ear's Limits, and Ultrasound in Medicine

The human ear hears from about 20 Hz to about 20 000 Hz — and even inside that band it does not treat all frequencies equally. The ear is MOST sensitive in the middle of its range, around the frequencies of human speech; near the extreme low and high ends, a sound needs a much larger amplitude to seem equally loud. That is why loudness is not the same thing as amplitude: loudness is the ear's impression, built from the amplitude AND the ear's sensitivity at that frequency. Age and loud-noise damage trim the top of the range first, which is why a phone ringtone at 17 kHz can be piercing to a teenager and silent to the teacher.

Above 20 kHz the ear gives up, but medicine does not. An ultrasound scanner presses a transducer against the skin and sends pulses of very high-frequency sound into the body, where they travel at about 1 500 m·s⁻¹ — soft tissue behaves acoustically much like water. At every boundary between tissues — muscle to bone, fluid to skin — part of the pulse REFLECTS. The machine times each returning echo, uses the echo rule to work out how deep that boundary lies, and assembles the depths into a picture.

Feel the scale of the timing. A boundary 0,06 m deep returns its echo after time equals 2 times 0,06 divided by 1 500 — that is 0,12 divided by 1 500, which is 0,000 08 s. Eighty millionths of a second, measured routinely, thousands of times per sweep.

Why sound and not X-rays for a pregnancy? Because ultrasound is a mechanical pressure wave, NOT ionising radiation. X-rays and gamma rays carry enough energy per photon to strip electrons from atoms and damage cells; ultrasound does not, so a developing baby can be scanned safely and repeatedly. Beyond imaging, focused ultrasound shatters kidney stones without surgery, cleans instruments, and probes metal castings for hidden cracks — every one of these an echo story.

The final questions of this part are in front of you: the ear's range and its uneven sensitivity, the scanner's echo logic, and the safety argument stated properly.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same physics with a thunderstorm, a supermarket shelf of wavelengths, and a torch that shines sound instead of light.

## Subtopic: Counting the Storm

A Highveld storm rolls in. Lightning flashes — and you start counting. One, two, three — then the thunder arrives. How far away is the storm?

The flash and the bang happened at the same instant, in the same place. The light reached you essentially immediately. The sound trudged toward you at 340 metres every second. Three seconds of trudging: 340 times 3 is 1 020 metres. The storm is about a kilometre away. Count six seconds next time and it is about two kilometres — the storm is leaving. Every three seconds of gap is roughly another kilometre.

Now the important contrast. In storm counting you do NOT halve anything. The sound made a ONE-WAY trip — from the lightning to you. Halving belongs to ECHOES, where sound goes out and comes back: shout at a cliff, ping the seabed, squeak at a moth. One journey, no halving; round trip, halve. Before any sound calculation, ask one question: did the sound come back to where it started? Write the answer down, and the factor of two sorts itself out.

And remember whose property the 340 is. It is the air's number, not the storm's. The loudest thunder and the faintest rumble cross town at the same speed — thunder cannot hurry by being angry.

Some questions on the storm count are with you now, and the one-way-versus-round-trip decision is the heart of them.

## Subtopic: Shopping With Five-Millimetre Waves

Why do bats squeak so absurdly high? Not to be dramatic — to SHOP for small things.

Here is the picture. Waves only bounce properly off things around their own size or bigger. Think of sea waves and a jetty: long rolling swells slide past a thin pole as if it were not there, but the same pole stops and scatters tiny ripples. The pole is a "small thing"; only short waves notice it.

Now put numbers on the bat. Squeaking at 68 000 vibrations per second in air gives waves 5 millimetres long — moth-sized waves. To a five-millimetre wave, a moth is a wall: the squeak slaps off it and races back. If the bat called at our comfortable speaking pitch instead, its waves would be a metre long, and a metre-long wave flows around a moth like a swell around the jetty pole. The moth would be invisible. So the rule of the shop is: the smaller the thing you want to detect, the shorter the wave you must send — and shorter wave means higher frequency, because speed is fixed by the air.

The dolphin plays the same game underwater with two-centimetre waves, fine enough to single out one fish. And the timing side is your storm count plus the echo halving: the bat hears its squeak return after one hundredth of a second, knows the round trip was 3,4 metres, halves it, and strikes at a moth 1,7 metres ahead.

Squeak short, hear it return, halve the trip. Your questions on nature's sonar are up now.

## Subtopic: Seeing With Echoes

A torch shows you a dark room by bouncing light off things into your eyes. An ultrasound scanner shows a doctor the inside of a body by bouncing SOUND off things into a detector — a torch made of sound, shone through skin.

The scanner sends in tiny clicks, pitched far, far above anything an ear can hear. Inside the body, every time the click crosses from one kind of tissue into another — muscle to bone, fluid to baby — a little echo splashes back. The machine is a champion storm counter: it times every echo, and since sound in the body moves at about 1 500 metres a second, an echo that took eighty millionths of a second came from a boundary six centimetres deep — out and back, halved, exactly the cliff rule. Thousands of these timed echoes, side by side, and a picture forms on the screen: the outline of a heart valve moving, or a baby sucking its thumb months before it is born.

Why not just X-ray? Because X-rays are the harsh chemistry kind of ray — energetic enough to damage the cells they pass through, which is the last thing a growing baby needs. Ultrasound is only sound: pushes and squashes, the gentle mechanical kind of wave, with no cell damage. That is why the scan can be done safely, and repeated as often as needed.

And once you can see with echoes, you can do more than look. Aim strong, focused ultrasound at a kidney stone and the pressure waves hammer it into sand the body can pass — surgery with no cut. Engineers fire the same clicks into metal to find invisible cracks; jewellers rattle the dirt off rings in ultrasonic baths.

Last questions of the lesson are with you right now: echoes timed, round trips halved, short waves for small things, and sound chosen over X-rays for the gentlest of reasons. That is sound, working.
