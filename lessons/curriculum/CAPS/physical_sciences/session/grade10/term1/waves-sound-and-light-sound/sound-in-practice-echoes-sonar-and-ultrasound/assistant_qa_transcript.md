**Thandi:** Before you move on — the questions that come up a lot with this topic. The first: why does the frequency stay the same when sound crosses from air into water? People want to know who decided that.

**Tutor:** Continuity decided it — count the compressions at the border and there is no other possibility. Picture the water's surface with 500 compressions per second arriving from the air side. Each compression that arrives pushes on the water and launches a compression into the water. One in, one on: the surface cannot store compressions in a queue, and it certainly cannot invent extra ones. So whatever arrives per second is what continues per second — 500 — and frequency is locked to the source forever, through every medium the sound will ever cross. Now the equation v equals f lambda has to reconcile that fixed f with a speed that jumped from 340 to 1 500 metres per second. The only free quantity is the wavelength, so it stretches from 0,68 metres to 3 metres — the compressions keep their arrival rate but space themselves out, like cars keeping their count per minute while speeding up on a wider road: same number passing, bigger gaps between them. The exam sentence to memorise: when sound changes medium, the frequency is unchanged, while the speed and wavelength both change, in proportion. Test yourself with the pitch check — a friend calling to you underwater sounds like the same note, not a higher one, because pitch follows frequency, and frequency never renegotiates.

***

**Thandi:** Next: in the two-part sonar question, people halve the wavelength as well as the depth, or forget to halve anything. How do you keep the two parts straight?

**Tutor:** By recognising that the two parts answer two different kinds of question with two unrelated tools, and labelling each before substituting a single number. Part one is a JOURNEY question: how far did the pulse travel in 1,6 seconds? Journey questions use speed times time, and then you must ask the round-trip question — did the sound return to its starting point? For sonar, yes: down to the seabed and back. So 1 500 times 1,6 gives 2 400 metres of total travel, and the seabed sits at half of that, 1 200 metres. The two belongs to the journey because the journey doubled. Part two is a SHAPE question: how long is one wave? Shape questions use lambda equals v over f — 1 500 divided by 30 000 gives 0,05 metres — and no journey appears anywhere in it. The wavelength is a property of the wave itself, identical whether the pulse travels one metre or ten kilometres, outbound or returning; halving it would be like halving the length of a taxi because it drove to Durban and back. So write two labelled headings in your answer: journey — echo rule, halve the round trip; shape — wave equation, no halving. One habit, two clean calculations, and the marker sees you knew exactly which physics belonged where.

**Thandi:** Then the favourite: why must a bat use ultrasound at all? A loud ordinary squeak seems like it should echo off a moth just fine.

**Tutor:** Loudness cannot fix what wavelength gets wrong. Whether a wave reflects off an object depends on how the object's size compares with the wave's length. A wave is a stretched-out thing: if the obstacle is much smaller than one wavelength, the wave bends around it and closes up behind, almost as if the obstacle were absent — the technical word is diffraction, and you have watched it at the beach, where long swells glide past a thin jetty pole while tiny ripples scatter off the same pole. Now put the bat's options side by side. An audible squeak at, say, 1 000 hertz in air is 340 divided by 1 000 — a wavelength of 0,34 metres. That is thirty times the size of a moth, so the sound flows around the insect and the returning echo is uselessly faint, no matter how loud the squeak was — turning up the volume turns up a reflection of almost nothing. At 68 000 hertz the wavelength is 5 millimetres, moth-sized, and the moth becomes a wall the squeak slaps off. That is the design rule for every echo-locator: choose a wavelength no bigger than your target. Dolphins with 2 centimetre waves resolve single fish; sonar at 5 centimetres maps wrecks; medical scanners push wavelengths below a millimetre to see fine structure. High frequency is not a stylistic choice — it is the price of seeing small.

**Thandi:** Last one: the lesson says the ear is more sensitive to middle frequencies than to high and low ones. What does that actually change in real life?

**Tutor:** It changes the difference between what a sound IS and what a sound SEEMS, and the syllabus wants that distinction in words. Amplitude is a physical property — how hard the source compressed the air; the ear's sensitivity is biological — how strongly your hearing responds at each frequency. Loudness is the combination: the impression built from both. The ear responds most strongly in its middle band, around the frequencies of human speech — roughly a few hundred to a few thousand hertz — and progressively less near its edges at 20 hertz and 20 000 hertz. So two sounds with exactly equal amplitude are not equally loud: a deep 50 hertz hum can be physically bigger than a 1 000 hertz tone yet seem softer, because the ear meets it half-asleep. Real-life consequences follow immediately. A baby's cry sits near the sensitive band, which is why it cuts through a noisy room. Ageing and noise damage take the top frequencies first, which is why a 17 kilohertz ringtone divides a classroom into teenagers wincing and adults hearing silence. And the exam consequence: never write that loudness is determined by amplitude alone. The full sentence is that loudness depends on amplitude AND on the sensitivity of the ear at that frequency — one physical ingredient, one biological one, and both named for both marks.
