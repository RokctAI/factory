**Thandi:** Quick round of the questions everyone whispered during this one. First: when sound goes from air into water, why is it the frequency that must stay the same? Why can't the frequency change and the wavelength stay put instead?

**Tutor:** Because the boundary has no way to store or invent compressions — count them through the surface and you will see the lock. The speaker sends 250 compressions at the water every second. Each one that arrives pushes on the surface and launches a compression into the water. If the water carried on at, say, 200 per second, then 50 compressions would have to pile up somewhere at the boundary every second, growing without limit — physically absurd. If the water somehow ran at 300 per second, it would be creating compressions from nothing. So arrivals per second must equal departures per second: the frequency is locked by simple counting, and only the source can ever set it. The wavelength has no such lock, because it is not conserved by anything — it is just the spacing that results once the medium applies its own speed. Water hurries each compression along at 1 500 metres per second while they still arrive only 250 times a second, so the spacing stretches: 1 500 divided by 250 gives 6 metres, against 1,36 metres in the air. The sentence to write in any answer: the frequency is unchanged because it is fixed by the source and the same number of compressions must cross the boundary per second; the speed changes because the medium changed; the wavelength changes with it so that v equals f lambda still holds.

***

**Thandi:** Next: in the sonar problem, some of us halved the wavelength as well as the depth — six centimetres became three. Why is that wrong, when the pulse clearly travelled down and back?

**Tutor:** Because the two numbers describe completely different things, and only one of them is a journey. The depth question is about DISTANCE TRAVELLED: the clock ran for 1,2 seconds, the pulse was moving the whole time, so it covered 1 800 metres — and since that journey was down and up, the seabed sits at half of it. The factor of two lives inside the phrase "travelled there and back". Now look at the wavelength. It is not a distance the pulse covered; it is the SPACING between neighbouring compressions inside the pulse — a property the wave carries with it, like the gap between carriages of a moving train. Ask yourself: does a train's carriage spacing halve because the train made a return trip? Obviously not — the spacing is set by how the train was built, and the wave's spacing is set by v equals f lambda: 1 500 divided by 25 000, six centimetres, on the way down, on the way up, and at every instant in between. So the discipline is to caption your working. Write "timing calculation" above the depth lines — that is where the two belongs — and "wave equation" above the wavelength lines, where a two would be an intruder. One journey, two different questions about it: how far did it go, and how is it spaced. Keep those apart and sonar problems become routine.

**Thandi:** Then the bat: I get that four-millimetre waves bounce off moths. But why do long waves ignore small objects? What are they actually doing when they "flow around" the moth?

**Tutor:** Picture what a wave is doing as it travels: each patch of air pushes the patch ahead of it, and the wavefront advances as a broad coordinated line. Now stand a small obstacle in the way — small compared with the wavelength. The wave's coordination operates on the scale of a wavelength: over any stretch of the front about one wavelength wide, the neighbouring patches of air are shoving in step and can fill in behind an obstruction. To a wave one metre long, a two-centimetre moth interrupts a tiny sliver of a front that is coordinated across a full metre — the neighbouring air simply closes ranks behind the moth, the front heals itself, and almost no energy is sent back toward the bat. The wave has not "decided" anything; its own spreading smooths the dent away. That healing has a name you will meet later — waves bend and fill in around obstacles comparable to or smaller than their wavelength. But shrink the wavelength to four millimetres and the same moth now spans hundreds of wavelengths: to this wave the moth is a broad wall, whole stretches of front strike it and cannot fill in around it, so a strong reflection heads back to the hunter. That is the deep version of the shop rule: reflection wins when the obstacle is large compared with the wavelength; filling-in wins when it is small. The bat's high squeak is simply a purchase of short wavelength — because the air's speed is fixed, the only way to shorten lambda is to raise f.

**Thandi:** Last one, exam craft: these problems mix depths, wavelengths, one-way trips and unit conversions. What is a checklist that catches the mistakes before the final answer goes down?

**Tutor:** Four checks, in the order the errors actually happen. Check one — units at the door: sweep the question for kilohertz, centimetres and milliseconds, and convert them in writing before any formula appears. 25 kHz becomes 25 000 Hz on its own line; a wavelength answer of 0,06 m may be reported as six centimetres afterwards, but it is born in metres. Check two — journey or property: for every quantity you compute, say which it is. Distances travelled come from speed times time and might need halving; wavelengths and frequencies come from v equals f lambda and never do. Check three — the bounce question: if it is a journey, ask whether the sound returned to its starting point. Echo off a cliff, ping to the seabed, squeak to the moth: round trip, divide by two, and show the division. Thunder to your ears, starter's gun across a field: one way, leave it whole. Check four — the sanity glance: hold the answer against the physical picture. A suburban quarry wall should be hundreds of metres away, not thirty kilometres; a wavelength used to spot a fish should be centimetres, not metres; a medical echo should return in millionths of a second, because centimetres of tissue at fifteen hundred metres per second cannot take longer. If a number fails the glance, the error is nearly always a missed conversion or a stray factor of two — which the first three checks will find. Units, journey-or-property, bounce, sanity: run them every time and this whole topic marks itself.
