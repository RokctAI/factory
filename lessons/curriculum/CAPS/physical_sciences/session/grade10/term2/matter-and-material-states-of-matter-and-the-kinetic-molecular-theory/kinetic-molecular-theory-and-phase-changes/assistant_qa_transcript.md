**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! The first one comes up every year: if the pot is being heated the whole time, why does the temperature just sit there while the water boils? It feels like the energy is disappearing.

**Tutor:** Nothing disappears — the energy simply changes job, and the thermometer is only qualified to report one of those jobs. Temperature is a measure of the average KINETIC energy of the particles: how fast they are moving or vibrating. While a substance is warming, every joule you supply goes into that kinetic energy, so the particles speed up and the thermometer climbs. At the boiling point the job changes. The particles are now moving fast enough that the next thing to happen is escape, and escape means breaking the forces of attraction that hold each particle to its neighbours. Breaking those forces costs energy, and that energy is stored as POTENTIAL energy in the newly separated particles — the way lifting a brick stores energy without making the brick move any faster. Since none of the incoming energy is going into speed, the average kinetic energy is unchanged, and a thermometer measuring average kinetic energy reads a flat line. The moment the last drop has turned to steam, there are no more grips left to break, the energy goes back into speed, and the temperature climbs again.

**Thandi:** Next: I get melting and boiling, but I keep losing marks on sublimation and deposition. Are they just rare curiosities, or is something real going on?

**Tutor:** They are real, they are examinable, and they are easier than they look once you stop treating liquid as a compulsory stop. A substance goes solid, liquid, gas as its particles gain freedom — but nothing in physics insists on passing through the middle stage. If the forces holding the solid together are weak enough, and the surrounding pressure is low enough, particles at the surface of the solid can pick up enough energy to leave directly for the gas state. That is sublimation, and solid carbon dioxide — dry ice — does it at ordinary room pressure, which is exactly why it appears to smoke without ever making a puddle. Iodine crystals do it too when gently warmed, giving a striking purple vapour. Deposition is the same road travelled backwards: gas particles losing energy and locking straight into a solid without pausing as liquid. You have almost certainly seen it — frost on the grass on a cold Highveld morning is water vapour from the air depositing as ice on a surface that is below freezing. No rain fell, no dew formed; the vapour went solid on the spot. Endothermic for sublimation, exothermic for deposition, and both examinable in exactly those words.

**Thandi:** This one caused an argument: someone said a swimming pool at twenty degrees contains more heat than a boiling kettle. That cannot be right, can it?

**Tutor:** It can, and the argument is the best possible way into the distinction the syllabus wants. Temperature and heat are different quantities. Temperature tells you how hard the particles are jiggling ON AVERAGE — it is an average per particle, and it takes no notice of how many particles there are. Heat is the total energy transferred, and it very much depends on how much substance you have. The kettle's particles are jiggling far harder, so its temperature is higher. But a swimming pool contains an enormous number of particles, each carrying a modest amount of energy, and the total can easily exceed the kettle's. A useful test: ask which one could melt more ice. The pool, easily — and that is a heat question, not a temperature question. This is also why an exam answer that says "the substance gets more heat so its temperature rises" is risky. Adding heat raises temperature only when the substance is not changing state; during melting or boiling, heat is added and the temperature stubbornly stays put.

**Thandi:** Last one: someone in class said sweating cools you down because the sweat is cold. That did not sound right, but nobody could say why.

**Tutor:** Correct instinct — the sweat leaves your body at body temperature, so coolness is not something it brings with it. It is something it TAKES. Look at what evaporation actually is. In any liquid, the particles have a spread of kinetic energies: some sluggish, some average, some unusually fast. Only the fastest ones, and only those at the surface heading outwards, have enough energy to break free of the forces holding them in the liquid. When those fastest particles leave, they take their above-average energy with them — so the average kinetic energy of what remains is LOWER than it was. Lower average kinetic energy means lower temperature. The liquid left on your skin is now cooler than your skin, so heat flows out of you into it, and you feel cooler. Notice the two conditions this explanation predicts, both of which you can check on a hot afternoon: evaporation cools better when the air is dry, because escaped particles are carried away instead of returning, and better when there is a breeze, for the same reason. On a humid Durban day the sweat sits there and you stay hot — not because the physics failed, but because the escaping particles have nowhere to go.
