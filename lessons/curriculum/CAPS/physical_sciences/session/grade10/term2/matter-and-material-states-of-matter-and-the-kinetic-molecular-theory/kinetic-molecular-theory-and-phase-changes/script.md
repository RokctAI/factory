# Part 1 — Expert

This session builds the model that explains every physical property you have ever observed: the Kinetic Molecular Theory. The worked problem carrying it is the heating curve of water — a two-kilogram block of ice at minus twenty degrees Celsius heated steadily until it is steam above one hundred degrees — and the question examiners ask about it every year: why does the temperature stop rising while the heating continues? Answer that in the language of particles, energy and forces, and the whole topic is yours.

## Subtopic: Matter Is Particles — and Here Is the Evidence

The Kinetic Molecular Theory rests on five statements. Learn them as a list, because "explain in terms of the kinetic molecular theory" is an instruction that expects them.

One: all matter is made of particles — atoms, molecules or ions. Two: those particles are in constant, random motion. Three: the particles possess kinetic energy, and the average kinetic energy of the particles increases as the temperature rises. Four: there are spaces between the particles. Five: there are forces of attraction between the particles — intermolecular forces — and the strength of those forces relative to the particles' kinetic energy decides which state the substance is in.

Two pieces of observable evidence support the model, and both are named in the syllabus.

Diffusion is the spreading of one substance through another because of the random motion of its particles. Open a bottle of vanilla essence at one end of a kitchen and the smell reaches the other end unaided. Drop a crystal of potassium permanganate into still water and the purple creeps outwards through the whole beaker. Nothing stirred it; the particles moved themselves. Diffusion is faster in gases than in liquids, and faster at higher temperature, because higher temperature means greater average kinetic energy.

Brownian motion is the sharper evidence. Smoke particles in air under a microscope jerk about along jagged, unpredictable paths. The specks are far too big to be moving themselves — they are being bombarded, unevenly and from all sides, by air particles too small to see. That is direct evidence for statement two: invisible particles exist and they are permanently in motion.

Keep one distinction sharp from the start. Temperature measures the AVERAGE kinetic energy of the particles; heat is energy transferred because of a temperature difference. A substance can absorb a great deal of heat with no change in temperature at all — as the heating curve is about to prove.

The questions for this section are with you now: the five statements listed, diffusion and Brownian motion each explained as evidence, and heat distinguished from temperature.

## Subtopic: Solid, Liquid and Gas in the Kinetic Molecular Theory

Three states, and for each one the syllabus wants four things described: the arrangement of the particles, their motion, the strength of the forces between them, and the resulting bulk properties.

A SOLID: particles closely packed in a fixed, regular arrangement — a lattice. They cannot move from place to place; they vibrate about fixed positions. The forces between them are strong, and the particles' kinetic energy is too small to overcome those forces. Consequences: fixed shape, fixed volume, very hard to compress because there is almost no space between particles, and high density.

A LIQUID: particles still close together, so the spaces remain small, but the arrangement is irregular and the particles slide past one another. The forces are still significant, but the particles now have enough kinetic energy to break free of fixed positions while remaining held to the body of the liquid. Consequences: a fixed volume but no fixed shape — a litre of water takes the shape of the bottle, the pot or the puddle — and still very hard to compress.

A GAS: particles far apart, in rapid, random, straight-line motion, colliding with one another and with the container walls. The forces between them are negligible, because the particles have far too much kinetic energy to be held. Consequences: no fixed shape and no fixed volume — a gas fills any container completely — easily compressed because most of the volume is empty space, and very low density. Gas pressure is simply the collisions of those particles against the container walls.

Read the pattern running through all three: a contest between the particles' kinetic energy and the forces of attraction between them. Forces winning gives a solid; forces and energy roughly matched gives a liquid; energy winning outright gives a gas. Heating supplies kinetic energy, cooling removes it, and every change of state is the moment the contest tips.

Stop for this section's questions now: arrangement, motion, forces and bulk properties given for each state, and the energy-against-forces contest used to explain which state a substance occupies.

## Subtopic: The Six Changes of State and the Energy They Need

Six changes, six names, and the examiner expects the name and the direction.

Melting: solid to liquid. Freezing, also called solidification: liquid to solid. Evaporation and boiling: liquid to gas. Condensation: gas to liquid. Sublimation: solid straight to gas, skipping the liquid entirely — solid carbon dioxide, sold as dry ice, does exactly this at ordinary pressure, and so does iodine when gently warmed. Deposition: gas straight to solid, which is how frost forms on Highveld grass on a winter night, water vapour becoming ice without ever being liquid.

Sort them by energy, because that sorting is worth marks. Melting, evaporation and sublimation all move a substance towards a state with weaker forces and more freedom, so all three ABSORB energy — they are endothermic. Freezing, condensation and deposition all move towards stronger holding, so all three RELEASE energy — they are exothermic. That is why sweat cooling your skin works: evaporating water takes its energy from you.

Two definitions must be exact. The melting point is the temperature at which a solid changes into a liquid, and it is identical to the freezing point of the same substance approached from the other side. The boiling point is the temperature at which a liquid changes into a gas throughout the liquid, when its vapour pressure equals the surrounding atmospheric pressure. Pure water melts at zero degrees Celsius and boils at one hundred degrees Celsius at sea level.

Distinguish evaporation from boiling. Evaporation happens at ANY temperature, only at the SURFACE, and only for particles with above-average kinetic energy heading upwards — which is precisely why the liquid left behind gets cooler. Boiling happens only AT the boiling point, throughout the liquid, with bubbles of vapour forming inside it.

And a South African consequence of that pressure definition: atmospheric pressure falls with altitude, so water boils at close to one hundred degrees at the Durban beachfront but at roughly ninety-five degrees on the Highveld — lower pressure, lower boiling point, and pasta that genuinely takes longer to cook in Gauteng.

This section's questions are in front of you now: the six changes named with their directions, sorted into endothermic and exothermic, and evaporation properly distinguished from boiling.

## Subtopic: Reading a Heating Curve — Why the Temperature Stops Rising

Now the worked curve. Two kilograms of ice at minus twenty degrees Celsius, heated at a steady rate, with temperature plotted upwards and time along. The graph has five parts, and you must name and explain each.

Part one: a rising line from minus twenty degrees to zero. The energy supplied increases the average kinetic energy of the particles, which vibrate faster; the substance is entirely solid ice. Part two: a horizontal plateau at zero degrees — melting, with ice and liquid water present together and the temperature fixed while any ice remains. Part three: a rising line from zero to one hundred degrees, all liquid water, kinetic energy and temperature climbing again. Part four: a longer horizontal plateau at one hundred degrees — boiling, liquid and steam together. Part five: a rising line above one hundred degrees, all steam.

Now the question that carries the marks: energy is still being supplied during the plateaus, so why does the temperature not rise? Because temperature measures average KINETIC energy, and during a change of state the supplied energy does not go into kinetic energy at all. It goes into overcoming the forces of attraction between the particles — increasing their POTENTIAL energy, pulling them apart into a freer arrangement. Particles are being separated, not sped up. Only once the last ice has melted does further energy raise the kinetic energy again, and only then does the thermometer move.

Note the shape detail examiners reward: the boiling plateau is markedly longer than the melting plateau, because separating liquid particles completely into a gas takes far more energy than loosening a lattice into a liquid.

The cooling curve is the exact mirror: steam cools, plateaus at one hundred while condensing, cools as liquid, plateaus at zero while freezing, then cools as ice — the plateaus now marking energy RELEASED as the forces re-form.

Reading state off the curve is the other standard question, and the rule is simple: below the melting point, solid only; on the melting plateau, solid and liquid together; between the plateaus, liquid only; on the boiling plateau, liquid and gas together; above it, gas only.

Three traps. One: writing that "no energy is being added" during a plateau — energy is added continuously; it is doing a different job. Two: confusing heat with temperature, and so expecting every joule to move the thermometer. Three: kelvin and Celsius muddled — add 273 to the Celsius reading, so zero degrees Celsius is 273 kelvin and one hundred degrees Celsius is 373 kelvin.

The final questions of this part are with you now: each section of the curve named and explained, the plateau accounted for in terms of potential energy and forces, and the state identified at a stated temperature.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same particles from school assembly, a packed taxi rank and an open field — because you have already watched all three states of matter behave, without knowing that was what you were watching.

## Subtopic: The Assembly, the Crowd and the Open Field

Everything around you is made of tiny particles, they never stop moving, and there are gaps and pulls between them. That is the whole model. What changes between ice, water and steam is not the particles — it is how tightly they are held and how much they are jiggling.

Picture a school assembly. Learners stand in neat rows, everyone in their spot, shoulder to shoulder. Nobody walks around, but nobody is frozen either — there is constant small fidgeting on the spot. The rows keep their shape, and you could not squash the crowd much tighter. That is a SOLID: fixed places, fixed shape, fixed size, particles vibrating where they stand.

Now assembly ends and everyone floods into the corridor. Still shoulder to shoulder, still packed — but now people slide past one another, and the crowd takes the shape of whatever corridor it is poured into. Same bodies, same space, completely different behaviour. That is a LIQUID: still close, no longer fixed, keeping its volume but taking the shape of its container.

Then everyone runs onto the sports field. Far apart now, moving fast in every direction, bumping only occasionally, spreading until the whole field is occupied. Push the fences in and they fit easily, because it is mostly empty space out there. That is a GAS: far apart, fast, filling whatever it is given, and easy to squash.

One idea decides which picture you are in: a tug-of-war between how much the particles jiggle and how strongly they pull on each other. Pull winning, everyone stays in rows — solid. Fairly even — liquid. Jiggle winning, everyone scatters — gas. Heating turns up the jiggle; cooling turns it down.

And how do we know invisible particles are really moving? Because you smell vanilla essence from across the kitchen with nobody fanning it. And because a speck of smoke under a microscope jitters like it is being shoved, which it is, by air particles too small to see.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on this piece are coming to you right now: the three pictures matched to the three states, and the tug-of-war used to say why each one behaves as it does.

## Subtopic: Why the Pot Sticks at One Hundred

Here is the thing that trips almost everybody, and a kettle proves it in your own kitchen.

Put a pot of water on the stove with a thermometer in it. The temperature climbs steadily — forty, sixty, eighty, ninety-nine, one hundred. Then it stops. The stove is still on full, the electricity is still costing you money, and the reading refuses to move past one hundred until the pot is nearly empty. Where is all that energy going?

Not into making the particles faster. Temperature just measures how hard the particles jiggle on average — so if the thermometer is not moving, the jiggling is not increasing. The energy is doing a different job: pulling the particles apart. Water particles hold onto each other, and turning liquid into steam means breaking every one of those grips and setting each particle free. That takes an enormous amount of energy, and while it is being spent on separation, none is left over to speed anything up.

Think of it as paying off a debt before you can save. Every joule goes to the debt collector — the forces holding the particles together — and only once the debt is settled does the balance climb again.

Exactly the same happens at the bottom of the scale. Ice from the deep freeze at minus twenty warms steadily to zero, then sticks at zero as a slushy mixture of ice and water until the last piece of ice is gone. Only then does the water start warming towards one hundred.

Draw the whole journey as a graph of temperature against time and you get a staircase with two flat steps: a short one at zero while it melts, a long one at one hundred while it boils. The boiling step is much longer because setting particles completely free is a far bigger job than loosening them from their rows.

Run it backwards — steam in a cold room — and you get the same shape upside down, the flat parts now being energy given back out as the particles grab hold of each other again. That is why steam burns are so nasty: the steam dumps all that separation energy into your skin as it condenses.

Your questions for this part are up now: the flat parts explained, the energy accounted for, and the reason the boiling step is the long one.

## Subtopic: Six Words for Changing, and the Traps That Cost Marks

Last piece: the six words, and the three mistakes that cost more marks than anything else in this topic.

The six changes, and you know most of them from the kitchen. Ice to water is melting. Water to ice is freezing. Water to steam is boiling, or evaporating if it happens quietly from a surface. Steam to water on a cold window is condensation. Then the two that skip the middle: solid straight to gas is sublimation — dry ice smoking away without ever making a puddle — and gas straight to solid is deposition, which is exactly how frost appears on the grass on a cold Highveld morning. No rain fell; the water vapour turned solid on the spot.

Sort them by whether they need energy or give it back, and one question does it: am I loosening the grip, or tightening it? Loosening — melting, evaporating, subliming — needs energy in. Tightening — freezing, condensing, deposition — gives energy out. That is why sweat cools you: evaporating takes its energy off your skin.

Now the traps. Trap one, the biggest: writing "no energy is going in" during a flat part of the graph. Energy is pouring in the whole time; it is just being spent pulling particles apart instead of speeding them up. Say "the energy breaks the forces between the particles" and the mark is yours.

Trap two: treating heat and temperature as the same word. Heat is energy moving; temperature is how hard the particles jiggle on average. A pot of boiling water and a swimming pool at boiling point are at the same temperature, but the pool holds vastly more heat.

Trap three: evaporation and boiling used as if identical. Boiling only happens at the boiling point, right through the liquid, with bubbles. Evaporation happens at any temperature, only from the surface — which is how the washing on the line dries perfectly well at twenty degrees.

And one local detail worth remembering: water boils at around ninety-five degrees on the Highveld instead of one hundred at the coast, because there is less air pressing down up there.

Here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: the six words matched to their directions, sorted into energy-in and energy-out, and the three traps named before they can catch you.
