Hey, it's Thandi — if this topic feels like a pile of definitions you're supposed to swallow whole, I promise it is actually one idea wearing seven hats. Let's find the idea.

Here it is: everything is made of tiny particles, they are always jiggling, and they pull on each other. That's it. Whether you're looking at ice, water or steam depends entirely on who is winning — the jiggling or the pulling. Pulling wins, the particles stay in rows: solid. Roughly even, they slide but stay together: liquid. Jiggling wins, they scatter: gas. Every single description in this topic is that one sentence, retold.

The graph question is where the marks hide, and it has one answer. When the line goes flat, energy is still pouring in — it is just being spent breaking the forces between particles instead of speeding them up. Write "the energy overcomes the forces of attraction between the particles, increasing their potential energy" and you have the mark. Never, ever write "no energy is going in".

The habit that protects you: whenever a question says "explain in terms of the kinetic molecular theory", answer with three things every time — arrangement, motion, forces. Say those three words under your breath before you write. Markers are literally looking for them, and learners who describe only the shape of the thing lose half the marks for a correct-sounding answer.

And the six change-of-state words sort themselves with one question: am I loosening the grip or tightening it? Loosening needs energy in; tightening gives energy out. That's melting, evaporating and subliming in one basket, freezing, condensing and deposition in the other.

One idea, three words, one question. You already watch this happen every time you boil a kettle. You've got this!
