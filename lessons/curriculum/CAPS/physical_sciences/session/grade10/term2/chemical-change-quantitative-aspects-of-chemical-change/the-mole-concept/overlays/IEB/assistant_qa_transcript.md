**Thandi:** Before you head off — the questions everyone asks about the mole. First: is a mole a mass, a volume, or a number? People treat it as all three and get tangled.

**Tutor:** It is a number, full stop — and the tangle comes from the fact that this one number has a known mass and, for gases at STP, a known volume. Keep the three roles in order. The mole itself is a count: six comma zero two times ten to the twenty-three particles, no more a mass than a dozen is a mass. But because every substance's particles have a definite mass, a counted mole of any particular substance also has a definite mass — forty-four grams if the particles are propane molecules, twelve grams if they are carbon atoms. That is the molar mass, and it changes from substance to substance while the count never does. And because gas molecules at the same temperature and pressure space themselves out identically, a counted mole of any gas at STP also has a definite volume — twenty-two comma four cubic decimetres, the same for every gas. So the honest sentence is: a mole is a number, which drags a substance-specific mass and, for gases at STP only, a universal volume along behind it. When a question says "how many moles", it is asking for the count; you reach that count from mass by dividing by the molar mass, or from STP volume by dividing by twenty-two comma four. Say "the mole is the count in the middle" before every problem and the three roles never blur again.

***

**Thandi:** Next: why does one mole of heavy propane fill exactly the same space as one mole of feather-light hydrogen? That still feels impossible.

**Tutor:** It feels impossible because you are imagining a gas as packed, like marbles in a jar — and a gas is nothing like that. In a gas the molecules are tiny specks tearing around a volume that is overwhelmingly empty; the spacing between molecules is enormous compared with the size of any molecule. So the volume a gas fills is set by how many specks there are and how the temperature and pressure spread them out — not by how fat each speck is. Double the temperature story or squeeze the pressure and the volume changes; swap fat specks for thin specks and it barely does, because the specks themselves were never what filled the room. That is Avogadro's law from the inside: equal volumes of any gases, at the same temperature and pressure, hold equal numbers of molecules. The hall picture from the lesson is exact — the chairs are bolted to the same grid whoever sits in them, so the hall seats the same crowd whether the crowd is rugby players or toddlers. One warning attaches: the moment particles touch — in a liquid or a solid — particle size is everything, spacing is nothing, and the law dies instantly. That is why twenty-two comma four belongs only to gases, and only at the stated conditions.

***

**Thandi:** Then the classic disaster: on the way from moles to molecules, half the class multiplies by Avogadro's number and the other half divides. Is there a way to never get that wrong?

**Tutor:** There is, and it needs no memory at all — only a size check before you press a key. Particles are the tiny, ridiculous, numerous thing; moles are the tame human-sized count. So travelling from moles to particles the answer must explode into something astronomical, and travelling from particles to moles it must collapse into something small. Multiply when you need the explosion; divide when you need the collapse. Run it on our leak: 0,2 moles of propane to molecules — I expect an astronomical answer, so multiply: 0,2 times six comma zero two times ten to the twenty-three gives 1,204 times ten to the twenty-three. Had I divided by mistake, I would be staring at ten to the minus twenty-four of a molecule — a number smaller than one molecule, claiming to count molecules in nine grams of gas. The absurdity is impossible to miss if you look. The same guard works on every road of the mole map: a mass answer for 0,2 moles of propane must come out below forty-four grams, because you hold less than a full bag; an STP volume must come out below twenty-two comma four litres. Predict the size, then calculate, then compare. Ten seconds, and the multiply-or-divide disaster is extinct.

***

**Thandi:** Last one: the numbers 6,02 times ten to the twenty-three and 22,4 — do they need to be memorised, and what exactly must be written when using them?

**Tutor:** Know them, yes — but more importantly, know their job descriptions, because each number is only legal in one situation. Avogadro's number is the bag size: it converts between moles and particle count, and nothing else. It never converts mass, and it never converts volume; if the question is about grams or litres and you find yourself reaching for six comma zero two, stop and re-read. It carries no conditions — a mole is that many particles in a freezer or a furnace. Twenty-two comma four is the opposite: it is nothing but conditions. It converts between moles and volume only for a gas, and only at STP — zero degrees Celsius, two hundred and seventy-three kelvin, one hundred and one comma three kilopascals. Write the conditions down as part of the working: "at STP, V equals n times twenty-two comma four." If the question names a warm laboratory or gives no conditions, the number is off duty, and Grade 11's gas equations take over. And in every calculation, carry units through the working — grams over grams per mole leaves moles; moles times cubic decimetres per mole leaves cubic decimetres. If the units that survive are not the units the question asked for, the itinerary was wrong, however confident the digits look. Units are the map reading itself back to you — let them.
