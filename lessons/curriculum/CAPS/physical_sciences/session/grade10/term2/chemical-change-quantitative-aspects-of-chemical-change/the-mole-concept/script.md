# Part 1 — Expert

This session builds the mole — chemistry's counting unit — and the three conversions that run through it. One exam problem drives the whole hour: calculate the number of moles in 8,8 grams of carbon dioxide, then the number of molecules that represents, and finally the volume that gas occupies at STP. One sample of gas, three different descriptions — mass, count, and volume — and the mole is the bridge between all three.

## Subtopic: Why Chemists Count in Moles

Atoms are absurdly small. A single carbon dioxide molecule has a mass of about seven times ten to the minus twenty-three grams — no balance on Earth weighs one molecule. Yet chemical reactions happen molecule by molecule: one propane reacts with exactly five oxygens, not five grams of oxygen. Chemistry needs a way to COUNT particles while WEIGHING grams.

The solution is the same trick shops use: count in bulk units. Eggs come in dozens; paper comes in reams. Chemists count in moles — and one mole is simply a fixed, enormous number of particles: six comma zero two times ten to the twenty-three. That number is Avogadro's number, N A. A mole of molecules is six comma zero two times ten to the twenty-three molecules, the way a dozen eggs is twelve eggs.

Why that bizarre number and not a clean trillion? Because it is chosen to make the arithmetic beautiful: one mole of a substance has a mass in GRAMS equal to the substance's relative molecular mass. Carbon's relative atomic mass is twelve — so one mole of carbon atoms weighs exactly twelve grams. The mole is the hinge connecting the invisible world of particle masses to the visible world of laboratory balances.

The gram-mass of one mole is called the molar mass, symbol M, units grams per mole. And the central formula of today: n equals m over M — the number of moles equals the mass you have, divided by the mass of one mole. Learn to read it as a sentence: "how many moles? — weigh what you have and divide by the weight of one mole's worth." The same logic as: how many dozens of eggs? Weigh your eggs, divide by the weight of a dozen.

The questions for this section are with you now. Attend to three things as you answer: what a mole actually counts, why its size was fixed at that particular number rather than a round one, and whether you can read n equals m over M as a sentence — moles equal the mass you have divided by the mass of one mole — before you read it as symbols.

## Subtopic: Moles from Mass — the 8,8 Gram Question

Step one of the circuit: how many moles in 8,8 grams of CO2?

First, the molar mass — and this is pure periodic-table work. Carbon: twelve grams per mole. Oxygen: sixteen — and CO2 carries TWO oxygens. So M equals twelve plus two times sixteen: twelve plus thirty-two — forty-four grams per mole. One mole of carbon dioxide weighs forty-four grams.

Pause on the discipline: the formula's subscripts command the arithmetic. Two oxygens means sixteen counted twice. The commonest molar-mass error is counting a subscripted atom once — CO2 computed as twenty-eight instead of forty-four wrecks every later step. Read the formula atom by atom, multiply by subscripts, then add.

Now apply the central formula: n equals m over M — 8,8 grams over 44 grams per mole. Compute: 8,8 divided by 44 is 0,2. We have 0,2 moles of carbon dioxide.

Sense-check immediately: 8,8 grams is a small fraction of 44 grams — one fifth, exactly — so a fifth of a mole is precisely what it should be. If your calculator had shown 5 moles, the upside-down division would be caught right here: five moles of CO2 would weigh two hundred and twenty grams, not eight comma eight.

Units matter throughout: mass in grams, molar mass in grams per mole — the grams cancel, leaving pure moles. Whenever an exam gives kilograms, convert to grams before dividing; the formula speaks grams.

Work through this section's questions now. They examine the molar mass assembled subscript by subscript — forty-four grams per mole, never twenty-eight — the division of 8,8 grams by 44 grams per mole, and the sense-check that catches an inverted quotient before it reaches the memo.

## Subtopic: From Moles to Molecules, and to Litres at STP

Step two: how many molecules is 0,2 moles?

The mole is a count in bulk: one mole holds Avogadro's number of particles. So the number of molecules, N, equals n times N A: 0,2 times six comma zero two times ten to the twenty-three.

Compute the front: 0,2 times 6,02 is 1,204. The power of ten rides along: 1,204 times ten to the twenty-three molecules. Roughly one hundred and twenty thousand billion billion molecules — in under nine grams of gas. Let that number teach you why nobody counts atoms directly.

Step three: the volume at STP. Here enters one of chemistry's most elegant facts — Avogadro's law: at the same temperature and pressure, equal volumes of ANY gases contain equal numbers of molecules. The gas's identity is irrelevant to the space it fills — a mole of CO2, a mole of hydrogen, a mole of oxygen: same conditions, same volume.

At STP — standard temperature and pressure, zero degrees Celsius (273 kelvin) and one hundred and one comma three kilopascals — that shared volume is twenty-two comma four cubic decimetres per mole. The molar volume, V m. Remember that a cubic decimetre is the same thing as a litre.

So: V equals n times V m — volume equals the number of moles times the molar volume — 0,2 moles times 22,4 cubic decimetres per mole, which is 4,48 cubic decimetres. Our 8,8 grams of carbon dioxide fills 4,48 litres at STP — about two large cooldrink bottles.

The condition matters: twenty-two comma four ONLY at STP. On a warm day, at different pressure, the volume differs — Grade 11 gives you the full gas equations for that. If the question does not say STP or standard conditions, the 22,4 shortcut is not licensed.

This section's questions are in front of you. Expect the particle count, the molar volume of twenty-two comma four cubic decimetres per mole, and the conditions that license it to be examined together — and treat the STP condition as part of the answer rather than as decoration on it.

## Subtopic: The Mole Map — Navigating Every Conversion

Step back and see what we built: one quantity of gas, described three ways. Mass: 8,8 grams. Count: 1,204 times ten to the twenty-three molecules. Volume at STP: 4,48 cubic decimetres. And at the centre of all three descriptions sits the same 0,2 moles.

That picture generalises into the tool that will carry you through matric: the mole map. Draw moles, n, in the centre. Three roads lead outward. Road one, to mass: multiply by M; travel inward by dividing — n equals m over M. Road two, to particle count: multiply by Avogadro's number; inward, divide by it. Road three, to gas volume at STP: multiply by 22,4; inward, divide by 22,4.

Every mole problem — every single one — is a journey on this map: start where the given quantity sits, travel INTO the centre to find moles, then travel OUT along whichever road the question demands. Our problem entered from mass — divide by 44 grams per mole — and exited twice: once along the particle road, once along the volume road. A question giving volume and asking mass? In by dividing by 22,4, out by multiplying by 44. The map never changes; only the itinerary does.

The error museum, briefly. Exhibit one: dividing by Avogadro's number when travelling outward from moles to particles — the map fixes direction: out means multiply. Exhibit two: molar mass with ignored subscripts — forty-four, not twenty-eight, for CO2. Exhibit three: using 22,4 for a gas NOT at STP, or worse, for a liquid or solid — the molar volume belongs to gases at standard conditions only. Exhibit four: unit chaos — kilograms fed into a grams formula, or cubic centimetres confused with cubic decimetres; one thousand cubic centimetres make one cubic decimetre.

Where does the map lead? Next term, the coefficients of your balanced equations become mole ratios, and the map powers stoichiometry: grams of reactant to moles, ratio across the equation, moles back to grams of product. Grade 11 makes that the core of quantitative chemistry; matric runs acids-and-bases and electrolysis on it. The Chemical Change section — the largest block of marks in the matric paper — is, at its heart, journeys on the map you drew today. Practise the three roads until the directions are reflex: in, divide; out, multiply.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the three roads of the map, the direction rule — inward divide, outward multiply — and the four exhibits of the error museum. Answer them with the map in mind rather than with the formulae recalled one by one in isolation.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same mole from the wholesaler's counter — bags, invoices, and a fifth of a bag of sugar.

## Subtopic: Nobody Counts Rice Grains — They Count Bags

Picture the wholesaler where the spaza shops buy. Nobody at that counter counts grains of rice — grains are too small and far too many, so rice moves in bags, every bag the same, and the shop counts bags. Want more rice? Take more bags. Want to know how much rice is in your bakkie? Put it on the scale.

Chemistry has the same problem, several sizes worse: no scale on Earth will ever weigh one molecule, yet reactions happen molecule by molecule, the way a recipe asks for two eggs and not for two hundred grams of egg. So chemists did what the wholesaler did — they fixed a pack size, and counted packs.

The pack is called the mole, and its size is six comma zero two times ten to the twenty-three particles — Avogadro's number. A mole of molecules is that many molecules, the way a dozen eggs is twelve eggs and a tray is thirty. Nothing mystical is happening: a mole is a number, and only a number.

Why such an awkward number for a pack? Because that size makes the scale honest. With that pack size, one mole of a substance weighs, in grams, exactly the number the periodic table prints for it. Carbon's number is twelve, so a mole of carbon weighs twelve grams. Oxygen's is sixteen, so a mole of oxygen atoms weighs sixteen grams. The periodic table is the price list, written in grams per pack. That per-pack mass has a name — the molar mass, symbol M, in grams per mole — and once you know it, weighing tells you how many packs you hold: the number of moles equals the mass divided by the molar mass, n equals m over M.

Your questions for this section are ready now, so take them slowly and enjoy them. Three ideas are being checked: what a mole counts, what the periodic table is quietly telling you in grams, and why weighing is allowed to stand in for counting. Get those and the hardest idea in the topic is already behind you.

## Subtopic: A Fifth of a Bag of Sugar

Now the exam's first question, in shop language: you are holding 8,8 grams of carbon dioxide. How many packs is that?

Start with what one pack weighs. Read the formula CO2 like an invoice: one carbon, and — mind the little two — two oxygens. Carbon costs twelve grams a mole, each oxygen sixteen. Twelve plus sixteen plus sixteen is forty-four, so one mole of carbon dioxide weighs forty-four grams. The commonest slip in this whole topic is paying for one oxygen when the invoice lists two, landing on twenty-eight and wrecking everything downstream. Say the atoms out loud first — "C, O, O" — then add.

Now divide, because grams to packs is a dividing trip: 8,8 grams divided by 44 grams per mole. Before touching a calculator, notice that 8,8 is one fifth of 44 — so the answer has to be a fifth of a pack. And it is: 0,2 moles. The grams cancel top and bottom and leave a clean count of moles, which is exactly what you wanted. Had the calculator said 5 moles, the shop check kills it on the spot: five bags at forty-four grams each would weigh two hundred and twenty grams, not eight comma eight.

And how many molecules sit in a fifth of a pack? A full pack holds six comma zero two times ten to the twenty-three, so a fifth of it holds 0,2 times that — 1,204 times ten to the twenty-three molecules. Packs out to particles you always multiply, because particles are the tiny numerous thing, so the answer must come out enormous. Roughly one hundred and twenty thousand billion billion molecules, in under nine grams of gas. That is precisely why nobody counts.

Take the win: you have just run a complete mole calculation in ordinary words. The questions for this section are with you now — the forty-four, the fifth of a bag, and the molecule count. If anything wobbles it will be the subscript, so check the invoice before you commit.

## Subtopic: Same Room for Any Gas — and the Rank in the Middle

Last piece: the space a gas takes up, and then the one picture that runs every question in this topic.

Here is a fact that sounds wrong the first time you hear it. Take one mole of carbon dioxide, one mole of hydrogen and one mole of oxygen — different substances, very different masses — hold them at the same temperature and pressure, and they fill the same volume. A gas is mostly empty space; its molecules sit so far apart that the size of each one hardly matters, the way a hall with its chairs set out on a fixed grid seats the same number of people whichever people walk in. That is Avogadro's law: same conditions, same volume, same number of molecules, whatever the gas.

At STP — standard temperature and pressure, zero degrees Celsius, which is 273 kelvin, and 101,3 kilopascals — that shared space is twenty-two comma four cubic decimetres for every mole. A cubic decimetre is just a litre by another name. So our fifth of a pack takes 0,2 moles times 22,4 cubic decimetres per mole, which is 4,48 cubic decimetres — 4,48 litres, about two big cooldrink bottles, from under nine grams of gas. In words: volume equals the number of moles times the molar volume, V equals n times V m. And that twenty-two comma four is on loan only at STP — no STP in the question, no twenty-two comma four in your answer, and never for a liquid or a solid, whose particles are touching.

Now the picture that ties it all together, and it is honestly just a taxi rank. Moles stand in the middle — that is the rank. Three routes run out of it: one to mass in grams, one to the number of particles, one to the volume of a gas at STP. Riding out of the rank you multiply — by the molar mass, by Avogadro's number, by twenty-two comma four. Coming in to the rank you divide by those same three. In, divide; out, multiply. Every question in this topic is one trip: come in from whatever you were given, stand at the rank counting moles, then ride out on the route the question asks for. Ours came in from grams — divide by forty-four — and rode out twice, once to molecules and once to litres.

The last questions of the lesson are up now, and you are ready for them. Hold the rank in your head while you answer: which route am I on, and am I riding in or out? Answer that way and you are no longer remembering formulae one at a time — you are navigating, and that is the skill that carries you into stoichiometry next term.
