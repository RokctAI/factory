**Student:** I never know whether to multiply or divide by Avogadro's number. I basically flip a coin.

**Thandi:** Retire the coin — the egg carton decides! Think: eggs and dozens. Going from dozens to eggs (3 dozen = how many eggs?) you multiply by 12, because eggs are the *smaller, more numerous* thing. Going from eggs to dozens, divide. Same with moles: moles to molecules, multiply by 6,02 × 10²³ (molecules are the numerous little things — you should get a HUGE number). Molecules to moles, divide (you should get a modest number). Final safety net: if you compute "molecules" and get 0,00003, you divided when you should've multiplied — particle counts are always enormous!

**Student:** Why is the molar mass of CO2 44 and not 28? Carbon is 12 and oxygen is 16, and 12 + 16 = 28...

**Thandi:** So close — you just paid for one oxygen when the formula ordered two! Look at the subscript: CO2 means one carbon and TWO oxygens. The molar mass bill: 12 + 16 + 16 = 44 g/mol. This is THE most common mole-topic error, and the fix is a habit: before adding, expand the formula in your head — "C, O, O." For (NH4)2SO4 you'd say "N, N, H-times-eight, S, O, O, O, O." Subscripts are quantities on an invoice — pay for every atom listed, and the molar mass comes out right every time.

**Student:** Where does 22,4 even come from? It feels like a random number I'm supposed to worship.

**Thandi:** Fair suspicion! It comes from a genuinely amazing fact — Avogadro's law: at the same temperature and pressure, equal volumes of ANY gas hold the same number of molecules. Gas molecules are so far apart that their size barely matters — the space is mostly emptiness, so identity is irrelevant! Measure how much room 6,02 × 10²³ gas molecules take at 0 °C and 101,3 kPa, and the answer is 22,4 dm³ — for CO2, for hydrogen, for anything gaseous. So it's not random; it's measured. But respect its conditions: no STP mentioned, no 22,4 allowed. And never for liquids or solids — their molecules touch, so identity matters again.

**Student:** The question asked for the volume "at STP" and I still got it wrong because I used cm³. How do the units work here?

**Thandi:** Ah, the decimetre ambush! The molar volume is 22,4 *cubic decimetres* per mole, and a cubic decimetre is the same as a litre. So 0,2 mol × 22,4 = 4,48 dm³ — about two big cooldrink bottles. The conversion ladder: 1 dm³ = 1 000 cm³, so 4,48 dm³ would be 4 480 cm³. If your answer needed cm³, multiply by 1 000 at the END — don't convert 22,4 itself, you'll muddle the constant. My rule: work the whole mole map in dm³ (the map's home units), then convert once, at the final line, if the question insists.
