**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! First one, and it comes up every single year: how do we know whether to multiply or divide by Avogadro's number? People are honestly guessing.

**Tutor:** Stop guessing and let the scale of the two quantities decide, because they live in different worlds. Moles are a modest, human-sized number — nought comma two, three, half a mole. Particles are astronomical: ten to the twenty-third and beyond. Travelling from moles to particles you are moving towards the numerous thing, so you multiply by six comma zero two times ten to the twenty-three, and the answer must come out enormous. Travelling from particles back to moles you are bundling them into packs, so you divide, and the answer must come out modest. Our own numbers do it: nought comma two moles times six comma zero two times ten to the twenty-three per mole gives one comma two zero four times ten to the twenty-three molecules. Then keep the safety net. If you set out to find a number of molecules and the calculator offers nought comma zero zero zero zero three, you have divided where you should have multiplied. If you set out to find moles and get something times ten to the twenty-third, the same error has run the other way. Five words hold it: out of moles, multiply; into moles, divide.

**Thandi:** Next: a good number of us wrote the molar mass of carbon dioxide as twenty-eight. Carbon is twelve, oxygen is sixteen, twelve plus sixteen is twenty-eight. Where exactly is the mistake?

**Tutor:** The mistake is paying for one oxygen when the formula ordered two. CO2 is not carbon plus oxygen; it is one carbon atom with two oxygen atoms, and the subscript two is an instruction to count that oxygen twice. So the molar mass is twelve plus sixteen plus sixteen — forty-four grams per mole. The habit that prevents the slip is to expand the formula into single atoms before adding: say "C, O, O", then add. Take a harder one, ammonium sulfate, formula N H four in brackets, subscript two, then S O four. Expanded, that is two nitrogens, eight hydrogens, one sulfur, four oxygens — because the bracket's subscript multiplies everything inside it. Two nitrogens at fourteen is twenty-eight grams; eight hydrogens at one is eight; sulfur is thirty-two; four oxygens at sixteen is sixty-four. Twenty-eight plus eight plus thirty-two plus sixty-four is one hundred and thirty-two grams per mole. And count the cost of the original error: with twenty-eight instead of forty-four, 8,8 grams comes out as nought comma three one four moles instead of nought comma two, and the molecule count and the volume both inherit the wrong start. One misread subscript takes the whole question with it.

**Thandi:** Then this one, which several people asked in the same words: where does twenty-two comma four actually come from? It feels like a number we are told to worship without being told why.

**Tutor:** It is measured, not decreed, and the reason it works for every gas is genuinely surprising. Avogadro's law says that at the same temperature and pressure, equal volumes of any gases contain equal numbers of molecules. The reason lies in how empty a gas is: the particles are separated by distances vastly larger than the particles themselves, so what fixes the volume is how far apart they sit — which temperature and pressure decide — and not how fat or how heavy each one happens to be. Put six comma zero two times ten to the twenty-three gas particles at zero degrees Celsius, which is 273 kelvin, and at 101,3 kilopascals, measure the space they occupy, and you get twenty-two comma four cubic decimetres. Carbon dioxide, hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen — the same. Two conditions guard the number. First, the gas must be at STP; change the temperature or the pressure and the spacing changes with it, which is exactly what the gas laws in Grade 11 handle. Second, it belongs to gases alone. In a liquid or a solid the particles are in contact, so their real sizes matter again: one mole of liquid water occupies roughly eighteen cubic centimetres, nowhere near twenty-two comma four cubic decimetres.

**Thandi:** Last one, and it stings: someone did the whole calculation correctly and still lost the mark, because the answer was wanted in cubic centimetres. How do the units work here?

**Tutor:** The molar volume is twenty-two comma four cubic decimetres per mole, and a cubic decimetre is simply another name for a litre — so the natural answer to our problem is nought comma two moles times twenty-two comma four cubic decimetres per mole, which is four comma four eight cubic decimetres. If the question demands cubic centimetres, convert at the end and only at the end: one cubic decimetre is one thousand cubic centimetres, so four comma four eight cubic decimetres is four thousand four hundred and eighty cubic centimetres. Never convert the constant itself — a rewritten molar volume is how learners end up multiplying by twenty-two thousand four hundred in one line and by twenty-two comma four in the next. Work the whole problem in the map's home units, grams and cubic decimetres, then convert once on the final line. The same discipline covers mass: the formula moles equals mass over molar mass speaks grams, so kilograms must become grams before you divide — 0,0088 kilograms is 8,8 grams. Units earn method marks in their own right, so carry them through every line rather than attaching them at the end.
