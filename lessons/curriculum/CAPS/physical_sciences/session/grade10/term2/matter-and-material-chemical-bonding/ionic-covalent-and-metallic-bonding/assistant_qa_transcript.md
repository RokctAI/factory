**Thandi:** Before you move on — the questions that come up a lot with this topic. First: how do I decide, fast, whether a compound is covalent or ionic? Some people guess from the name and hope.

**Tutor:** Never guess — read the periodic table, because the decision is geographic. Find the elements in the compound and ask one question: which side of the staircase does each come from? Two non-metals — both from the upper right — means neither atom is willing to surrender electrons, so they must share: covalent, forming molecules. Water, carbon dioxide, ammonia, all non-metal pairs, all sharers. A metal with a non-metal — one from the left, one from the upper right — means a willing giver has met a willing taker, and transfer beats sharing: ionic, forming a lattice of ions. Sodium chloride, calcium chloride, magnesium bromide. Metal alone — a wire, a coin, a filing — is metallic bonding, the electron sea. The names carry confirming fingerprints too: covalent names use counting prefixes, mono, di, tri — carbon DIoxide — because a molecule has exact atom counts worth announcing; ionic names never count — calcium chloride, not calcium dichloride — because the formula is just a ratio, with subscripts already decided by charge balance. So the drill is: locate the elements, classify by staircase, and let the name confirm you. Ten seconds, no guessing, and every property question that follows — brittle or bendy, conductor or insulator — flows from that classification.

**Thandi:** Next: if opposite charges attract, why is sodium chloride written NaCl and calcium chloride CaCl two? Where do the subscripts actually come from?

**Tutor:** From one accounting rule: a compound is electrically neutral, so the plus charges and minus charges must cancel exactly to zero. The charges themselves come off the periodic table before any formula is written. Sodium, group 1, loses its single valence electron: plus one. Calcium, group 2, loses two: plus two. Chlorine, group 17, gains one: minus one. Oxygen's family, group 16: minus two. Now balance the books. Sodium chloride: one plus meets one minus — cancelled — so the ratio is one to one, N a C l, no subscripts needed. Calcium chloride: calcium brings plus two, but each chloride only brings minus one; one chloride leaves a plus one uncancelled, which nature does not permit, so a second chloride joins — C a C l two. Magnesium bromide follows identically: M g B r two. The deeper point behind the arithmetic: the subscript records how many electrons were transferred — calcium's TWO lost electrons had to land somewhere, and each chlorine could only accept one. So the formula is really a transfer receipt. Check every ionic formula you ever write with the zero test: total plus, total minus, sum to zero. If it does not, a subscript is missing — and the periodic table will tell you which.

**Thandi:** Then the property puzzle: salt and copper are both giant lattices of positive ions, but salt shatters and copper bends. What is the real difference?

**Tutor:** The difference is what sits BETWEEN the positive ions, and what happens to it when the layers move. In salt, between every pair of sodium ions sits a chloride ion: the structure alternates plus, minus, plus, minus in strict order, and the attractions depend on that order staying intact. Strike the crystal and one layer shifts by a single position — suddenly plus faces plus and minus faces minus. Attraction flips to repulsion along the whole plane in an instant, and the crystal does not bend; it cleaves, splitting along that plane. Rigid order, catastrophic failure: brittle. In copper, between the positive ions there is no fixed pattern of partners at all — only the delocalised electron sea, owned by the whole metal and free to flow anywhere. Push a layer of copper ions along and the sea instantly redistributes itself around the new arrangement; every ion is still bathed in negative charge, the attraction survives the movement, and the metal simply takes its new shape. That single difference — fixed alternating partners versus a fluid communal glue — also settles conduction. Salt's charges are all locked in the lattice, so solid salt cannot conduct; only melting or dissolving frees the ions to carry charge. Copper's electrons are mobile from the start, so it conducts at any temperature. One question — what carries on between the ions — and both personalities are explained.

**Thandi:** Last one: formula-mass calculations keep going wrong on the same compounds — calcium chloride especially. What is the habit that stops the bleeding?

**Tutor:** The habit is to refuse to touch numbers until the formula has been read aloud as an inventory. The classic wrong answer for calcium chloride is 75,5 — that is 40 plus one 35,5 — and it comes from the eye sliding over the subscript two. Nobody who first said "one calcium, TWO chlorines" out loud has ever made that slip, because the inventory step forces the subscript into consciousness before arithmetic begins. So: three beats, in order, every time. Beat one — inventory: read the formula as words. C a C l two: one calcium, two chlorines. M g B r two: one magnesium, two bromines. Beat two — multiply: each count times its atomic mass from the table. Two chlorines at 35,5 is 71; two bromines at 80 is 160. Beat three — add: 40 plus 71 is 111; 24 plus 160 is 184. Keep the decimal comma masses honest — chlorine is 35,5, and it stays 35,5 whether the compound is ionic or covalent. One naming footnote for full marks: call the answer relative MOLECULAR mass for covalent molecules like water's 18 and carbon dioxide's 44, and relative FORMULA mass for ionic compounds like salt's 58,5 — the arithmetic is identical, but the examiner listens for the right word. And practise the beats now, because next topic the mole makes these exact numbers the grams you weigh in every stoichiometry problem to matric.
