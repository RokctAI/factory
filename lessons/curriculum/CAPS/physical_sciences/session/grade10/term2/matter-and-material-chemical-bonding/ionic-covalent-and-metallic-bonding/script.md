# Part 1 — Expert

Why is salt a brittle crystal that dissolves in water, wax a soft solid that melts on a warm day, and copper a shiny metal that bends and conducts? Because their atoms are held together in three different ways. This session covers the three types of chemical bond — covalent sharing, ionic transfer, and the metallic sea — plus the Lewis diagrams that draw them, the naming and formula rules for each, and the formula-mass calculations that close every bonding question.

## Subtopic: Why Atoms Bond — Valence Electrons and Lewis Diagrams

A CHEMICAL BOND is a force that holds atoms together, and every bond in this course is formed for the same reason: atoms interact so as to reach a FULL OUTER ENERGY LEVEL — the stable electron arrangement of a noble gas. For most atoms that means eight electrons in the outer level, the octet; for the smallest atoms, hydrogen chief among them, it means two, matching helium. Full outer levels are low-energy, stable arrangements, and chemistry is the business of getting there.

Only the VALENCE electrons — the outer-level electrons from the previous topic — take part. So chemistry needs a shorthand that shows just those, and that is the LEWIS DIAGRAM, also called an electron-dot diagram: the element's symbol standing for the nucleus plus all inner electrons, with the valence electrons drawn as dots around it, placed singly on the four sides before pairing up.

Describe a few aloud. Hydrogen: the symbol H with one dot. Carbon, group 14: C with four single dots, one per side. Nitrogen, group 15: N with five dots — one pair and three singles. Oxygen, group 16: O with six dots — two pairs and two singles. Chlorine, group 17: C l with seven dots — three pairs and one single. Sodium, group 1: N a with a single dot. The pattern to bank: the number of dots equals the group's valence count, and the UNPAIRED dots are the atom's bonding hooks — carbon has four hooks, oxygen two, chlorine one.

Now the three strategies for reaching a full level, previewed. Two non-metals, neither willing to surrender electrons: they SHARE — covalent bonding. A metal and a non-metal, one eager to lose and one eager to gain: they TRANSFER — ionic bonding. Metal atoms alone, all wanting to lose: they pool their electrons — metallic bonding. Take this section's questions now: the reason for bonding, and dots counted for any main-group element.

## Subtopic: Covalent Bonding — Sharing Pairs

COVALENT BONDING is the sharing of one or more PAIRS of electrons between two non-metal atoms, so that each atom counts the shared pair toward its own outer level. The bonded unit is a MOLECULE, and each shared pair is one covalent bond.

Build the syllabus molecules in words. Hydrogen, H two: each H brings one electron; the two form one shared pair — a single bond — and each hydrogen now counts two electrons, helium's arrangement. Chlorine, C l two, and fluorine, F two: each atom offers its one unpaired dot, forming a single bond, with three lone pairs left on each atom. Hydrogen chloride, H C l, and hydrogen fluoride, H F: one shared pair between the two atoms. Water, H two O: oxygen has two hooks, so it bonds one hydrogen on each, leaving two lone pairs on the oxygen — two single bonds. Ammonia, N H three: nitrogen's three hooks each hold a hydrogen, one lone pair remaining. Methane, C H four: carbon's four hooks, four hydrogens, no lone pairs — a perfect cross of four single bonds.

Some atoms must share MORE than one pair. Oxygen, O two: each oxygen needs two more electrons, so the atoms share TWO pairs — a DOUBLE bond, with two lone pairs remaining on each atom. Nitrogen, N two: each needs three, so they share THREE pairs — a TRIPLE bond, one lone pair on each atom. That triple bond is among the strongest in chemistry, which is why nitrogen gas drifts through every breath you take without reacting.

Naming covalent compounds uses counting prefixes on the second element and, where needed, the first: carbon dioxide — one carbon, two oxygens; nitrogen dioxide; carbon monoxide; dinitrogen tetroxide would signal two nitrogens and four oxygens. The formula records the actual atom counts in one molecule.

This section's questions are with you now: pairs shared, lone pairs left, and single, double and triple bonds assigned correctly.

## Subtopic: Ionic Bonding — Transfer and the Lattice

IONIC BONDING begins with a TRANSFER: a metal atom gives one or more valence electrons to a non-metal atom. The metal becomes a positive CATION, the non-metal a negative ANION, and the bond is the electrostatic ATTRACTION between the oppositely charged ions.

Sodium chloride in words. Sodium — one valence electron, expensive to complete a level, cheap to empty it — releases its single outer electron: N a plus, with neon's stable arrangement underneath. Chlorine — seven valence electrons — catches it: C l minus, with argon's arrangement. Plus attracts minus, and the ions bind. The Lewis picture shows the sodium symbol with no dots and a plus charge, beside the chloride symbol with eight dots in brackets and a minus charge.

Predict every ion charge straight off the periodic table: group 1 forms plus one; group 2, plus two; group 16, minus two; group 17, minus one. Then build formulae by BALANCING CHARGE to zero. Potassium chloride: plus one meets minus one — K C l, one to one; potassium bromide likewise, K B r. Calcium chloride: calcium's plus two needs TWO chloride minus ones — C a C l two. Magnesium bromide: M g B r two, by the same arithmetic. Speak the Lewis diagram of calcium chloride: a calcium symbol, no dots, two plus; and two separate bracketed bromide-style chloride ions, eight dots each, minus one apiece.

Now the structural point that separates ionic from covalent. Ionic compounds do NOT form molecules. The ions stack into a giant three-dimensional CRYSTAL LATTICE — every plus surrounded by minuses, every minus by pluses, attraction in all directions. So the formula N a C l is a FORMULA UNIT: the simplest ratio of ions in the crystal, one to one, not a two-atom particle. The lattice explains the properties: strong attractions throughout mean high melting points; rigid ion positions mean brittle crystals; and the ions conduct electricity only when freed — molten, or dissolved in water. Naming is simple: metal first unchanged, non-metal second with the ending i d e — sodium chloride, magnesium bromide.

Take the questions for this section now: transfer, charges, lattice, and formulae balanced to zero.

## Subtopic: Metallic Bonding, and Formula Masses

The third bond belongs to metals alone. In a piece of copper every atom is a metal atom; nobody can hand electrons to anybody, and nobody will take them. Instead, every atom RELEASES its valence electrons into a shared pool. METALLIC BONDING is the attraction between the resulting lattice of POSITIVE METAL IONS — sometimes called kernels — and the SEA OF DELOCALISED ELECTRONS drifting freely among them. Delocalised is the key word: the electrons belong to the whole metal, not to any one atom.

The sea explains the metal personality in one breath. Conduction: the delocalised electrons are already mobile, so charge — and heat — flow easily. Malleability and ductility: push one layer of ions past another and the sea flows around the new arrangement; the bonding survives, so metal bends where an ionic crystal would shatter. Shine: the loose electrons interact strongly with light. Three properties, one cause — say "delocalised electrons" and the reasons write themselves.

Close the topic with the calculation that ends nearly every bonding question: RELATIVE MOLECULAR MASS for covalent molecules, RELATIVE FORMULA MASS for ionic formula units — same method, different name. Add the relative atomic masses from the periodic table, respecting subscripts. Water, H two O: two hydrogens at 1 each, plus oxygen at 16 — 18. Carbon dioxide, C O two: 12 plus two sixteens — 44. Ammonia, N H three: 14 plus 3 — 17. Sodium chloride: 23 plus 35,5 — 58,5. Calcium chloride, C a C l two: 40 plus two times 35,5 — 40 plus 71 — 111. Magnesium bromide, M g B r two: 24 plus two times 80 — 184. The classic error is ignoring a subscript — counting one chlorine in calcium chloride instead of two. Read the formula like an atom inventory, multiply before adding, and the marks are safe.

The final questions of this part are in front of you now: the sea described, the properties explained by it, and masses added with every subscript honoured.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same three bonds as three ways people share at home — same chemistry, kitchen-table pictures first.

## Subtopic: Sharing the Data, Swapping the Chairs

Why do atoms bother bonding at all? Because every atom wants its outer shelf of electrons FULL — eight for most, two for the tiny ones. A full shelf is calm and settled, like the noble gases, the neighbours who need nothing from anybody. Every bond in chemistry is just an atom's plan for filling its shelf, and there are only two plans between neighbours: share, or swap.

Plan one — sharing — is two friends with one data bundle between them. Neither can afford the full bundle alone, so they pool: both phones run off the shared data, and both count it as theirs. That is covalent bonding: two non-metals, each contributing an electron to a shared pair that sits between them, counted by both. Water is oxygen sharing with two hydrogens; methane is carbon sharing with four. Some pairs need more: two oxygens share two pairs — a double bond — and two nitrogens share three, the famous triple bond that makes nitrogen gas so lazy in the air.

Plan two — swapping — is a chair trade that suits both sides. Sodium owns one awkward chair it does not want; chlorine has seven chairs and one gap. Sodium simply GIVES its chair away. Now sodium's shelf below is full and it carries a plus badge; chlorine's shelf is complete and it wears a minus badge. Plus and minus attract — and THAT attraction, not the handover itself, is the ionic bond.

How do you know which plan two atoms will pick? Read the table: two non-metals, they share; a metal and a non-metal, they swap. Questions on the two plans are with you now.

## Subtopic: The Salt Grid and the Electron Sea

What happens AFTER the swap explains everything about salt. The plus and minus ions do not pair off into little couples — they stack. Every plus surrounds itself with minuses, every minus with pluses, out in all directions, millions upon millions deep: a giant grid, the crystal lattice. A grain of salt is one tiny piece of that grid.

The grid explains salt's whole personality. It is hard to melt, because you must break attractions running in every direction at once. It is brittle — hit it and it shatters rather than bends, because shifting a layer forces plus against plus, and the grid rips itself apart. And the formula N a C l is not a molecule; it is the RECIPE RATIO — one plus for every minus. Calcium chloride's recipe reads C a C l two because calcium wears a two-plus badge and needs two minus-one chlorides to balance to zero. Charge always balances to zero; that single rule writes every ionic formula.

Metals play a third game entirely. In a copper wire, every atom is a metal — no one to swap with. So they all toss their outer electrons into one communal pot, and the loose electrons wash freely around the grid of positive leftovers: the electron sea. The sea is why metal is metal. Wiggle the sea and charge flows — that is electrical conduction. Bend the metal and the sea simply flows around the rearranged ions — the metal dents and bends where salt would shatter. Even the shine comes from that loose, restless sea.

Three structures, three personalities: molecules for the sharers, a rigid grid for the swappers, a sea for the metals. Your questions on the grid and the sea are up now.

## Subtopic: Weighing the Recipe

The last skill is pure supermarket arithmetic: weighing a recipe by adding up its ingredients.

Every formula is a recipe, and the periodic table is the price list — every element with its relative atomic mass in the corner of its block. To weigh the recipe, read the atom counts honestly and add. Water, H two O: two hydrogens at 1 each is 2, plus one oxygen at 16 — total 18. Carbon dioxide: one carbon, 12, plus two oxygens at 16 each, 32 — total 44. Ammonia: nitrogen 14 plus three hydrogens — 17.

Same game for the swapped compounds. Table salt: sodium 23 plus chloride 35,5 — 58,5. Calcium chloride, C a C l two: here is where the marks are won and lost. The little two applies to the chlorine — TWO chlorides at 35,5 each is 71, plus calcium's 40 — total 111. Magnesium bromide, M g B r two: 24 plus two eighties — 184. The name changes with the bond — molecular mass for sharers, formula mass for swappers — but the adding is identical.

The one error that eats these marks: skimming past a subscript. Read every formula out loud as an inventory before touching the numbers — "one calcium, two chlorines" — then multiply each count by its mass, then add. Inventory, multiply, add. Three beats, every time.

And why does this little sum matter so much? Because next topic, the mole turns these numbers into GRAMS you can weigh on a scale: 58,5 becomes the grams in a mole of salt. Every stoichiometry calculation from now to matric opens by weighing a recipe exactly like this.

Last questions of the lesson are with you right now: share or swap decided from the table, the grid and the sea explained, and recipes weighed with every subscript counted. Three bonds, three pictures, and the whole of Paper 2 chemistry now has its foundations poured.
