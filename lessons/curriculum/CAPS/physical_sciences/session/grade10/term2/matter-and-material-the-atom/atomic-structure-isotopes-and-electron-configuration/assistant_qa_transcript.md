**Thandi:** Before you move on — the questions that come up a lot with this topic. The first: how can an atom be mostly empty space? A desk feels completely solid.

**Tutor:** The solidity you feel is real — but it is electrical, not material. Scale the atom up to a stadium and the nucleus is a pea at the centre spot, with the electrons thinly spread through the rest; by volume, the atom is overwhelmingly nothing. So why does your hand not pass through the desk? Because touching is not about matter meeting matter — it is about electron clouds meeting electron clouds. As your hand approaches the desk, the outer electrons of your atoms and the desk's atoms come face to face, negative against negative, and like charges repel. That repulsion climbs steeply as the clouds close in, until it easily balances the push of your hand. You have never actually touched anything in the matter sense; you have always been pushing against electric fields. The gold-foil experiment is the honest witness: nearly all the alpha particles flew straight through the foil because there was almost nothing to hit, and only the rare one that lined up with a nucleus bounced back. Keep both truths side by side — mostly empty by volume, firmly solid by electricity — and the model stops feeling like a contradiction.

**Thandi:** Next: why do atoms only ever gain or lose electrons to become ions — why not lose a proton or two instead?

**Tutor:** Because of where each particle lives and what holds it there. The electrons occupy the outskirts of the atom, held by ordinary electrostatic attraction to the nucleus — an outermost electron is both far from the pull and screened by the electrons below it, so ordinary chemistry has enough energy to strip it away or push an extra one on. The protons live in the nucleus, bound by forces enormously stronger than anything a chemical reaction can supply; changing the proton count takes nuclear processes — radioactivity, reactors, stars — millions of times more energetic than any test-tube event. And there is an identity point stacked on top: the proton count IS the element. Remove an electron from sodium and you have a sodium ion — same element, new charge. Remove a proton and it is not sodium at all any more; it is neon, a different element altogether. That is why the bookkeeping in ion questions always fixes the protons first: a two-plus calcium ion still has calcium's 20 protons, and the two-plus tells you the electrons dropped from 20 to 18. Charge changes are electron stories, always — the nucleus sits untouched through the whole of chemistry.

**Thandi:** Then the isotope average: people keep answering 36 for chlorine, splitting the difference between 35 and 37. Why is that wrong?

**Tutor:** Because splitting the difference assumes the two isotopes turn up in equal numbers, and nature is under no such obligation. The correct tool is the weighted average — each isotope's mass counted in proportion to its share of the crowd. You already run this calculation every term with your marks: if one test counts three times and another once, your average leans hard toward the triple-counted test. Chlorine's crowd is 75 percent light twins at mass 35 and 25 percent heavy twins at mass 37 — three to one. So: 0,75 times 35 gives 26,25; 0,25 times 37 gives 9,25; add them and the answer is 35,5 — closer to 35 precisely because the light isotope dominates. The unweighted 36 would only be right in the special case of a fifty-fifty split. And use that closeness as a reading skill in reverse: when a periodic table shows copper at 63,5, sitting near 63, you can say before any calculation that the lighter copper isotope must be the more abundant. One more definition to keep crisp for the exam: the isotopes themselves differ ONLY in neutron count — same protons, same electrons, same chemistry — which is exactly why a mass average is the only difference the periodic table needs to record.

**Thandi:** Last one: in electron configurations, why does the 4s orbital fill before 3d? The numbers seem completely out of order.

**Tutor:** The numbers are out of order because the ENERGIES are, and electrons obey energy, not numbering. The floor numbers 1, 2, 3, 4 label how far a level sits from the nucleus, and mostly, further means costlier. But within each floor the room types differ too — s rooms sit slightly lower in energy than p rooms on the same floor — and by the time the building reaches its third and fourth floors, those two effects overlap. The 3d rooms, which belong to floor 3, turn out to cost slightly MORE energy than the 4s room of floor 4; the sub-levels of neighbouring floors interleave. An electron is perfectly lazy: offered a cheap 4s room on floor 4 and a pricier 3d room downstairs, it takes the 4s. Hence the filling order the syllabus gives you — 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s — and for every element up to number 20 that is the complete story; potassium's last electron and both of calcium's final pair go into 4s. Your working method stays mechanical: write the order, spend the electrons left to right — two, two, six, two, six, two at most — and then run the free self-check: the superscripts must add back to the atomic number. Calcium: 2 plus 2 plus 6 plus 2 plus 6 plus 2 is 20. If the sum misses, an orbital was skipped or overfilled, and the error announces itself before any marker sees it.
