# Part 1 — Expert

This session builds the two circuit patterns that every electricity question for the next three years is assembled from: series and parallel. The worked problem runs both patterns on the same components — a 12-volt battery with a 4-ohm resistor and an 8-ohm resistor — first joined in one loop, then joined side by side, and we calculate every current, every potential difference and every total resistance in both. The rules are short. The marks are lost almost entirely to mixing them up.

## Subtopic: What a Circuit Actually Moves — Charge, Energy and Resistance

Three quantities describe every circuit, and each one has a definition the examiner expects word for word.

Current, symbol I, is the rate of flow of charge. The formula: I equals Q over delta t — current equals the charge that passes a point, divided by the time it takes. Charge in coulombs, time in seconds, current in amperes; one ampere is one coulomb passing a point every second. Conventional current is drawn from the positive terminal, around the external circuit, back to the negative — even though the electrons themselves drift the other way.

Potential difference, symbol V, is the work done per unit charge. The formula: V equals W over Q — potential difference equals the energy transferred, divided by the charge that carried it. Joules per coulomb, and one joule per coulomb is one volt. Say the meaning aloud: 12 volts across a resistor means every coulomb passing through it hands over 12 joules. The emf of a battery is that same quantity at the source — the energy the battery gives each coulomb — and terminal potential difference is what remains available once the battery's own internal resistance has taken its share.

Follow the energy, because that is what a circuit is for. Chemical potential energy in the cell becomes electrical energy in the moving charge; in a resistor it becomes heat and light. The charge is not used up — the same coulombs circulate forever — but the energy is spent, and that is exactly why a battery goes flat.

Resistance, symbol R, unit the ohm, is the opposition a conductor offers to charge flow. Microscopically, drifting electrons collide with the vibrating atoms of the lattice and lose energy as heat. Four things change it: a longer conductor resists more, a thicker one resists less, a hotter metal resists more, and the material matters — copper resists far less than nichrome.

Two instrument rules, and they are marks every year. An ammeter measures current, so the charge must flow through it: connect it in SERIES, with very low resistance so it does not change what it measures. A voltmeter measures the energy difference across a component, so it goes ACROSS it: connect it in PARALLEL, with very high resistance so almost no current is diverted through it.

The questions for this section are with you now: the two defining formulas stated in words and symbols, the energy path through the circuit traced, and the ammeter and voltmeter each connected correctly.

## Subtopic: Ohm's Law and the Series Circuit — One Path, Shared Current

The relationship linking the three quantities is Ohm's law: V equals I R — the potential difference across a conductor equals the current through it multiplied by its resistance. Rearranged: I equals V over R, and R equals V over I. At constant temperature, the current through a metal conductor is directly proportional to the potential difference across it — double the volts, double the amps.

Now build the series circuit, and picture it as I describe it. Start at the positive terminal of a 12-volt battery. One wire leaves it and runs to the first resistor, 4 ohms. A single wire leaves that resistor and runs into the second, 8 ohms. One wire leaves the second and returns to the negative terminal. One unbroken loop, no junctions anywhere — that is what "series" means.

Two rules follow from having one path only. Rule one: the current is the SAME everywhere. Charge cannot pile up or leak out, so every coulomb that leaves the battery passes through the 4-ohm resistor and then the 8-ohm resistor — not shared, not used up, identical at every point. Rule two: the potential differences ADD to the emf, and the total resistance is the plain sum — R series equals R one plus R two, and so on.

Work the numbers. Total resistance: R series equals 4 plus 8, which is 12 ohms. Total current: I equals V over R, which is 12 volts over 12 ohms, giving 1 ampere — flowing through both resistors.

Potential difference across the 4-ohm resistor: V one equals I times R one, which is 1 ampere times 4 ohms, giving 4 volts. Across the 8-ohm resistor: V two equals I times R two, which is 1 ampere times 8 ohms, giving 8 volts. Check: 4 volts plus 8 volts equals 12 volts — the battery's full emf accounted for. A series circuit is a potential-difference divider, and it divides in proportion to resistance: twice the ohms, twice the volts.

Two consequences worth naming: add another resistor in series and the total resistance rises, so the current everywhere falls and every component dims; and break the loop at any single point — one blown globe in an old string of fairy lights — and the entire circuit dies, because there is no second path.

Stop for this section's questions now: Ohm's law stated and rearranged, the two series rules justified, and the 4-volt and 8-volt split reproduced from the resistances alone.

## Subtopic: The Parallel Circuit — Shared Potential Difference, Divided Current

Rewire the same two resistors. From the positive terminal of the same 12-volt battery, a wire runs to a junction. At that junction the path splits: one branch carries the 4-ohm resistor, the other the 8-ohm resistor. The two branches rejoin at a second junction, and a single wire returns to the negative terminal. Two separate paths between the same pair of junctions — that is what "parallel" means.

Rule one: the potential difference is the SAME across every branch. Both branches begin at one junction and end at the other, so both experience the same 12 volts. This is the rule learners lose most often — in parallel, volts are shared equally, not divided. Rule two: the branch currents ADD to the total leaving the battery, because a junction splits the flow and loses nothing. Rule three: the total resistance obeys the reciprocal formula — one over R parallel equals one over R one plus one over R two.

Work the numbers. One over R parallel equals one over 4 plus one over 8. Put both over eight: two over eight plus one over eight equals three over eight. Now invert — and inverting is the step everyone forgets — R parallel equals eight over three, which is 2,67 ohms to two decimal places.

Look hard at that answer: 2,67 ohms is SMALLER than either 4 ohms or 8 ohms. Not an error — the defining feature of parallel. A second path gives the charge somewhere else to go, so the combination opposes flow less than either resistor alone. The parallel total is always less than the smallest individual resistor, and that is your instant sanity check.

Total current: I equals V over R, which is 12 volts over 2,67 ohms, giving 4,5 amperes. Through the 4-ohm branch: I one equals 12 volts over 4 ohms, giving 3 amperes. Through the 8-ohm branch: I two equals 12 volts over 8 ohms, giving 1,5 amperes. Check the addition: 3 amperes plus 1,5 amperes equals 4,5 amperes — exactly the total. A parallel circuit is a current divider, and it divides inversely with resistance: the smaller resistance takes the larger current.

The questions on this section are in front of you now: the reciprocal formula applied and then inverted, the branch currents calculated and summed, and the reason a parallel total falls below the smallest resistor explained.

## Subtopic: Series Against Parallel — the Rules, the Traps and the Wiring in Your Walls

Set the two results side by side, because the contrast is the whole topic. Same battery, same two resistors. In series: total resistance 12 ohms, current 1 ampere everywhere, potential differences 4 volts and 8 volts. In parallel: total resistance 2,67 ohms, 12 volts across both, currents 3 amperes and 1,5 amperes, total 4,5 amperes. Identical components, four and a half times the current — because the connection pattern, not the components, sets the total resistance.

Compress the rules into a pair you can recite. SERIES: same current everywhere; potential differences add to the emf; resistances add; the total is larger than any one resistor; one break kills everything. PARALLEL: same potential difference on every branch; branch currents add to the total; reciprocals add; the total is smaller than the smallest branch; one break leaves the other branches working.

The error museum, four exhibits. Exhibit one: writing one over R parallel down as the answer — three over eight is the reciprocal of the resistance; invert it to get 2,67 ohms. Exhibit two: swapping the "same" quantity — equal currents claimed in parallel branches, or equal potential differences across series resistors. Fix it at the source: same PATH means same CURRENT; same PAIR OF JUNCTIONS means same VOLTS. Exhibit three: unit slips — milliamperes fed into a formula expecting amperes, kilo-ohms into one expecting ohms; convert before you substitute. Exhibit four: an answer with no unit. A bare number earns nothing.

Then the reason this matters outside a test paper. The plugs, lights and geyser in a South African home are wired in PARALLEL, and you can now say why in exam language: every appliance receives the full 230 volts regardless of what else is on, and switching off the kettle does not switch off the lights, because each appliance sits on its own branch. The cost is the third rule — every extra appliance lowers the total resistance and raises the total current, which is why an overloaded multiplug melts and why the breaker in the distribution board trips. Series wiring survives where you WANT one break to stop everything: the switch controlling a light is in series with it, and so is the breaker protecting the circuit.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the two rule sets recited without mixing them, the reciprocal inversion performed correctly, and household parallel wiring justified in the language of current and potential difference.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same two circuits from the road — one route through town, then two — because current behaves remarkably like traffic, and every rule you just met is something a taxi driver already knows.

## Subtopic: One Road Through Town

Think of charge as taxis and the battery as the rank where they get loaded up.

The battery does one job: it gives every taxi a full load of energy. Twelve volts means every coulomb leaves the battery carrying twelve joules — that is all "volts" ever means, energy handed to each passing bit of charge. The taxis drive the route, drop that energy wherever the road gets rough, and come back empty for another load. The taxis never wear out or disappear; the energy does. That is why a battery goes flat but never runs out of charge.

Now the single-road circuit. One road leaves the rank, passes through a rough patch, then through a rougher patch, then returns. No turn-offs. Because there is only one road, every taxi must pass through both patches — so the flow of traffic is exactly the same at every point. Count taxis at the start, in the middle, at the end: the same number per minute. Nothing gets used up, because taxis are not fuel.

What DOES get shared out is the energy. The first patch shakes a bit of it out of every taxi; the rougher patch shakes out more. Twelve joules go in, and by the time the taxi is home all twelve are gone. The rougher the patch, the bigger its share.

Put numbers on it. The patches are 4 ohms and 8 ohms — ohms just measure roughness. On one road the roughness adds: 4 plus 8 is 12 ohms to fight through. Twelve volts pushing against twelve ohms settles the traffic at one amp — one coulomb per second past any point. The energy split then follows the roughness: 4 volts at the 4-ohm patch, 8 volts at the 8-ohm patch, and 4 plus 8 gives back the full 12. Double the roughness, double the share.

Two things fall out of "one road only". Add another rough patch and the whole route slows down — everything dims together. And close the road anywhere, like one broken globe in an old string of fairy lights, and every taxi stops, because there is no way round. The proper name for one-road wiring is a series circuit.

Quick check before we carry on — the questions on this piece are coming to you right now: same traffic everywhere, shared-out energy, and the roughness simply added up.

## Subtopic: Two Roads, Two Queues

Now open a second road, and everything flips.

Same rank, same twelve joules per taxi. But this time the road splits at a junction: one route goes through the 4-ohm patch, the other through the 8-ohm patch, and the two rejoin further on. Both routes start at the same corner and finish at the same corner.

Here is the flip. Every taxi, whichever route it picks, sets out with twelve joules and arrives home empty — so BOTH routes drop the full twelve volts. Not six each. Twelve each. When two paths run between the same two corners they share the same energy drop, and that is the rule people mix up most: on separate branches, everybody gets the full voltage.

What splits instead is the traffic. Twelve volts through the 4-ohm route gives 12 divided by 4, which is 3 amps. Twelve volts through the 8-ohm route gives 12 divided by 8, which is 1,5 amps. Add them where they rejoin: 3 plus 1,5 is 4,5 amps leaving the rank altogether. Less roughness, more taxis — the easy road is always the busy one.

And now the fact that surprises everyone. What is the roughness of the WHOLE two-road system? Not twelve. Not even four. Opening a second road makes it easier to get through than either road alone — so the combined roughness must be less than the smaller one. The sum works on the upside-down numbers: a quarter plus an eighth is two-eighths plus one-eighth, which is three-eighths. But three-eighths is upside down — flip it back and you get eight-thirds, which is 2,67 ohms. Check it: 12 volts over 2,67 ohms gives 4,5 amps, the same total we counted at the junction.

Write that flip on your hand, because it is where half the marks in this topic die: add the upside-down numbers, then turn the answer the right way up again. If your final resistance came out bigger than the smallest road, you forgot to flip. Two-road wiring is called a parallel circuit.

Your questions for this part are up now: everyone gets the full twelve, the traffic splits with the easy road busier, and the upside-down sum turned back the right way up.

## Subtopic: Why Your House Is Wired the Clever Way

Last piece — and it is standing in your kitchen.

Look at what changed when we opened the second road. Same battery, same two rough patches, and yet: one road gave 12 ohms and 1 amp; two roads gave 2,67 ohms and 4,5 amps. Four and a half times the traffic, purely from how things were joined. Wiring is not decoration; it decides everything.

So which does a house use? Imagine your home wired as one road: the kettle, the TV, the lights and the fridge on a single loop. Everything shares out the voltage, so nothing gets its full share and everything runs weakly. Switch off the kettle and you have broken the road — the lights die too. Blow one globe and the whole house goes dark. Nobody would live like that.

Your house is wired the two-road way — parallel — and you can now say exactly why in physics language. Every plug point and every light sits on its own branch between the same two corners, so every one gets the full supply voltage whether the rest of the house is on or off. Switch the kettle off and the lights carry on, because breaking one branch leaves the others untouched.

There is a price, and it is the third rule. Every appliance you switch on is another road opened, so the total roughness drops and the total current climbs. That is why cramming heaters and kettles into one multiplug makes the cord hot enough to melt, and why the trip switch in the DB board snaps off — it is counting amps and it has had enough. It is also why an electrician runs a heavier cable to the geyser than to a bedside lamp. And one-road wiring still has its place: the wall switch is deliberately in series with the light, and so is the circuit breaker, because there you WANT one break to stop everything.

So carry two short sentences out of this lesson. One road: same traffic everywhere, energy shared out, roughness adds. Two roads: same energy drop on every branch, traffic splits, roughness drops below the smallest — after you flip the fraction back. Here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: the two rule sets kept apart, the flip done properly, and your own kitchen explained in volts and amps.
