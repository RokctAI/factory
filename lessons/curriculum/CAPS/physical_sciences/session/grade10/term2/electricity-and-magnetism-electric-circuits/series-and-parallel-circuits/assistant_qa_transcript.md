**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! First one, and it bothered a lot of us: if the current is the same before and after a resistor, what exactly does the resistor DO? Surely something must be smaller on the way out.

**Tutor:** Something is smaller on the way out — but it is the energy, not the charge. Keep the two apart and the confusion dissolves. Charge is conserved absolutely: every coulomb that enters a resistor leaves it, so the ammeter reading is identical on both sides. Nothing is consumed, because charge is the carrier, not the fuel. What the resistor takes is the energy each coulomb is carrying. A coulomb arrives holding a certain number of joules, collides its way through the vibrating lattice of the conductor, and leaves holding fewer — the difference having become heat, or in a globe, heat and light. That energy loss per coulomb is precisely what a voltmeter across the resistor measures, because potential difference is defined as work done per unit charge, V equals W over Q. So the correct sentence is: the current is unchanged, the potential drops. If you ever write "the current is used up", you have made charge behave like petrol, and every later step will misbehave.

***

**Thandi:** Next: I keep mixing up which quantity stays the same. Is there something better than memorising four rules that all sound alike?

**Tutor:** Yes — derive them instead of memorising them, from one question: how many paths are there? In series there is one unbroken path, so every coulomb must travel through every component in turn. That single fact forces the current to be identical everywhere, and it forces the energy each coulomb carries to be spent across the components one after another, which is why the potential differences add up to the emf. In parallel there are two or more paths that begin at the same junction and end at the same junction. Two points, one energy difference between them — so every branch experiences the same potential difference, no matter what it contains. And since the flow divides at the junction and recombines afterwards, the branch currents must add to the total. So: same PATH forces same CURRENT; same PAIR OF JUNCTIONS forces same POTENTIAL DIFFERENCE. Trace the circuit with your finger, count the paths, and the rules arrive on their own.

**Thandi:** This one felt like a trick: how can adding a resistor make the total resistance go DOWN? A resistor resists — surely more of them must resist more.

**Tutor:** It sounds like a trick and it is honest physics, and the wording of the question hides the answer. A resistor added in SERIES does raise the total, exactly as your instinct says, because it lengthens the single path every coulomb must struggle through. A resistor added in PARALLEL does the opposite, because it is not added to the path — it is an additional path. Think of a hall emptying after assembly. Every extra door you open makes it easier for the crowd to get out, even if the new door is narrow, because nobody is forced to use it. Charge behaves the same way: it takes every route available at once, so total opposition falls. The arithmetic agrees. A four-ohm resistor alone gives three amperes on twelve volts. Put an eight-ohm resistor beside it and the four-ohm branch still carries its three amperes, untouched, plus one comma five amperes now flowing in the new branch. More current for the same voltage means less resistance — two comma six seven ohms. That is also your check: a parallel answer is always smaller than the smallest branch.

**Thandi:** Last one, from someone whose exam answer was marked wrong even though the number was right: how do we lose marks when the arithmetic is correct?

**Tutor:** Three ways, and all three are avoidable. The first is the missing unit. Ohms, volts and amperes carry meaning, and a bare "2,67" does not say resistance any more than "12" says volts; memos award the unit as its own mark. The second is the unfinished reciprocal. When you write one over R parallel equals three-eighths, you have found the reciprocal of the resistance, not the resistance. Learners write 0,375 ohms and lose the whole question, when one keystroke — the inverse key — would have given two comma six seven ohms. Build the habit of saying "flip" out loud at that line. The third is unit mismatch at the substitution step: a current given in milliamperes or a resistance in kilo-ohms fed straight into a formula that expects amperes and ohms. Convert at the moment you list the data, before any calculation. And one bonus habit that catches everything: after each answer, ask whether it is physically sensible. A parallel total bigger than its smallest branch, or a branch current bigger than the total leaving the battery, is impossible — and noticing that in the exam room is worth more than any formula.
