Hey, it's Thandi — circuits panic people because everything has a symbol and everything has a rule, and the rules look like each other. Take a breath: there are only two patterns in the whole of Grade 10, and each one has exactly three lines to remember.

One path, no junctions, is series. Its three lines: current is the same everywhere, the voltages add up to the battery's, and the resistances just add. Two paths between the same two junctions is parallel. Its three lines: voltage is the same on every branch, the currents add up to the total, and the resistances add upside down and then flip back.

That flip is where most of the lost marks live. One over R equals a quarter plus an eighth equals three-eighths — and three-eighths is NOT your answer. Turn it over: eight-thirds, two comma six seven ohms. Your safety check is beautiful and takes one second: a parallel answer must always come out smaller than the smallest resistor in the bunch. Bigger? You forgot to flip.

The other habit that protects marks is boring and it works: before you calculate anything, put a finger on the circuit and trace the path from the positive terminal. If your finger never has to choose, it's series. If it reaches a fork, it's parallel from there. Decide the pattern first, write the three lines for that pattern, and only then reach for the calculator.

And every answer wears its unit — ohms, volts, amperes. A naked number scores nothing, no matter how correct it is.

Two patterns, three lines each, one flip and one unit check. That is genuinely the whole topic, and you already use it every time you switch on a kettle without the lights going out. You've got this!
