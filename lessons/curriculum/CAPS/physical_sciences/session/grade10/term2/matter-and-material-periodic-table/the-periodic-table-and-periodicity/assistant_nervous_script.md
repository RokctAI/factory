Hey, it's Thandi — if the periodic table looks like a hundred boxes of random facts waiting to be memorised, put the highlighter down and breathe — this topic is one map and one tug-of-war, and you never memorise what you can read off.

The map first. Elements are numbered by protons, in strict order. The row tells you how many electron floors the atom has. The column tells you how many electrons live on the top floor — and the top floor is the only part other atoms ever meet, which is why a column is a family with shared behaviour. Address in, chemistry out.

Then the tug-of-war, which replaces all four trends. The nucleus pulls the outer electrons like a team pulling a rope. Across a row: more pullers, same rope — grip tightens, atoms shrink, electrons get expensive to remove, pulling power rises. Down a column: new floor each step — longer rope, muffled pull — atoms swell and the grip fades. Every trend question is answered by "more pull" or "longer rope". Two phrases, four trends, done.

The families come free after that. Column 1: one coin to give, gives it recklessly, wilder as you go down. Column 2: two coins, calmer. Column 17: collectors hunting one missing coin, fiercest at the top. Column 18: full set, needs nobody. And the mirror rule — givers strengthen downward, collectors weaken downward — is just the rope again.

So walk into the practice questions with a map and a rope instead of a hundred flashcards. Read the address, feel the grip, state the reason. That is the whole topic, and it is far more yours than you think. You've got this!
