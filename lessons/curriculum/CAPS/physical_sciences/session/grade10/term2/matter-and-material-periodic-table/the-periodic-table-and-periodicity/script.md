# Part 1 — Expert

The periodic table is not a poster of facts — it is a machine for predicting chemistry. Built on the electron configurations of the previous topic, this session covers how the table is organised and why position reveals electron structure, where the metals, non-metals and metalloids sit, the four great trends that run from lithium to argon, and the family chemistry of groups 1, 2, 17 and 18 — the reactivity stories every exam draws on.

## Subtopic: The Table's Architecture — Atomic Number, Groups and Periods

The modern periodic table arranges the elements in order of increasing ATOMIC NUMBER — proton count, not mass. Read it like a book, left to right, top to bottom: hydrogen 1, helium 2, lithium 3, and onward, no gaps, no exceptions.

The rows and columns are where the meaning lives. A horizontal row is a PERIOD, and the period number equals the number of ENERGY LEVELS occupied by the element's electrons. Sodium's configuration — 1s two, 2s two, 2p six, 3s one — uses three levels, so sodium sits in period 3. A vertical column is a GROUP, and elements in the same group carry the same number of VALENCE electrons — electrons in the outermost level — which is why they share chemical behaviour. Number the main groups 1 and 2 on the left and 13 to 18 on the right; for groups 13 to 18, the units digit gives the valence count — group 17 elements have seven valence electrons, group 18 have eight, a full outer level.

So position and electron structure are two spellings of the same fact, and you must translate both ways. Given the configuration 1s two, 2s two, 2p six, 3s two, 3p five: three occupied levels — period 3; seven valence electrons — group 17; the element is chlorine. Given a position — period 2, group 2 — you can write the configuration back: two levels, two valence electrons: 1s two, 2s two — beryllium.

Between the two blocks of main groups sits the long central slab of TRANSITION METALS — iron, copper, zinc and their neighbours — treated at this level as a single family of typically hard, dense metals.

Take this section's questions now: order by atomic number, period equals levels, group tracks valence electrons, and the translation run in both directions.

## Subtopic: Metals, Non-Metals and Metalloids

Draw a staircase line starting beside boron and stepping down to the right, and the table splits into its three broad kingdoms.

To the LEFT of the staircase — the great majority of elements — lie the METALS: typically shiny, malleable rather than brittle, ductile enough to draw into wires, and good conductors of both heat and electricity, because their structure holds a sea of mobile electrons. Most main-group metals form POSITIVE ions: group 1 metals lose their single valence electron to form plus-one cations; group 2 lose two for plus-two.

To the UPPER RIGHT lie the NON-METALS: dull, brittle when solid, poor conductors — insulators — and inclined to GAIN electrons, forming negative anions: group 17 gains one electron for minus-one; oxygen's family typically gains two for minus-two. Those two habits, metal-loses and non-metal-gains, are the engine room of the cation and anion table used for writing formulae.

ALONG the staircase itself sit the METALLOIDS — the in-betweeners, notably silicon and germanium — with mixed properties. Electrically they are SEMICONDUCTORS: conducting better than insulators, worse than metals, and controllably so, which is precisely why silicon runs the entire electronics industry from cellphones to solar panels.

The syllabus also tags magnetic behaviour: a few metals — iron, cobalt, nickel — are strongly magnetic, while most materials are not. And keep the conductor spectrum as a single sentence: metals conduct, non-metals insulate, metalloids semiconduct.

This section's questions are with you now: the three kingdoms located, their properties contrasted, and the ion habits of each side of the staircase.

## Subtopic: Trends Across and Down — Radius, Ionisation Energy, Electronegativity

From lithium to argon, four properties change in step, and one physical idea explains all four: crossing a period adds protons to the SAME outer level, so the nuclear pull on the outer electrons strengthens; descending a group adds a whole NEW level, so the outer electrons sit further out, screened by more inner electrons.

Trend one: ATOMIC RADIUS — the size of the atom. ACROSS a period, radius DECREASES: more protons pulling on the same shells reels them inward, so chlorine is smaller than sodium despite having more electrons. DOWN a group, radius INCREASES: each row adds a level, like adding a ring to an onion.

Trend two: IONISATION ENERGY — the energy needed to remove an electron from the atom. Across a period it INCREASES: the stronger pull grips the outer electrons harder, so stripping one costs more. Down a group it DECREASES: the outer electron is further away and well screened, so it leaves cheaply — which is why the big group 1 metals shed electrons so eagerly.

Trend three: ELECTRON AFFINITY — the energy change when an atom GAINS an electron. Across a period, atoms accept extra electrons more and more readily; the halogens, one electron short of a full level, are the most eager electron-catchers on the table.

Trend four: ELECTRONEGATIVITY — the strength with which an atom in a bond pulls SHARED electrons toward itself. It rises across a period and falls down a group, making fluorine, top right, the strongest puller in chemistry, with cesium, bottom left, the weakest. The noble gases sit out this trend — with full outer levels, they rarely bond at all.

One mental picture holds all four: across a period the nucleus wins; down a group distance and screening win. Take the questions for this section now, stating each trend in both directions with its reason.

## Subtopic: Group Portraits — Alkali Metals to Noble Gases

Four families, four personalities — each one written by its valence electrons.

GROUP 1, the ALKALI METALS — lithium, sodium, potassium. One lonely valence electron, cheap to lose, so these are the most reactive metals known: soft enough to cut with a knife, stored under oil because they attack air and water. Dropped into water they fizz, melt, and can burst into flame, forming an alkaline solution — hence the name. Reactivity INCREASES down the group: potassium's outer electron sits further out than sodium's, is more screened, and leaves even more easily. All form plus-one ions.

GROUP 2, the ALKALINE EARTH METALS — magnesium, calcium. Two valence electrons, forming plus-two ions. Reactive, but a clear step calmer than group 1 — losing two electrons costs more than losing one. Magnesium burns with a brilliant white flare; calcium compounds build cement, bones and teeth.

GROUP 17, the HALOGENS — fluorine, chlorine, bromine, iodine. Seven valence electrons, one short of a full level, so they hunt a single extra electron — forming minus-one ions — and are the most reactive NON-metals. Here reactivity DECREASES down the group: fluorine, small and unscreened, grips new electrons ferociously; iodine, large and well screened, only mildly. Notice the mirror: metals grow more reactive going down; non-metals less.

GROUP 18, the NOBLE GASES — helium, neon, argon. Full outer levels: nothing to lose, nothing to gain. They are chemically UNREACTIVE — inert for this course — and exist as single atoms. Their only role is to be everyone else's ambition: ions and bonds form precisely to reach a noble-gas electron arrangement, the idea the entire next topic stands on.

The final questions of this part are in front of you now: each family's electron count, ion charge, and reactivity direction with its reason.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same table as a street map, a tug-of-war, and four families from your own neighbourhood — same chemistry, plain words first.

## Subtopic: The Street Map of the Elements

Think of the periodic table as the street map of a town where every element has an address — and the address TELLS you about the resident, the way a real address tells you about schools and taxi routes before you ever knock on the door.

The house numbers run in strict order: 1, 2, 3, up the proton count, left to right, row by row. No house is skipped. The STREET you live in — the row, called a period — tells you how many floors your house has: row 1 houses have one floor of electrons, row 3 houses have three. Sodium lives in row 3 because its electrons occupy three levels.

The SIDE of town — the column, called a group — tells you who your family is, because everyone in a column has the same number of electrons on their top floor. And the top floor is all that matters socially: those valence electrons are the only ones other atoms ever meet. One top-floor electron: column 1, restless and generous. Seven top-floor electrons: column 17, one short of a full set and hungry. Eight: column 18, complete and content.

So a single address answers two questions instantly. Where is chlorine? Row 3 — three floors; column 17 — seven electrons upstairs. And the reverse works too: an element with configuration ending 2s two is on row 2 with two upstairs — that is beryllium's address. Learn to read addresses both ways and half of Paper 2's periodic-table marks are already yours.

Questions on the map are with you now: streets, sides of town, and the two-way address reading.

## Subtopic: The Tug-of-War for Electrons

All four famous trends are one playground game: the nucleus pulling on the outer electrons like a tug-of-war team pulling on a rope. Two things decide who wins — how many pullers, and how long the rope.

Walk ACROSS a row, sodium to chlorine. Each step adds a proton to the team, but the outer electrons stay on the same floor — same rope length. More pullers, same rope: the grip tightens. So going across, atoms get SMALLER — the rope is reeled in; electrons get harder to steal — that is ionisation energy going up; and the atom tugs harder on shared and incoming electrons — electronegativity and electron affinity rising. One story, four marks.

Now walk DOWN a column, lithium to potassium. Each step adds a whole new floor, so the outer electrons sit further from the nucleus — a longer rope — and the crowded inner floors stand between, muffling the pull. Longer rope, muffled pull: the grip weakens. Atoms get BIGGER going down; the top-floor electron gets easier to remove; the atom's tug on electrons fades.

Put the two walks together and the corners explain themselves. Top right — fluorine — short rope, big team: the fiercest electron-puller in chemistry. Bottom left — the giant soft metals — long rope, muffled pull: electrons practically fall off. You never memorise four separate trends; you replay one tug-of-war and read off whichever property the question names.

Have a go at this section's questions now — every answer is either "more pull" or "longer rope".

## Subtopic: Four Families You Already Know

Meet the four families the examiners love, personality first.

Column 1, the alkali metals, are the impulsive givers. One coin in the pocket — one valence electron — and they cannot wait to spend it. Sodium hits water and fizzes furiously; potassium does the same but wilder. Why wilder? Potassium's coin sits a floor higher — longer rope, weaker grip — so it drops even more easily. The giving family gets MORE reactive as you go down. And having spent the coin, each becomes a plus-one ion: chemistry's version of lighter and happier.

Column 2 are the careful givers: two coins, and parting with two takes more persuading, so magnesium and calcium are reactive but not reckless — steady citizens who end up as plus-two ions, building cement and bones.

Column 17, the halogens, are the collectors. Seven coins, one short of the full set, and they will snatch the missing coin from almost anyone — the most reactive non-metals, ending as minus-one ions. But collectors weaken going DOWN: fluorine, tiny with a short rope, snatches hardest; iodine, big and muffled, barely bothers. Givers strengthen downward, collectors weaken downward — the mirror is the exam's favourite comparison.

Column 18, the noble gases, are the family that needs nothing: full set, complete top floor. Helium, neon and argon react with essentially nobody — which is why party balloons and old shop signs are filled with them. And their contentment is the secret motor of everything: every ion formed and every bond made in the next topic is some atom's attempt to look like a noble gas.

Last questions of the lesson are with you right now: givers, careful givers, collectors, and the content ones — with each family's ion charge and reactivity direction attached. Read the map, feel the rope, and the table stops being a poster and starts being a prediction machine.
