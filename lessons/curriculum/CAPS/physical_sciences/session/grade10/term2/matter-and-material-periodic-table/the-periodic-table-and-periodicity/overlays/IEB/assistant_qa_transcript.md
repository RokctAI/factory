**Thandi:** Before you go — the four questions that always come back with this topic. First: why is the table ordered by proton count and not by mass? Mass feels like the more obvious thing to sort by.

**Tutor:** Mass was the original sorting key, and the table itself exposed its flaw. Sort strictly by mass and a few neighbours land in the wrong families — pairs exist where the slightly heavier element clearly belongs one seat earlier, because its chemistry matches the earlier column. The chemistry was shouting that something other than mass was the true organising quantity. That quantity is the proton count — the atomic number — and the reason is a chain you already own: protons fix the element's identity; in a neutral atom, protons fix the electron count; the electron count fixes the configuration; and the outer configuration fixes the chemistry. Mass, meanwhile, is polluted by neutrons — isotopes shuffle mass around without changing chemistry one bit, which is exactly why mass occasionally lies about where an element belongs. Order by protons and every element falls into the family its behaviour demands, no exceptions, with the added elegance that the sequence runs one, two, three with no gaps: a missing number would be an undiscovered element, and historically those gaps were predicted, hunted and found. So when you read the table, hear it as an electron-structure catalogue indexed by proton count — the one ordering that can never be fooled by a few extra neutrons.

***

**Thandi:** Next: the trends. Students can recite "radius decreases across" but freeze when asked WHY chlorine is smaller than sodium when it has more electrons. What is the clean answer?

**Tutor:** The freeze comes from a hidden wrong assumption — that more electrons must mean more bulk. Electrons are not bricks; they are held in shells by attraction, and the SIZE of the atom is set by how hard those shells are pulled inward. Walk from sodium to chlorine along period 3. Every step adds one proton to the nucleus and one electron to the SAME third level — no new floor is opened. The pulling team grows from eleven protons to seventeen, while the rope — the distance to the outer level — stays essentially the same. Seventeen pullers grip the third shell far harder than eleven do, so the whole electron cloud is reeled inward: chlorine's extra electrons live in a smaller, tighter house than sodium's fewer ones. The moment a new level DOES open — stepping down from sodium to potassium — the story flips: the outer electron now sits a full floor further out, with an extra layer of inner electrons standing between it and the nucleus, muffling the pull. That is why radius grows downward. Answer every radius question with those two ingredients — pull strength and rope length — and never with electron count alone, because electron count without pull tells you nothing about size.

***

**Thandi:** Then the families. Why do the metal families get MORE reactive going down while the halogens get LESS? It seems inconsistent.

**Tutor:** It becomes perfectly consistent the moment you notice the two families are playing opposite games, judged by the same rope. Reactivity, for both, measures how easily the family does its signature move — but the moves point in opposite directions. The alkali metals' move is LOSING an electron. Going down the group, the outer electron sits further out on a longer, muffled rope, so the nucleus holds it more feebly, and the loss gets easier: potassium outperforms sodium, and the family grows wilder downward. The halogens' move is GAINING an electron. An incoming electron is attracted by the nucleus — and that attraction is strongest when the landing spot is close in and barely screened. Fluorine, tiny, offers an arrival right beside a barely-shielded nucleus: ferocious capture. Iodine's landing spot is floors away behind crowds of inner electrons: the welcome is lukewarm. So the same physics — grip weakening down the group — helps the losers and hinders the gainers. One rope, two opposite games, mirror-image trends. When a comparison question pairs the two families, write that sentence and both directions come out right automatically: weaker grip means easier losing and harder gaining.

**Thandi:** Last one: what is the fastest way to use the table in a test — the habits that turn it from a poster into the prediction machine you keep promising?

**Tutor:** Five habits, each a few seconds, and together they answer most questions before any memorising. Habit one: read the address both ways. Period gives levels, group gives valence electrons — so a configuration hands you a position, and a position hands back a configuration; practise until the translation is instant. Habit two: place the staircase. Left of it, metal — expect shine, bending, conduction, cation; upper right, non-metal — expect dull, brittle, insulating, anion; on it, metalloid — expect a semiconductor, and think of silicon. Habit three: predict ion charges from the column — plus one, plus two on the left; minus two, minus one before the noble gases — and formulas follow by balancing to zero, exactly as in the formula-writing lesson. Habit four: run the tug-of-war before quoting any trend — ask "more pull or longer rope?" and read off radius, ionisation energy, affinity or electronegativity as required, remembering the noble gases sit out electronegativity. Habit five: for reactivity, name the family's game first — giver or gainer — then apply the rope: givers wilder downward, gainers tamer. Address, staircase, charge, rope, game. Five glances, and the table is doing the remembering for you.
