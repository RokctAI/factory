## Subtopic: Charges Must Cancel — the One Law of Formula Writing

A chemical formula is not spelling to memorise — it is arithmetic to perform. Ionic compounds are built from ions: atoms or groups that carry electric charge. Metals lose electrons and become positive ions — cations. Non-metals gain electrons and become negative ions — anions.

And here is the single law that writes every formula: the compound as a whole must be electrically NEUTRAL. Total positive charge plus total negative charge must equal exactly zero. The formula's job is to declare the ratio of ions that achieves that zero.

Where do the charges come from? The periodic table, plus the ion table on your data sheet. Group one metals — sodium, potassium — form one-plus ions. Group two — magnesium, calcium — two-plus. Aluminium in group thirteen: three-plus. Across the table: group seventeen, the halogens — chlorine, bromine — form one-minus ions. Group sixteen — oxygen, sulphur — two-minus. Group fifteen's nitrogen: three-minus.

Practise reading a known formula through this law before writing new ones. Sodium chloride: NaCl. Sodium is one-plus, chloride one-minus — one of each gives plus one minus one: zero. Neutral, so the ratio is one-to-one and no subscripts appear. Magnesium oxide: Mg two-plus meets O two-minus — again one of each cancels: MgO. But magnesium chloride: Mg two-plus against Cl one-minus — ONE chloride cannot cancel two-plus. TWO chlorides can. MgCl2. The subscript two is not decoration; it is the arithmetic of neutrality made visible.

That is the entire theory. Everything that follows is technique for doing this arithmetic quickly and without error.

## Subtopic: Calcium Chloride and the Crossover Method

First exam compound: calcium chloride. Harvest the ions. Calcium: group two — Ca two-plus. Chloride: chlorine's ion, group seventeen — Cl one-minus.

Reason it from the law: one calcium supplies plus two. Each chloride brings minus one. To reach zero, the two-plus needs two one-minuses. Formula: CaCl2. Done by pure logic.

Now the speed technique everyone uses: the crossover method. Write the ions side by side with their charges: Ca two-plus, Cl one-minus. Take the SIZE of each charge — ignore the sign — and cross it over: calcium's two becomes chlorine's subscript; chlorine's one becomes calcium's subscript. Result: Ca one Cl two — written CaCl2, because subscripts of one are never shown.

Why does crossing work? Because it manufactures equal totals automatically: two chlorides at one-minus match one calcium at two-plus. The crossover is the neutrality law running on rails.

But the rails have two safety rules. Safety rule one: SIMPLIFY after crossing. Cross magnesium two-plus with oxide two-minus and you get Mg2O2 — but the ratio two-to-two is just one-to-one in disguise: the correct formula is MgO. Always reduce the subscripts to the smallest whole-number ratio. Safety rule two: never show a subscript of one — CaCl2, not Ca1Cl2.

And the professional's habit: verify by charge audit. CaCl2 holds one calcium at plus two — total plus two — and two chlorides at minus one each — total minus two. Sum: zero. Neutral, proven. Run this silent audit on every formula you write; it costs ten seconds and catches everything.

## Subtopic: Ammonium Sulphate — Polyatomic Ions and the Bracket Rule

Second compound: ammonium sulphate. Neither word names a single element — both name POLYATOMIC ions: charged teams of atoms that travel and react as one unit. Your data sheet lists them; learn today's two.

Ammonium: N H four plus — one nitrogen, four hydrogens, net charge one-plus. Note it well: ammonium is the rare POSITIVE polyatomic ion, a cation without a metal in sight. Sulphate: S O four two-minus — one sulphur, four oxygens, charge two-minus. Do not confuse sulphate with sulphide, which is the simple S two-minus ion — the "-ate" ending signals oxygen aboard.

Balance the charges: ammonium brings plus one; sulphate demands minus two. Two ammoniums per sulphate. The crossover agrees: cross the one and the two — two ammoniums, one sulphate.

Now the rule this compound exists to teach: when a POLYATOMIC ion takes a subscript, wrap the whole ion in BRACKETS first. Two ammonium ions: open bracket, N H four, close bracket, subscript two. The full formula: (NH4)2SO4.

Why are the brackets non-negotiable? Read the disasters without them. NH42SO4 claims forty-two hydrogens. NH8SO4 — someone doubling the hydrogens by hand — destroys the ammonium ion's identity and loses the nitrogen count. The bracket says: THIS ENTIRE TEAM, taken twice. It is the only honest way to multiply a team.

Audit the finished formula, atom by atom and charge by charge. Atoms: the bracket's subscript two multiplies everything inside — two nitrogens, eight hydrogens — then one sulphur, four oxygens. Charges: two ammoniums at plus one give plus two; one sulphate at minus two gives minus two. Zero. Neutral, verified.

Sulphate stays bracket-free here because only ONE sulphate appears — brackets earn their place only when a subscript must multiply a team. Calcium nitrate, with two nitrate teams, would be Ca(NO3)2 — same rule, other side.

## Subtopic: Iron(III) Oxide — Roman Numerals and the Complete Method

Third compound: iron three oxide — written iron, Roman numeral three in brackets, oxide. Why the numeral? Because iron is a transition metal, and transition metals are two-faced: iron forms BOTH Fe two-plus and Fe three-plus ions. Both make real compounds. The name must therefore tell you which iron attended — and that is exactly what the Roman numeral does. Iron three means Fe three-plus, by instruction. The numeral IS the charge — assembly instructions inside the name.

So: Fe three-plus meets oxide, O two-minus. Neutrality arithmetic: the lowest common multiple of three and two is six. Two irons supply plus six; three oxides supply minus six. Zero. Formula: Fe2O3. The crossover delivers the same: cross the three under oxygen, the two under iron — Fe2O3 — and this time nothing simplifies, because two and three share no factor.

Contrast: iron TWO oxide. Fe two-plus with O two-minus — one each cancels — FeO. Different numeral, different compound entirely: FeO and Fe2O3 differ in colour, in behaviour, in mass. That is why the numeral can never be ignored: one Roman numeral separates two different substances.

Charge audit of Fe2O3: two irons at plus three — plus six; three oxides at minus two — minus six. Zero. Verified.

Now assemble the complete method, five steps for any ionic formula. Step one: name the two ions — data sheet and periodic table in hand; Roman numerals dictate transition-metal charges; "-ate" endings flag polyatomic ions. Step two: write both ions WITH their charges. Step three: find the neutral ratio — by logic or by crossover. Step four: simplify to the smallest ratio, add brackets wherever a polyatomic ion carries a subscript, and hide all subscripts of one. Step five: audit — total plus must cancel total minus, and the atom count must respect every bracket.

The error museum, four exhibits. Exhibit one: crossing without simplifying — Mg2O2 standing where MgO belongs. Exhibit two: missing brackets — NH42SO4 and its forty-two hydrogens. Exhibit three: the ignored numeral — writing FeO for iron three oxide because "iron is iron"; it is not. Exhibit four: charges misread from the table — sulphate as minus one, say — which poisons every later step; harvest charges carefully, they are the raw material.

Where does this skill flow? Straight into the next lesson: balancing chemical equations — where the formulae you write are LOCKED, and only coefficients may move. Write a formula wrongly and no amount of balancing can save the equation. Master the five steps tonight; the entire Chemical Change section — the biggest block of marks in matric Physical Sciences — is built on them.
