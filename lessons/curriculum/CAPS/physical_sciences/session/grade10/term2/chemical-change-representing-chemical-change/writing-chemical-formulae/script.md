# Part 1 — Expert

This session builds the skill of turning a compound's NAME into its exact symbolic recipe: writing chemical formulae. Three compounds carry the work — calcium chloride, ammonium sulphate, and iron three oxide: a simple ionic pair, a polyatomic ion demanding brackets, and a metal whose charge hides in a Roman numeral. Behind all three sits one law and one five-step method. Every chemical equation you will ever balance begins with formulae written correctly, so this hour is foundation work for the largest mark-block in matric chemistry.

## Subtopic: Charges Must Cancel — the One Law of Formula Writing

A chemical formula is not spelling to memorise — it is arithmetic to perform. Ionic compounds are built from ions: atoms or groups that carry electric charge. Metals lose electrons and become positive ions — cations. Non-metals gain electrons and become negative ions — anions.

And here is the single law that writes every formula: the compound as a whole must be electrically NEUTRAL. Total positive charge plus total negative charge must equal exactly zero. The formula's job is to declare the ratio of ions that achieves that zero.

Where do the charges come from? The periodic table, plus the ion table on your data sheet. Group one metals — sodium, potassium — form one-plus ions. Group two — magnesium, calcium — two-plus. Aluminium in group thirteen: three-plus. Across the table: group seventeen, the halogens — chlorine, bromine — form one-minus ions. Group sixteen — oxygen, sulphur — two-minus. Group fifteen's nitrogen: three-minus.

Practise reading a known formula through this law before writing new ones. Sodium chloride: NaCl. Sodium is one-plus, chloride one-minus — one of each gives plus one minus one: zero. Neutral, so the ratio is one-to-one and no subscripts appear. Magnesium oxide: Mg two-plus meets O two-minus — again one of each cancels: MgO. But magnesium chloride: Mg two-plus against Cl one-minus — ONE chloride cannot cancel two-plus. TWO chlorides can. MgCl2. The subscript two is not decoration; it is the arithmetic of neutrality made visible.

That is the entire theory. Everything that follows is technique for doing this arithmetic quickly and without error.

Pause here — the questions for this section are with you now: charges harvested correctly from the table, and the neutrality law applied to a known formula until it sums to zero.

## Subtopic: Calcium Chloride and the Crossover Method

First exam compound: calcium chloride. Harvest the ions. Calcium: group two — Ca two-plus. Chloride: chlorine's ion, group seventeen — Cl one-minus.

Reason it from the law: one calcium supplies plus two. Each chloride brings minus one. To reach zero, the two-plus needs two one-minuses. Formula: CaCl2. Done by pure logic.

Now the speed technique everyone uses: the crossover method. Write the ions side by side with their charges: Ca two-plus, Cl one-minus. Take the SIZE of each charge — ignore the sign — and cross it over: calcium's two becomes chlorine's subscript; chlorine's one becomes calcium's subscript. Result: Ca one Cl two — written CaCl2, because subscripts of one are never shown.

Why does crossing work? Because it manufactures equal totals automatically: two chlorides at one-minus match one calcium at two-plus. The crossover is the neutrality law running on rails.

But the rails have two safety rules. Safety rule one: SIMPLIFY after crossing. Cross magnesium two-plus with oxide two-minus and you get Mg2O2 — but the ratio two-to-two is just one-to-one in disguise: the correct formula is MgO. Always reduce the subscripts to the smallest whole-number ratio. Safety rule two: never show a subscript of one — CaCl2, not Ca1Cl2.

And the professional's habit: verify by charge audit. CaCl2 holds one calcium at plus two — total plus two — and two chlorides at minus one each — total minus two. Sum: zero. Neutral, proven. Run this silent audit on every formula you write; it catches everything.

Stop for this section's questions now — the crossover performed, the result simplified to its smallest ratio, and the charge audit closed at zero.

## Subtopic: Ammonium Sulphate — Polyatomic Ions and the Bracket Rule

Second compound: ammonium sulphate. Neither word names a single element — both name POLYATOMIC ions: charged teams of atoms that travel and react as one unit. Your data sheet lists them; learn today's two.

Ammonium: N H four plus — one nitrogen, four hydrogens, net charge one-plus. Note it well: ammonium is the rare POSITIVE polyatomic ion, a cation without a metal in sight. Sulphate: S O four two-minus — one sulphur, four oxygens, charge two-minus. Do not confuse sulphate with sulphide, which is the simple S two-minus ion — the "-ate" ending signals oxygen aboard.

Balance the charges: ammonium brings plus one; sulphate demands minus two. Two ammoniums per sulphate. The crossover agrees: cross the one and the two — two ammoniums, one sulphate.

Now the rule this compound exists to teach: when a POLYATOMIC ion takes a subscript, wrap the whole ion in BRACKETS first. Two ammonium ions: open bracket, N H four, close bracket, subscript two. The full formula: (NH4)2SO4.

Why are the brackets non-negotiable? Read the disasters without them. NH42SO4 claims forty-two hydrogens. NH8SO4 — someone doubling the hydrogens by hand — destroys the ammonium ion's identity and loses the nitrogen count. The bracket says: THIS ENTIRE TEAM, taken twice. It is the only honest way to multiply a team.

Audit the finished formula, atom by atom and charge by charge. Atoms: the bracket's subscript two multiplies everything inside — two nitrogens, eight hydrogens — then one sulphur, four oxygens. Charges: two ammoniums at plus one give plus two; one sulphate at minus two gives minus two. Zero. Neutral, verified.

Sulphate stays bracket-free here because only ONE sulphate appears — brackets earn their place only when a subscript must multiply a team. Calcium nitrate, with two nitrate teams, would be Ca(NO3)2 — same rule, other side.

The questions on this section are in front of you now: the two polyatomic ions named with their charges, the bracket placed where a subscript multiplies a team, and the atom count of the finished formula read correctly.

## Subtopic: Iron(III) Oxide — Roman Numerals and the Complete Method

Third compound: iron three oxide — written iron, Roman numeral three in brackets, oxide. Why the numeral? Because iron is a transition metal, and transition metals are two-faced: iron forms BOTH Fe two-plus and Fe three-plus ions. Both make real compounds. The name must therefore tell you which iron attended — and that is exactly what the Roman numeral does. Iron three means Fe three-plus, by instruction. The numeral IS the charge — assembly instructions inside the name.

So: Fe three-plus meets oxide, O two-minus. Neutrality arithmetic: the lowest common multiple of three and two is six. Two irons supply plus six; three oxides supply minus six. Zero. Formula: Fe2O3. The crossover delivers the same: cross the three under oxygen, the two under iron — Fe2O3 — and this time nothing simplifies, because two and three share no factor.

Contrast: iron TWO oxide. Fe two-plus with O two-minus — one each cancels — FeO. Different numeral, different compound entirely: FeO and Fe2O3 differ in colour, in behaviour, in mass. That is why the numeral can never be ignored: one Roman numeral separates two different substances.

Charge audit of Fe2O3: two irons at plus three — plus six; three oxides at minus two — minus six. Zero. Verified.

Now assemble the complete method, five steps for any ionic formula. Step one: name the two ions — data sheet and periodic table in hand; Roman numerals dictate transition-metal charges; "-ate" endings flag polyatomic ions. Step two: write both ions WITH their charges. Step three: find the neutral ratio — by logic or by crossover. Step four: simplify to the smallest ratio, add brackets wherever a polyatomic ion carries a subscript, and hide all subscripts of one. Step five: audit — total plus must cancel total minus, and the atom count must respect every bracket.

The error museum, four exhibits. Exhibit one: crossing without simplifying — Mg2O2 standing where MgO belongs. Exhibit two: missing brackets — NH42SO4 and its forty-two hydrogens. Exhibit three: the ignored numeral — writing FeO for iron three oxide because "iron is iron"; it is not. Exhibit four: charges misread from the table — sulphate as minus one, say — which poisons every later step; harvest charges carefully, they are the raw material.

Where does this skill flow? Straight into balancing chemical equations — where the formulae you write are LOCKED, and only coefficients may move. Write a formula wrongly and no amount of balancing can save the equation.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the Roman numeral read as a charge, iron two oxide and iron three oxide told apart, and the five steps applied in order to a name you have not seen before.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same three formulae from behind the counter of a spaza shop, where the till must come to zero before anyone goes home.

## Subtopic: The Till Must Balance

Closing time at the shop. Whatever went into the drawer must match whatever went out — fifty rand over or fifty rand short, somebody stays late until the books come to zero. A chemical formula is that till, and the atoms are the money.

Some atoms hand things out and some take things in. The metals are the givers: sodium hands out one, calcium hands out two, aluminium hands out three, and giving leaves them positive — like a person who has lent money and is owed. The non-metals are the takers: chlorine takes one, oxygen takes two, nitrogen takes three, and taking leaves them negative, like a person carrying a debt. The columns of the periodic table hand you those amounts.

Now the shop arithmetic on calcium chloride. Calcium hands out two. Each chloride takes only one, and one taker cannot absorb what two-plus is handing over — so you need two chlorides. Plus two, minus one, minus one: the drawer closes at zero. Two chlorides for every calcium — CaCl2, the little two sitting under the chlorine.

The shortcut does that thinking for you. Put the two down with their numbers — calcium two, chloride one — and swap the numbers across, diagonally: calcium's two drops under the chlorine, chlorine's one under the calcium. One calcium, two chlorines, and nobody bothers with a little one. That swap is the crossover method, and it is only making both sides of the till match.

Two habits keep it honest. Cancel down: magnesium gives two and oxygen takes two, so crossing gives Mg2O2 — like pricing "two loaves for two rand" when you mean "a loaf a rand". Same deal, clumsy words: MgO. And never a little one — CaCl2, not Ca1Cl2. Then count the drawer out loud: one calcium at plus two, two chlorides at minus one each — zero. That zero is you being right, and you can feel it.

Questions on this piece are coming to you right now, so take your time: givers and takers, the swap, and the drawer counted to zero.

## Subtopic: The Team Rides in One Taxi

Some ions never travel alone. Ammonium is five atoms — one nitrogen and four hydrogens — that get in, ride and get out together, as ONE passenger paying ONE fare of plus one. Sulphate is another: one sulphur with four oxygens, one passenger, fare minus two. One ammonium cannot settle a minus two; two of them can, so the mix is two ammonium groups to one sulphate. The only question left is how to say "two of that group" without wrecking the group.

Think of the rank. When two full groups travel, you do not scribble the head count into the middle of the group — you load the whole group into one taxi and say "two taxis". The brackets are the taxi: open the bracket, N H four, close the bracket, and the two goes outside; sulphate follows behind. That is (NH4)2SO4.

Leave the taxi out and watch the crash. N H four two S O four reads as forty-two hydrogens on one nitrogen — a passenger chemistry has never met. Doubling only the hydrogens leaves the nitrogen standing at the rank. The bracket is the one honest way to say "this whole group, twice".

Counting is then easy: the two outside multiplies everything inside — two nitrogens, eight hydrogens — and sulphate rides once, so one sulphur and four oxygens. Money check: plus one twice against minus two once — zero.

Notice sulphate got no bracket: only one sulphate is travelling, and you never say "one taxi-load of one person". Need two nitrate groups, as in calcium nitrate? Then nitrate gets the taxi: Ca(NO3)2. And the name whispers which passenger you have — "-ide" is a lone atom, so sulphide is plain S at minus two, while "-ate" means oxygens are aboard, so sulphate is S O four at minus two. Chemists call the travelling groups polyatomic ions; the brackets are simply their taxi.

Nice work — that was the hardest bracket in grade ten. Your questions for this part are up now: which passenger, how many taxis, and the atom count once the taxi is doubled.

## Subtopic: When the Name Whispers the Charge

Most metals have a fixed price: sodium always hands out one, calcium always two, and you never have to ask. Iron is not like that. Sometimes iron hands out two, sometimes three, and both versions are real substances you have touched — so the name says which one showed up, in brackets, in Roman numerals. Iron three means the version handing out three. That numeral is not decoration and it is not a count of atoms; it is the charge, given to you free of charge.

So build iron three oxide. Iron hands out three, oxygen takes two, and three and two do not match — so find the smallest number both fit into: six. Two irons hand out six; three oxygens take six. Zero. Fe2O3 — two irons, three oxygens — and you know this substance: it is rust, the red-brown crust on an old gate after the rain. Change one numeral and you change the substance: iron two oxide is two against two, one each cancels, FeO, black and different in every way. "Iron is iron" is exactly the thought that loses the mark.

Here is the whole recipe in kitchen language. One: name the two ions, letting the name help — a Roman numeral gives the metal's number, an "-ate" ending means a travelling group from the data sheet. Two: put both down with their numbers. Three: find the mix that comes to zero, by thinking or by swapping across. Four: cancel down, bracket any group that gets multiplied, drop every little one. Five: count the till — pluses against minuses, atoms with the brackets respected.

Four ways people lose marks, all avoidable: crossing and forgetting to cancel; forgetting the taxi and its forty-two hydrogens; ignoring the Roman numeral; and misreading a charge off the table, which spoils everything after it.

Look what you can do now — name any compound in the syllabus and you can build it, bracket it and prove it. Here come the last questions of the lesson: the numeral read as a charge, rust built from scratch, and the five-step recipe run start to finish.
