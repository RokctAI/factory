**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! First one, and it comes up every single year: why can't we just change a small number inside a formula? It balances the equation so much faster.

**Tutor:** Because that number is not a quantity you are choosing — it is part of the substance's identity. A formula is a name written in atoms: water is two hydrogens bonded to one oxygen, and that ratio is what makes it water. Alter it to two hydrogens and two oxygens and you have named hydrogen peroxide instead — a different liquid with different properties, the one used to clean a wound rather than the one you drink. So an equation "balanced" by editing subscripts is not a balanced equation at all; it is a correct-looking sentence about a reaction that never took place, and it earns nothing. The big number in front is different in kind: it counts how many of that substance take part, and choosing five oxygen molecules instead of one changes the quantity while leaving oxygen exactly oxygen. Quantities are yours to choose; identities are given by the chemistry. That distinction is the single most examined idea in this topic.

***

**Thandi:** Next: why balance carbon and hydrogen before oxygen? Someone in class does oxygen first and says it works out anyway.

**Tutor:** It can work out, and it usually takes three times as long — the order is about efficiency, not legality. Look at where each element lives. In a burning-fuel equation, carbon appears in exactly one product, the carbon dioxide, and hydrogen appears in exactly one product, the water. Each can therefore be settled in a single move, and once settled, nothing you do afterwards disturbs it. Oxygen is different: it appears in BOTH products, so its total demand depends on how many carbon dioxides and how many waters you have already placed. Fix oxygen first and you have balanced it against numbers that are still going to change — then every later adjustment unbalances it again, and learners loop around the equation adjusting and re-adjusting until the time is gone. Settle the elements with one home each, let them fix the demand, then satisfy the element with two homes last. The rule generalises beyond fuels: balance the elements appearing in fewest places first, and leave the widely spread element for the end.

***

**Thandi:** This one is from someone who lost marks: he balanced it perfectly but left three and a half oxygen molecules in the answer. Why is that wrong if the atoms genuinely tally?

**Tutor:** The atoms do tally, which is why it earns partial credit rather than nothing — but the equation as written claims something physically impossible. A coefficient counts whole molecules, and half an oxygen molecule is not oxygen: split that pair and you have single oxygen atoms, a different and ferociously reactive species, not the gas in the air. So the fraction describes a reactant that does not exist. The remedy is simple and never wrong: multiply every coefficient in the equation by two, which clears the half while keeping all the ratios intact — for ethane that gives two ethane, seven oxygen, four carbon dioxide and six water, all whole. Then perform the mirror check, because convention wants the smallest whole numbers that work: look for a common factor, and if every coefficient could be halved, halve it. Clear the fractions, then reduce. Those two moves take ten seconds and protect the final mark.

***

**Thandi:** Last one: how do we know the products are carbon dioxide and water in the first place? The question tells us — but what if it doesn't?

**Tutor:** For complete combustion of a hydrocarbon you can predict them confidently, and the prediction rests on what the fuel is made of. A hydrocarbon contains only carbon and hydrogen; the reaction supplies oxygen; and conservation of mass says every atom must reappear on the right. Carbon with plenty of oxygen goes to carbon dioxide, hydrogen with oxygen goes to water — there is nowhere else for them to go and no fourth element in the mixture to make anything else. So "burns completely in oxygen" is effectively an instruction: write carbon dioxide and water, then balance. Two cautions worth carrying. First, INCOMPLETE combustion — a starved flame with too little oxygen — produces carbon monoxide and sometimes soot, which is unburnt carbon; that is a different equation, and it is exactly why a paraffin heater in a closed room is genuinely dangerous. Second, fuels containing other elements break the pattern: burn something containing sulfur and sulfur dioxide joins the products. The rule is reliable inside its conditions — hydrocarbon, complete combustion — and knowing where those conditions end is what separates understanding from memorisation.
