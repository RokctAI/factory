# Part 1 — Expert

This session builds the skill that turns a description of a reaction into a lawful statement of chemistry: balancing chemical equations. The exam problem carrying the work is the combustion of propane — C three H eight plus oxygen gives carbon dioxide plus water. One equation, three elements to track, and a strategy that makes the whole thing mechanical. Every stoichiometry calculation from next term until matric opens with a balanced equation, so today's method is permanent equipment.

## Subtopic: Why Equations Must Balance — Conservation of Mass

A chemical equation is a sentence: reactants on the left, an arrow for "react to form", products on the right. Propane plus oxygen gives carbon dioxide plus water: C3H8 plus O2, arrow, CO2 plus H2O. Every formula in that sentence is correct — and yet, as it stands, the sentence lies.

Count the atoms. Left: three carbons, eight hydrogens, two oxygens. Right: one carbon, two hydrogens, three oxygens. Two carbons have vanished; six hydrogens are missing; an oxygen has appeared from nowhere. The unbalanced equation claims atoms were created and destroyed.

And that claim breaks a law — the law of conservation of mass: in a chemical reaction, atoms are neither created nor destroyed; they are only REARRANGED. Every atom that enters on the left must leave on the right, wearing new molecular clothing but present and accounted for. The mass of the reactants equals the mass of the products, because the atoms are the same atoms.

Balancing is the act of fixing the COUNTS so the equation obeys the law. We adjust how MANY of each molecule participates until every element's atoms match across the arrow. When the counts agree, the equation stops lying and starts being chemistry.

Hold onto the picture of what combustion is doing here, because meaning makes memory: propane — braai gas — burns in oxygen; its carbons leave as carbon dioxide, its hydrogens leave as water vapour. The reaction rearranges every atom of the fuel into those two products. Our job is bookkeeping that rearrangement honestly.

Pause here — the questions for this section are with you now: the law stated precisely, and the atom count of the unbalanced equation used to show exactly where it lies.

## Subtopic: The One Commandment — Coefficients, Never Subscripts

Before balancing a single atom, learn the commandment that governs the whole craft: you may change only the COEFFICIENTS — the big numbers written in FRONT of formulas. You may NEVER touch the subscripts inside a formula.

Why so absolute? Because the two kinds of number do completely different jobs. A subscript defines a substance's IDENTITY: H2O — two hydrogens, one oxygen — is water. Change the subscript to H2O2 and you have not adjusted a count; you have manufactured a different substance — hydrogen peroxide, the antiseptic, not the drink. An equation "balanced" by editing subscripts describes a reaction that never happened.

A coefficient, by contrast, is a MULTIPLIER of the whole formula: three CO2 means three complete carbon-dioxide molecules — three carbons, six oxygens. Coefficients change amounts without touching identities. That is exactly the freedom balancing needs, and the only freedom it is allowed.

Practise reading coefficients against subscripts, because audits depend on it. Four H2O: four molecules, each with two hydrogens and one oxygen — eight hydrogens, four oxygens in total. The coefficient multiplies EVERY atom in the formula. Five O2: ten oxygen atoms. And a formula with no written coefficient carries an invisible one.

One more piece of strategy before the main event: in combustion equations, balance in the order carbon, then hydrogen, then oxygen — C, H, O. The reason: carbon and hydrogen each appear in exactly ONE product, so their coefficients settle immediately. Oxygen appears in BOTH products AND stands alone as O2 on the left — so its count depends on everything else. Balance the dependent element last, and the lone O2 lets you set it with a single coefficient that disturbs nothing. Balancing oxygen first is the classic tail-chase: every later fix breaks it again.

This section's questions are in front of you now: coefficient distinguished from subscript, a coefficient read across a whole formula, and the reason oxygen is left until last.

## Subtopic: Balancing the Propane Equation — C, then H, then O

Now the equation, worked with the discipline of the order.

Carbon first. Left: three carbons, locked inside C3H8. Right: each CO2 carries one. Three carbons need three CO2 — coefficient three. The equation reads: C3H8 plus O2 gives three CO2 plus H2O.

Hydrogen second. Left: eight hydrogens. Right: each water carries two. Eight over two — four waters. Coefficient four: C3H8 plus O2 gives three CO2 plus four H2O.

Oxygen last — and count the RIGHT side first, because that is where the demand lives. Three CO2 hold three times two — six oxygens. Four H2O hold four times one — four more. Total demand: ten oxygen atoms. The left supplies oxygen in pairs — O2. Ten atoms over two per molecule: five O2. Coefficient five.

The balanced equation: C3H8 plus five O2 gives three CO2 plus four H2O.

Now the audit — never optional, always the closing move. Carbon: left three; right, three CO2 — three. Match. Hydrogen: left eight; right, four waters times two — eight. Match. Oxygen: left, five O2 — ten; right, six in the carbon dioxides plus four in the waters — ten. Match. Three elements, three matches: the equation obeys conservation of mass, and you have PROVEN it, not assumed it.

Notice how the strategy carried the weight: carbon settled in one move because it lived in one product; hydrogen likewise; and oxygen, the element with fingers in every pie, waited until the pies were fixed and then balanced in a single clean step. Order was the difference between a solution that runs straight through and a frustrating loop.

Stop for this section's questions now — each coefficient justified in turn, and the closing audit run element by element until all three counts match.

## Subtopic: Fractions, Final Form, and the Road to Stoichiometry

Sometimes the oxygen step lands on an odd number — and produces a fraction. Balance ethane: C2H6 plus O2 gives CO2 plus H2O. Carbon: two CO2. Hydrogen: three H2O. Oxygen demand: four plus three — seven atoms — which is seven halves of O2. The equation balances with the coefficient seven over two — but Grade 10 convention demands whole numbers. The cure: double EVERY coefficient. Two C2H6 plus seven O2 gives four CO2 plus six H2O. Audit it: carbons four and four; hydrogens twelve and twelve; oxygens fourteen and fourteen. Whole numbers, smallest set, lawful.

That phrase — smallest set — is the final-form rule. Coefficients must be the smallest whole numbers that balance. Two C3H8 plus ten O2 gives six CO2 plus eight H2O is balanced but unfinished: every coefficient divides by two. Check your final line by asking: does any number divide them all? For one, five, three, four — nothing does. Final.

The error museum, four exhibits. Exhibit one — the capital crime: balancing by subscript, turning water into peroxide; identity theft, never legal. Exhibit two: forgetting that a coefficient multiplies the WHOLE formula — reading three CO2 as three carbons and two oxygens instead of three and six; audits die here. Exhibit three: oxygen first — the tail-chase; respect the C, H, O order. Exhibit four: leaving a fraction or an unsimplified set as the final answer; clear fractions by doubling, reduce to the smallest set.

The complete method, six steps. One: write correct formulae — they are locked from this moment; balancing never edits them. Two: count every element on both sides. Three: balance elements that appear in one place first — for combustion: carbon, then hydrogen. Four: balance the everywhere-element — oxygen — last, using the lone O2 to set the count in one move. Five: clear any fraction by doubling everything; reduce to the smallest whole-number set. Six: audit every element aloud — left count, right count, match — before moving on.

Where does this lead? Next term the coefficients become the star of the show: one, five, three, four turn into MOLE RATIOS — one mole of propane consumes five moles of oxygen and delivers three of carbon dioxide and four of water. Stoichiometry, the mass-and-mole arithmetic of Grade 11 and the engine of matric's Chemical Change section, reads its ratios straight off the balanced equation. Marking memos reserve the opening marks of nearly every chemistry calculation for the balanced equation itself. Six steps, one commandment, one audit — practise until the propane equation runs without hesitation, and those opening marks are yours for three years.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the fraction cleared by doubling, the smallest whole-number set identified, and the coefficients read as the ratios they will become.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same equation from the braai side of the fence, where the gas bottle, the fire and the smoke all have to add up.

## Subtopic: Nothing Goes Missing at the Braai

Light the gas braai and something looks like magic: the bottle empties, the flame roars, and nothing seems to be left behind. But nothing left, ever. Weigh the gas that went in, weigh the air the flame swallowed, and then catch every wisp of smoke and steam that came out — the two piles weigh exactly the same. Atoms cannot be burned away. They get taken apart and reassembled, like bricks from one wall built into another.

That is the only idea behind the whole topic. The gas in the bottle is propane: three carbons and eight hydrogens, holding hands. The air brings oxygen, which travels in pairs. The flame pulls the propane apart and hands every carbon to some oxygen — that pairing is the carbon dioxide going up in the heat haze. Every hydrogen also gets oxygen — and hydrogen with oxygen is water, which leaves as steam. Three carbons in, three carbons out. Eight hydrogens in, eight hydrogens out. Nobody escapes.

So when we write the reaction down and the two sides do not tally, it is not chemistry that is wrong — it is our counting. Read the first attempt honestly: propane plus oxygen gives carbon dioxide plus water. Left side: three carbons, eight hydrogens, two oxygens. Right side: one carbon, two hydrogens, three oxygens. Two carbons have gone missing, six hydrogens have gone missing, and an extra oxygen has appeared from nowhere — as if you put three loaves in the bag at the shop and pulled one out at home, plus a loaf you never paid for. Impossible.

Balancing is just fixing the count until both sides tally. The posh name for the rule is the law of conservation of mass, but the shop version says it better: what goes in the bag comes out of the bag.

Questions on this piece are with you right now, so take them slowly: what actually burns, what actually leaves, and where the first count goes wrong.

## Subtopic: Buy More Packets, Don't Repack Them

Here is the one rule that decides whether your balancing is legal — and it is a shopping rule.

At the shop you may buy more packets. Three packets of chips, five bottles of gas, four two-litre Cokes: nobody minds, because the packet itself stays exactly what it is. What you may NOT do is open a packet and change what is inside. A two-litre Coke with an extra litre poured in is not a two-litre Coke any more; it is a different product with a different label.

Chemistry has exactly those two numbers. The big number in front of a formula is the number of packets — five O2 means five packets of oxygen, each holding two atoms, so ten oxygen atoms. Change that number as much as you like; oxygen is still oxygen. The little number tucked inside a formula is part of the label — H2O is water precisely because it holds two hydrogens and one oxygen. Nudge that little number and you have not balanced anything, you have swapped the product: H2O2 is hydrogen peroxide, the stuff for cleaning a wound, not the stuff you drink. An equation "fixed" that way describes a reaction that never happened, and it earns nothing.

So: the big numbers in front are yours to change. The little numbers inside are locked. That is the entire law of the craft, and the fancy words for the two numbers are coefficient for the big one and subscript for the small one.

One counting habit goes with it: the big number multiplies everything in the packet. Four H2O is four packets, each with two hydrogens and one oxygen — so eight hydrogens and four oxygens, not four and four. Three CO2 is three carbons and six oxygens. And a formula with no big number in front is carrying an invisible one, the way a single loaf on the counter is still one loaf.

Get that habit right and half the marks in this topic are already safe. Your questions for this part are up now: which number you may touch, and what a big number does to every atom behind it.

## Subtopic: Easy Ones First, Then the Awkward One

When you divide up chores at home, you hand out the simple jobs first and leave the one that depends on everything else for last. Balancing works the same way, and the order for a burning fuel is carbon, then hydrogen, then oxygen.

Why that order? Carbon has only one place to go — the carbon dioxide. Hydrogen has only one place to go — the water. Each of them settles in one move and then stops bothering you. Oxygen is the awkward one: it turns up in the carbon dioxide AND in the water, and on the other side it arrives on its own in pairs. Sort oxygen first and every later change breaks it again — that is the loop that eats twenty minutes in a test.

So, propane. Three carbons on the left, one in each carbon dioxide, so you need three carbon dioxides. Eight hydrogens on the left, two in each water, so eight divided by two is four waters. Now count what those products are demanding in oxygen: three carbon dioxides carry two oxygens each, which is six, and four waters carry one each, which is four — ten oxygen atoms in all. Oxygen arrives in pairs, and ten divided by two is five pairs. Five oxygen packets.

The finished line: one propane plus five oxygen gives three carbon dioxide plus four water. Say it as a sentence and it stops being scary.

Then the last move, which is never optional — count both sides out loud. Carbons: three on the left, three on the right. Hydrogens: eight and eight. Oxygens: ten and ten. Three matches, and now you know rather than hope.

Two small things can still trip you. Sometimes the oxygen demand comes out odd — burning ethane needs seven oxygen atoms, which is three and a half pairs. You cannot buy half a bottle, so double the whole shopping list: two ethane, seven oxygen, four carbon dioxide, six water. And once everything is whole, check that no single number divides them all — if every coefficient can be halved, halve it, because the answer must be the smallest set that works.

That is the whole trade: fix the easy ones, leave the awkward one for last, clear any halves, count out loud. Here come the last questions of the lesson: the order defended, the propane numbers rebuilt from scratch, and a fraction cleared the honest way.
