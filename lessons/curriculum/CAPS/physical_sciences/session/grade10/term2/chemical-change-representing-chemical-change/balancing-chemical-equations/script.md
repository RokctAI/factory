Welcome, student. Today we learn to balance chemical equations — the skill that turns a description of a reaction into a lawful statement of chemistry. Our exam problem: balance the combustion of propane — C three H eight plus oxygen gives carbon dioxide plus water. One equation, three elements to track, and a strategy that makes the whole thing mechanical. Every stoichiometry calculation you will do from next term until matric opens with a balanced equation, so today's method is permanent equipment.

## Subtopic: Why Equations Must Balance — Conservation of Mass

A chemical equation is a sentence: reactants on the left, an arrow for "react to form", products on the right. Propane plus oxygen gives carbon dioxide plus water: C3H8 plus O2, arrow, CO2 plus H2O. Every formula in that sentence is correct — and yet, as it stands, the sentence lies.

Count the atoms. Left: three carbons, eight hydrogens, two oxygens. Right: one carbon, two hydrogens, three oxygens. Two carbons have vanished; six hydrogens are missing; an oxygen has appeared from nowhere. The unbalanced equation claims atoms were created and destroyed.

And that claim breaks a law — the law of conservation of mass: in a chemical reaction, atoms are neither created nor destroyed; they are only REARRANGED. Every atom that enters on the left must leave on the right, wearing new molecular clothing but present and accounted for. The mass of the reactants equals the mass of the products, because the atoms are the same atoms.

Balancing is the act of fixing the COUNTS so the equation obeys the law. We adjust how MANY of each molecule participates until every element's atoms match across the arrow. When the counts agree, the equation stops lying and starts being chemistry.

Hold onto the picture of what combustion is doing here, because meaning makes memory: propane — braai gas — burns in oxygen; its carbons leave as carbon dioxide, its hydrogens leave as water vapour. The reaction rearranges every atom of the fuel into those two products. Our job is bookkeeping that rearrangement honestly.

## Subtopic: The One Commandment — Coefficients, Never Subscripts

Before balancing a single atom, learn the commandment that governs the whole craft: you may change only the COEFFICIENTS — the big numbers written in FRONT of formulas. You may NEVER touch the subscripts inside a formula.

Why so absolute? Because the two kinds of number do completely different jobs. A subscript defines a substance's IDENTITY: H2O — two hydrogens, one oxygen — is water. Change the subscript to H2O2 and you have not adjusted a count; you have manufactured a different substance — hydrogen peroxide, the antiseptic, not the drink. An equation "balanced" by editing subscripts describes a reaction that never happened.

A coefficient, by contrast, is a MULTIPLIER of the whole formula: three CO2 means three complete carbon-dioxide molecules — three carbons, six oxygens. Coefficients change amounts without touching identities. That is exactly the freedom balancing needs, and the only freedom it is allowed.

Practise reading coefficients against subscripts, because audits depend on it. Four H2O: four molecules, each with two hydrogens and one oxygen — eight hydrogens, four oxygens in total. The coefficient multiplies EVERY atom in the formula. Five O2: ten oxygen atoms. And a formula with no written coefficient carries an invisible one.

One more piece of strategy before the main event: in combustion equations, balance in the order carbon, then hydrogen, then oxygen — C, H, O. The reason: carbon and hydrogen each appear in exactly ONE product, so their coefficients settle immediately. Oxygen appears in BOTH products AND stands alone as O2 on the left — so its count depends on everything else. Balance the dependent element last, and the lone O2 lets you set it with a single coefficient that disturbs nothing. Balancing oxygen first is the classic tail-chase: every later fix breaks it again.

## Subtopic: Balancing the Propane Equation — C, then H, then O

Now the equation, worked with the discipline of the order.

Carbon first. Left: three carbons, locked inside C3H8. Right: each CO2 carries one. Three carbons need three CO2 — coefficient three. The equation reads: C3H8 plus O2 gives three CO2 plus H2O.

Hydrogen second. Left: eight hydrogens. Right: each water carries two. Eight over two — four waters. Coefficient four: C3H8 plus O2 gives three CO2 plus four H2O.

Oxygen last — and count the RIGHT side first, because that is where the demand lives. Three CO2 hold three times two — six oxygens. Four H2O hold four times one — four more. Total demand: ten oxygen atoms. The left supplies oxygen in pairs — O2. Ten atoms over two per molecule: five O2. Coefficient five.

The balanced equation: C3H8 plus five O2 gives three CO2 plus four H2O.

Now the audit — never optional, always the closing move. Carbon: left three; right, three CO2 — three. Match. Hydrogen: left eight; right, four waters times two — eight. Match. Oxygen: left, five O2 — ten; right, six in the carbon dioxides plus four in the waters — ten. Match. Three elements, three matches: the equation obeys conservation of mass, and you have PROVEN it, not assumed it.

Notice how the strategy carried the weight: carbon settled in one move because it lived in one product; hydrogen likewise; and oxygen, the element with fingers in every pie, waited until the pies were fixed and then balanced in a single clean step. Order was the difference between a thirty-second solution and a frustrating loop.

## Subtopic: Fractions, Final Form, and the Road to Stoichiometry

Sometimes the oxygen step lands on an odd number — and produces a fraction. Balance ethane: C2H6 plus O2 gives CO2 plus H2O. Carbon: two CO2. Hydrogen: three H2O. Oxygen demand: four plus three — seven atoms — which is seven halves of O2. The equation balances with the coefficient seven over two — but Grade 10 convention demands whole numbers. The cure: double EVERY coefficient. Two C2H6 plus seven O2 gives four CO2 plus six H2O. Audit it: carbons four and four; hydrogens twelve and twelve; oxygens fourteen and fourteen. Whole numbers, smallest set, lawful.

That phrase — smallest set — is the final-form rule. Coefficients must be the smallest whole numbers that balance. Two C3H8 plus ten O2 gives six CO2 plus eight H2O is balanced but unfinished: every coefficient divides by two. Check your final line by asking: does any number divide them all? For one, five, three, four — nothing does. Final.

The error museum, four exhibits. Exhibit one — the capital crime: balancing by subscript, turning water into peroxide; identity theft, never legal. Exhibit two: forgetting that a coefficient multiplies the WHOLE formula — reading three CO2 as three carbons and two oxygens instead of three and six; audits die here. Exhibit three: oxygen first — the tail-chase; respect the C, H, O order. Exhibit four: leaving a fraction or an unsimplified set as the final answer; clear fractions by doubling, reduce to the smallest set.

The complete method, six steps. One: write correct formulae — they are locked from this moment; balancing never edits them. Two: count every element on both sides. Three: balance elements that appear in one place first — for combustion: carbon, then hydrogen. Four: balance the everywhere-element — oxygen — last, using the lone O2 to set the count in one move. Five: clear any fraction by doubling everything; reduce to the smallest whole-number set. Six: audit every element aloud — left count, right count, match — before moving on.

Where does this lead? Next term the coefficients become the star of the show: one, five, three, four turn into MOLE RATIOS — one mole of propane consumes five moles of oxygen and delivers three of carbon dioxide and four of water. Stoichiometry, the mass-and-mole arithmetic of Grade 11 and the engine of matric's Chemical Change section, reads its ratios straight off the balanced equation. Marking memos reserve the opening marks of nearly every chemistry calculation for the balanced equation itself. Six steps, one commandment, one audit — practise until the propane equation takes you ninety seconds, and those opening marks are yours for three years.

Class is dismissed.
