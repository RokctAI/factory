**Student:** Why does ammonium sulphate need brackets when calcium chloride doesn't? Both have a subscript 2!

**Thandi:** Look at what each 2 has to multiply! In CaCl2 the 2 sits on Cl — one single atom — so there's no confusion: two chlorines, done. But ammonium is a *team*, NH4, that acts as one ion. Write NH42SO4 and chemistry reads "42 hydrogens on one nitrogen"! The bracket in (NH4)2 says "this entire team, twice": two nitrogens AND eight hydrogens, identities intact. Rule of thumb: subscript on a single atom — no bracket. Subscript on a polyatomic team — brackets, always. The bracket is the team's jersey.

**Student:** How am I supposed to know iron is 3+ here? The periodic table doesn't give charges for the transition metals!

**Thandi:** Right — and that's exactly why the NAME does the job instead! Iron is two-faced: it forms Fe²⁺ and Fe³⁺, and both make real compounds — FeO and Fe2O3 are genuinely different substances. So chemists bake the charge into the name: iron(III) means "use Fe³⁺, please." The Roman numeral IS the charge — that's the entire code, nothing else to decode. So when you spot a Roman numeral, relax: the examiner just handed you the hardest ingredient for free. Your only job is the neutrality arithmetic afterwards.

**Student:** The crossover method gave me Mg2O2 for magnesium oxide and I wrote it down. Why was it marked wrong when the method is supposed to work?

**Thandi:** The crossover did its job — you just skipped its cleanup step! Crossing charges guarantees the charges cancel, but it doesn't promise the *smallest* ratio. Mg2O2 balances (+4 versus −4), but the ratio two-to-two is one-to-one wearing a coat: MgO says the same thing in lowest terms, and lowest terms is the convention formulas demand. So make it a two-beat move, always: cross over, then simplify. It only bites when the two charges share a factor — 2 and 2, or 2 and 4 — which is precisely when examiners love to ask it!

**Student:** What's the difference between sulphate and sulphide? I used S²⁻ for ammonium sulphate and everything fell apart.

**Thandi:** Ah, the "-ate/-ide" trap — it gets everyone once! The ending is a code: **-ide** means the simple, lone ion — sulphide is just S²⁻, chloride just Cl⁻, oxide just O²⁻. **-ate** means oxygen came along for the ride — sulphate is SO4²⁻, a whole team of one sulphur and four oxygens; nitrate is NO3⁻; carbonate CO3²⁻. So ammonium sulphate needs the SO4²⁻ team, giving (NH4)2SO4 — while ammonium sulphide would be (NH4)2S, a different compound. Before writing any formula, check the ending: -ide, lone atom ion; -ate, oxygen team from the data sheet.
