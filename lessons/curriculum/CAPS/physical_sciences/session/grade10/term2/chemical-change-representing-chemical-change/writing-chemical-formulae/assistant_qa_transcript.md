**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! The first one is about brackets: why does ammonium sulphate need them when calcium chloride doesn't? Both formulae have a subscript two in them.

**Tutor:** The difference is what each subscript is being asked to multiply. In CaCl2 the two sits on chlorine, and chlorine is one single atom — so "two" can only mean two chlorine atoms; there is nothing to misread. Ammonium is not one atom. It is a team of five — one nitrogen and four hydrogens — that reacts as a single ion with a single charge of one-plus. If the subscript two is placed directly after that team without brackets, the symbols read as N, H, forty-two, S, O, four: one nitrogen and forty-two hydrogens, which is not a substance. Doubling the hydrogens by hand instead — N H eight S O four — is equally wrong, because it leaves only one nitrogen where two are needed and the ammonium ion's identity is destroyed. Brackets solve both problems in one stroke: they say "this entire team, taken twice", so the subscript reaches every atom inside — two nitrogens and eight hydrogens. The working rule is short: subscript on a single atom, no brackets; subscript on a polyatomic ion, brackets every time.

**Thandi:** Next: how are we supposed to know iron is three-plus in iron(III) oxide? The periodic table doesn't give charges for the transition metals at all.

**Tutor:** It does not, and that is precisely why the NAME carries the information instead. Iron is multivalent: it forms an ion of two-plus and an ion of three-plus, and both build genuine compounds — iron two oxide and iron three oxide are different substances with different colours, different masses and different reactions. A name that said only "iron oxide" would therefore be ambiguous, so chemists build the charge into the name using a Roman numeral in brackets. Iron three means "use the three-plus ion" — the numeral IS the charge, stated as an instruction. It is not a count of atoms, which is the classic misreading: iron three oxide does not contain three irons; it contains iron that carries three-plus. From there the arithmetic is ordinary. Three-plus meets oxide at two-minus; the lowest common multiple of three and two is six; two irons supply plus six and three oxides supply minus six; the totals cancel and the compound is Fe2O3. So a Roman numeral in a question is good news: the examiner has handed you the hardest ingredient for free.

**Thandi:** This one caused real frustration: someone used the crossover method for magnesium oxide, got Mg2O2, and it was marked wrong. If the method is correct, why was the answer not?

**Tutor:** The method did its job — the cleanup step was skipped. Crossing the charges guarantees that the positive total and the negative total cancel; it does not guarantee that the ratio is in its lowest terms. Magnesium is two-plus and oxide is two-minus, so crossing gives two of each: Mg2O2. Audit it and the charges do cancel — plus four against minus four. But the ratio two-to-two is one-to-one wearing a coat, and chemical formulae are written in the smallest whole-number ratio, because the formula states the ratio in which the ions combine, not a batch size. So the correct answer is MgO. Make the crossover a two-beat move permanently: cross, then cancel. It only bites when the two charges share a common factor — two with two, or two with four — which is exactly where examiners set the trap. Calcium oxide is the same trap: two-plus with two-minus gives Ca2O2, which must be cancelled to CaO. Iron three oxide is safe, because three and two share no factor, so Fe2O3 stands as it is.

**Thandi:** Last one, and it broke somebody's whole answer: what is the actual difference between sulphate and sulphide? They used S two-minus for ammonium sulphate and everything after it collapsed.

**Tutor:** That ending is a code, and once you know it you never lose those marks again. The ending "-ide" signals a simple ion made of one kind of atom: sulphide is plain S at two-minus, chloride is Cl at one-minus, oxide is O at two-minus. The ending "-ate" signals a polyatomic ion with oxygen aboard: sulphate is S O four at two-minus, nitrate is N O three at one-minus, carbonate is C O three at two-minus, phosphate is P O four at three-minus. So ammonium sulphate needs the sulphate team — two ammoniums at plus one against one sulphate at minus two — giving ammonium bracketed and doubled, then S O four. Using plain sulphide instead builds ammonium sulphide, a completely different compound: two ammoniums against one S two-minus. The charges happen to work out either way, which is what makes the error so dangerous — nothing feels wrong while you are doing it. So make the ending the first thing you check when a name arrives: "-ide" means a lone atom from the periodic table; "-ate" means a team you look up on the data sheet.
