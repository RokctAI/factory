**Thandi:** Before you move on — the questions that come up a lot with this topic. First: dissolving confuses everyone. The salt disappears completely, so why is that not a new substance?

**Tutor:** Because disappearing from sight and ceasing to exist are two very different fates, and chemistry only cares about the second. When salt dissolves, the crystal's ions separate and spread out among the water molecules until the pieces are far too small to scatter light — that is why the solution looks clear. But run the inventory at particle level: every sodium ion still a sodium ion, every chloride ion still a chloride ion, every water molecule intact. Nothing new has been manufactured; the ingredients have only been shuffled together very finely. And the substance itself testifies: the solution tastes of salt, conducts like salt water, and — the decisive test — hand the water back to the sun and the identical salt crystals reappear, which is exactly how the coastal salt works harvest salt from seawater in evaporation ponds. A change you can undo by mere evaporation never made anything new. Contrast the impostor case: a fizzing headache tablet also vanishes into water, but it fizzes — carbon dioxide gas pouring out that was never in the tablet as gas. Evaporate that solution and you do not get the original tablet back. Same visual disappearance, opposite verdicts — because the verdict never comes from what your eyes lose track of, only from what exists before and after.

***

**Thandi:** Next: people want to use energy as the test — if it got hot, it was chemical. Why does that shortcut fail?

**Tutor:** It fails in both directions, which is what makes it such a dangerous shortcut. Direction one: plenty of physical changes move serious energy. Melting ice absorbs energy the whole time the block sits in your drink; freezing releases it; condensing steam releases so much that steam burns are worse than boiling-water burns. Energy in, energy out — and yet water, water, water throughout: no new substance, physical every time. Direction two: some chemical changes are thermally quiet. Rust creeps over a gate for months with no temperature drama at all, yet iron is steadily becoming a different substance. So heat is a clue, never a verdict. The correct role for energy in this topic is the labelling job: once you have already established that a change is chemical, you classify its energy direction — exothermic if the reaction releases energy, like cement warming itself as it sets, endothermic if it absorbs energy, like the reactions that turn a beaker cold. The classification question and the energy question are two separate questions asked in a fixed order: first, is there a new substance — that decides physical versus chemical; second, which way did the energy flow — that decides exothermic versus endothermic, and only applies once chemistry is confirmed. Keep the order and the shortcut never tempts you again.

***

**Thandi:** Then the boiling versus electrolysis pair: both start with water, both produce gas. Why does one count as physical and the other chemical?

**Tutor:** Because the gases are different substances, and the difference is testable. Boil water and the bubbles are steam — water molecules, complete and intact, that gained enough energy to leave the liquid. Cool that steam on any cold surface and it condenses straight back to water; molecule in, molecule out, nothing new. Physical. Now pass an electric current through water instead. The electrical energy does what heat cannot: it breaks the bonds INSIDE the water molecules, splitting H two O apart. At one electrode hydrogen gas collects; at the other, oxygen — two substances with properties water never had. Bring a glowing splint to the oxygen and it flares into flame; bring a flame to the hydrogen and it pops explosively. Try either test on steam and you get a wet splint — steam extinguishes rather than ignites. And there is no undoing electrolysis by cooling: hydrogen and oxygen sit in their tubes indefinitely, and recombining them requires a whole new chemical reaction, not a change of temperature. So the pair is the perfect drill for the deciding question. Same starting substance, gas produced in both — but boiling rearranges intact molecules while electrolysis rebuilds them into new ones. Bubbles are the surface; the substance inventory is the truth.

***

**Thandi:** Last one: why does the mass stay the same even when something burns away to almost nothing? The braai grid weighs so much less by morning.

**Tutor:** The mass all survives — it has simply left in forms a braai grid cannot hold. Burning is a regrouping: the carbon and hydrogen atoms of the wood combine with oxygen from the air, becoming carbon dioxide and water vapour, and those products drift away as invisible gas. Your morning weighing counts only the ash — the few solids that stayed put — and mourns the rest as destroyed. But run the experiment honestly, in a sealed container where nothing escapes, weighing everything before and everything after, including the air's oxygen on the input side of the books. The totals match exactly, every time. Atoms are never created or destroyed in a chemical change; every atom that entered the fire exists afterwards, wearing new molecular clothing. That is the law of conservation of mass, and notice it holds with equal force in physical changes — 50 grams of ice melts to 50 grams of water — so it is the one law that spans the entire topic. It is also the launchpad for the whole next topic: balancing chemical equations is nothing more than this law enforced with arithmetic — every atom on the left accounted for on the right. Answer the braai question well and you have already written the first sentence of the next chapter: mass is never lost; it is only relocated and regrouped.
