# Part 1 — Expert

Ice melts, and it is still water. Wood burns, and it is never wood again. Those two sentences hold the entire boundary between the physics and chemistry of matter — and this session patrols that boundary. The definitions of physical and chemical change, the particle-level story behind each, the observable evidence that a chemical change has happened, and the conservation laws that hold across both — plus the tricky cases examiners use to catch a memorised answer.

## Subtopic: Two Kinds of Change — the Deciding Question

Matter changes constantly — freezing, burning, dissolving, rusting, boiling, cooking. Chemistry sorts every one of those changes with a single deciding question: IS A NEW SUBSTANCE FORMED?

A PHYSICAL CHANGE is a change in which NO new substance forms. The substance may change state, shape, or size — ice to water, a sheet of paper torn to shreds, sugar disappearing into tea — but the particles themselves are unchanged. Melted ice is still H two O; torn paper is still paper; the sugar in sweet tea is still sugar, recoverable by evaporating the water. Because the particles survive intact, physical changes are usually EASY TO REVERSE: freeze the water, evaporate the tea.

A CHEMICAL CHANGE — also called a chemical reaction — is a change in which one or more NEW substances form, with properties DIFFERENT from the originals. Burning wood produces ash, carbon dioxide and water vapour — none of which is wood. Rusting iron produces iron oxide — a crumbly brown solid utterly unlike the grey metal. In a chemical change, bonds between atoms are BROKEN and NEW bonds are formed; the atoms regroup into different molecules. And because the original substance no longer exists, chemical changes are usually DIFFICULT OR IMPOSSIBLE to reverse by simple means: no freezer un-burns wood.

So the analysis of any change runs: name the substance before; name the substance after; compare. Same substance, physical. New substance, chemical. Every question in this topic is that comparison wearing different clothes. Take this section's questions now — the two definitions, and the deciding question applied cleanly.

## Subtopic: Physical Change Up Close — Same Particles, New Arrangement

Put a physical change under the particle microscope and one fact organises everything: the PARTICLES THEMSELVES DO NOT CHANGE — only their arrangement, spacing or energy changes.

Changes of STATE are the headline examples, straight from the kinetic molecular theory. Melting, evaporation, condensation, freezing, sublimation: in each, the water molecule — or iodine molecule, or carbon dioxide molecule — remains exactly itself. Boiling water does not split water into hydrogen and oxygen; it merely gives the intact H two O molecules enough energy to escape the liquid. Steam, water and ice are one substance in three costumes. That is why state changes reverse so cleanly: cool the steam and the same molecules settle back into liquid.

DISSOLVING is the second family. Stir salt into water and the crystal's ions separate and spread among the water molecules — but every sodium ion, every chloride ion, every water molecule survives unchanged. Proof: evaporate the water and the salt crystals return, chemically identical. Sea-salt production works this way along the West Coast — evaporation ponds running a physical change in reverse, at industrial scale.

Changes of SHAPE and SIZE are the third: tearing, cutting, grinding, bending, stretching. Grinding a copper sulfate crystal to powder makes it look different — but powder and crystal answer every chemical test identically, because the particles are the same.

Two footnotes for precision. First, mixing is generally physical — mixing iron filings with sulfur powder leaves both recoverable, a magnet pulling the iron straight out. Second, a physical change CAN involve energy — melting absorbs heat, freezing releases it — so an energy change alone does NOT prove chemistry happened. The proof must always be a new substance. ## Subtopic: Chemical Change Up Close — New Substances, and the Evidence

Chemical change is atoms REGROUPING: bonds break, atoms exchange partners, new molecules assemble. You cannot watch a bond break — so the syllabus arms you with the observable EVIDENCE that regrouping has happened. Five signs, each worth naming precisely.

One: a GAS is produced — bubbles or fizzing where no boiling is happening. Drop a piece of zinc into dilute acid and hydrogen gas streams up; vinegar on baking soda foams with carbon dioxide.

Two: a PRECIPITATE forms — an insoluble solid appearing when two clear solutions meet. Two transparent liquids combine and a cloudy solid snows out: new substance, beyond argument.

Three: a permanent COLOUR CHANGE — not dilution, but transformation. Grey iron turning brown-red as rust; a bright copper coin dulling to black copper oxide in a flame.

Four: a TEMPERATURE change without external heating or cooling — the reaction itself releases or absorbs energy. Cement mixing with water warms itself: bonds forming release energy. Some reactions turn their containers cold, drinking in energy instead. Energy released or absorbed — EXOTHERMIC or ENDOTHERMIC — is chemistry doing its accounting in heat.

Five: LIGHT or sound emitted — the flare of burning magnesium, the crack of a firework.

Everyday chemical changes to recognise on sight: combustion of any fuel; rusting; cooking an egg — the runny white turning solid and staying solid; milk souring; a battery discharging. Each is one-way traffic: the new substances will not return to the old by cooling, filtering or evaporating.

One caution to carry: any single sign can occasionally mislead — bubbles also appear in boiling, which is physical. The signs are evidence, and the verdict is always the same question: has a NEW substance appeared? Take the questions for this section now — five signs, named examples, and the verdict question.

## Subtopic: Mass, Energy and the Tricky Cases

Two conservation laws hold across BOTH kinds of change, and then the tricky cases test whether your definitions are real.

First: CONSERVATION OF MASS. In a physical change this feels obvious — melt 50 g of ice and you hold 50 g of water. But it holds equally in every chemical change: atoms are never created or destroyed, only regrouped, so the total mass of products equals the total mass of reactants. Burning seems to destroy mass only because the smoke and gases escape unweighed; trap them, and the books balance. This law is the foundation the next topic — balancing equations — is built on.

Second: ENERGY IS CONSERVED, but it moves. Physical changes move energy without making anything new: melting absorbs, freezing releases. Chemical changes move energy by breaking and forming bonds: burning wood releases stored chemical energy as heat and light; photosynthesis absorbs light energy into new chemical bonds. Direction of energy flow never decides the classification — only the new-substance question does.

Now the tricky cases. Boiling water versus ELECTROLYSIS of water: boiling gives steam — still water, physical. But pass an electric current through water and it splits into hydrogen gas and oxygen gas — two new substances, chemical. Same starting material, opposite verdicts. Dissolving salt: physical, the salt returns on evaporation. But a fizzing headache tablet in water is chemical — the fizz is new carbon dioxide that was never in the tablet as gas. Cooking: melting butter, physical; frying the egg beside it, chemical. And the candle, the classic double act: the wax MELTING at the top is physical — cooled, it hardens back to wax — while the wax BURNING at the wick is chemical, becoming carbon dioxide and water vapour that never return.

## Subtopic: The Ice Tray and the Braai Fire

Two Saturday scenes settle the whole topic.

Scene one: the ice tray. You pour water into the tray, freeze it, drop the blocks into cool drink, and they melt back to water. Freeze, melt, freeze, melt — around and around, as many times as you like, and at every stage it is WATER. Nothing new was ever made. The molecules never changed; they only huddled into a solid or loosened into a liquid. That is physical change: same stuff, different costume, and the costume comes off again. Tearing bread, grinding pepper, dissolving sugar — same family. The sugar seems to vanish into the tea, but taste it: still sugar, and boiling off the tea returns it, the way the salt pans on the coast get salt back out of seawater with nothing but sunshine.

Scene two: the braai fire. Wood goes in — solid, pale, splintery. Fire does its work, and what remains is grey ash, plus smoke and gases gone into the evening air. Now try to reverse it. Cool the ash overnight: still ash. Compress it, freeze it, soak it: ash. The wood is not hiding in there — it is GONE, rebuilt atom by atom into new substances with new properties. That is chemical change: the old stuff stops existing and new stuff takes its place, and there is no costume to take off because the actor has changed.

So the test you carry out of this lesson is one question: can I get the original back by simple means — cooling, evaporating, sieving? If yes, physical. If the original no longer exists, chemical. Ice tray or braai fire. Your questions on the two scenes are with you now.

## Subtopic: Clues at the Scene

You cannot see atoms regrouping — so you work like a detective: you read the clues a chemical change leaves at the scene. Five clues make the full kit.

Clue one: unexpected bubbles. Fizzing where nothing is boiling means a new gas is being born — vinegar on baking soda foaming, a headache tablet roaring in water. The gas was not in there before; something made it.

Clue two: a solid from nowhere. Two clear liquids meet and a cloudy solid snows down. You cannot filter that solid out of either original liquid, because it did not exist until they met.

Clue three: a colour that will not come back. The red-brown creeping over a neglected garden gate is not paint fading — it is iron becoming a different substance, rust. Scratch it: the change goes deep, and no cooling undoes it.

Clue four: heat you did not add, or cold you did not order. A wall of fresh cement warms itself for hours — the reaction is releasing energy. Some reactions do the opposite and turn an ice-cold beaker. Either way, energy moving on its own says bonds are breaking and forming.

Clue five: light from the change itself — the blinding white of a burning sparkler, not the glow of something merely hot.

One caution, detective: a single clue can mislead. Bubbles alone might just be boiling, and boiling is physical. So clues raise suspicion, but the conviction always needs the same verdict question: is there a NEW SUBSTANCE at the scene? The egg that went into the pan runny and came out solid — and stays solid forever — answers yes. Quick questions on the clue kit are up now.

## Subtopic: The Candle Doing Both at Once

One candle flame runs both kinds of change at the same time, a few millimetres apart — which is exactly why examiners love it.

Watch the top of the candle. The flame's heat MELTS the wax into a clear little pool. Tilt the candle and the pool dribbles down the side, cools, and hardens into wax again — you can peel it off and remould it. Melted, hardened, still wax: physical change, the ice tray in miniature.

Now watch the wick. The liquid wax climbing it meets the flame and BURNS — combines with oxygen from the air and becomes carbon dioxide and water vapour, both invisible, both gone into the room. That wax is not coming back. No freezer, no filter, no patience will ever reassemble it. Chemical change, the braai fire in miniature. And that is why the candle gets shorter night after night: the melted wax returns, the burnt wax never does.

Hold the pair together and you have the exam answer in two sentences: melting at the top — physical, no new substance, reversible by cooling. Burning at the wick — chemical, new substances formed, not reversible.

And one last dinner-table fact that ties the topic to the next one: even in the burning, not one atom was destroyed. Weigh everything — candle burnt, gases captured — and the mass balances exactly. Atoms regroup; they never vanish. That bookkeeping is the law of conservation of mass, and it is the exact rule you will use next, balancing chemical equations.

Last questions of the lesson are with you right now: the one deciding question, the five clues, and a candle doing both changes at once. Same stuff back — physical. New stuff made — chemical. That line runs through the whole of chemistry, and you can now draw it anywhere.
