**Thandi:** Time for the questions that come up a lot with this topic. First: volts and amps still blur together for many of us. What is the cleanest way to keep them apart for good?

**Tutor:** Attach each to its own question. Amps answer "how MUCH charge is passing per second?" — a counting rate, coulombs per second past a point, the traffic census of the circuit. Volts answer "how much ENERGY does each coulomb gain or spend between two points?" — joules per coulomb, the price tag. Notice the grammar the definitions force on you: current flows THROUGH a component, one point at a time, which is why an ammeter splices into the loop; potential difference exists ACROSS a component, between two points, which is why a voltmeter bridges it with two leads. One quantity, one preposition. And the pairing runs deep: I equals Q over delta t needs one location, V equals W over Q needs two. Test yourself with the torch bulb: half an amp through it means half a coulomb arrives every second; six volts across it means each of those coulombs spends six joules between entering and leaving. Say "through" and "across" out loud in every answer and the two quantities will never trade places again.

**Thandi:** Next: why is the terminal potential difference lower than the emf the moment the switch closes? Where do the missing volts go?

**Tutor:** They are spent inside the battery itself. A battery is not a perfect pump; its own chemical innards resist the current passing through them. With the switch open, no current flows, nothing is spent internally, and the terminals show the full promise — the emf, the open-circuit reading. Close the switch and every coulomb must now push through the battery's insides on its way out; a small share of each coulomb's energy is spent there, warming the battery, before the coulomb ever reaches the external circuit. The terminals then show what SURVIVES for the outside world — the terminal potential difference, slightly less than the emf. You have felt the evidence: a battery working hard grows warm, and a car's headlights dim for a moment while the starter motor draws its huge current, because the bigger the current, the bigger the internal spend. Grade 10 asks for the two definitions, which reads higher, and this qualitative reason; the machinery for calculating the internal loss arrives in Grade 12 under the name internal resistance.

**Thandi:** Third: if the electrons drift from negative to positive, why do we draw the current the other way? It feels like teaching a known mistake.

**Tutor:** It is a historical accident, kept deliberately, and completely harmless once you see why. The convention was fixed in the era before anyone knew which particles move in a metal: scientists chose to define current as flowing from positive to negative through the external circuit, and a century of physics, every formula and every diagram, was built on that choice. When the electron was discovered carrying negative charge — drifting the opposite way — there was a decision to make: relabel the entire edifice, or note the fact and keep the convention. Physics kept the convention, because it changes no answer to any question. Positive charge flowing clockwise and negative charge flowing anticlockwise are mathematically identical descriptions of the same transfer; every calculation of current, energy and resistance comes out the same either way. So the syllabus asks two things of you, and only two: draw and state current from the positive terminal, through the circuit, to the negative terminal — and know, if asked, that the electrons in the metal drift opposite to that arrow. Hold both facts and the "mistake" dissolves into bookkeeping.

**Thandi:** Last one: exam answers on why resistance rises with temperature keep losing marks for vagueness. What does a full-marks microscopic answer contain?

**Tutor:** Four ingredients, in a causal chain, each earning its own tick. Ingredient one, the setting: a metal is a lattice of positive ions bathed in free electrons, and current is those electrons drifting through the lattice. Ingredient two, the mechanism of resistance itself: the drifting electrons collide with the vibrating ions, and each collision transfers kinetic energy from electron to lattice, which appears as heat — resistance IS the effect of these collisions. Ingredient three, the change: raising the temperature makes the ions vibrate more violently, so the collisions become more frequent and each drifting electron makes less forward progress per second. Ingredient four, the conclusion: more collisions means greater opposition to the drift — the resistance increases. Vague answers say "the atoms get in the way more"; full-marks answers name the ions, name the vibration, name the collision, name the energy transfer, and only then conclude. The same chain, run in reverse, explains the other factors: a longer wire is more lattice to cross, a thicker wire is more parallel paths for the drift, and copper against nichrome is simply a lattice that interrupts the drift less against one that interrupts it more.
