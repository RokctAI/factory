# Part 1 — Expert

This session is the physics revision sweep for the final examination — the year's mechanics and electricity reorganised into the four toolkits that earn the marks: describing motion with graphs and equations, running the energy ledger, commanding charge and current, and working the two circuit patterns with a compass-point of magnetism attached. Nothing here is new; everything here is the exam-day version — compressed, ordered, and drilled through worked examples chosen to expose the classic traps.

## Subtopic: Motion — Graphs, Definitions and the Three Equations

Motion questions begin with vocabulary, and the vocabulary is pairs. Distance is the full length of the path travelled — a scalar, size only. Displacement is the straight line from start to finish with a direction attached — a vector. Speed is distance over time; velocity is displacement over time, direction included. Acceleration is the rate of change of velocity: a equals delta v over delta t.

Run the year's standard worked example. A car pulls away from rest and accelerates uniformly at 2 metres per second squared for 5 seconds. Final velocity: v equals u plus a t, which is zero plus 2 times 5, giving 10 metres per second. Displacement: delta x equals u t plus a half a t squared, which is zero plus a half times 2 times 25, giving 25 metres. Cross-check with the third equation: v squared equals u squared plus 2 a delta x — zero plus 2 times 2 times 25 is 100, and the square root of 100 is 10 metres per second. Two different equations, one answer: that agreement is your proof.

Now the graphs, because half the motion marks live there. On a position-time graph, the GRADIENT is the velocity: a straight slope means constant velocity, a curve means acceleration. On a velocity-time graph, the gradient is the acceleration, and the AREA between the graph and the time axis is the displacement. Check it on the car: the velocity-time graph is a straight line from zero to 10 over 5 seconds — a triangle whose area is a half times 5 times 10, which is 25 metres. The graph and the equation tell the same story, and the examiner loves asking for the story both ways.

The recognition drill: three equations, and the question's given information selects the right one. No displacement mentioned — use v equals u plus a t. No final velocity — use delta x equals u t plus a half a t squared. No time — use v squared equals u squared plus 2 a delta x. List the knowns first, and the equation chooses itself.

This section's questions are with you now — definitions paired, the car's numbers reproduced, and the gradient and area rules applied.

## Subtopic: Energy — the Ledger That Never Lies

Two energy accounts, one total. Gravitational potential energy is the energy an object has because of its position: E p equals m g h, with g at 9,8 metres per second squared. Kinetic energy is the energy of motion: E k equals a half m v squared. Mechanical energy is their sum, and the principle of conservation of mechanical energy states: in the absence of friction and air resistance, the mechanical energy of an object stays constant. In symbols: E k one plus E p one equals E k two plus E p two.

Work the ledger on a 3-kilogram ball dropped from 10 metres, ground as reference point. At the top: E p equals 3 times 9,8 times 10, which is 294 joules; E k is zero; total 294 joules — sealed for the whole fall. Halfway down, at 5 metres: E p equals 3 times 9,8 times 5, which is 147 joules, so E k must be the difference, 147 joules. Extract the speed: a half times 3 times v squared equals 147, so v squared equals 98, and v equals 9,90 metres per second, to two decimal places. At the ground: E p is zero, E k is the full 294 joules, so v squared equals 196 and v equals 14 metres per second.

Now the observation the examiners adore. Set a half m v squared equal to m g h and the mass cancels: v squared equals 2 g h. The landing speed of a freely falling object does not depend on its mass — a 3-kilogram ball and a 30-kilogram ball from 10 metres both arrive at 14 metres per second. Heavier means more energy, but also more energy needed for the same speed, and the two effects cancel exactly.

The fine print is a mark every year: mechanical energy is conserved ONLY while no friction or air resistance acts. The phrases "ignore air resistance" and "frictionless" are your licence for the equation — underline them. With friction present, total energy is still conserved, but mechanical energy leaks away as heat.

Take this section's questions now — both formulas, the three-snapshot ledger, the cancelled mass, and the exact condition on the word conserved.

## Subtopic: Electricity — Charge, Current and Potential Difference

Electricity starts with charge. Two kinds, positive and negative; unit the coulomb. Charge obeys two unbreakable rules. Conservation: charge is never created or destroyed, only transferred. Quantisation: charge comes in whole-number multiples of the charge on one electron, 1,6 times 10 to the minus 19 coulombs. The formula Q equals n q converts between total charge and particle count: a sphere carrying minus 8 times 10 to the minus 19 coulombs holds 8 over 1,6, which is 5 excess electrons.

Conservation runs the classic identical-spheres question. Two identical conducting spheres, one at plus 6 nanocoulombs and one at minus 2 nanocoulombs, touch and separate. Total charge: plus 6 plus minus 2, which is plus 4 nanocoulombs; identical spheres share equally, so each carries plus 2 nanocoulombs. Total before, plus 4; total after, plus 4 — conservation, audited.

Now set the charge moving. Current, symbol I, is the rate of flow of charge: I equals Q over delta t, and one ampere is one coulomb per second. If 12 coulombs pass a point in 4 seconds, the current is 3 amperes. Conventional current runs from the positive terminal around the external circuit to the negative, even though the electrons drift the other way.

Potential difference, symbol V, is the work done per unit charge: V equals W over Q, in volts, and one volt is one joule per coulomb. If 24 joules are transferred by 2 coulombs passing through a resistor, the potential difference across it is 12 volts. The emf of a battery is the same quantity measured at the source. And the instrument rules: an ammeter goes in SERIES, because the charge must flow through it; a voltmeter goes in PARALLEL across a component, because it compares the energy on either side.

This section's questions are ready — Q equals n q both ways, the spheres shared and audited, and current and potential difference defined word for word.

## Subtopic: Series, Parallel and the Magnetic Field

Ohm's law links the three circuit quantities: V equals I R — potential difference equals current times resistance. Then every circuit is one of two patterns, and the rules come in mirrored pairs.

Series: one loop, no junctions. The current is the SAME everywhere; the potential differences ADD up to the emf; resistances add: R series equals R one plus R two. Work it: a 12-volt battery with a 4-ohm and an 8-ohm resistor in series. Total resistance 12 ohms; current I equals 12 over 12, which is 1 ampere through both. Potential differences: 1 times 4 is 4 volts, and 1 times 8 is 8 volts — and 4 plus 8 returns the 12 of the battery. That closing audit line is a mark.

Parallel: side by side across the battery. The potential difference is the SAME across each branch — the full 12 volts; the currents ADD at the junction. Branch currents: 12 over 4 is 3 amperes, and 12 over 8 is 1,5 amperes; total from the battery, 4,5 amperes. Effective resistance: R equals V over I, which is 12 over 4,5, giving 2,67 ohms — SMALLER than the smallest branch, always, because a second path helps the charge through. Same components, different pattern, completely different numbers: that comparison is the whole topic.

Magnetism closes the year in three facts. A magnet has two poles, north and south, and like poles repel while unlike poles attract. The magnetic field is the region where the magnet exerts a force, drawn as field lines that run from north to south outside the magnet and never cross. The Earth itself has a magnetic field, which is why a compass needle settles pointing north — and why a compass placed near a bar magnet swings to follow the local field lines instead.

The final questions of this part are with you now — the mirrored rule pairs kept apart, both circuits fully solved, and the field-line facts stated.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same revision with the physics jacket off — three pictures that carry every method: a road-trip logbook, a two-pocket wallet, and water in pipes.

## Subtopic: The Logbook, the Wallet and the Water Pipe

Picture one: motion is a road-trip logbook. The odometer records distance — every winding kilometre, no direction, a scalar. The straight arrow from home to the destination is displacement — a vector. Speed is what the odometer says per hour; velocity is the arrow per hour. And acceleration is the speedometer needle MOVING: 2 metres per second squared means the reading grows by 2 metres per second, every second. From rest that is 2, then 4, then 6, then 8, then 10 after five seconds — exactly the car from earlier, no formula needed yet.

Picture two: energy is a wallet with two pockets. The height pocket holds m g h; the speed pocket holds a half m v squared. As long as nothing rubs — no friction, no air resistance — the wallet is sealed: coins move between pockets but the total never changes. Drop the 3-kilogram ball and 294 joules stream from the height pocket into the speed pocket all the way down: 294 plus zero, then 147 plus 147, then zero plus 294. If the two pockets ever fail to add to the sealed total, an arithmetic slip is hiding, and the count finds it before the marker does.

Picture three: a circuit is water in pipes. The battery is the pump, and its emf is the pressure it provides. Current is the flow of water; resistance is a narrow section of pipe; potential difference across a component is the pressure drop from its one side to the other. The water is not used up — it circulates — but the pressure the pump provides is spent pushing through the narrow sections. That is the whole reason charge is never consumed while energy is.

Your questions for this section are ready — one picture matched to each topic, and the sealed wallet counted at three heights.

## Subtopic: Series and Parallel Without Tears

Run the water picture into the two circuit patterns, because it makes the mirrored rules impossible to swap.

Series is one narrow pipe after another on a single loop. The same water flows through both narrow sections — so the current is the same everywhere; there is nowhere else for it to go. Each narrow section takes its own bite of the pump's pressure, and the bites add up to the full pressure — potential differences add to the emf. And two narrow sections back to back are harder to push through than one — resistances add, the total current shrinks.

Parallel is a fork in the pipe: two channels side by side, rejoining after. Each channel feels the pump's FULL pressure across it — potential difference the same on every branch. The water divides between the channels, more through the wider one, and the streams add back together at the junction — currents add. And here is the surprise worth saying twice: opening a second channel makes the total flow EASIER, so the combined resistance is smaller than either branch alone. Two open doors pass more people than one, even if the second door is narrow.

Now the two classic exam moments. Moment one: a bulb in series is added to another — both go dimmer, because total resistance rose and the shared current fell. Moment two: a bulb is added in parallel — the original bulb keeps its brightness, because it still feels the full emf, but the battery now supplies more total current and drains faster. Reason it through pressure and flow, and the answer writes itself.

Take this section's questions now — the fork against the single loop, and both bulb moments explained through the pipes.

## Subtopic: The Trap List and the Final Check

Revision is mostly about the traps, because in the examination the physics is familiar but the traps are fresh. Five exhibits, each with its antidote.

Trap one: using 10 for g out of habit. CAPS uses 9,8 metres per second squared — 294 joules becomes a wrong 300, and the mark leaves with it. Antidote: write g equals 9,8 at the top of every energy and motion solution.

Trap two: forgetting to square the speed in kinetic energy, or dropping the half. A half times 3 times 14 squared is 294 — not 21, not 588. Antidote: say the formula aloud as you substitute: a half, times the mass, times the speed, squared.

Trap three: swapping the series and parallel rules. Antidote: one question before every circuit — is there a junction? No junction means series, same current, potential differences add. A junction means parallel, same potential difference, currents add.

Trap four: treating scalars like vectors and vectors like scalars — quoting a displacement without direction, or adding a direction to energy. Antidote: label each quantity scalar or vector before writing its answer line.

Trap five: the misplaced meter — an ammeter wired in parallel or a voltmeter in series. Antidote: the ammeter is a turnstile, the flow must pass through it; the voltmeter is a comparer, it stands across.

And the last habit, the one that converts near-marks into full marks: check by a second route. Displacement from the equation, then again as the area under the velocity-time graph. Speed from the energy ledger, then the wallet audit at every snapshot. Series potential differences summed back to the emf, parallel branch currents summed back to the battery. Every toolkit in this lesson carries its own proof, and the proof is free.

The final questions of the lesson are with you now — five traps, five antidotes, and every answer checked by its second route.
