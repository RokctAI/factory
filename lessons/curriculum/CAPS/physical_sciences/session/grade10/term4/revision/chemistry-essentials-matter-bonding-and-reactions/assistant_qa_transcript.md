**Thandi:** The revision questions that come up every year, straight at them. First: the periodic table is enormous — what is the smallest slice of it worth actually memorising for the examination?

**Tutor:** Far less than most learners fear, because the table is designed to be READ, not memorised. Own three things. First, the geography: columns are groups sharing valence electrons and chemistry, rows are periods filling one more energy level — and the four named families, alkali metals in group 1, halogens in group 17, noble gases in group 18, transition metals in the middle block. Second, the two trends with their single shared reason: across a period the nuclear charge grows while the shell count stays fixed, so atoms shrink and electronegativity climbs; down a group a whole new shell is added, so atoms grow and electronegativity falls. Third, the reading skills: pull an element's proton count from its atomic number, its electron configuration from its position, and its ion charge from its group — group 2 gives 2 plus, group 17 gives 1 minus. Everything else — molar masses, exact values — the examination paper's table supplies. The skill being marked is navigation, not recall, and a little practice at location questions pays far better than a mountain of memorising.

***

**Thandi:** Next, bonding: learners can define all three bonds and still freeze when a question gives a mystery substance's properties and asks for the bond. What is the method?

**Tutor:** Work backwards through the one property that cannot lie: conduction, asked three ways. Does it conduct as a solid? Only a metallic sea of free electrons manages that — metallic bond, done. Does it conduct only when molten or dissolved in water? Then charged particles exist but are locked in place until freed — that is an ionic lattice, and the high melting point will agree. Does it never conduct at all, solid or liquid or dissolved? Then there are no charged particles anywhere — covalent molecules, and the low melting point will agree. Notice the logic: each answer is not a memorised pairing but a deduction from what carries current — free electrons, freed ions, or nothing. Run melting point second as confirmation, never first, because it only says high or low while conduction says why. And in the written answer, give the reason with the name: "ionic, because it conducts only when the ions are free to move" earns the reasoning mark that the bare word "ionic" leaves behind. Property to particle to bond — three steps, every mystery substance solved.

***

**Thandi:** The balancing panic: some equations balance in seconds, then one refuses, the numbers chase each other in circles, and time disappears. Is there an order that always works?

**Tutor:** There is, and it is the reverse of what instinct suggests: balance the LONELIEST element last, not first. Oxygen and hydrogen usually appear in several substances on each side, so fixing them early means every later coefficient disturbs them again — that is the chasing-in-circles feeling. Start instead with an element that appears in exactly one formula on each side: in methane's combustion, carbon first, one to one; then hydrogen, four left demanding a 2 before the water; oxygen last, count the right side — two in the carbon dioxide, two in the waters, four total — and a single 2 before O 2 closes the books in one move, because nothing else contains oxygen alone. Two more disciplines complete the method. Never touch a subscript — the moment H 2 O becomes H 2 O 2 you are balancing a different universe. And always run the closing audit aloud, element by element, both sides — it costs almost nothing and catches nearly every slip. Loneliest last, subscripts sacred, audit aloud: no equation in the paper resists that order.

***

**Thandi:** Last one: any advice for structuring the chemistry revision itself — what actually moves marks in these final weeks?

**Tutor:** Three shifts, in order of payoff. First, drill the conversions until they are reflexes, because the mole sits inside more than half of Paper 2's calculation marks: grams to moles, moles to concentration, moles to gas volume at STP — one triangle of formulas, one central currency, and every route passes through moles. A short set of mixed conversions, done often, outperforms any amount of rereading. Second, rebuild the year's equations from their reaction TYPES rather than from memory: combustion, synthesis, precipitation — write the reactants, predict the products, balance, audit. That practice covers formula writing, the diatomic seven and balancing in a single exercise. Third, keep a one-page trap list in your own handwriting — subscripts never change, volumes convert to cubic decimetres before concentration, equation ratios speak moles not grams, dissolving is physical — and read it before every practice session, then confirm each trap was dodged after. Reflex conversions, type-driven equations, a personal trap list: those three carry more marks than any amount of passive summarising, and they fit comfortably into the weeks that remain.
