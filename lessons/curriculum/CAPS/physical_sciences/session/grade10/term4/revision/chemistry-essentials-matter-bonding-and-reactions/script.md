# Part 1 — Expert

This session is the chemistry revision sweep for the final examination — the year's matter and chemical change reorganised into the four toolkits that earn the marks: reading the atom and the periodic table, choosing the right bond, representing reactions correctly, and counting with the mole all the way into solution. Nothing here is new; everything here is the exam-day version — compressed, ordered, and drilled through worked examples chosen to expose the classic traps.

## Subtopic: The Atom and the Periodic Table

Every atom is described by two numbers. The atomic number is the number of protons — it defines the element and never changes. The mass number is protons plus neutrons. Electrons match the protons in a neutral atom. Isotopes are atoms of the SAME element with DIFFERENT numbers of neutrons: chlorine-35 has 17 protons and 18 neutrons; chlorine-37 has 17 protons and 20 neutrons. Same chemistry, different mass — and the relative atomic mass on the periodic table, 35,5 for chlorine, is the weighted average: roughly three-quarters of natural chlorine is chlorine-35 and one quarter is chlorine-37, and a quick check confirms it — 0,75 times 35 plus 0,25 times 37 is 26,25 plus 9,25, which is 35,5.

Electrons live in orbitals, written in s p notation. Sodium, eleven electrons: 1 s 2, 2 s 2, 2 p 6, 3 s 1. Chlorine, seventeen: 1 s 2, 2 s 2, 2 p 6, 3 s 2, 3 p 5. The outermost electrons — the valence electrons — do all the chemistry: sodium has one, chlorine has seven, and that pair of facts is the whole story of table salt, coming in the next subtopic.

The periodic table is the map. Columns are groups — elements with the same number of valence electrons and therefore the same chemistry: group 1 the alkali metals, group 17 the halogens, group 18 the noble gases with full outer shells and almost no reactions. Rows are periods — each row fills one more energy level. And the two trends the examiners test: moving ACROSS a period, atoms get smaller and electronegativity rises; moving DOWN a group, atoms get bigger and electronegativity falls.

This section's questions are with you now — the two defining numbers, the isotope average checked, both configurations written, and the trends run in both directions.

## Subtopic: Bonding — Ionic, Covalent and Metallic

Three bonds, and the periodic table's two sides decide which one forms. Metal with non-metal: ionic. Non-metal with non-metal: covalent. Metal with metal: metallic.

Ionic bonding is TRANSFER. Sodium gives its single valence electron to chlorine; sodium becomes the positive ion N a plus, chlorine becomes the negative ion C l minus, and the two opposite charges attract into a crystal lattice. Formulae follow from balancing charge, and the swap rule does the work: calcium is 2 plus and chloride is 1 minus, so calcium chloride needs two chlorides — C a C l 2. Aluminium at 3 plus with oxide at 2 minus crosses over to A l 2 O 3: two aluminiums supply plus 6, three oxides supply minus 6, and the compound is neutral — always audit the charges to zero.

Covalent bonding is SHARING. Two non-metals both want electrons, so they share pairs: two hydrogens each share with one oxygen in H 2 O; carbon shares two pairs with each oxygen in C O 2, a double bond each side. And seven elements never travel alone — hydrogen, nitrogen, oxygen, fluorine, chlorine, bromine, iodine are DIATOMIC: written H 2, N 2, O 2 and so on whenever they appear as free elements.

Metallic bonding is POOLING. Positive metal kernels sit in a lattice, and the valence electrons detach into a shared sea around them. The sea explains the properties in one breath: free electrons carry current, so metals conduct as solids; layers of kernels slide without breaking the bond, so metals are malleable.

The properties table follows the bonds. Ionic compounds: high melting points, and they conduct only when molten or dissolved — the ions must be free to move. Covalent molecular substances: low melting points, no conduction — no charged particles at all. Metals: conduct always. Given a mystery substance's properties, name the bond; given the bond, predict the properties — the question runs both ways.

Take this section's questions now — the metal and non-metal decision made, two formulae built by charge balance, the diatomic seven recited, and each property traced to its bond.

## Subtopic: Representing Reactions — Change, Formulae and Balancing

First, the year's most quietly examined distinction. A physical change makes NO new substance: ice melting is still water, and the change reverses easily. A chemical change makes NEW substances with new properties: magnesium burning becomes magnesium oxide, and energy is taken in or given off. In BOTH kinds of change, total mass is conserved — atoms are never created or destroyed, only rearranged. That is the law of conservation of mass, and it is the reason balancing works at all.

A chemical equation is the sentence that records the rearrangement. Reactants on the left, products on the right, and state symbols in brackets: s for solid, l for liquid, g for gas, a q for dissolved in water.

Balancing has one absolute law: change COEFFICIENTS, never subscripts. The subscripts are the substance's identity — rewrite H 2 O as H 2 O 2 and water has become hydrogen peroxide, a different chemical entirely. Balance methane burning. Unbalanced: C H 4 plus O 2 gives C O 2 plus H 2 O. Carbon is fine, one each side. Hydrogen: four on the left, two on the right — put a 2 before the water. Now oxygen: two in the carbon dioxide plus two in the waters makes four on the right, so put a 2 before the O 2. Balanced: C H 4 plus 2 O 2 gives C O 2 plus 2 H 2 O. Audit every element: one carbon each side; four hydrogens each side; four oxygens each side. That audit line is the mark-saver — run it every time.

One more, the term's classic: magnesium in oxygen. Unbalanced: M g plus O 2 gives M g O. Oxygen: two left, one right — 2 M g O. Now magnesium: one left, two right — 2 M g. Balanced: 2 M g plus O 2 gives 2 M g O. Two magnesiums, two oxygens, each side. Balanced equations are the grammar of the subject, and every calculation in the next subtopic reads its ratios straight off them.

This section's questions are ready — the two changes told apart, state symbols placed, both equations balanced, and every audit spoken aloud.

## Subtopic: The Mole and Reactions in Solution

The mole is the chemist's counting unit: one mole is 6,02 times 10 to the 23 particles — Avogadro's number. Three formulas convert between the exam's currencies. Mass to moles: n equals m over M, where M is the molar mass read off the periodic table. Solution: c equals n over V — concentration in moles per cubic decimetre. Gas at STP: one mole of any gas occupies 22,4 cubic decimetres.

Run each one. Water's molar mass: two hydrogens at 1 plus one oxygen at 16, giving 18 grams per mole; so 36 grams of water is 36 over 18, which is 2 moles. Calcium carbonate, C a C O 3: 40 plus 12 plus three sixteens, which is 40 plus 12 plus 48, giving 100 grams per mole; 25 grams is 0,25 moles. Solution: 0,2 moles of salt dissolved to make 0,5 cubic decimetres gives c equals 0,2 over 0,5, which is 0,4 moles per cubic decimetre. Gas: 0,5 moles at STP occupies 0,5 times 22,4, which is 11,2 cubic decimetres. Four conversions, one hub — everything passes through moles.

Now take the chemistry into water, the term's closing topic. Ionic compounds that dissolve DISSOCIATE into free ions: sodium chloride splits into N a plus and C l minus, and the free ions make the solution an ELECTROLYTE — it conducts. Sugar dissolves without splitting into ions, so sugar water does not conduct. And when two solutions meet and a pair of ions insoluble together find each other, a PRECIPITATE drops out: silver nitrate meets sodium chloride, and silver chloride appears as a white solid — A g N O 3 aqueous plus N a C l aqueous gives A g C l solid plus N a N O 3 aqueous. The silver and chloride ions paired up and left the water; the sodium and nitrate ions stayed dissolved as spectators.

The final questions of this part are with you now — all four conversions run, the electrolyte explained by free ions, and the precipitation equation written with its state symbols.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same revision with the lab coat off — a box of building bricks, a parts catalogue, and a kitchen scale that counts by weighing.

## Subtopic: Bricks, the Catalogue and Three Ways to Click

Picture every atom as a building brick, and the periodic table as the parts catalogue. The catalogue is not alphabetical — it is organised by BEHAVIOUR. Each column is a family of bricks that click the same way, because they carry the same number of connection points: the valence electrons. Group 1 bricks have one point, group 17 bricks are one point short of full, group 18 bricks are complete and click with nothing — which is exactly why the noble gases sit out the whole subject.

The brick's serial number is the atomic number — the proton count, the one thing that never changes. Isotopes are the same brick in a slightly heavier batch: same clicks, same chemistry, extra neutrons in the core. The catalogue's mass, like chlorine's 35,5, is just the average across the batches in the box.

And there are exactly three ways bricks click together. Way one, the swap: a metal brick with a spare point meets a non-metal brick with a gap, the electron changes owner, and the two now-charged bricks snap together — ionic. Way two, the share: two non-metal bricks both short of full lock a pair of electrons between them like two hands on one handle — covalent. Way three, the pool: metal bricks pile into a stack and tip all their spare electrons into a shared sea that glues the whole stack — metallic.

The properties follow the click. Swapped bricks build hard crystals that only conduct when the ions are set free by melting or dissolving. Shared bricks build small separate molecules that melt easily and never conduct. Pooled bricks conduct always, because the sea is always free. Name the click, and the properties recite themselves.

Your questions for this section are ready — the catalogue read by column, the three clicks told apart, and one property traced back to each.

## Subtopic: Counting Eggs by Weighing the Crate

Chemists have a counting problem: atoms are too small to count one by one, but reactions happen atom by atom. The solution is the oldest trick in any kitchen — count by weighing. If one egg weighs 60 grams, then 720 grams of eggs is 12 eggs. No counting happened; the scale did it.

The mole is the chemist's dozen — just absurdly larger: 6,02 times 10 to the 23 particles. And the periodic table is the price list that makes the trick work: it tells you what one mole of each element weighs. Carbon, 12 grams per mole. Oxygen, 16. So one mole of water — two hydrogens and an oxygen — weighs 1 plus 1 plus 16, which is 18 grams. Put 36 grams of water on the scale and you KNOW, without counting, that you hold 2 moles: more than a trillion trillion molecules, counted by a kitchen scale.

Every mole formula is this same trick pointed at a different measuring tool. Weighing solids: n equals m over M — the scale. Solutions: c equals n over V — the measuring jug, moles per cubic decimetre. Gases at STP: 22,4 cubic decimetres per mole — the balloon. Three tools, one currency, and every exam question is a currency exchange: grams to moles, moles to concentration, moles to gas volume, always THROUGH the mole, never directly.

The exchange discipline: convert into moles first, do the chemistry, convert out last. A question that mixes grams on one side and cubic decimetres on the other is not hard — it is two exchanges with a mole step in the middle.

Take this section's questions now — one egg-crate count, one exchange in each direction, and the mole standing in the middle of all of them.

## Subtopic: The Trap List and the Final Audit

Revision is mostly about the traps, because in the examination the chemistry is familiar but the traps are fresh. Five exhibits, each with its antidote.

Trap one: balancing by changing subscripts. Turning H 2 O into H 2 O 2 to make oxygen balance does not balance water — it invents hydrogen peroxide. Antidote: subscripts are identity, coefficients are quantity; only coefficients may move.

Trap two: forgetting the diatomic seven. Oxygen released in a reaction is O 2, not O; hydrogen gas is H 2. Antidote: recite the seven — hydrogen, nitrogen, oxygen, fluorine, chlorine, bromine, iodine — whenever a free element enters an equation.

Trap three: the units of volume. Concentration wants cubic decimetres, and the question gives 500 cubic centimetres. Divide by 1000: 0,5 cubic decimetres. Antidote: convert volume before touching c equals n over V, every single time.

Trap four: mixing mass with moles mid-calculation — dividing 25 grams by a coefficient from the equation. The equation's ratios speak MOLES, never grams. Antidote: the exchange discipline — into moles, chemistry, out of moles.

Trap five: calling every dissolving a chemical change. Salt dissolving is physical — the ions separate but no new substance forms; the salt returns when the water evaporates. Antidote: ask the only question that matters — is there a NEW substance?

And the final audit, the habit that converts near-marks into full marks. Balanced an equation? Count every element on both sides, out loud. Computed moles? Check the units cancelled to leave the unit you wanted. Predicted a precipitate? Name the two spectator ions still swimming. Every toolkit in this lesson carries its own proof, and the proof is free.

The final questions of the lesson are with you now — five traps, five antidotes, and every answer audited before it leaves your desk.
