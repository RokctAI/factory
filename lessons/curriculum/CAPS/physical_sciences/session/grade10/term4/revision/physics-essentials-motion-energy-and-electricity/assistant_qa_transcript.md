**Thandi:** Revision brings the same worries every year, so let's take them head on. First: three equations of motion, one question — how do we pick the right equation fast, without trying all three?

**Tutor:** Let the missing quantity choose for you, because each equation is missing exactly one of the four movement quantities. Write the knowns down the margin first — initial velocity, acceleration, time, displacement, final velocity — and mark what the question gives and what it wants. If displacement appears nowhere, use v equals u plus a t. If final velocity appears nowhere, use delta x equals u t plus a half a t squared. If time appears nowhere, use v squared equals u squared plus 2 a delta x. That is the whole selection method, and it is mechanical — no judgement required. The margin list also protects you from the two classic substitution slips: a car "starting from rest" means u is zero, and a direction chosen as positive at the start must hold for every quantity to the end. Candidates who lose motion marks are rarely weak at algebra; they are substituting before listing, and the wrong equation costs triple the time the list would have.

**Thandi:** Next, the energy ledger: if the potential energy keeps changing and the kinetic energy keeps changing during a fall, what exactly are we allowed to trust?

**Tutor:** Trust the sum, and only the sum — that is the entire principle. At every instant of a frictionless fall, whatever joules the height account loses, the speed account gains, cent for cent, so the mechanical total is a fixed number you compute ONCE, at whichever position is easiest. That is why the method always starts at the top: a 3-kilogram ball at rest 10 metres up holds 294 joules, and that number is now locked for the whole journey. Any later question — kinetic energy at 5 metres, speed at the ground — is answered by asking where the 294 joules are sitting, never by starting a fresh calculation. And the licence matters: the phrase "ignore air resistance" is what seals the total. If a question says the surface is rough, the mechanical total is no longer constant — energy still exists, but some has left as heat, and the correct statement is that mechanical energy decreased while total energy stayed conserved. Reading for that licence phrase is worth a mark nearly every year.

**Thandi:** The circuit rules are mirrored pairs, and under exam pressure they swap themselves around. Same current or same potential difference — how do we keep series and parallel straight?

**Tutor:** Anchor each rule to its physical reason and the pair cannot swap, because the reasons are not symmetric. Series is one unbroken loop: charge has a single path, it cannot pile up and it cannot leak away, so the current — the flow rate — must be identical at every point; what divides between the resistors is the battery's energy, so the potential differences add up to the emf. Parallel is a junction: each branch connects directly across the battery's terminals, so each branch must feel the full emf — same potential difference — while the flow is what divides, and the branch currents add back together at the junction. Notice the deep pattern: the quantity that is the same is the one the geometry forces; the quantity that adds is the one being shared out. Then run the audit both ways in the exam: in series, sum your potential differences and confirm they return the emf; in parallel, sum your branch currents and confirm they return the battery current. If an audit fails, a number upstream is wrong, and you have caught it for free.

**Thandi:** Last one: any advice for structuring physics revision itself in these final weeks — what actually moves marks?

**Tutor:** Three shifts, in order of payoff. First, revise by solving, not by rereading. Reading a worked solution feels like progress and stores almost nothing; closing the book and reproducing the solution cold is what installs the method. Take a past paper, work the mechanics and electricity sections without notes, then mark yourself against the memo — the gaps you find are your real revision list. Second, drill the traps by name, because the November physics is familiar but the traps are always fresh: g at 9,8 not 10, the unsquared speed in kinetic energy, the swapped circuit rule, the vector quoted without direction, the ammeter wired across instead of through. Write the five on one page with one example each and rehearse recognising them. Third, end every solution with its second-route check: displacement against the graph area, energy against the wallet audit, circuit numbers against the emf and junction sums. The check converts an uncertain answer into a certain one while you are still in the room, and that certainty steadies every question that follows. Solve cold, name the traps, check by second route — those three carry more marks than any amount of note-reading.
