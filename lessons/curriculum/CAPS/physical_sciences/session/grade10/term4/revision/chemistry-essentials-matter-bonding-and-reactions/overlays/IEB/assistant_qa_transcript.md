**Thandi:** Revision questions, rapid fire. First: four toolkits is still a lot of chemistry. If someone is short on time, what order should they revise in, and why that order?

**Tutor:** Revise in the order of dependence, because chemistry stacks: atom, then bonds, then equations, then moles. The atom and the periodic table come first since everything downstream reads off them — valence electrons decide bonding, molar masses feed every calculation. Bonding second, because it is mostly classification: metal with non-metal swaps, non-metal with non-metal shares, metal with metal pools, and the properties follow the click. Equations third, because balancing is a mechanical skill that sharpens fast with practice — ten equations balanced aloud with their audits does more than an hour of reading. Moles last and longest, because mole work compounds all the earlier layers and carries the heaviest marks: every path runs through n equals m over M, c equals n over V, and 22,4 cubic decimetres per mole at STP. And within each toolkit, revise by DOING: close the notes, work a fresh example cold, then check. Reading a worked solution feels like progress and stores very little; reproducing one from a blank page is what installs the method. If time is desperately short, drill the mole exchanges and the balancing audits — those two skills alone touch more questions in the final than everything else combined.

**Thandi:** Next: the isotope average. The decimal masses on the periodic table — like boron's 10,8 — confuse people every year. What exactly is being averaged, and how do the calculation questions work?

**Tutor:** What is averaged is the natural MIXTURE of isotopes, weighted by how common each one is. No single boron atom has a mass of 10,8 — atoms come in whole batches: boron-10 with five protons and five neutrons, boron-11 with five protons and six. Nature supplies them in fixed proportions, roughly one fifth and four fifths, and the table quotes the average you would measure across any natural sample: 0,2 times 10 plus 0,8 times 11 equals 10,8. The calculation questions run in both directions, and both use the same one-line template — each isotope's mass times its fraction, summed. Forward direction: given the isotopes and their percentages, compute the average — multiply and add, exactly as we just did. Reverse direction: given the average and the isotope masses, find the percentages — call one fraction x and the other one minus x, set up the same line, solve. Two habits protect the marks: convert percentages to decimals before multiplying, and sanity-check the answer — the average must land BETWEEN the isotope masses, closer to the more abundant one. Boron's 10,8 sits near 11 because boron-11 dominates. An average outside the range, or exactly in the middle when the abundances are unequal, is your own alarm bell ringing.

**Thandi:** Third: state symbols. They feel like decoration — four little letters in brackets. Why do markers care so much about them?

**Tutor:** Because the state symbols ARE chemistry, compressed. Consider how much each letter asserts. Write a q after an ionic compound and you have claimed it is dissolved — dissociated into free, mobile ions, a conducting solution. Write s after a product in a solution reaction and you have claimed a precipitate — the visible event of the reaction, the milky flash or the golden snow. In the lead iodide equation, the s on P b I 2 is the entire observable result; omit it and the equation no longer says anything happened. The g on a product claims bubbles and a gas escaping, which is the driving force of gas-forming reactions. So a marker reading your state symbols is reading whether you understand what the beaker actually LOOKS like — symbols are the difference between recording chemistry and rearranging letters. The practical drill: after balancing any equation, do a second pass just for states, asking one question per substance — where is this, physically, right now? Solid on the bench, dissolved in the water, escaping as gas, liquid water itself? Four letters, one pass, and the equation earns its full value. And in precipitation questions specifically, name the spectators too: the ions still wearing a q on both sides are the ones that never left the water.

**Thandi:** Last one: any advice for structuring chemistry revision in the final weeks — what actually moves marks?

**Tutor:** Three shifts, in order of payoff. First, solve cold rather than reread: take a set of practice questions spanning all four toolkits, work them without notes under a clock, then mark yourself honestly against the solutions — the gaps you uncover are your genuine revision list, and nothing else finds them as fast. Second, drill the traps by NAME, because in the final the chemistry is familiar but the traps are freshly painted: subscripts edited to force a balance, the diatomic seven forgotten, cubic centimetres fed raw into c equals n over V, grams pushed through mole ratios, dissolving mislabelled a chemical change. Write the five on a single page with one example each and rehearse recognising them — recognition is the whole defence. Third, end every answer with its built-in audit: elements counted on both sides of a balanced equation, units cancelled through a mole calculation, spectators named after a precipitate. The audit converts an uncertain answer into a certain one while you are still at the desk, and that certainty steadies every question after it. Solve cold, name the traps, run the audits — those three habits carry more marks into the final than any quantity of highlighting.
