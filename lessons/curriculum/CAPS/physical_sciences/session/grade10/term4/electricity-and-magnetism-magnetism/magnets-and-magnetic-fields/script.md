# Part 1 — Expert

This session builds magnetism from its first idea — a force that acts without touching — to the largest magnet you will ever stand on: the Earth itself. Four pieces of equipment by the end: the definition of a magnetic field and how it compares with the electric and gravitational fields you already know; the unbreakable rule that magnetic poles come in pairs; the skill of drawing and reading magnetic field lines, which is where the marks in this topic live; and the Earth's own field, the reason a compass works and the reason the sky near the poles sometimes catches fire with light. No formulas today — this topic is examined in words and drawings, which means precision in language IS the calculation.

## Subtopic: Force Without Touching — the Idea of a Field

Hold a magnet near a steel paper clip and the clip jumps the gap. Nothing visible crosses that gap — no string, no push of air — and yet a force acts. Physics handles action-at-a-distance with one powerful idea: the field.

The definition, word for word: a magnetic field is a region in space where another magnet or a ferromagnetic material will experience a force. Region in space — not a substance, not a beam, but a volume of influence surrounding the magnet, invisible and real.

You have met this shape of idea twice already. A gravitational field is a region in space where a mass experiences a gravitational force — the Earth's field is why your dropped phone accelerates downward. An electric field is a region in space where an electric charge experiences an electric force — the field of a charged balloon is why it lifts your hair without contact. Line the three up and the pattern is exact: each field is a region of influence; each acts on its own kind of victim — mass for gravity, charge for electricity, magnets and ferromagnetic materials for magnetism; and each acts without contact.

The differences matter as much as the pattern. Gravity acts on every mass and only attracts. The electric field acts on any charge, and can attract or repel, because charge comes in two kinds that can exist separately — a lone positive, a lone negative. The magnetic field also attracts and repels, but its two kinds — the poles — can NEVER be separated, a strangeness we take on fully in the next section.

Which materials feel a magnetic force? Ferromagnetic ones — iron, steel, cobalt, nickel. Copper, aluminium, glass, plastic and paper do not respond. That is why a magnet through a plastic bag still grips paper clips, and why fridge magnets hold notes to a steel door but slide off an aluminium pot.

The questions for this section are with you now: the field definition stated exactly, the three fields matched to what each one acts on, and the ferromagnetic short-list separated from the indifferent materials.

## Subtopic: Poles Come in Pairs — and the Pair Cannot Be Divorced

Every magnet has two poles, named north and south. Hang a bar magnet from a thread and it settles pointing along a north-south line; the end that seeks geographic north is the north pole of the magnet, the other end its south pole.

The interaction rule reads exactly like electrostatics: like poles repel, opposite poles attract. North pushes north away, south pushes south away, north and south pull together. With two bar magnets you can feel both — slide north toward north and your hands sense a spring pushing back; flip one magnet and the same gap snaps closed.

Now the rule with no exception anywhere in nature. Cut a bar magnet in half, hoping to isolate its north pole, and you fail — each half is a complete magnet with its own north AND south. Cut again, and again, down to fragments, down to dust: every piece, however tiny, carries both poles. An isolated pole — a magnetic monopole — has never been found. Contrast electrostatics deliberately: a lone electron carrying pure negative charge exists in every atom, but a lone magnetic north exists nowhere.

Why? Look inside the material. Every electron moving inside any object has a tiny magnetic field associated with it. In most materials those little fields point in all directions, cancel, and the object is not magnetic. In ferromagnetic materials, the fields line up across small regions called domains. In an unmagnetised iron nail the domains point randomly; magnetise the nail and the domains align, their fields adding into one net field. Now the cutting mystery dissolves: magnetism is distributed through the whole material as aligned domains, so any piece you cut still contains aligned domains — a complete two-poled magnet. There is no north substance at one end to slice off; there is one pattern running through the whole.

Take this section's questions now: the interaction rule applied to pole pairs, the fate of a magnet cut in half stated with its reason, and the domain picture told for both the unmagnetised and the magnetised nail.

## Subtopic: Drawing the Invisible — Magnetic Field Lines

The field is invisible; field lines are how physics draws it, and examiners mark the drawings against four strict rules.

Rule one: direction. Field lines outside a magnet run FROM the north pole TO the south pole, and every line carries an arrow saying so. Rule two: spacing means strength. Where lines crowd close together, the field is strong; where they spread apart, it is weak. Field lines bunch densely at the poles — the strongest regions — and thin out with distance. Rule three: field lines never cross. A crossing would claim the field points two directions at one point, which is meaningless. Rule four: the field is three-dimensional, filling the space all around the magnet, though our drawings flatten it to the page.

Now draw the bar magnet in your mind, arrows and all. From the north pole, lines fan outward in every direction, curve through the surrounding space in great arcs, and converge into the south pole. Close to each pole the lines pack tight; far from the magnet they spread wide. The pattern looks like a fountain leaving the north end and raining round into the south.

You can make this pattern real on a desk. Lay a sheet of paper over a bar magnet and scatter iron filings on top: each filing becomes a tiny magnet, twists to lie along the field, and the filings assemble the arc pattern before your eyes — dense fur at the poles, sweeping curves between. Filings show the shape but not the direction; for direction, place small compasses around the magnet, and each needle's north end points along the field line at that spot, from the magnet's north round toward its south.

Two magnets together make the exam's favourite pictures. Opposite poles facing: the lines leave one magnet's north and sweep across the gap into the other's south, dense and direct — a field bridging the gap, the drawing of attraction. Like poles facing: the lines from each north bend sharply AWAY from each other, leaving an empty region between the poles called a neutral point — the drawing of repulsion, two fields refusing to connect.

Work this section's questions now: the four rules, the bar-magnet pattern with correct arrows, what filings show that compasses do not, and the two-magnet pictures matched to attraction and repulsion.

## Subtopic: The Earth Is a Magnet

Float a magnetised needle on a leaf in still water and it slowly swings to point north-south. Nothing pushed it — nothing visible. The needle sits inside the field of the planet.

The Earth behaves as if a colossal bar magnet lay buried along its axis, its field lines emerging near one geographic pole, arcing through the space around the planet, and re-entering near the other. A compass is nothing but a small magnet on a frictionless pivot: its needle aligns with whatever field it sits in, so anywhere on Earth it settles along the Earth's field lines — and that is the entire mechanism of the compass, an instrument that guided ships across oceans for a thousand years.

Now the detail examiners love. The end of the needle that points north is itself a NORTH pole — and opposite poles attract — so the place it points to must behave magnetically like a SOUTH pole. The Earth's magnetic pole in the northern hemisphere is, magnetically speaking, a south pole. We still call the place "magnetic north" because it is the spot in the north that compasses seek.

Distinguish two norths. The geographic North Pole is the northernmost point of the Earth's axis of rotation — the fixed point maps are drawn around. The magnetic north pole is the point the compass seeks, and it lies hundreds of kilometres away from the geographic pole — and drifts, year by year, as the churning molten iron in the Earth's core rearranges the field. Navigators must correct for the difference between the two norths.

Finally, what the field does for us. The Sun streams out charged particles — the solar wind — that would scour away our atmosphere and endanger life. The Earth's magnetic field deflects most of this wind around the planet, a shield the size of a world. Some particles are funnelled along the field lines toward the poles, where they crash into the upper atmosphere and set it glowing in sheets of green and red light — the auroras, the northern and southern lights. And when the Sun erupts violently, the arriving surge rattles the field in a magnetic storm, which can disrupt radio, satellites and power grids.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the compass explained as a small magnet in a big field, the two norths separated, the magnetic identity of the pole in the north argued from attraction, and the shield, the auroras and the storms each tied to the field.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same magnetism from your kitchen — a fridge magnet, a pin you cannot pull apart, and a needle that always knows where north is.

## Subtopic: The Invisible Bubble

Take the fridge magnet off the door and bring it back slowly. A little before it touches, you feel the pull — and then the door snatches it from your fingers. Stop and honour how strange that is: the magnet grabbed the door before they touched. Something reached across the gap.

That something is the magnet's field: an invisible bubble of influence in the space around it. Inside the bubble, magnets and things made of iron or steel feel the force; outside, nothing. The bubble is strongest right at the magnet and fades fast as you back away — which you can feel by lifting the magnet slowly off the door and noticing exactly where the tug lets go.

You already live inside two other invisible bubbles. The Earth's gravity bubble pulls on anything with mass — drop your phone anywhere and the field finds it. A rubbed balloon wears an electric bubble that pulls on charge — hold it near your arm and the hairs lean in without any touch. Magnetism is the third member of the family, and its chosen victims are other magnets and a short guest list of metals: iron, steel, cobalt and nickel.

That guest list explains half your kitchen. The magnet grips the steel fridge door and holds your shopping list against it. It does nothing at all for the aluminium pot, the copper wire, the glass, the plastic or the paper — which is exactly why the magnet holds paper TO the door: the paper is invisible to the field, and the steel behind it does all the work.

Quick check — the questions on this piece are with you now: what the bubble is, where it is strongest, the two other bubbles you already knew, and which materials get an invitation.

## Subtopic: The Pin You Cannot Divorce

Every magnet has two ends with two names: north and south. And they follow the rule you already guessed from electrostatics: like poles push apart, opposite poles pull together. Play with two fridge magnets and you can feel it — one way round they snap together; flip one and they fight your fingers, sliding off sideways like two people refusing to shake hands.

Now the experiment that sounds easy and is actually impossible. Take a magnetised pin: north at the tip, south at the eye. Snip it in the middle to keep just the north half. Test your half-pin — and it has a brand-new south end at the cut. Snip again: same story. Grind it to powder and every speck is still a complete little magnet, two poles each. Nobody, in any laboratory on Earth, has ever produced a lone north or a lone south.

Why not? Because a magnet is not north substance glued to south substance. Inside the steel, countless electrons each make a tiny magnetic field. In ordinary steel these tiny fields point every which way and cancel — no magnetism. In a magnetised pin they have lined up, patch by patch — the patches are called domains — all facing the same way, like a stadium crowd doing the same wave. The pin's magnetism is that TEAMWORK, spread through the whole metal. Cut the crowd in half and each half is still doing the wave. There was never a north half to keep.

This also tells you why dropping a magnet weakens it: the jolt shakes some domains out of line, breaking up the teamwork. And it is why stroking a steel needle with a magnet, always the same direction, magnetises it: stroke by stroke you are talking the domains into facing the same way.

Your questions for this section are up now: the push-pull rule, the fate of the snipped pin, and the crowd-doing-the-wave picture that explains it.

## Subtopic: The Needle That Knows North

Magnetise a needle by stroking it, float it on a leaf in a bowl of still water, and watch: it turns, slowly, and settles pointing north-south. Every time. Spin it and it comes back. That floating needle is a compass, and the question is who is turning it.

The Earth is. Our planet acts like an enormous bar magnet buried along its core, wrapping the whole world in one gigantic magnetic bubble. Your needle is a tiny magnet sitting inside that bubble, and like every magnet in a field, it swings to line up with the field around it. A compass is just a needle allowed to answer the planet.

One twist to keep for exam day: the end of the needle that points north is itself a north pole, and opposites attract — so the place in the Arctic it points to must behave like a magnetic SOUTH pole. We still call that place magnetic north, because it is where north-seeking needles aim. And it is not the same spot as the geographic North Pole, the fixed point the Earth spins around: the magnetic point sits hundreds of kilometres away and wanders slowly as the molten iron churning in the core shifts the field. Two norths — the map's and the needle's — and serious navigators correct for the gap between them.

The planet's bubble does far more than steer needles. The Sun blasts out a constant storm of charged particles, and without protection that storm would slowly strip our air away. The Earth's field bends the storm around us like a windscreen in a hailstorm. Near the poles, some particles slide down the field lines into the sky and make it glow — curtains of green and red light called auroras, the field's shield made visible. When the Sun flares hard, the whole shield shudders — a magnetic storm — and radios, satellites and power grids can stumble.

So the humble fridge magnet and the glowing polar sky are one topic: fields, poles in stubborn pairs, and lines you can draw. Here come the last questions of the lesson: the needle explained, the two norths kept apart, the south-pole-in-the-north twist argued out loud, and the shield in the sky given its proper name.
