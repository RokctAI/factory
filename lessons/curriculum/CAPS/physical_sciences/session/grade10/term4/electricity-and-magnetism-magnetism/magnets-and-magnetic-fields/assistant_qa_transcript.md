**Thandi:** Time for the questions that come up a lot with this topic. First: if nobody can see or touch a field, how do we know it is actually there and not just a story?

**Tutor:** Because it does measurable work on demand, and because we can map it. Hold a compass at any point near a bar magnet and the needle swings to a definite direction — the same direction every time you return to that point, and a smoothly different direction at the next point over. Scatter iron filings and thousands of tiny magnets independently assemble one consistent pattern of arcs. Move a paper clip through the region and you can feel where the pull strengthens and where it fades. A story would not produce repeatable directions at every point in space, and it certainly would not deflect the solar wind or steer ships. What IS true is that the field lines themselves are our drawing convention, not physical threads — nothing is strung through the air. The field is the real, invisible condition of the space; the lines are how we write it down, the way contour lines on a map record a real mountain without the mountain wearing stripes. Examiners probe exactly that distinction, so hold both halves: field real, lines representational.

***

**Thandi:** Next, the cutting mystery again, because it feels wrong: WHY does half a magnet grow a new pole? Where does the new south come from?

**Tutor:** Nothing new grows — the cut merely exposes what was always there. The mistake hiding in the question is picturing a magnet as north material welded to south material, like a red end and a blue end. It is not. A magnet is countless atomic-scale magnets — the fields of moving electrons, organised into domains — all aligned the same way, nose to tail down the length of the bar. The north face and the south face are simply the two ENDS of that aligned parade: where the parade points out of the metal we call north; where it points in, south. Cut the parade at any point and you have two shorter parades, each still aligned, each with a head and a tail — a north and a south, instantly, at the cut faces. Take it to the extreme and even a single electron carries a complete little field with both magnetic aspects, so no amount of dividing can ever strand a lone pole. Poles are not substances to be separated; they are the two ends of one alignment, and every piece of an alignment has two ends.

***

**Thandi:** Third: the north end of a compass points north — but opposite poles attract, so people keep tying themselves in knots. Untangle it once and for all?

**Tutor:** Walk the logic in three short steps and it stays untangled. Step one: a compass needle is a small bar magnet, and its ends were NAMED by behaviour — the end that seeks the Earth's north was simply labelled the north-seeking pole, shortened to north. Step two: the attraction rule — opposite poles attract — is not negotiable. Step three: combine them. The needle's north end is genuinely a magnetic north pole, and it is being ATTRACTED toward the Arctic; the only thing that attracts a north pole is a south pole; therefore the Earth's magnetic pole in the northern hemisphere must behave, magnetically, as a SOUTH pole. There is no contradiction — only a naming accident. The place is called magnetic north because of WHERE it is and what points to it, not because of its magnetic character. In an exam, write the reasoning exactly in that order: needle end is north, opposites attract, therefore the pole in the north is magnetically south. Stated as a bare fact it looks like a slip; stated as a three-step argument it earns full marks.

***

**Thandi:** Last one: the Earth's field feels far away from marks — shields, auroras, wandering poles. What exactly should a Grade 10 be able to DO with all that?

**Tutor:** Five concrete performances, all straight from the syllabus. One: explain how a compass indicates the direction of a magnetic field — the needle is a small magnet that aligns with the field it sits in, so its north end points along the field line at that location. Two: compare the Earth's field to a bar magnet's, in words and drawings — the planet behaves as if a giant bar magnet lay along its axis, with field lines emerging near one pole, arcing around the Earth, and re-entering near the other, the same fountain pattern you drew on the desk, scaled to a world. Three: distinguish the geographic North Pole — the northernmost point of the axis of rotation — from the magnetic north pole, the drifting point compasses seek, hundreds of kilometres away. Four: name phenomena the field produces — the auroras and magnetic storms — and say what causes each: charged particles funnelled down field lines, and solar eruptions rattling the field. Five: explain qualitatively that the field deflects the solar wind, shielding the atmosphere and the life underneath it. Every one of those is a words-and-drawings answer — no formula anywhere — which is exactly why precise sentences are the whole game in this topic.
