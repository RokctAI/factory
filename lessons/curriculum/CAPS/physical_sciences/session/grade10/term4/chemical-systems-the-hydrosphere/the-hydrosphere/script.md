# Part 1 — Expert

This session studies the hydrosphere — all the water of planet Earth, in every form and every hiding place — and it closes the year's chemistry by pointing every skill you have built at one system. Four goals. First, identify the hydrosphere: what it contains, where its water sits, and in what proportions. Second, trace its interactions with the other global systems — the atmosphere, the lithosphere and the biosphere — because water refuses to stay in its own compartment. Third, examine what happens when people interrupt the flow with dams: who benefits, what breaks, and how to weigh the two honestly. Fourth, bring the chemistry home — testing and purifying water, using the very ion tests you already know. The emphasis throughout is application and interpretation, not rote equations; this topic asks you to THINK with water.

## Subtopic: Identifying the Hydrosphere

The hydrosphere consists of all the Earth's water, and the syllabus asks you to identify it in its three states and their locations. Liquid water: the oceans, rivers, lakes, wetlands — and, easy to forget, GROUNDWATER, the water soaked into rock and soil below the surface, feeding boreholes and springs. Frozen water: the polar ice sheets, glaciers, icebergs — and permafrost, soil that stays frozen year-round in cold regions with ice locked between its grains. Water vapour: the invisible gas phase spread through the atmosphere, the reservoir from which every cloud condenses.

Now the proportions, because they reshape how you see the planet. Of all the water on Earth, roughly 97 percent is ocean — salt water, undrinkable and unusable for crops without expensive desalination. Of the small fresh remainder, most is locked away in ice sheets and glaciers. What is left for rivers, lakes and accessible groundwater — the fresh, liquid, reachable water that every farm, every city and every living thing on land competes for — is well under one percent of the planet's total. A water-rich world, and a water-scarce humanity: both statements are true at once, and South Africa, a dry country by world standards, feels the second one sharply.

One more identification the exam expects: the hydrosphere is a RESERVOIR SYSTEM, not a cycle. The water cycle is the set of processes MOVING water between reservoirs; the hydrosphere is the water itself, wherever it currently sits. Keep the noun and the verb separate — the store and the moving.

The questions for this section are with you now: the three states with two locations each, the 97 percent and the under-one-percent stated and interpreted, groundwater and permafrost placed correctly, and the store-versus-cycle distinction made cleanly.

## Subtopic: One Water, Four Spheres

The Earth's systems are named like a family: the atmosphere — the air; the lithosphere — the rocks and soil; the biosphere — the living things; and the hydrosphere threads through all three, moving by processes you can name and follow.

Into the atmosphere: the sun drives EVAPORATION from oceans, lakes and soil, loading the air with vapour. Cooling drives CONDENSATION — vapour to droplets, droplets to clouds — and precipitation returns the water as rain, snow and hail. Every drop of rain is hydrosphere that visited the atmosphere: warming sent it up, cooling brought it down.

Into the lithosphere: rain soaks into rock and soil and becomes groundwater, moving slowly through the pores underground. And as it moves, chemistry happens — the chemistry of this whole term. Water is the polar solvent, and it DISSOLVES minerals out of the rock it passes through: calcium, magnesium, carbonates, chlorides ride along as aqueous ions. Where conditions change, water DEPOSITS those minerals again — that is how dripstone caves grow their stone icicles, one deposited layer at a time. Dissolving and depositing, cooling and warming: the syllabus's own four words for water's work as it moves.

Into the biosphere: living things are largely water — your own body is roughly 60 percent water — and life's chemistry runs in aqueous solution, exactly the solution chemistry you studied. Plants drink through roots and exhale vapour through leaves, a flow called transpiration that returns enormous volumes to the atmosphere; animals drink and excrete; every cell is a beaker of dissolved ions doing chemistry. No water, no life — not as a slogan, but as a statement about where biochemistry happens.

Hold the whole picture as one loop you can narrate: ocean to air by evaporation, air to land by rain, land to underground by soaking — dissolving rock on the way — underground and overland back to the ocean, with plants and animals borrowing water at every stage. One water, four spheres, endlessly circulating.

Take this section's questions now: each sphere named with its water pathway, evaporation against condensation as warming against cooling, dissolving and depositing tied to groundwater, and transpiration placed in the loop.

## Subtopic: Dams — the Price of Stored Water

South Africa interrupts its rivers deliberately, and the syllabus asks you to explain how building a dam affects the lives of people and the ecology of a region — a weigh-both-sides question, and one of the most human in the course.

Why dam a river at all? Because rain is seasonal and unreliable, while thirst is constant. A dam stores wet-season water for dry-season use: drinking water for cities, irrigation for farms, and in large dams, hydroelectric power from water released through turbines. The Gariep Dam on the Orange River is the country's largest storage dam; the Vaal Dam anchors the supply to Gauteng's industry and millions of taps. Reliable water where and when people need it — that is the benefit, and it is enormous.

Now the costs, and treat them as seriously. Upstream, the dam floods a valley: farmland, homes, sometimes whole communities and their histories disappear under the new lake, and people are moved whether they wish it or not. Downstream, the river is diminished: the natural flow — floods that spread nutrients onto floodplains, seasonal pulses that trigger fish spawning and bird breeding — is flattened into a managed trickle. The wall itself blocks fish migration. And the dam traps SEDIMENT: the silt that once fertilised downstream floodplains and built the river's banks settles uselessly behind the wall, slowly filling the reservoir while starving the lands below. Around the still water, ecology shifts again — water plants and mosquitoes favour a lake over a flowing river.

How would you actually STUDY these effects, rather than assert them? The syllabus recommends real investigation methods: interview people who lived in the area for many years before and after the wall went up — memory is data; and consult the literature about the region. Compare the river's ecology above and below the dam. That is science applied to a community question, and the exam rewards answers that name benefits AND costs, then weigh them, rather than sloganeering either way.

Work this section's questions now: two benefits and four costs, each tied to its mechanism; the sediment story told in full; and the investigation methods that turn opinions about a dam into evidence.

## Subtopic: Testing and Purifying Water

Chemistry's last job of the year: deciding whether water is fit to drink, and making it so — and every tool involved is one you already own.

What might be dissolved in a water sample? Ions picked up from rock, farmland and pipes: carbonates, chlorides, sulphates, nitrates and nitrites among them. Most are harmless in moderation; some are warnings — nitrates and nitrites in a borehole often signal fertiliser runoff or sewage contamination, and they matter because in high concentrations they harm human health, infants especially, and they feed the choking green algal blooms you see on over-enriched dams.

How do you test? Exactly as you tested mystery solutions. The pH of the sample first — acids and alkalis announce themselves on the scale from 0 through neutral 7 to 14. Then the ion tests: dilute nitric acid, then silver nitrate — white snow says chloride. Nitric acid, then barium nitrate — stubborn white snow says sulphate; fizzing under the acid said carbonate before you even reached the reagents. Commercial test strips bundle the checks for pH, nitrates and nitrites into a colour chart, and a microscope or magnifying glass adds the biological check: a drop of dam water is a zoo, and some of its citizens cause disease.

How do you purify? A treatment works runs stages, each removing what the previous one cannot. SEDIMENTATION: let the water stand so suspended grit settles out. FILTRATION: pass it through sand and gravel beds — or filter paper and a funnel, at laboratory scale — trapping the fine particles. Then the step no filter can perform: DISINFECTION. Filtering removes particles, but microorganisms slip through, so chlorine is added to kill them — the faint swimming-pool smell of tap water is that final stage doing its job. At home, with infrastructure down, boiling water for a minute achieves the same kill. And note what even boiling cannot do: dissolved ions sail through filters and survive any boil — remember the salt that vanished into clear solution and came back when the water evaporated. Removing dissolved salt takes distillation or other desalination, which is why purifying sea water is expensive and why fresh water is precious.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the ion tests re-aimed at a water sample, nitrates read as a contamination warning, the three purification stages in order with the reason filtering alone is never enough, and the one contaminant that survives both filter and flame.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same hydrosphere at human scale — a bucket, a long round trip, and a river with people arguing on both banks.

## Subtopic: The Bucket Planet

Imagine every drop of water on Earth poured into one ten-litre bucket. Oceans, ice caps, rivers, clouds, the water underground, the water inside every living thing — all of it, one bucket.

Now look at what you actually have. About nine and a half litres of the bucket is sea water — salty, undrinkable, useless on a farm. Skim off the fresh water and most of THAT is ice, locked in the polar sheets and glaciers, beautiful and out of reach. What remains — the fresh, liquid, gettable water in rivers, lakes and wells that every city, farm, animal and plant on land must share — is in the order of a few teaspoons. Teaspoons, from a ten-litre bucket.

That is the hydrosphere: a planet drenched in water and a civilisation living off the teaspoons. It is why a dry country like ours prices water, meters it, and builds dams to save the wet season for the dry one — and why "plenty of water on Earth" and "not enough water for people" are both true.

Know where the teaspoons hide, too. Rivers and lakes you can see. But a huge share of the usable fresh water is invisible — groundwater, soaked into the rock under your feet like water into a sponge, which is what a borehole drinks from. And in the coldest places, soil stays frozen all year with ice in its pores — permafrost — water in storage nobody can pour. Liquid, ice and vapour; surface, underground and sky: that is the full inventory.

Quick check — the questions on this piece are with you now: the bucket's proportions, where groundwater and permafrost fit, and why scarce and abundant are both the right word.

## Subtopic: The Longest Round Trip

Follow one drop of ocean water on the journey it has been making since before the dinosaurs.

Morning sun warms the sea surface; our drop evaporates and rises as invisible vapour. High up, the air cools and the vapour condenses into a cloud droplet — warming lifted it, cooling caught it. The cloud drifts inland over the escarpment, and our drop falls as rain onto a mountainside. That is one border crossed: hydrosphere through atmosphere and back.

Landing, it has choices. It might run off into a stream and race for the sea. Instead, it soaks in — down through soil, into the tiny gaps in the rock, joining the slow underground crowd of groundwater. And here is the part that makes this chemistry and not just geography: as our drop creeps through the rock, it DISSOLVES it. Polar little solvent that it is, it picks up ions — calcium, magnesium, a carbonate here, a chloride there — and carries them along. That is why borehole water tastes of minerals, why kettles fur up with scale in some towns, and how caves grow stone icicles: water dissolving rock in one place and depositing it in another, drip by drip over centuries.

Years later, our drop seeps into a river. A thorn tree's roots drink it; it climbs the trunk, exits a leaf as vapour — transpiration, the plant's exhale — condenses into another cloud, rains again, and this time a girl fills a cup from a tap fed by a dam, and drinks. Now the drop is IN the biosphere, doing cell chemistry in a human being — because your body is more than half water, and every thought you have runs in solution.

Eventually — breath by breath, river by river — the drop returns to the sea, carrying its dissolved ions with it. Which, by the way, is why the sea is salty: rivers have been delivering dissolved salts to the ocean for billions of years, and evaporation takes only the water back, leaving the salt behind. Every sphere visited, nothing lost, the same water forever: the drop in your glass has been ocean, cloud, rock, river and probably several dinosaurs.

Your questions for this section are up now: the drop's crossings named — evaporation, condensation, soaking, dissolving, transpiration — and the riddle of the salty sea answered with the round trip.

## Subtopic: The River With Two Banks of Opinions

Last piece: build a dam in your head, and listen to both banks of the river argue about it.

The case for the wall is short and strong. Rain comes in seasons; people are thirsty every day. A dam banks the flood for the drought: taps that run in October, fields irrigated through winter, and at the big dams, turbines spinning electricity out of falling water. The Vaal Dam keeps Gauteng alive; the Gariep is our biggest water bank. When the drought comes — and here, it always comes — the dam is the difference between restrictions and catastrophe.

Now walk the costs, upstream to downstream. Behind the wall, the valley drowns: grazing land, ancestral homes, whole villages sometimes, moved and submerged — ask the elders who lived there what the lake covered; their memories are evidence, and interviewing them is genuine scientific method for studying a dam's impact. Downstream, the river is tamed to a trickle: the floods that once spread nutrients over the plains stop coming, fish that migrate upstream to breed meet a concrete full stop, and the silt that fed the floodplains now settles uselessly behind the wall — slowly filling the dam itself while the fields below grow poorer. Still water breeds different life than moving water: more water weeds, more mosquitoes, different fish. The river below a dam is a different river.

So is a dam good or bad? That is the wrong question, and the exam knows it. The right questions: who gains, who loses, what does the ecology pay, and can the losses be softened — walls with fish ladders, managed flood releases, compensation and consultation for the moved. A full answer names benefits AND costs and weighs them for the specific place. Sloganeering either way loses marks; honest weighing earns them.

And whichever bank you argue from, the water itself must still pass the chemist's checkpoint before anyone drinks it: settle it, filter it, then chlorinate or boil it — because filtering catches particles but never microbes, and boiling kills microbes but never removes dissolved salt. The teaspoons are precious; the chemistry is how we keep them safe. Here come the last questions of the lesson: both banks argued, the sediment trap explained, the study methods named, and the three-stage clean-up in order.
