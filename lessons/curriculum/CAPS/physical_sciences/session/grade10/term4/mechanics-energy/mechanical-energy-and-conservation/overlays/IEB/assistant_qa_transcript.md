**Thandi:** Let's dig into the questions everyone brings to this topic. First: two conservation statements get thrown around — the big law of conservation of energy, and conservation of mechanical energy. When a question says "state the law", how do we know which one it wants, and how different are they really?

**Tutor:** They are parent and child, and the wording of the question points at one or the other. The parent is universal and unconditional: energy cannot be created or destroyed, only transferred between objects or converted between forms. It holds everywhere, always — friction included, explosions included. The child is conditional and specific: IN THE ABSENCE of friction and air resistance, the MECHANICAL energy — kinetic plus gravitational potential — of an object stays constant. Two clues identify which is wanted. Clue one, the name: "law of conservation of energy" is the parent; "principle of conservation of mechanical energy" is the child. Clue two, the context: if the question involves E k one plus E p one equals E k two plus E p two, or a frictionless fall, it wants the child, condition included — and omitting the condition costs the mark, because the condition is what makes the statement true. The cleanest habit: whenever you write the child, open with the licence phrase yourself — "in the absence of friction and air resistance" — before the rest of the sentence. Then the two can never blur, and both statements are always mark-complete.

***

**Thandi:** Next, reference points. We are told we can put h equals zero anywhere. That freedom makes people nervous — surely the answers change if the zero moves?

**Tutor:** The balances change; the physics does not — and seeing why builds real confidence. Take the 4-kilogram ball on the 15-metre roof. Ground as zero: potential energy 588 joules at the top, zero at the bottom. Now move the zero to the ROOF instead: potential energy zero at the top, and MINUS 588 joules at the ground — heights below your zero are negative. The numbers in each account look completely different, yet every measurable answer survives, because speeds come from energy DIFFERENCES, and the difference is 588 joules in both bookkeeping schemes. The reference point is like choosing where a ruler's zero sits: it shifts every reading but no length. Two practical rules follow. Rule one: choose the zero that makes life easiest — usually the lowest point in the problem, so no height ever goes negative and no sign errors lurk. Rule two, the one that actually protects marks: never move the zero mid-problem. One floor per problem, declared in writing at the start. The freedom is total, but it is a one-time freedom, spent when you write the words "taking the ground as reference".

***

**Thandi:** Third: the mass cancellation. The claim that a 4-kilogram ball and a 40-kilogram toolbox land at the same speed feels genuinely wrong. Can you make it feel right?

**Tutor:** Run the wallet argument with real numbers, because feeling follows arithmetic here. The toolbox starts with ten times the height money — 5 880 joules against 588 — so intuition says it should arrive faster. But check the price list for speed: kinetic energy is half m v squared, so reaching any given speed costs the toolbox exactly ten times what it costs the ball. At 17,15 metres per second, the ball's speed pocket needs 588 joules; the toolbox's needs 5 880. Ten times the income, ten times the price: the ratio is one, and the two land together. The algebra says it in one line — half m v squared equals m g h, the m divides out, v squared equals 2 g h — no mass anywhere in the answer. And the reason your intuition resists is that daily life never shows you the frictionless case: a feather drifts because air resistance dominates it, not because gravity treats it differently. Take away the air — as the licence phrase does — and heavy and light truly fall as one. In the exam, that cancellation is also a strategy: whenever mass multiplies every term, divide it away early and the arithmetic halves.

***

**Thandi:** Last one: questions that mix friction in — a ball rolling on grass, a skateboarder slowing down. What is the Grade 10 way to handle those without the machinery we haven't learned yet?

**Tutor:** Grade 10 asks you to REASON about the leak, not to calculate its size — the calculating tools arrive later with the work-energy theorem. So build the answer from three fixed sentences. Sentence one names the broken seal: friction, or air resistance, acts, so mechanical energy is NOT conserved. Sentence two tracks the leak: the missing mechanical energy transferred to heat — and a little sound — through the rubbing surfaces. Sentence three protects the parent law: total energy is still conserved; nothing was destroyed, it merely left the mechanical accounts. Practise that trio until it flows, because each sentence is typically its own mark. Then read questions for the signal words. "Frictionless", "smooth", "ignore air resistance": seal intact, write the conservation equation and calculate. "Rough", "eventually stops", "brakes", "slows down": seal broken, deploy the three sentences and do NOT write the equality — using the sealed-wallet equation on a leaking wallet is the single most common error in this topic. One glance for the signal word, then the matching routine: that is the whole decision tree at this grade.
