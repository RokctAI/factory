# Part 1 — Expert

This session puts the principle of conservation of mechanical energy to work on the four situations CAPS names explicitly: objects dropped or thrown vertically, the swinging pendulum, roller coasters, and inclined planes. The centrepiece problem is a 200-kilogram roller-coaster car that crests a 25-metre hill at 3 metres per second, and the question every version of this topic asks: how fast is it moving at the bottom? One principle answers it, answers the pendulum, answers the ball thrown upward, and answers the skateboard ramp — and it does so while ignoring the shape of the track entirely, which is the superpower we are here to earn.

## Subtopic: The Working Tool — Two Points and One Equality

The tool is a single equality: E k one plus E p one equals E k two plus E p two. Kinetic plus potential at the first point equals kinetic plus potential at the second, provided no friction or air resistance acts. Everything in this lesson is that sentence, applied with discipline.

The discipline has four moves. Move one: choose the two points you will compare — the top of the hill and the bottom, the release point of the pendulum and its lowest swing, the throw and the peak. You choose them; choose the points the question asks about. Move two: set the reference level where height is zero, and take the LOWEST of your two points for it whenever you can, so no height goes negative. Move three: write all four energy terms, computing each with E p equals m g h and E k equals one half m v squared, and writing zero explicitly where a term dies — at rest means E k is zero; at the reference level means E p is zero. Move four: solve for the single unknown left standing.

Watch the zeros, because they carry the physics. An object DROPPED starts with E k zero. An object at its HIGHEST point in vertical flight has, for that instant, speed zero and E k zero. A pendulum at the bottom of its arc has E p zero, if you have put your reference there. Every famous special case is just the master equality with the right terms set to zero.

And note what the equality never asks for: the time, the acceleration, the path. Energy conservation is blind to how the journey happened — a fact that makes it faster than the equations of motion wherever both could work, and the ONLY tool where the path curves.

The questions for this section are with you now: the equality itself, the four moves in order, and which energy term is zero at a drop, at a peak, and at the bottom of a swing.

## Subtopic: Straight Up and Straight Down — Throws and the Pendulum

Application one: the vertical throw. A ball leaves a hand at 15 metres per second, straight up. Ignoring air resistance, how high does it rise? Point one is the hand, which we make the reference level: E p one is zero, and E k one is one half m times 15 squared. Point two is the peak, where the ball pauses: E k two is zero, E p two is m g h. The equality says one half m times 225 equals m times 9,8 times h. The mass cancels — as it always will when mass appears in every surviving term — leaving 112,5 equals 9,8 h, so h equals 11,48 metres, to two decimal places.

Run the film backwards and you get the drop for free: an object falling 11,48 metres from rest arrives at 15 metres per second. Without air resistance, the upward and downward journeys are mirror images, and the speed at any height going up equals the speed at that same height coming down. That symmetry is an exam question in its own right.

Application two: the pendulum. A bob on a string is pulled aside until it sits 0,2 metres higher than its lowest point, then released. How fast is it moving at the bottom of the swing? The bob travels along an arc — curved path, changing direction — and the equations of motion are helpless. The energy tool does not notice. Point one, the release: E k zero, E p equals m g times 0,2. Point two, the bottom: E p zero, E k equals one half m v squared. Cancel the mass: v squared equals 2 times 9,8 times 0,2, which is 3,92, so v equals 1,98 metres per second.

The bob then climbs the far side, trading every joule back, and rises to exactly 0,2 metres again — no higher, because there is no energy to pay for more. A real pendulum creeps lower each swing precisely because air resistance leaks a little mechanical energy per pass; the leak, not the principle, is what dies away.

Take this section's questions now: the throw worked to 11,48 metres, the mirror symmetry stated, the pendulum's 1,98 metres per second recovered, and the reason the far-side rise can never beat the release height.

## Subtopic: The Roller Coaster — Working the Big One

Now the centrepiece, with the full presentation an examiner wants to see.

A 200-kilogram roller-coaster car crests the top of a 25-metre hill moving at 3 metres per second. Ignoring friction, calculate its speed at the bottom of the hill.

Step one, points and reference: point one is the crest, point two the bottom, and the bottom is the reference level, h equals zero.

Step two, the energy account at point one. Kinetic: E k one equals one half times 200 times 3 squared, which is one half times 200 times 9, giving 900 joules. Potential: E p one equals 200 times 9,8 times 25, which is 49 000 joules. Mechanical energy at the top: 49 900 joules.

Step three, the account at point two. Potential: zero, by our choice of reference. So the entire 49 900 joules must sit in the kinetic term: one half times 200 times v squared equals 49 900.

Step four, solve. One half times 200 is 100, so 100 v squared equals 49 900, giving v squared equals 499, and v equals 22,34 metres per second — a little over 80 kilometres per hour.

Two traps live inside this specific problem, and both appear in marking reports every year. Trap one: forgetting the 900. The car was already moving at the top, and dropping its initial kinetic energy gives v squared equals 490 and a wrong 22,14 metres per second — close enough to look right, which is what makes it dangerous. When an object starts in motion, E k one is not zero. Trap two: cancelling the mass by reflex. Mass cancels only when it multiplies every term; here it does — divide the whole equation by 200 and you get one half v squared equals 4,5 plus 245 — but if a question ever asks for the ENERGY itself, in joules, the mass must stay.

Halfway checks work here too. At a height of 10 metres on the descent: E p equals 200 times 9,8 times 10, which is 19 600 joules, so E k equals 49 900 minus 19 600, which is 30 300 joules, and v squared equals 303, giving v equals 17,41 metres per second. Any point on the track surrenders its speed this way — that is the ledger at full power.

Work this section's questions now: the four steps reproduced on the coaster, the 22,34 confirmed, the halfway speed at 10 metres, and the two traps named and dodged.

## Subtopic: Inclined Planes and the Independence of Path

Application four: the inclined plane, and with it the deepest fact in the topic.

A skateboarder starts from rest at the top of a ramp 5 metres high and rolls to the bottom. Ignoring friction, the finishing speed comes straight off the ledger: m g h at the top becomes one half m v squared at the bottom, the mass cancels, v squared equals 2 times 9,8 times 5, which is 98, and v equals 9,90 metres per second.

Now the fact. Nothing in that calculation mentioned the ramp's length, its steepness, or its shape. A cliff drop of 5 metres, a gentle 50-metre slope losing 5 metres, a curving skate bowl 5 metres deep — identical finishing speeds, 9,90 metres per second, every time, provided friction stays out. In conservation problems the path taken can be ignored; the only quantities that matter are the object's speed and its height above the reference point. Steeper just means sooner — the steep ramp delivers its speed in less time — but energy accounting does not price time, only heights and speeds.

This is exactly why roller coasters are built the way they are: every later hill on the ride is lower than the first, because the car's budget was banked at the first crest, and a later hill taller than the budget allows simply cannot be crested. Real rides lose a little to friction on every metre of track, so each successive hill must shrink by more than geometry alone would demand.

The boundary of the tool, stated once more and precisely: the moment a question introduces friction or air resistance, mechanical energy is no longer conserved, and E k one plus E p one equals E k two plus E p two becomes false. At Grade 10, such questions ask you to reason — to say that the mechanical energy decreased and where it went — rather than to calculate the loss; the calculating machinery arrives with the work-energy theorem in Grade 12. Know the boundary and you will never apply the principle where its licence has expired.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the ramp's 9,90 metres per second, path independence stated as CAPS states it, the roller-coaster hill logic, and the exact moment the conservation equation loses its validity.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same principle at the playground and the skate park, where you have been running energy experiments your whole life without charging yourself for the data.

## Subtopic: The Swing Knows Your Height

Go back to the swing you rode as a child. Someone pulls you back and up — and the moment they let go, you fall forward along the arc, fastest at the very bottom, then up the other side, slowing, until you hang for one weightless instant at the top of the far swing. Then it all runs in reverse.

That swing is a savings account with two forms of money, and you can hear the trades. Pulled back and up: your height money is loaded, your speed money is zero. Bottom of the arc: height money spent to nothing, speed money full — that is why the bottom is where your stomach drops. Far side, top of the rise: speed money traded back into height money, and for a heartbeat you have no speed at all.

And here is the detail you already know from riding it: the far side lifts you to the SAME height you were released from. Never higher. You cannot end a trade richer than you started — the swing has only the money it was given at release. Physics calls the release height and the far-side height equal because mechanical energy is conserved along the swing.

Why does the swing eventually die down if nobody pushes? Air brushes past you on every pass, and every brush is a tiny withdrawal that leaves as warmth in the air. Small leak, many passes — the swing sinks a little lower each time until the account is empty. Nothing was destroyed; it was spent on stirring the air.

Numbers, quickly, because the exam wants them: released from 0,2 metres above the lowest point, the speed at the bottom is the square root of 2 times 9,8 times 0,2 — 1,98 metres per second — and it does not matter whether the rider is heavy or light, because heavier riders carry more money and pay higher prices in exact proportion.

Quick check — the questions on this piece are with you now: where the speed peaks, why the far side never wins, and where a dying swing's energy actually goes.

## Subtopic: Only the Drop Counts

Now to the skate park, for the single most surprising fact in this lesson.

Two ramps drop into the same bowl. One is a steep, scary, almost-vertical face. The other is a long, lazy slope. Both start 5 metres above the bottom. Two skaters roll from rest, one down each ramp. Who is moving faster at the bottom?

Neither. Both arrive at exactly 9,90 metres per second. The steep-ramp skater gets there SOONER — steeper means the trade happens fast — but not FASTER. Speed at the bottom is bought entirely with height, and both skaters spent the same 5 metres. The path is just the shape of the shop; the price and the purchase are the same.

That is the meaning of the line in your syllabus that says the path can be ignored: for energy accounting, only two numbers about a position ever matter — how high, and how fast. Length of track, angle of slope, curves and wiggles: invisible to the ledger. It is also why the energy method beats the equations of motion on any curved path. Equations of motion need a straight line and a constant acceleration; the ledger needs a starting balance.

Run the roller coaster this way and it almost solves itself. The car crests the first hill of 25 metres already rolling at 3 metres per second: its account holds 900 joules of speed money plus 49 000 of height money — 49 900 in total. At the bottom, all of it is speed money: half of 200 times the speed squared equals 49 900, and the speed is 22,34 metres per second. The one classic blunder: forgetting the 900 because the car "was basically at rest". It was not; it was crossing the crest at 3 metres per second, and the ledger counts every cent it carries.

Your questions for this section are up now: the two-ramp race called correctly, the reason the ledger ignores the path, and the coaster's starting kinetic energy kept safely in the sum.

## Subtopic: Why the Second Hill Is Always Lower

Last piece — look at the skyline of any roller coaster and read it like a bank statement.

The first hill is always the tallest. Everything the ride will ever do is paid for at that first crest: the chain drags the car up, loading the account, and from there gravity runs the show. Every later hill must be cheaper than the balance — a hill taller than the first would demand more height money than the car ever owned, and the car would stall on the slope, roll backwards, and leave a queue of very unhappy passengers.

And in real life every later hill is lower by MORE than the maths of a sealed account demands. Real tracks rub. Wheels, bearings, air — every metre of track takes a small toll in heat and noise, so the account shrinks all ride long. Designers measure the leak and shave every hill to fit under the falling balance. When the ride ends, the brakes take whatever money is left, and the smell of warm brake pads at the platform is the final entry in the statement.

This picture answers the exam's favourite reasoning questions in one breath each. Why can the pendulum never swing higher than its release? Same balance, no income. Why does a ball thrown up at 15 metres per second come back to your hand at 15 metres per second, ignoring air resistance? Perfect trade out, perfect trade back. Why does the skateboarder eventually stop rolling around the bowl? The rubbing toll, paid on every pass, empties the account. When may you use the beautiful equality at all? Only while the account is sealed — the words "ignore friction and air resistance" are the seal, and without them the equality is a lie.

So leave with three sentences. One: energy problems are two snapshots and one unchanging total — pick your two points, count height money and speed money at each. Two: only the drop counts — never the path, never the time, and mostly not even the mass. Three: rubbing is the only thief, and it steals to heat, never to nowhere. Here come the last questions of the lesson: the coaster skyline explained, the seal recognised, and one full two-snapshot count from crest to bottom.
