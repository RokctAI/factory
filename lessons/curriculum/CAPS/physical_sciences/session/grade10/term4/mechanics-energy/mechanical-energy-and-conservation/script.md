# Part 1 — Expert

This session assembles the single most powerful bookkeeping tool in Grade 10 physics: mechanical energy and the law that keeps its total fixed. You already own the two ingredients — gravitational potential energy, E p equals m g h, and kinetic energy, E k equals one half m v squared. Today they merge into one quantity, mechanical energy, and one principle: in the absence of friction and air resistance, that total never changes. The worked problem carrying the method is a 2-kilogram ball dropped from a rooftop 20 metres up, and by the end we will know its energy account and its speed at the top, at the halfway mark, and at the ground — without touching a single equation of motion.

## Subtopic: Two Accounts, One Total — Defining Mechanical Energy

Recall the two energies an object can hold because of where it is and how it moves. Gravitational potential energy is the energy an object has because of its position in the gravitational field relative to a chosen reference point: E p equals m g h — the mass, times the gravitational acceleration of 9,8 metres per second squared, times the height above the reference point. Kinetic energy is the energy an object has because of its motion: E k equals one half m v squared — half the mass times the speed squared. Both are measured in joules, and both are scalars: no direction, only size.

Mechanical energy is simply their sum. E M equals E k plus E p — the mechanical energy of an object equals its kinetic energy plus its gravitational potential energy. That one-line definition is a mark in almost every paper, so say it precisely: the sum of the gravitational potential energy and the kinetic energy.

Feed the ball its numbers at the top of the roof. Mass 2 kilograms, height 20 metres, held at rest. Potential energy: E p equals m g h, which is 2 times 9,8 times 20, giving 392 joules. Kinetic energy: the ball is at rest, so v is zero and E k is zero. Mechanical energy at the top: E M equals zero plus 392, which is 392 joules.

Notice the choice hiding in that calculation: the height was measured from the ground, which means we chose the ground as the reference point where h equals zero. That choice is ours to make — the ground, a table top, the lowest point of a swing — but once made it must hold for the entire problem. Energy differences are what matter physically; the reference point just sets where the counting starts.

The questions for this section are with you now: both formulas stated in symbols and words, the definition of mechanical energy as a sum, the 392 joules reproduced, and the unit and scalar nature of every energy in sight.

## Subtopic: The Law of Conservation and Its Mechanical Special Case

First, the big law, which governs the entire universe and not merely this ball. The law of conservation of energy states: energy cannot be created or destroyed; it can only be transferred from one object to another or converted from one form to another. Total energy is never manufactured and never lost — it changes hands and changes clothing.

Now the special case that does today's work. In the absence of air resistance and friction — in the absence of any dissipative force — the mechanical energy of an object moving in the Earth's gravitational field is constant. It is conserved. This is the principle of conservation of mechanical energy, and it earns its own equation: E k one plus E p one equals E k two plus E p two — the kinetic plus potential energy at the first position equals the kinetic plus potential energy at the second position. Position one and position two are any two points on the journey you care to compare.

Understand what the principle does and does not claim. It does not claim the potential energy stays fixed, nor the kinetic — each changes constantly as the ball falls. It claims the SUM stays fixed: whatever potential energy is lost is gained as kinetic energy, joule for joule, at every instant of the fall. The two accounts trade; the total stands still.

And mind the fine print, because examiners certainly do. The word "conserved" applies to mechanical energy only while no friction or air resistance acts. The moment a dissipative force joins the problem, mechanical energy leaks away as heat and sound — total energy is still conserved, because the big law never breaks, but MECHANICAL energy is not. Questions signal the safe case with phrases like "ignore air resistance" or "a frictionless surface"; underline them when they appear, because they are your licence for the equation.

Take this section's questions now: the big law stated word-perfectly, the mechanical special case with its condition attached, the two-position equation, and the difference between total energy conserved always and mechanical energy conserved only without dissipation.

## Subtopic: The Falling Ball — Working the Energy Ledger

Run the ball's whole journey through the ledger, and watch the method do what equations of motion would need three separate calculations to achieve.

Position one, the rooftop: E p is 392 joules, E k is zero, E M is 392 joules. That total is now locked in for the entire fall, because air resistance is ignored.

Position two, halfway down, at 10 metres. Potential energy: E p equals 2 times 9,8 times 10, which is 196 joules — half the height, half the potential energy. The principle says the mechanical energy is still 392 joules, so the kinetic energy must be the difference: E k equals 392 minus 196, which is 196 joules. Exactly half the account has moved from the height column to the speed column. Now extract the speed: one half m v squared equals 196, so one half times 2 times v squared equals 196, giving v squared equals 196, and v equals 14 metres per second.

Position three, the instant before the ground. Height zero, so E p is zero and every joule sits in the kinetic account: E k equals 392 joules. Then one half times 2 times v squared equals 392, so v squared equals 392, and v equals 19,80 metres per second, to two decimal places.

Audit the whole journey in one line: at the top, 392 plus zero; halfway, 196 plus 196; at the bottom, zero plus 392. Three snapshots, one unchanging total — that audit line is exactly the presentation examiners reward, and writing it costs seconds.

One more observation, and it is the kind that turns a good learner into a dangerous one. Solve for the speed symbolically: one half m v squared equals m g h, and the mass cancels from both sides, leaving v squared equals 2 g h. The speed of a freely falling object does not depend on its mass. A 2-kilogram ball and a 5-kilogram ball dropped from 20 metres both arrive at 19,80 metres per second. The heavier ball carries more energy, but it also needs more energy to reach the same speed, and the two effects cancel exactly.

Work this section's questions now: the ledger at all three positions, the 14 and the 19,80 recovered from the energy differences, and the cancellation that frees the final speed from the mass.

## Subtopic: Reference Points, Leaks and the Habits That Bank the Marks

The method, compressed into four steps you can perform on any conservation problem. Step one: confirm the licence — no friction, no air resistance stated or implied. Step two: choose the reference point where h equals zero, usually the lowest point in the problem, and declare it. Step three: write E k one plus E p one equals E k two plus E p two, and fill in every term at both positions, writing zero explicitly where an energy vanishes. Step four: solve for the unknown and attach the unit — joules for energies, metres per second for speeds.

Now the traps, each named after the mark it destroys. Trap one: using 10 for g out of habit — CAPS uses 9,8 metres per second squared, and 392 joules becomes a wrong 400. Trap two: forgetting to square the speed in kinetic energy, or forgetting the half; one half times 2 times 14 squared is 196, not 28. Trap three: mixing reference points mid-problem — measuring the first height from the ground and the second from a balcony hands the ledger two different currencies. Trap four: applying conservation of mechanical energy where friction is explicitly present; when a question says a surface is rough, mechanical energy is NOT constant, and the correct statement is that the loss went to heat. Trap five: treating energy as a vector and attaching directions — energy has none.

Where does this tool go next? Everywhere. In Grade 11 it joins forces and work; in Grade 12 the work-energy theorem and dissipative-force calculations sit at the centre of Paper 1, and conservation thinking runs through momentum, electricity and even the photoelectric effect. The habit of writing an energy ledger — total in, total out, every leak named — is permanent equipment for matric and beyond.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the four steps in order, g at 9,8, the squared speed with its half, one reference point held for a whole problem, and the exact condition under which the mechanical total is allowed to change.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same law with the physics jacket off — because you have run this exact accounting every time you have handled pocket money with two pockets.

## Subtopic: The Two-Pocket Wallet

Picture a wallet with exactly two pockets. Every object that moves near the Earth carries one. The first pocket is the HEIGHT pocket: money goes in whenever the object climbs, and the amount is the mass times 9,8 times how high it sits above the floor you are measuring from. The second pocket is the SPEED pocket: money goes in whenever the object moves faster, and the amount is half the mass times the speed times the speed again.

Physics calls the money energy and counts it in joules. It calls the height pocket gravitational potential energy and the speed pocket kinetic energy. And it calls the total in the wallet — both pockets added together — mechanical energy. Nothing deeper than that: mechanical energy is just what you get when you empty both pockets onto the table and count.

Here is the rule the whole lesson stands on: as long as nothing rubs — no friction, no air resistance — the wallet is sealed. Money can move between the two pockets as often as it likes, but not one cent can enter or leave the wallet. Drop something, and coins stream from the height pocket into the speed pocket the whole way down. Throw something upward, and the stream runs the other way. The total never budges.

That is why the rule is called conservation: the total is conserved — kept safe. And the big version of the law is even grander: in the whole universe, energy is never created and never destroyed; it only moves between owners and changes its form. The sealed wallet is that law wearing its no-friction special case.

One small decision before any counting starts: you choose where "the floor" is — where height counts as zero. The ground, a desktop, the bottom of a slide. Any choice works. The only crime is changing floors halfway through the sum.

Quick check before the ball drops — the questions on this piece are with you now: which pocket is which, what seals the wallet, and who decides where zero height sits.

## Subtopic: Counting the Ball's Money

Now do the rooftop ball properly, coins and all. A 2-kilogram ball, held at rest, 20 metres above the ground. Floor chosen: the ground.

At the top, count the wallet. Height pocket: 2 times 9,8 times 20, which is 392 joules. Speed pocket: the ball is not moving, so zero. Total in the wallet: 392 joules. Seal it — no air resistance in this problem, so 392 is the total for the whole trip, and every later question is answered by asking where those 392 joules are sitting.

Halfway down, at 10 metres. The height pocket now holds 2 times 9,8 times 10, which is 196 joules — the ball has spent half its height money. A sealed wallet means the missing 196 joules did not vanish; they moved. Speed pocket: 196 joules. And a speed pocket holding 196 means half of 2 times the speed squared is 196, so the speed squared is 196, and the speed is 14 metres per second.

The instant before the ground: height pocket empty. All 392 joules are in the speed pocket. Half of 2 times the speed squared equals 392, so the speed squared is 392, and the speed is 19,80 metres per second — about 71 kilometres per hour, which is why you should not stand under rooftops.

Look at how little work that took. No timing, no acceleration, no equations of motion — just one total, counted once at the top, then tracked from pocket to pocket. Say the three snapshots aloud like a bank statement: 392 plus nothing; 196 plus 196; nothing plus 392. If your two pockets ever fail to add to the sealed total, an arithmetic slip is hiding somewhere, and the statement finds it before the marker does.

Your questions for this section are up now: count the wallet at the top, track the coins at halfway, and squeeze the speed out of the pocket at the bottom.

## Subtopic: Where the Money Leaks

Two loose ends, and they are the two things examiners most love to probe.

First: why did the ball's mass never matter to its speed? A 5-kilogram ball from the same roof lands at exactly the same 19,80 metres per second. Think it through with the wallet. The heavier ball starts richer — more than double the joules in its height pocket. But speed is expensive for a heavy object: filling the speed pocket to any given speed costs more joules, in exactly the same proportion. Richer wallet, pricier speed — the two effects cancel to the cent. That is why, with no air resistance, heavy and light objects fall together, a fact people have found hard to believe for centuries.

Second: the seal on the wallet is a promise with conditions. Rub the moving object against anything — rough ground, thick air — and the wallet springs a leak. The leaked joules are not destroyed; the universe never destroys energy. They dribble away as heat and a little sound: touch a bicycle's brake rim after a long stop and you are feeling exactly where the speed money went. But the leaked joules are no longer in either pocket, so the MECHANICAL total drops, and the sealed-wallet equation stops being true. That is why every exam question that wants you to use it says "ignore air resistance" or "frictionless" — that phrase is the seal, and you should underline it like it pays your marks directly.

So carry the whole lesson in three short sentences. Two pockets: height money, m g h, and speed money, half m v squared. Sealed wallet: no rubbing means the total never changes, only shifts between pockets. Leaks are heat: friction drains mechanical energy out of the wallet, though never out of the universe.

Here come the last questions of the lesson: why mass cancels, where leaked energy actually goes, which words in a question guarantee the seal — and one full wallet count from roof to ground, done the two-pocket way.
