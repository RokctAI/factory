**Thandi:** Time for the questions that come up a lot with this one. First: on the roller coaster, why does the 3 metres per second at the top matter so much? It looks tiny next to a 25-metre hill.

**Tutor:** Tiny in the account, decisive in the marking. Compute both terms honestly: the height contributes 200 times 9,8 times 25, which is 49 000 joules, while the motion contributes one half times 200 times 3 squared, which is 900 joules — less than two percent of the total. But drop it and your bottom-of-the-hill equation reads 100 v squared equals 49 000 instead of 49 900, giving 22,14 metres per second instead of 22,34. The answer looks entirely plausible, sails past your own sense check, and still loses the marks — an omitted term is a method error, not a rounding slip. There is also a principle at stake: the ledger's whole authority comes from counting EVERYTHING the object carries at point one. The moment you start judging terms as too small to bother with, you are no longer doing conservation of energy; you are doing estimation. Write every term, including the zeros; let the arithmetic, not your intuition, decide what is negligible.

***

**Thandi:** Next, the one that sounds like cheating: how can a curving, twisting track give the same answer as a straight drop? Surely the shape must do something.

**Tutor:** The shape does plenty — it just does nothing to the energy account. What the track controls is the JOURNEY: how long it takes, which direction you face, how the speed builds along the way. A steep face converts height to speed in moments; a lazy slope draws the same conversion out over many seconds. But the arrival speed is fixed by what was traded, and the trade is height for speed at a fixed rate — 9,8 joules per kilogram for every metre of drop, regardless of the route the metres took. That is why the syllabus says the path can be ignored: the only relevant quantities are the speed and the height above the reference point. There is one honest condition: the track must be frictionless, because a longer path in the real world means more metres of rubbing and a bigger leak — path independence is a property of the sealed account only. And notice what you gain: the equations of motion demand a straight line and constant acceleration, so for a pendulum's arc or a skate bowl's curve, energy is not merely the easier tool — it is the only tool you own.

***

**Thandi:** Third one: a ball thrown up at 15 metres per second — why does it land at exactly 15 metres per second? It feels like it should lose something on the trip.

**Tutor:** It feels that way because in daily life it does lose something — to the air. Strip the air away and watch the trade. On the way up, every metre of climb converts kinetic energy into potential energy at exactly 9,8 joules per kilogram per metre; the ball arrives at 11,48 metres with an empty speed account and a full height account. On the way down, the identical conversion runs in reverse, at the identical rate, over the identical metres. The account refills to precisely its starting balance, so the hand receives the ball at precisely 15 metres per second. Symmetry is not an extra assumption — it is conservation read twice, once in each direction. More useful still: at ANY height, the upward speed equals the downward speed, because both are set by the same balance at that height. Now let the air back in: it withdraws energy on BOTH legs of the trip, never deposits, so the return speed drops below 15 and the peak sits below 11,48. When an examiner asks how air resistance changes the answer, that is the expected reasoning — lower peak, slower return, and the missing joules now warming the air.

***

**Thandi:** Last one: when a problem says the track is rough, what are we actually supposed to write? The nice equation is suddenly illegal.

**Tutor:** Illegal is exactly the right word, and knowing it is illegal is most of the mark. Write three things. First, the diagnosis: friction is a dissipative force, so mechanical energy is NOT conserved on this track — E k one plus E p one no longer equals E k two plus E p two, and using the equality would be a physics error, not a shortcut. Second, the direction of the change: the mechanical energy DECREASES from the first point to the second — the final kinetic energy is smaller than the frictionless prediction, the crest reached is lower, the pendulum's far side falls short. Third, the destination: the missing mechanical energy was transferred to heat, and a little to sound, in the surfaces that rubbed — the law of conservation of energy still holds in full, because those heat joules are the missing mechanical joules exactly. At Grade 10 that reasoned account is the complete expected answer; the machinery for calculating the size of the loss is the work-energy theorem, which arrives in Grade 12. Until then, your job is to know precisely when the beautiful equation applies — and to say precisely why, when it does not.
