**Thandi:** Time for the questions that come up a lot with this topic. The first is almost a complaint: if the potential energy keeps changing and the kinetic energy keeps changing, what exactly is staying the same?

**Tutor:** The sum — and only the sum. Picture the fall as a running trade: at every instant the ball surrenders a little potential energy and receives exactly that much kinetic energy in return. Neither account is constant; each is changing as fast as the other, in opposite directions. What never moves is the total, 392 joules for our ball, fixed at the moment the fall began. That is why the equation compares totals at two positions — E k one plus E p one equals E k two plus E p two — rather than tracking either energy on its own. And the audit is worth performing every time: at 20 metres, 392 plus zero; at 10 metres, 196 plus 196; at the ground, zero plus 392. If your two numbers at any position fail to add to the original total, you have made an arithmetic slip, and the conservation principle itself has just marked your work before the marker could.

***

**Thandi:** Next one: why does the dropped 5-kilogram ball land no faster than the 2-kilogram one? Surely more energy should mean more speed.

**Tutor:** More energy, yes — two and a half times more, since potential energy is m g h and the mass appears as a factor. But speed is bought with kinetic energy, one half m v squared, and the mass appears there too, as the price per unit of speed squared. A heavier ball is richer AND faces higher prices, in exactly the same ratio. Write the conservation statement and watch it happen algebraically: one half m v squared equals m g h. The mass stands on both sides, so it cancels completely, leaving v squared equals two g h — an expression containing only the gravitational acceleration and the height. No mass anywhere. So identical drop heights give identical landing speeds, 19,80 metres per second from 20 metres, whether the object is a cricket ball or a brick. The exception you see in daily life — a feather drifting down — is air resistance breaking the no-dissipation condition, not the principle failing.

***

**Thandi:** Then a definitions worry: what is the actual difference between the law of conservation of energy and the principle of conservation of mechanical energy?

**Tutor:** Scope, and the presence of a condition. The law is universal and unconditional: energy cannot be created or destroyed, only transferred between objects or converted between forms. It holds during friction, during explosions, during everything — no exceptions exist anywhere in physics. The principle is a special case with an entry requirement: IN THE ABSENCE of friction and air resistance, the mechanical energy — kinetic plus gravitational potential — of an object moving in the Earth's gravitational field stays constant. Add friction and the principle's condition fails: mechanical energy drains away into heat and sound, so the mechanical total drops. The law still holds perfectly — those heat joules are the missing mechanical joules, cent for cent. In an exam, quote the law when asked about energy in general, and quote the principle, condition included, before using E k one plus E p one equals E k two plus E p two. Omitting the condition costs the mark, because without it the statement is simply false.

***

**Thandi:** Last one, about the reference point: if everyone may choose their own zero for height, how can everyone still get the same answer?

**Tutor:** Because the physics lives in differences, never in the absolute numbers. Suppose you measure our rooftop problem from the ground: the potential energy falls from 392 joules to zero, a change of 392. Your friend measures from the rooftop instead: the potential energy falls from zero to minus 392, a change of — again — 392 joules. The individual values disagree; the CHANGE, which is what feeds the kinetic energy, is identical, so both of you extract the same 19,80 metres per second. The zero is like sea level on a map: Johannesburg does not become higher when you measure from a different datum, and the drop from roof to ground does not change when you relabel the heights. Two working rules follow. Choose the zero that makes arithmetic easiest — usually the lowest point in the problem, so no negative heights appear. And having chosen, hold that choice for every position in the problem, because mixing reference points is the one way the freedom becomes an error.
