# Part 1 — Expert

This session takes chemistry into the medium where most of it actually happens: water. Four pieces of equipment by the end. First, WHY water dissolves ionic solids — the polar molecule doing the pulling, and the dissolution equations that record it. Second, electrolytes — which solutions conduct electricity, and what conductivity does and does not tell you. Third, precipitation — the reaction where two clear solutions meet and a solid crashes out — with the balanced equations examiners want. Fourth, the ion test kit: the standard laboratory tests that name the invisible ion hiding in a solution, and the four reaction types that organise all of it. The thread problem is a detective story: an unlabelled bottle of clear solution, and by the end we will identify its ion with two reagents and a fizz.

## Subtopic: Water the Puller-Apart — Ions in Solution

Why is water chemistry's favourite solvent? Because the water molecule is POLAR. In H2O, the oxygen end carries a slight negative charge and the hydrogen end a slight positive charge — one molecule, two charged faces. That structure makes water a molecular tow truck for anything built from charges.

Now put a crystal of table salt into it. Solid sodium chloride is an ionic lattice: positive sodium ions and negative chloride ions locked in a repeating grid. Water molecules crowd the crystal's surface and choose their targets by charge — oxygen ends toward the positive sodium ions, hydrogen ends toward the negative chloride ions. Tugging from all sides, they prise ions off the lattice one by one. Each freed ion is instantly surrounded by an escort of water molecules; that surrounding is called HYDRATION. The overall process — the ionic crystal breaking up into free ions in water — is called DISSOLUTION, or dissociation.

Record it as an equation, with the phase labels doing real work: NaCl solid, arrow, Na plus aqueous, plus Cl minus aqueous. Read it aloud: solid sodium chloride dissociates into aqueous sodium ions and aqueous chloride ions. The label s means solid; the label aq — aqueous — means dissolved in water, wearing the water escort. Those little labels are marks: an equation without them is incomplete.

Practise the pattern on two more. Potassium permanganate, the purple crystal: KMnO4 solid, arrow, K plus aqueous, plus MnO4 minus aqueous — the violet colour that spreads through the glass is the permanganate ion itself, wandering free. Sodium hydroxide: NaOH solid, arrow, Na plus aqueous plus OH minus aqueous. Check every dissolution equation two ways: atoms balance, AND charge balances — the charges on the right must sum to the charge on the left, which for a neutral solid is zero. Sodium's plus one and chloride's minus one sum to zero: balanced.

The questions for this section are with you now: the polar molecule described with its two faces, dissolution and hydration defined, the sodium chloride equation with correct phase labels, and a charge-balance check performed.

## Subtopic: Electrolytes — Solutions That Conduct

Here is an experiment you can picture completely. A beaker of solution, two electrodes standing in it, connected through a battery and a light bulb. If the bulb glows, the solution conducts; the brighter the glow, the better the conduction. That beaker is a conductivity tester, and it sorts all solutions into a hierarchy.

Distilled water alone: the bulb stays dark — almost no conduction. Dissolve table salt: the bulb blazes. Dissolve sugar instead: dark again, exactly like pure water. Same water, same-looking clear solutions, opposite results — and the difference is the entire concept.

Conduction in a solution requires mobile CHARGE CARRIERS, and in water those carriers are IONS. Salt dissociates into sodium and chloride ions, so a salt solution is a crowd of mobile charges, and the solution conducts. A substance whose solution conducts electricity is called an ELECTROLYTE. Sugar dissolves beautifully — but sugar is a molecular substance: its molecules separate from each other and drift into the water WHOLE, intact and electrically neutral. No ions, no carriers, no glow. Sugar is a non-electrolyte, and so is every substance that dissolves as neutral molecules.

The brightness of the bulb reads the CONCENTRATION of ions: more dissolved salt, more carriers, brighter glow. That makes conductivity a probe of how far a substance splits into ions — strong electrolytes like sodium chloride dissociate essentially completely; weak ones only partly; non-electrolytes not at all.

But mind the trap the syllabus states explicitly: conductivity is NOT a general measure of solubility. Sugar is enormously soluble and conducts nothing. Solubility asks how much dissolves; conductivity asks whether what dissolved carries charge. Two different questions — a substance can score full marks on the first and zero on the second.

Take this section's questions now: the tester circuit described, electrolyte and non-electrolyte defined through ions, salt against sugar explained at the particle level, and the solubility-conductivity distinction stated cleanly.

## Subtopic: Precipitation — When Two Clear Solutions Make a Solid

Two colourless solutions: silver nitrate in one beaker, sodium chloride in the other. Pour one into the other, and instantly the mixture turns milky — a white solid snows out of clear liquid. That solid is a PRECIPITATE, and the event is a precipitation reaction.

What happened at the particle level? Each solution was a crowd of free ions: silver and nitrate ions in the first; sodium and chloride ions in the second. Mixed, the crowds mingle — and one new pairing, silver with chloride, forms a compound that is INSOLUBLE in water. Insoluble means water cannot hold it as free ions, so every silver ion that meets a chloride ion locks solid, and solid silver chloride rains out. The other pairing, sodium with nitrate, stays dissolved — a spectator pairing that never leaves the water.

Write it as the full balanced equation, phase labels carrying the chemistry: AgNO3 aqueous plus NaCl aqueous, arrow, AgCl solid plus NaNO3 aqueous. Read it: aqueous silver nitrate plus aqueous sodium chloride give solid silver chloride plus aqueous sodium nitrate. The s on silver chloride IS the precipitate — that one label records the milky flash you saw.

This family of reactions has a general name: ION EXCHANGE. The two positive ions swapped partners — silver took chloride, sodium took nitrate — and the reaction was DRIVEN by one product escaping the solution as a solid. No insoluble pairing, no reaction: mix sodium chloride with potassium nitrate and every combination stays soluble, the ions simply mingle, and nothing happens at all.

So which pairings are insoluble? Grade 10 needs a working short-list, not an encyclopaedia. Carry these: all nitrates are soluble — nitrate never precipitates. Common sodium, potassium and ammonium compounds are soluble. The famous insolubles: silver chloride, silver bromide and silver iodide; barium sulphate; and most carbonates, like calcium carbonate and barium carbonate. That list powers both the equations and the ion tests coming next.

Work this section's questions now: precipitate defined, the silver chloride equation balanced with labels, the driving force of ion exchange named, and the soluble-insoluble short-list applied to fresh pairings.

## Subtopic: The Ion Test Kit and the Four Reaction Types

Now the payoff: the unlabelled bottle. A clear solution, ion unknown. The laboratory's standard tests turn precipitation into identification — each test is a reagent chosen to force a KNOWN insoluble pairing, plus an acid added first to clear out impostors.

The halide tests. To the sample, add dilute nitric acid, then a few drops of silver nitrate solution. A WHITE precipitate signals chloride — silver chloride. A CREAM precipitate signals bromide — silver bromide. A YELLOW precipitate signals iodide — silver iodide. White, cream, yellow: the colours deepen down the group.

The sulphate test. Add dilute nitric acid, then barium nitrate solution. A white precipitate that STAYS — insoluble even in the acid — signals sulphate: barium sulphate.

The carbonate test. Add barium nitrate and a white precipitate forms — barium carbonate. Now add nitric acid: this precipitate DISSOLVES, fizzing as it goes, releasing carbon dioxide gas. A white solid that dissolves in acid with effervescence is the carbonate signature.

Why the acid in every test? Because carbonate is the great impostor: barium carbonate and silver carbonate are also white and insoluble, so an untreated carbonate could fake a sulphate or chloride result. The nitric acid destroys any carbonate first — fizzing it away as carbon dioxide — so whatever precipitate survives is the genuine article. Acid first, reagent second, colour observed: that is the drill.

So the unlabelled bottle: acid added — no fizz, so no carbonate. Silver nitrate — nothing. Barium nitrate — white precipitate, unmoved by the acid. Verdict: sulphate. Two reagents, one non-event and one snowfall, and the invisible ion has a name.

Finally, file everything in this topic into the syllabus's four reaction types. Type one: PRECIPITATION — ion exchange driven by an insoluble product; today's whole story. Type two: GAS-FORMING reactions — ion exchange driven by a gas escaping, like a carbonate fizzing in acid. Type three: ACID-BASE reactions — driven by the transfer of protons, acid plus base giving salt plus water. Type four: REDOX reactions — driven by the transfer of ELECTRONS between particles; you can spot one because atoms CHANGE CHARGE, as when a neutral zinc atom becomes a plus-two zinc ion by giving two electrons away. The first three are ion-exchange cousins — partners swap, charges keep their values. Redox is the outsider: charges change, because electrons moved. Full machinery for acids, bases and redox arrives in Grade 11; today you need the four names, the four driving forces, and the charge-change fingerprint that picks redox out of a line-up.

The final questions of this part are with you now: each test matched to its reagent, colour and conclusion, the acid's purpose explained, the bottle verdict re-derived, and the four reaction types sorted by their driving forces.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same chemistry from your own kitchen — a spoon of salt, a spoon of sugar, and the glass of water that treats them completely differently.

## Subtopic: What Water Does to Salt

Stir salt into water and it vanishes. Stir sugar into water and it also vanishes. Same clear glass, same disappearing act — but underneath, two totally different stories, and the difference runs this whole lesson.

Start with what a water molecule actually is: a tiny magnet-like thing with two different ends. The oxygen side is slightly negative; the hydrogen side is slightly positive. Chemists say water is polar. Nothing neutral about how it behaves: each end of the molecule is on the hunt for the opposite kind of charge.

Salt is a stack of charged particles — a crystal grid of positive sodium ions and negative chloride ions holding each other tight. Drop that grid into a sea of two-ended molecules and the mobbing starts: oxygen ends crowd every sodium; hydrogen ends crowd every chloride. Tug, tug, tug — and ions pop off the grid one by one, each instantly wrapped in its own water escort. The wrapping has a name, hydration; the whole breakup has a name, dissolution. The salt has not died — its ions are alive and swimming, just separated and escorted.

Write the story the short way: NaCl solid becomes Na plus aqueous plus Cl minus aqueous. The word aqueous is just chemistry for "swimming in water". Say the sentence version too: solid sodium chloride splits into swimming sodium ions and swimming chloride ions.

Sugar's story has the same beginning and a different ending. Water molecules surround sugar molecules and carry them off — but sugar molecules are neutral and they do NOT break into charged pieces. They float away whole. Dissolved, yes. Split into ions, no. Hold that difference; the next section turns it into a light bulb.

Quick check — the questions on this piece are with you now: the two-ended molecule, who gets pulled apart and who floats whole, and the little labels s and aq.

## Subtopic: The Light Bulb Detective

Here is a test you could build at home. Two wires into a glass of liquid, a battery and a torch bulb in the line. If charges can move through the liquid, the loop is complete and the bulb glows. If not, darkness.

Plain water: dark. Salt water: bright. Sugar water: dark. The bulb is a detective, and what it detects is IONS.

Electricity through a liquid needs charged particles that are free to move. Salt water is full of them — every dissolved ion is a swimming charge, and the crowd of them carries the current across the glass. Substances like salt, whose solutions conduct, are called electrolytes. Sugar water has plenty of dissolved particles, but they are whole neutral molecules — no charge, nothing for the battery to pull. Sugar is a non-electrolyte. And the glow keeps score: double the salt, more swimmers, brighter bulb. Conductivity literally measures how many ions are in the water.

Now the catch question examiners adore: does a dark bulb mean nothing dissolved? No! Sugar dissolves magnificently — the dark bulb only says that what dissolved carries no charge. Dissolving is about disappearing into the water; conducting is about carrying charge through it. Two different talents. Salt has both, sugar has only the first, and a stone at the bottom of the glass has neither.

This is also your first taste of why sports drinks advertise electrolytes: your body runs its nerves and muscles on ion traffic, and sweating loses ions, not sugar molecules. Same word, same chemistry, different marketing budget.

Your questions for this section are up now: the detective circuit, why salt glows and sugar does not, what brightness measures, and the dissolving-versus-conducting trap.

## Subtopic: Snow in a Glass — and Naming the Mystery Ion

The finale is the best magic trick in Grade 10 chemistry. Take two glasses of perfectly clear liquid — silver nitrate solution and salt solution. Pour one into the other. The instant they touch, white snow appears in the water, swirling and settling. Solid, from two liquids.

No magic: a partner swap. Each glass was a crowd of swimming ions. Mixed together, silver ions met chloride ions — and that particular couple cannot swim. Silver chloride is insoluble: water's tow trucks are not strong enough to hold the pair apart, so every silver-chloride meeting welds solid, and the solid snows out. Chemists call the snow a precipitate. The other new couple, sodium and nitrate, swims on happily — nothing to see. One line records everything: silver nitrate solution plus salt solution give solid silver chloride plus sodium nitrate solution — and the label s on the silver chloride is the snow.

Once you can force snow on demand, you can run the trick backwards and NAME an unknown ion — this is real laboratory work, exactly as the syllabus wants it. Mystery bottle, clear liquid. First add a splash of dilute nitric acid: if it fizzes, carbonate just gave itself away, bubbling off as carbon dioxide. No fizz? Add silver nitrate: white snow says chloride, cream says bromide, yellow says iodide — the traffic-light of the halides. Still nothing? Add barium nitrate: white snow that shrugs off the acid says sulphate. The acid splash is the anti-cheating step — carbonates make white snow too, so you fizz them away first, and whatever snow survives is telling the truth.

And the four family names to file it all under, each known by what DRIVES it: precipitation — driven by a solid escaping the water; gas-forming — driven by a gas escaping, like that carbonate fizz; acid-base — driven by protons handed across; and redox — the odd one out, driven by electrons handed across, which you can spot because atoms change their charge. Partner-swap reactions keep every ion's charge; redox changes charges. That one fingerprint sorts them.

Here come the last questions of the lesson: the snow explained with the couple that cannot swim, the full test drill on a mystery bottle — acid, silver, barium — and the four families matched to their driving forces. Run the detective story once more in your head, and this topic is yours.
