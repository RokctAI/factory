**Thandi:** Time for the questions that come up a lot with this topic. First: salt and sugar both vanish into water, so people keep writing that sugar "dissociates" too. Where exactly is the line?

**Tutor:** The line is what the particles look like AFTER the vanishing, and the two words mark the two sides of it. Salt is an ionic solid — a lattice of charged particles. Water's polar molecules pull that lattice apart into its ions, so dissolving salt is dissolving PLUS dissociation: NaCl solid becomes Na plus aqueous and Cl minus aqueous, two kinds of charged swimmer. Sugar is a molecular substance — its crystal is a stack of complete, neutral molecules held together weakly. Water separates the molecules from EACH OTHER, but never breaks a molecule into charged pieces: each sugar molecule floats away whole and neutral. So both substances dissolve; only salt dissociates. Every downstream difference follows from that one distinction — salt solution conducts because it contains mobile charges, sugar solution does not; salt is an electrolyte, sugar a non-electrolyte. When you write about sugar, the safe sentence is: sugar dissolves as neutral molecules, so no ions form. When you write about salt, show the dissociation equation with its charges and its aqueous labels. Use the right verb for each and the marker knows you see the particle picture.

***

**Thandi:** Next, a wording trap: the syllabus says conductivity relates to concentration of ions but is not always a measure of solubility. Unpack that sentence?

**Tutor:** It is two claims, one positive and one cautionary. The positive claim: within solutions that DO contain ions, conductivity keeps honest score — more dissolved ions per litre means more charge carriers, means a brighter bulb in the tester. Compare a pinch of salt with three spoons of salt and the conductivity ranks them correctly. The caution: do not promote conductivity into a general solubility meter, because the link breaks the moment a substance dissolves without ionising. Sugar is the standing counterexample — spectacularly soluble, utterly non-conducting, because its dissolved particles are neutral. So a dark bulb has two possible readings: nothing much dissolved, OR plenty dissolved but none of it ionic — and only other evidence can split those cases. In exam language: conductivity measures the concentration of IONS in solution; solubility measures how much SUBSTANCE dissolves; the two agree only when the substance is an electrolyte. Quote the sugar counterexample whenever you state the caution — it is the cleanest one sentence of proof you can offer.

***

**Thandi:** Third: in the ion tests, why must the nitric acid go in BEFORE the silver or barium nitrate? Learners keep asking what would actually go wrong.

**Tutor:** What goes wrong is a false conviction — the wrong ion gets named. Here is the failure, step by step. Suppose the mystery solution actually contains carbonate, and you skip the acid and go straight to silver nitrate. Silver carbonate is ALSO an insoluble whitish solid, so a precipitate appears — and you triumphantly announce chloride. Wrong verdict, delivered with confidence. Same story with barium nitrate: barium carbonate is white and insoluble, a perfect impostor for barium sulphate. Carbonate is the great faker in this kit, able to imitate the positive result of almost every other test. The nitric acid is the interrogation that breaks the impostor before the line-up: acid destroys carbonate on contact, fizzing it away as carbon dioxide gas. Two outcomes, both informative. If the sample fizzes, you have FOUND your ion — carbonate — and the test is over. If it does not fizz, carbonate is now certifiably absent, so any precipitate that forms afterwards is genuine. Acid first is not ritual; it is the step that makes every later observation trustworthy. And notice the drill order the acid enforces: fizz check, then silver nitrate for the halide traffic-light, then barium nitrate for sulphate.

**Thandi:** Last one: with four reaction types to memorise, what is the quickest honest way to classify a reaction in the exam?

**Tutor:** Ask one question first: did any atom CHANGE ITS CHARGE between the left and right of the equation? That single check splits the four types into one-against-three. If a charge changed — a neutral zinc atom became a plus-two zinc ion, a plus-two copper ion became neutral copper metal — electrons were transferred, and the reaction is REDOX, full stop. If every ion keeps its charge from start to finish, you are in the ion-exchange family, where partners swap but nobody gains or loses electrons — and then the products tell you which member of the family you have. A new SOLID crashing out of solution: precipitation, driven by the insoluble salt escaping. A GAS bubbling away, like a carbonate fizzing carbon dioxide in acid: gas-forming, driven by the gas escaping. An acid meeting a base to give salt and WATER: acid-base, driven by protons transferred. So the full algorithm is two questions long: charges changed? redox. Otherwise, what escaped or formed — solid, gas, or water from acid plus base? That names the type and, as a bonus, names the driving force, which is usually the very next mark on the paper.
