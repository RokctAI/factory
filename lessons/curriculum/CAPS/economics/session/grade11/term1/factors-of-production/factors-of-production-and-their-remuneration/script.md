# Part 1 — Expert

Grade 10 named the four factors of production and left them standing in a list. Grade 11 puts them to work: the defining characteristics of each factor, the income each one earns, how that income is determined, and how the four payments add up to exactly the value a business creates. The topic then turns outward — to who in South Africa can actually reach these factors, and to the planning, participation and procurement machinery built to widen that reach.

## Subtopic: The Four Factors and the Four Rewards

Four factors, four rewards, and the pairing must be exact.

NATURAL RESOURCES — the older syllabus name is land — are the gifts of nature used in production: soil, minerals, water, fisheries, forests, climate, even the radio spectrum. Their remuneration is RENT. LABOUR is human effort, physical and mental, applied to production; its remuneration is WAGES and SALARIES. CAPITAL is the man-made means of production — machinery, buildings, tools, vehicles — goods that were themselves produced and that exist to help produce further goods; its remuneration is INTEREST. ENTREPRENEURSHIP combines the other three: it initiates, organises, decides, innovates and bears risk. Its remuneration is PROFIT.

Two precision points prevent the standard errors. Capital in economics means REAL capital — the machine, not the money; money is financial capital, a claim on real capital, and a country grows richer by building machines, not by printing rand. And labour is not the labourer: what is hired is a service, delivered by a person who keeps their rights.

Now connect this to the circular flow you built in Grade 10: households OWN the factors and supply them in the FACTOR MARKET, firms buy them there, and rent, wages, interest and profit flow back to households as factor income — which is why national income and national product are one quantity read in two directions.

The trap examiners set is the mismatched pair. Interest rewards capital, not entrepreneurship; profit rewards entrepreneurship, not capital. Dividends are not a factor reward at all — they are profit distributed to shareholders after it has been earned.

Take this section's questions now, and answer them with the pairing stated precisely: factor, reward, and the market in which the payment is made.

## Subtopic: Natural Resources and Labour Under the Microscope

Each factor behaves differently, and the behaviour explains the payment.

NATURAL RESOURCES have four defining characteristics. They are not produced. They are unevenly distributed: a country either sits on a platinum reef or it does not. They are geographically immobile — a coal seam cannot move to where demand is, which is why towns grow around deposits rather than the reverse. And their total supply is fixed, so quantity supplied cannot expand when price rises. That last characteristic explains the payment: with supply fixed, RENT is determined almost entirely by demand. A well-placed shop site on a busy Durban street commands high rent not because it cost more to make, but because many businesses want it and nobody can manufacture another one. Sort them also into RENEWABLE — fish, forests, water — and NON-RENEWABLE — coal, gold, platinum, which use depletes. South Africa is exceptionally well endowed with minerals but is water-scarce, with only a modest share of high-potential arable land; and mineral rights vest in the state under the Mineral and Petroleum Resources Development Act, so the nation, not the surface landowner, owns what lies beneath.

LABOUR has its own characteristics. It is INSEPARABLE from the worker, which is why labour markets are regulated in a way steel markets are not. It is PERISHABLE: a day of labour not worked is lost permanently — it cannot be stored, which is exactly why unemployment is a pure loss rather than a postponement. It is HETEROGENEOUS: a welder and a radiographer are not interchangeable. And it is mobile only with friction.

Distinguish quantity from quality. QUANTITY is the labour force: everyone employed plus everyone unemployed and actively seeking. QUALITY is human capital — education, training, health, experience — and it is quality that raises PRODUCTIVITY, output per worker per hour. Wages come from demand and supply, but three things shape the South African outcome: collective bargaining through unions and bargaining councils, the national minimum wage as a legal floor, and productivity, which sets the ceiling an employer can afford. Name the structural puzzle in any good answer: South Africa runs very high unemployment alongside a shortage of high-level skills — a MISMATCH between skills supplied and skills demanded, not simply too few jobs.

Work through this section's questions now — you should be able to give the characteristics of a factor and then use one of them to explain how its reward is determined.

## Subtopic: Capital, Entrepreneurship and the Residual

CAPITAL is produced by an act of self-denial. To build a machine, resources must be diverted from making consumer goods — precisely the movement along the production possibility curve you drew in Grade 10, giving up consumption today so the whole curve can shift outward tomorrow. Saving frees the resources; investment converts them into capital.

FIXED capital is used repeatedly — buildings, machines, trucks; WORKING capital is used up in one round — raw materials, stock, the cash held for wages. And capital wears out — depreciation, or in national accounting language, consumption of fixed capital. Gross investment is all spending on new capital; NET investment is gross minus depreciation, and only the net amount enlarges capacity. The reward to capital is INTEREST — the price of the funds that acquired it, shaped by the repo rate, the borrower's risk and the term of the loan.

ENTREPRENEURSHIP is the organising factor: it decides what to produce, hires the other three, innovates, and carries the risk that cannot be insured — that customers simply do not come. Its reward, PROFIT, is a RESIDUAL. Wages, rent and interest are contractual and are paid whether the business succeeds or not; profit is whatever remains, and it can be negative. That is the entire logic of ownership — the party with no guaranteed payment is the party that decides.

Now the arithmetic. A metal-pressing firm in Gqeberha, one year. Sales — the value of output — R4 000 000. Bought-in steel, electricity, transport and accounting — R1 500 000. VALUE ADDED is output minus intermediate purchases: 4 000 000 minus 1 500 000 gives R2 500 000, and that pot must be shared among the factors that created it.

The share-out: wages and salaries R1 600 000; rent on the premises R240 000; interest on loans R160 000; consumption of fixed capital R200 000. Those four total R2 200 000. Profit is the residual: 2 500 000 minus 2 200 000 equals R300 000. Add all five back and you land on R2 500 000 exactly — that is your check.

Read the shares: labour 64 per cent of value added, rent 9,6 per cent, interest 6,4 per cent, depreciation 8 per cent, profit 12 per cent. Two observations earn marks. Depreciation is income to nobody — it is capital used up, set aside to replace the presses. And the R1 500 000 of steel and electricity is not a factor payment: it is intermediate consumption, and it already paid wages and profits inside the supplying firms. Counting it twice is the error the national accounts exist to prevent.

The questions on this section are with you now — expect to compute value added, distribute it among the factors, and say why the residual is a residual.

## Subtopic: Local Economic Planning, Access and Empowerment Procurement

Factors of production exist somewhere specific, and the syllabus follows them there.

LOCAL ECONOMIC PLANNING begins with a constitutional duty: promoting social and economic development is an object of local government. Every municipality must adopt an INTEGRATED DEVELOPMENT PLAN — the IDP — and review it annually, with local economic development inside it. The question an IDP asks is a factor question: which factors does this municipality actually possess — arable land, a mineral deposit, a coastline, a rail line, a pool of skills — and how can they be combined HERE rather than exported raw for someone else to add value to? Beneficiation, agro-processing and tourism routes are answers to it.

ACCESSIBILITY OF MARGINALISED GROUPS names who is blocked from reaching the factors: women, youth, people with disabilities, rural households, informal traders. Name the barriers precisely, because vagueness scores badly. Land — without registered title or secure tenure there is no collateral. Finance — banks price risk, so no collateral means no loan and the venture never starts. Information and skills — knowing a tender exists, and being able to complete it, is itself a resource. Space and transport — settlement patterns inherited from apartheid put households far from work, so a large share of a low income is spent simply travelling to earn it. Networks — supply contracts flow along existing relationships, and outsiders enter them slowly. The remedies map onto the barriers one by one: development finance through bodies such as the Small Enterprise Finance Agency and the National Youth Development Agency, tenure reform, incubators, learnerships and public transport investment.

COMMUNITY PARTICIPATION is a legal requirement of the IDP process, run through ward committees and representative forums. Justify it economically, not morally: local knowledge lowers the cost of a good decision, and infrastructure a community helped choose is maintained rather than vandalised.

EMPOWERMENT AND PROCUREMENT closes the topic. Broad-Based Black Economic Empowerment is measured on a scorecard whose elements include ownership, management control, skills development, enterprise and supplier development, and socio-economic development; the score places a firm on a B-BBEE level, and the level affects its access to work. Preferential procurement gives the policy teeth: under the Preferential Procurement Policy Framework Act, state tenders are scored on a points system in which the large majority of points go to price and a fixed remainder to specific goals such as transformation — the familiar 80/20 and 90/10 splits, with 90/10 applying to larger contracts. The economic reasoning: the state is the largest single buyer in the economy, so shifting a share of its purchasing toward previously excluded suppliers redirects DEMAND — and demand is what turns idle factors into factor incomes.

The last questions of this part are in front of you now — planning, access barriers, participation and procurement, each answered with a named mechanism rather than a general sentiment.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same four factors from a taxi rank at half past five in the morning, where every one of them is standing in front of you.

## Subtopic: The Rank, the Driver, the Kombi and the Owner

One route, township to town, one minibus taxi. Everything the syllabus calls a factor of production is somewhere on that route.

Start with what the earth gave: the ground the rank stands on, the diesel that came out of a well, the stone in the tar. Nobody manufactured those — they were found. Whoever owns them gets paid RENT.

Then the people: the driver at the wheel, the marshal calling the queue. Hands and heads. They get WAGES.

Then the things people built to help do the work: the kombi, the tyres, the card machine. Somebody made those, and they are not for enjoying — they are for earning with. That is CAPITAL, and the money borrowed to buy it costs INTEREST.

And last, the person who looked at that route years ago and thought "there is a business here" — who borrowed, chose the route, hires and fires, and lies awake when the gearbox goes. That is the ENTREPRENEUR, and what they get is PROFIT.

Two things people get wrong, and you can now dodge both. Money is not capital: the R400 000 in the account is not the factor, the kombi it buys is. And profit is not a salary: the driver's wage is agreed in advance, the owner's profit is whatever survives to month-end.

Say the four and their rewards out loud once — then go to this section's questions and pair them off without hesitating.

## Subtopic: Splitting the Day's Takings

Now watch the money get divided, because that is all "remuneration" means.

In a month the kombi takes R60 000 in fares. Straight away, some of that was never really the taxi's money: R22 500 went on diesel, tyres and repairs bought from other businesses. Take it off. What is left — R37 500 — is what this taxi business actually ADDED. That is value added, and it is the pot the factors get paid from.

Share it out. Driver and marshal: R18 000. Rank fees and the yard where the kombi sleeps: R2 500. The interest part of the loan instalment: R4 000. And money put aside because the kombi is a month closer to needing replacing: R6 000. Add them: 18 000 plus 2 500 is 20 500, plus 4 000 is 24 500, plus 6 000 is 30 500. The owner gets what is left: R37 500 minus R30 500 — R7 000.

Look hard at that order, because it is the whole lesson. Everyone else was paid FIRST, at an amount agreed beforehand; the owner was paid LAST, at an amount nobody promised. Blow a gearbox and the leftover is a minus — the driver still gets R18 000 and the owner carries the hole. That is exactly why the owner decides.

One more thing: that R22 500 of diesel and tyres already paid a wage at the garage and a profit at the tyre factory. Count it here too and you count the same money twice — the mistake the national accounts are built to avoid.

Questions on the money split are coming to you now — do the subtraction yourself, find the leftover, and say why the leftover belongs to the owner.

## Subtopic: Whose Town Is It — Planning, Doors and Tenders

Last piece, and it is about who gets to reach any of this.

Every municipality writes a plan — the IDP — and the honest version of the question inside it is: what have we actually GOT here, and how do we make a living from it? A district with good soil and no packhouse sends its tomatoes away raw and buys the sauce back. Local economic planning is the attempt to keep that value at home.

Now the doors. Picture three people with the same good idea for a bakery. The first has a title deed, so the bank has something to hold and lends. The second has no title deed but heard about a school feeding tender in time to apply. The third has neither, lives an hour and two taxis from town, and never knew the tender was open. Same idea, three outcomes — and none of the difference is the quality of the idea. That is accessibility of marginalised groups in one picture: no collateral, no loan; no information, no tender; no transport, no time. And it is why showing up matters — the ward meeting happens whether you are there or not, and the road, the clinic and the trading stalls go where the plan says.

Then the biggest customer in the country: the state. It buys chairs, cement, chalk, catering and uniforms on a scale no shop matches. When it puts work out to tender it scores the bids — most of the points for the best price, and a set handful for empowerment goals like ownership, skills training and growing small suppliers. That is B-BBEE and preferential procurement doing their job, and the point is not generosity. Demand is what creates factor incomes, and this is demand being pointed at people the old system pointed away from.

Here come the last questions of the lesson — plans, doors and tenders. Land these and the topic is yours: four factors, four rewards, one value-added pot, and a real argument about who gets to reach the pot at all.
