**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! First one, and half the room asked it: if capital earns interest, why does the person who owns the machine get profit? Surely the machine's owner and the business owner are usually the same person?

**Tutor:** They very often are the same person, and that is exactly why the distinction has to be made in the accounts rather than in the person. Economics classifies INCOMES by the function performed, not by who happens to collect them. Lend R500 000 to a business and you perform the function of supplying capital finance: your reward is interest, it is contractual, and it is paid whether the business thrives or fails. Take the decisions, hire the staff, choose the product and carry the risk that customers stay away, and you perform the entrepreneurial function: your reward is profit, and nobody guarantees it. When one person does both — the taxi owner who put in her own savings and drives the business — she is earning two distinct incomes that happen to land in one pocket. Accountants make this explicit: interest on the owner's own capital is often shown separately from the surplus, precisely so the owner can see whether the business is genuinely profitable or merely returning what the money would have earned in a bank. In an examination, always ask what function is being performed. Supplying funds earns interest. Bearing risk and organising earns profit.

**Bianca:** Next one: value added confused a lot of us. Why do we throw away the steel and the electricity? The firm really did pay for them — surely that is part of what it produced?

**Tutor:** It is part of what it SPENT, but not part of what it CREATED, and value added measures creation. Follow the steel backwards. Before it reached the metal-pressing firm, a steel producer melted and rolled it, and in doing so paid its own workers, its own landlord, its own lenders and its own owners. Every rand of that steel's price is already somebody's factor income. If the pressing firm counted the full R4 000 000 of sales as its contribution, that steel income would be counted a second time, and if you added up every firm in the country that way you would get a national total wildly larger than the country's real output. So we subtract intermediate purchases and keep only the difference: R4 000 000 minus R1 500 000, which is R2 500 000. That R2 500 000 is exactly the amount available to pay this firm's own factors — wages R1 600 000, rent R240 000, interest R160 000, depreciation R200 000, and profit R300 000 as the residual. The five add back to R2 500 000, and that identity is not a coincidence; it is the definition. Add every firm's value added across the whole country and you get gross value added, the production side of the national accounts.

**Bianca:** This one got heated: someone said preferential procurement means the state pays more than it needs to, so it must be bad economics. How do we answer that in an exam without just picking a side?

**Tutor:** Answer it the way economists do — state the cost, state the benefit, and name the condition that decides which is larger. The cost is real and you should concede it: awarding points for anything other than price can mean the winning bid is not the cheapest bid, so the same budget buys slightly fewer classrooms or clinics. The benefit is also real. Excluded suppliers face barriers that have nothing to do with efficiency — no collateral, no track record, no networks — so they never receive a first contract, never build a record, and never become competitive. A procurement preference is a deliberate purchase of that first contract, and it is a one-off cost buying a permanent supplier. Now the condition: the policy pays for itself if the supported firms genuinely become efficient and competitive, and it becomes a permanent tax on the budget if they do not. That is precisely why the points system is capped — the large majority of points still go to price, and only a fixed remainder to transformation goals — and why enterprise and supplier DEVELOPMENT sits on the B-BBEE scorecard alongside ownership. Write it that way and you demonstrate the higher-order skill the examiner is looking for: not a slogan, but a trade-off with a stated condition.

**Bianca:** Last one: is community participation actually economics, or is it just politics that got put in the syllabus?

**Tutor:** It is economics, and there is a precise reason it belongs here. Every planning decision is a decision about scarce resources: one budget, one piece of land, one road that can go through one of three routes. To allocate scarce resources well you need information about where the need is most binding, and that information is held by the people living with the shortage, not by an official reading a spreadsheet in another town. Participation is the cheapest way to collect it. Second, there is the maintenance problem. Infrastructure has a running cost, and a facility a community regards as its own is watched, reported on and repaired, while one imposed without consultation is vandalised and abandoned — so participation lowers the whole-life cost of the same capital. Third, it changes who reaches the factors of production: a trading stall placed where traders actually work, or a road that shortens the trip to the industrial area, converts an idle worker into an employed one, and the difference between those two states is a factor income that either exists or does not. So the economic case is efficiency, durability and access. Say those three words in an exam and you have converted what looks like civics into a proper economics answer.
