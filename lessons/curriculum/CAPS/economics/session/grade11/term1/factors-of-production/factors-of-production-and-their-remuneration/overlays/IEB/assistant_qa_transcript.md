**Bianca:** Class questions before we finish! The most-asked one today: the salon owner put her own savings into the clippers and chairs. So is her money earning interest or profit? It cannot be both — can it?

**Tutor:** It can be both, and seeing why is the cleanest test of whether you understand factor incomes. Economics rewards FUNCTIONS, not people, and one person can perform two functions at once. Function one: she supplied capital finance. Had she lent that same R150 000 to a stranger's salon, she would have charged interest, and the interest would have been contractual — payable in good months and bad. Function two: she organises the business and carries the risk — chooses the strip, hires the stylists, absorbs the loss when the geyser bursts. That function earns profit, the residual nobody guarantees. When both functions live in one person, both incomes land in one pocket, and it takes deliberate accounting to see them separately. Good accountants do exactly that: they impute interest on the owner's own capital — what the money would have earned safely elsewhere — and only call the amount above that true profit. The test matters practically: if the salon's leftover is R6 000 a month but her savings could earn R2 000 in a bank without lifting a finger, her entrepreneurial function is really earning R4 000, and she can now judge whether the sleepless nights are being paid for. In any question, ask which function the sentence describes: supplying funds earns interest; organising and risk-bearing earns profit — even inside one skull.

***

**Bianca:** Next: someone challenged the value-added subtraction. The salon genuinely paid R13 500 for shampoo and electricity — real money, real invoices. How can we just wave it away?

**Tutor:** We do not wave it away — we refuse to count it twice, which is a different thing entirely. Trace one bottle of shampoo backwards. The manufacturer blended it, paying its own workers and its own landlord; the distributor trucked it, paying its drivers; the wholesaler stocked it, paying its staff and shareholders. By the time that bottle reaches the salon shelf, every rand of its price has already been recorded as somebody's wage, rent, interest or profit. If the salon now counted its full R45 000 of takings as its own creation, the shampoo's value would enter the nation's books a second time — and if every business in the country did the same, national output would inflate into fantasy, with the same rand counted at every stage it passed through. So the subtraction is not about doubting the invoice; it is about locating the creation. The salon's genuine creation is the transformation — shampoo, electricity and skill turned into a finished haircut a client pays for — and the measure of that creation is takings minus bought-in inputs: R45 000 minus R13 500, which is R31 500. That R31 500 is precisely the pot available to pay the salon's own factors, and the five shares add back to it exactly. Sum every enterprise's value added across South Africa and you have built the production side of the national accounts from the ground up.

**Bianca:** This one sparked a debate: if profit is just "the leftover", why does the owner deserve it? The stylists did the actual cutting all month.

**Tutor:** Put the word deserve aside for a moment and look at the architecture of the deal, because the answer is in who agreed to what. The stylists chose a contract: R15 000, fixed, payable in the best month and in the worst. That certainty is valuable — they can sign a lease and plan school fees on it — and it is the owner who sells them that certainty by absorbing the uncertainty herself. In the month the geyser bursts and takings halve, the stylists still collect R15 000 in full; the owner collects a negative number. Profit is the price of standing at the back of the queue. Now notice what that position buys the economy, not just the owner. Because she alone gains from good decisions and bleeds from bad ones, she is the one with the sharpest incentive to choose the right strip, the right prices, the right stock — so tying the residual to the decision-maker steers resources toward people who bear the consequences of their own judgement. And nothing forces the arrangement: a stylist confident in her own judgement can open her own chair tomorrow, trading her guaranteed R15 000 for a residual of her own. Some do, and that voluntary swap — certainty exchanged for risk plus control — is the whole institution of ownership seen in one salon. State it that way and you have explained profit, not merely defended it.

**Bianca:** Last one: the second half of the lesson — IDPs, ward committees, tender points — feels like government studies. Give us the one thread that makes it economics.

**Tutor:** The thread is this: a factor of production that cannot be reached earns nothing, so access is not a side topic — it decides whether factor incomes exist at all. Run each piece along that thread. The IDP is a factor inventory: a municipality listing the land, infrastructure and skills it holds and asking how to combine them locally, because factors combined at home generate local wages and profits, while factors exported raw generate them somewhere else. The barriers are the reasons a factor sits idle: land without title cannot serve as collateral, so the capital never arrives; a tender unseen is demand that never reaches the supplier; a job two taxis away is labour that cannot profitably be sold. Every one of those is an income that failed to come into existence. Community participation is an information mechanism: the residents know which missing culvert or unlit street is strangling the most activity, and collecting that knowledge through a ward committee is cheaper than discovering it through failed projects. And procurement is the demand lever: the state is the country's largest buyer, and pointing tender points at excluded suppliers is pointing demand at idle factors — with the sums capped so price still carries most of the weight. One sentence holds it all together: production needs factors, factors need access, and this machinery is South Africa deliberately engineering the access. Argue it along that thread and it is economics through and through.
