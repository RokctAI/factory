# Part 1 — Expert

The Grade 10 circular flow showed spending leaving households, reaching firms and returning as income. Grade 11 puts numbers on the arrows. This session builds the expenditure side item by item — household consumption C, government consumption G, and gross fixed capital formation I — and then assembles the three aggregates the syllabus names: gross value added, gross national expenditure and gross national income, with the exact arithmetic that converts each one into the next.

## Subtopic: Three Ways to Measure One Economy

Start with the identity that makes the whole topic hold together. Everything produced in a year is sold, everything sold is bought with someone's expenditure, and every rand of that expenditure becomes somebody's income. Measure PRODUCTION, measure EXPENDITURE, measure INCOME, and you must get the same total. That is why South Africa's national accounts publish three routes to gross domestic product and why the difference between them is reported honestly as a residual item rather than hidden.

The production method adds up VALUE ADDED — each producer's output minus the intermediate goods and services it bought in. Note the discipline you met with the factors of production: intermediate purchases are subtracted because they already carry the income earned in the supplying firm, and counting them twice is the classic error. The expenditure method adds up all spending on FINAL goods and services. The income method adds up the factor incomes — compensation of employees plus net operating surplus, plus consumption of fixed capital to convert net into gross.

Two more distinctions you must be able to state. FINAL versus INTERMEDIATE: flour bought by a bakery is intermediate, flour bought by a household is final; the same physical good is classified by its use, not its nature. And GROSS versus NET: gross totals include consumption of fixed capital — the wearing-out of buildings and machines — while net totals subtract it, and only the net figure describes how much capacity actually grew.

Finally, DOMESTIC versus NATIONAL. Domestic means inside South Africa's borders, whoever owns the resources. National means earned by South African residents, wherever the resources are. Get that pair straight now, because it is the single hinge on which gross national income turns.

The questions for this section are with you now — expect to be asked which method is being used, and to justify why intermediate goods are excluded from every one of them.

## Subtopic: Household Consumption (C) and Government Consumption (G)

FINAL CONSUMPTION EXPENDITURE BY HOUSEHOLDS, written C, is spending by households on goods and services to satisfy wants directly. It is by a wide margin the largest component of a country's domestic expenditure — around three-fifths of the total in South Africa — which is why the retail sector is treated as a barometer of the whole economy.

The syllabus wants the composition in four classes. DURABLE goods last three years or more: motor vehicles, furniture, fridges, computers. SEMI-DURABLE goods last less: clothing, footwear, tyres, household textiles. NON-DURABLE goods are used up quickly: food, beverages, fuel, medicine, electricity. SERVICES are consumed as they are produced: rent, transport, medical care, education, insurance, telecommunications. Durable and semi-durable purchases are postponable, which is exactly why they collapse first in a downturn and recover first in an upswing, while non-durables hold steady — households must eat regardless.

The determinants of C: disposable income above all, then wealth, interest rates and the cost of credit, expectations about employment, the size and age structure of the population, and taxation.

FINAL CONSUMPTION EXPENDITURE BY GENERAL GOVERNMENT, written G, is current spending by national, provincial and local government on goods and services — the salaries of teachers, nurses, police and clerks, and the stationery, fuel, medicines and electricity consumed in delivering public services.

Now the two exclusions that examiners test relentlessly. First, TRANSFER PAYMENTS are not part of G. Social grants, bursaries and subsidies transfer purchasing power from one household to another; no good or service is bought by government in exchange. The grant is counted later, when the recipient household spends it — inside C. Counting it in both places would double count. Second, government CAPITAL spending — a new school building, a new hospital, a new dam — is not in G either. That is investment, and it belongs in I.

Take the questions on this section now, and be ready to classify a specific item into C or G, with the two exclusions stated by name.

## Subtopic: Gross Fixed Capital Formation (I)

GROSS FIXED CAPITAL FORMATION is expenditure on FIXED capital — assets that will be used repeatedly in production for more than a year. Definition first, composition second, importance third; that is the order the syllabus and the examiner both use.

The composition: residential and non-residential buildings; construction works such as roads, rail, dams, ports and power stations; machinery and other equipment; transport equipment; information and communication technology; and cultivated biological assets such as orchards, vineyards and breeding livestock. Note what is excluded — buying an existing second-hand factory is not capital formation for the country, because no new asset came into existence; only the ownership moved. Note also the near neighbour: the CHANGE IN INVENTORIES, the rise or fall in stocks of raw materials and unsold goods, is capital formation but not FIXED capital formation, and it is listed separately.

Who does the investing? Three institutional groups: private business enterprises, which are the largest source; public corporations such as the electricity, rail and port utilities; and general government, which builds the roads, schools, clinics and water schemes.

Now the gross-net distinction, with numbers. Suppose gross fixed capital formation in a year is R900 billion while consumption of fixed capital — the depreciation of the existing stock — is R560 billion. NET investment is 900 minus 560, which is R340 billion. Only that R340 billion adds new capacity; the rest merely replaces what wore out. A country whose gross investment falls below its depreciation is consuming its own capital stock.

The importance of I: it raises the economy's productive capacity, which is precisely the outward shift of the production possibility curve you drew in Grade 10; it raises labour productivity by giving workers better equipment; it creates employment both while the asset is built and while it operates; and it lowers costs across every user of the infrastructure. Against gross domestic product of R6 300 billion, our R900 billion of investment is 14,3 per cent — well short of the roughly 30 per cent of GDP that the National Development Plan sets as the target for the investment South Africa needs.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — define, compose, and calculate net from gross without hesitating.

## Subtopic: GVA, GNE and GNI — the Chain of Conversions

Now assemble the aggregates. Work in rand billions.

GROSS NATIONAL EXPENDITURE, GNE, is total spending by residents on final goods and services: C plus G plus I plus the change in inventories. Take C of 3 900, G of 1 400, I of 900 and inventories of plus 50. That gives 3 900 plus 1 400, which is 5 300; plus 900 is 6 200; plus 50 is R6 250 billion. Some sources print the identical aggregate as gross domestic expenditure, GDE — treat the two labels as the same total and use whichever the question uses.

GNE is not yet gross domestic product, because residents also buy imports and foreigners also buy our exports. Add exports of 1 900 and subtract imports of 1 850: net exports are plus 50, so GDP at market prices is 6 250 plus 50, which is R6 300 billion. Notice what that means — an economy can spend more than it produces, and the excess arrives as a trade deficit.

GROSS VALUE ADDED, GVA, is the production-side total, measured at BASIC prices: the sum of every producer's output minus intermediate consumption, before taxes on products are added. To move from GVA to GDP at market prices, add taxes on products such as VAT and excise duties, and subtract subsidies on products. So GVA of 5 640, plus taxes on products of 700, minus subsidies of 40, gives 6 300 — our GDP again. Reverse it to go the other way: 6 300 minus 700 plus 40 equals R5 640 billion.

GROSS NATIONAL INCOME, GNI, converts domestic into national. Add primary income received from the rest of the world — the wages, interest, dividends and rent South African residents earn abroad — and subtract primary income paid to the rest of the world. Received 90, paid 240, so net primary income is minus 150 and GNI is 6 300 minus 150, which is R6 150 billion. South Africa's GNI is structurally a little below its GDP, because a substantial part of the profit generated by mines, banks and factories inside the country is paid out as dividends and interest to owners abroad.

The last questions of this part are with you now — run the chain in both directions, GVA to GDP to GNI, and say in words what each conversion actually adjusts for.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same three aggregates from a household budget book and a hardware shop, where every one of these totals is something you have already counted.

## Subtopic: The Country's Till Slip

Picture the entire country doing its shopping for one year, and every purchase landing on one enormous till slip. Sort the slip into piles, and you have basically done the national accounts.

The biggest pile is households: groceries, taxi fares, school fees, airtime, a fridge, a pair of school shoes, the rent. That pile is C, and it is roughly three rand in every five. Inside it, sort by how long the thing lasts. The fridge and the couch last for years — durable. The shoes and the tyres last a while — semi-durable. The bread and the electricity are gone by Thursday — non-durable. And the taxi ride and the doctor's visit leave nothing behind at all — services. Handy fact: when money gets tight, nobody stops eating, but everybody postpones the couch. That is why furniture shops feel a downturn long before spaza shops do.

The second pile is government doing its day-to-day shopping: paying the nurse, buying the chalk, filling the police van, keeping the clinic lights on. That is G.

And here is the bit people get wrong. The social grant your neighbour receives is NOT in G. Government did not buy anything with it — it handed money to a household. When your neighbour spends it on maize meal, it gets counted right there in the grocery pile. Count it twice and the country looks richer than it is. Same for a brand-new clinic building: that is not day-to-day shopping, that is building something to use for thirty years, and it goes in the next pile entirely.

Questions on the till slip are coming to you now — sort a few real items into C or G, and catch the grant before it slips into the wrong pile.

## Subtopic: Spending That Builds Tomorrow

Third pile. A hardware shop owner buys R200 000 of stock to sell, R80 000 of paint, and then a R400 000 delivery bakkie. Which of those is different?

The bakkie. The stock and the paint get sold and are gone. The bakkie is still there next year, and the year after, doing the work. Anything bought to be USED IN PRODUCTION for more than a year, rather than used up, is capital formation — and when a whole country does it, it is called gross fixed capital formation, the letter I.

So the pile contains: buildings and houses, roads, rail, dams, ports and power stations, machines, delivery vehicles, computers and networks, and even orchards and vineyards, because a fruit tree is a machine that makes apples for twenty years.

Two things to watch. Buying a second-hand bakkie from another business is not capital formation for the country — no new bakkie came into the world, the keys just moved. And the word GROSS matters. Our shop owner's old bakkie is wearing out; call that R150 000 of wear this year. Spend R400 000 on the new one and the business is only R250 000 better off in real capacity. For the country: gross investment R900 billion, wear and tear R560 billion, so genuine new capacity is R340 billion. Spend less than the wear and tear and you are quietly living off the machines you already own.

And why it matters more than anything else in the budget: this is the only pile that makes NEXT year bigger. Grade 10 drew it — this is the production possibility curve stepping outward.

Your questions on this pile are up now — spot the capital item, and work out the net figure from the gross one.

## Subtopic: Three Totals, One Country

Last piece: three big words, and each is just a total with one honest adjustment.

GNE — gross national expenditure — is the whole till slip added up: households 3 900, government 1 400, investment 900, plus 50 for stock that built up on shelves. Total R6 250 billion. That is what the residents of this country SPENT.

But some of that spending went overseas, and some foreigners spent here. So add what we sold abroad, R1 900 billion, take off what we bought in, R1 850 billion, and you get R6 300 billion — the value of what the country actually PRODUCED. Spend more than you produce and the gap is imports; that is a trade deficit in one line.

GVA — gross value added — comes at the same R6 300 billion from the production side, but priced before the taxman touches it: R5 640 billion of value created, plus R700 billion of VAT and excise, minus R40 billion of subsidies, back to R6 300 billion. Same economy, different price tag.

GNI — gross national income — asks a different question: not what was produced HERE, but what South Africans actually KEEP. South Africans earned R90 billion abroad, and R240 billion of profit and interest earned here was paid out to owners overseas. Net, R150 billion walks out the door, so R6 150 billion stays. That gap is not an accounting quirk — it is the price of an economy whose mines and banks have many foreign shareholders.

Here come the last questions of the lesson — three totals, three adjustments. Get these and you can read the front page of any national accounts release and know exactly what each line is doing.
