**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! This one came up three times: if a social grant is not counted in G, and it is also not produced by anybody, does it just vanish from the national accounts?

**Tutor:** It never vanishes — it is counted exactly once, in the right place, and the whole point of the rule is to make sure of that. Think about what actually happens. Government takes tax from one set of households and hands cash to another set. No factory ran, no nurse treated anybody, no good or service was produced in that transfer, so nothing has been added to national output and it would be wrong to record production. But the receiving household then buys maize meal, paraffin and school shoes, and those purchases are real spending on real goods that somebody really produced. That spending is recorded in C, at full value, in the month it happens. So the grant enters the accounts as household consumption, not as government consumption. Test yourself with the mirror case: a government salary IS in G, because the state bought a service — a nurse's working month — and that service is part of output. Transfer versus purchase is the whole distinction. Ask one question of any government payment: did the state receive a good or a service in return? If yes, it is in G. If no, it is a transfer and it waits for the household to spend it.

**Bianca:** Next: why does South Africa's GNI come out lower than its GDP? Someone in class said it means we are being robbed, and that felt too simple.

**Tutor:** It is definitely too simple, and the accurate version is more interesting. GDP measures production inside the borders regardless of who owns the resources; GNI measures income earned by residents regardless of where the resources sit. The bridge between them is net primary income — wages, interest, dividends and rent flowing in from abroad, minus the same flowing out. In our figures, R90 billion came in and R240 billion went out, so R150 billion of income produced in South Africa belongs to owners elsewhere, and GNI is R6 150 billion against GDP of R6 300 billion. Now the reason, which is not theft. Building a deep-level mine, a smelter or a national bank branch network takes enormous capital, more than domestic savings alone have supplied, so foreign investors funded a large part of it. They own shares, and shares earn dividends. The dividend leaving the country is the agreed price of the capital that arrived earlier, and that capital built assets and jobs that would otherwise not exist. The genuine economic question is not whether the outflow is unfair but whether the investment earned more for South Africa than it costs to service — and that is why a country's saving rate matters so much: the more of your own investment you fund yourself, the more of the resulting income you keep.

**Bianca:** Here is one that tripped up almost everybody in the practice paper: a company buys a second-hand factory for R50 million. Why is that not investment? Real money changed hands.

**Tutor:** Real money changed hands, but no new factory came into the world — and the national accounts measure what the economy PRODUCED, not what changed ownership. Gross fixed capital formation records new fixed assets added to the country's stock in the period. When the factory was originally built, the bricklayers, engineers, steel suppliers and crane operators all did work and earned income, and that was recorded as investment in that year. Selling it on ten years later moves the title deed; the country has exactly the same number of factories after the sale as before. If we counted resale as investment, a single building traded five times in five years would appear as five factories in the statistics, and the capital stock would be fiction. The same logic covers second-hand cars, existing houses and shares on the stock exchange — none of them is investment in the national accounts sense, however much they feel like investing to the buyer. One qualification worth knowing: the estate agent's commission and the conveyancer's fee ARE counted, because those are genuine services produced this year. So the rule is clean — new asset, count it; change of owner, do not; and the service of arranging the transfer, count that.

**Bianca:** Last one: how do we keep GVA, GNE, GDP and GNI apart under pressure? In the test they all just blur into one.

**Tutor:** Do not memorise four definitions — memorise one starting point and three adjustments, and always travel in the same direction. Start at GNE, because it is pure addition: C plus G plus I plus the change in inventories, R6 250 billion, and its plain-language meaning is what residents SPENT. Adjustment one is trade: add exports, subtract imports, and R6 250 billion becomes R6 300 billion of GDP at market prices, which is what the country PRODUCED. Adjustment two is the price tag: strip out taxes on products and add back subsidies, and R6 300 billion becomes R5 640 billion of GVA at basic prices, which is what producers actually received. Adjustment three is ownership: add primary income earned abroad, subtract primary income paid abroad, and R6 300 billion becomes R6 150 billion of GNI, which is what residents KEEP. Four words carry the whole set — spent, produced, received, kept — and each adjustment answers one question: who bought it, at what price, and who owns the income. In an exam, write the chain down the margin before you start, then read the question to see which link it is asking about. That single habit turns four blurry abbreviations into one map.
