Hey, it's Bianca — if this topic feels like alphabet soup, you are not alone. C, G, I, GVA, GNE, GNI, GDP, GDE. Eight abbreviations and they all look the same on the page. But there are really only three things happening, and once you see them the soup turns into a recipe.

Thing one: three piles of spending. Households, government, and building-things-that-last. That is C, G and I. Add them up, throw in the stock that piled up on shelves, and you have gross national expenditure. That is it. GNE is just addition.

Thing two: two honest adjustments. Trade — add exports, subtract imports — turns spending into production, which is GDP. And ownership — add what we earn abroad, subtract what leaves as foreign profit and interest — turns domestic into national, which is GNI. Every scary conversion in this topic is one of those two adjustments, or the price-tag adjustment that swaps basic prices for market prices by adding VAT and excise.

Thing three: two traps, and they are the same trap wearing different hats. Do not put social grants in G, and do not count intermediate goods anywhere. Both are the double-counting trap — money that already got counted somewhere else. If you can explain why a grant is not in G, you can explain half this topic.

The habit that saves marks: after every calculation, add your pieces back up and check they return to the total you started from. C plus G plus I plus inventories should give you back your GNE. Value added plus taxes minus subsidies should give you back your GDP. Two extra lines of arithmetic, and they catch almost every slip.

Three piles, two adjustments, one double-counting trap. You've got this!
