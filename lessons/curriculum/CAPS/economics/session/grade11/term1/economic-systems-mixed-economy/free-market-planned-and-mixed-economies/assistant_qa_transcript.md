**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! First one, and it caused an argument: if the free market is so efficient, why does every country in the world interfere with it? Are they all just being sentimental?

**Tutor:** Not sentimental — corrective, and there are four precise reasons a market left entirely alone produces outcomes even its admirers reject. First, public goods. Street lighting, defence and policing are non-excludable: once provided, nobody can be kept from benefiting, so no rational person pays voluntarily and a pure market supplies almost none of them. Second, externalities. A factory discharging into a river imposes real costs on downstream users that never appear in its own accounts, so its price is too low and it produces too much; the market is efficient at the wrong target. Third, market power. Competition is what makes markets discipline producers, but competition tends to destroy itself — the successful firm grows, rivals exit, and the survivor raises price and reduces output, which is why South Africa has a Competition Commission. Fourth, distribution. The market answers "for whom" by ability to pay, and a person born without assets, skills or health has very little to pay with; the result can be perfectly efficient and still leave children unschooled. Notice that three of those four are efficiency arguments, not fairness arguments. That is the sophisticated version of the answer, and it is the one examiners reward: intervention is not the opposite of markets, it is the repair kit markets need to work.

**Bianca:** Next: if central planning has such big problems, how did planned economies ever industrialise so fast? Someone said the strengths in the textbook are just there for balance.

**Tutor:** They are there because they are real, and the historical record is genuinely instructive. A planning authority that owns the means of production can do something no market can do quickly: move an enormous share of national output out of consumption and into capital formation by decision, without waiting for anyone to choose to save. Build steel mills, dams, railways and power stations on that scale and heavy industry grows very fast — that is the outward shift of the production possibility curve you drew in Grade 10, purchased by compressing today's consumption. Planning also suppresses duplication, guarantees employment because the state is the employer, and can deliver near-universal basic schooling and health care regardless of income. So the strengths are not decoration. The problem appears at the next stage. Building more of a known thing needs mobilisation, which planning does well. Deciding WHICH things, in what varieties, at what quality, for changing tastes, needs information that only millions of prices generate — and that is what a planning office cannot manufacture. So planned economies historically performed strongly at catching up and poorly at consumer variety and innovation. Say exactly that in an answer and you have shown you understand the mechanism instead of taking a side.

**Bianca:** This confused a lot of us: mixed and dualistic. Aren't they the same thing? Both sound like "two things at once".

**Tutor:** They both mean "two things at once", but they are two completely different pairs, and mixing them up is one of the most common errors in this topic. MIXED describes who decides: private ownership and the price mechanism operating alongside state ownership, regulation and provision. It is a statement about the SYSTEM. Almost every country is mixed; they differ only in the proportions, and South Africa's mix shows up as private firms alongside state-owned electricity, rail and port utilities, with regulators policing markets and a large grant system redistributing income. DUALISTIC describes how the economy is STRUCTURED: a modern, formal, capital-intensive sector coexisting with an informal, survivalist, low-productivity one — the office tower and the airtime table, in the same currency, under the same laws. A country can be mixed without being sharply dualistic, and the difference matters for policy. Fixing the mix means changing how much government owns, regulates or redistributes. Fixing dualism means something else entirely: building bridges between the two sectors through land reform, skills, small-business finance, collateral substitutes and transport. If an examiner asks about dualism and you write about state-owned enterprises, you have answered a different question.

**Bianca:** Last one: someone asked whether South Africa should just pick a side — go fully free-market or fully planned — instead of being stuck in the middle. How do we handle a question like that?

**Tutor:** Handle it as an economist rather than as a supporter: neither extreme has a mechanism for the problem the other one solves, so a country that picked either would immediately have to reinvent the other. A fully free-market South Africa would still need public goods, still need someone to bear the cost of pollution, still need competition policing, and would still leave "for whom" answered by ability to pay in a country where a large share of people hold no productive assets. A fully planned South Africa would gain equality of provision and lose the price signals that tell an economy what to make, and the shortages would not be a policy failure but a structural consequence. So the real question is never market versus plan; it is where each boundary sits and how well the state performs the specific tasks assigned to it. And here the dualism matters, because it sharpens the answer: an economy with a large informal sector needs markets that outsiders can actually enter — licences that are cheap, credit that does not demand collateral nobody has, and transport that makes distant jobs reachable. That is a market-widening argument and a state-capacity argument at the same time. Write your answer with that structure — the boundary, the task, the capacity — and you will produce analysis instead of allegiance.
