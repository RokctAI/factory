# Part 1 — Expert

Every society, however it is organised, faces the same three questions: what to produce, how to produce it, and for whom. An ECONOMIC SYSTEM is simply the machinery a country uses to answer them. This session sets out three systems — the free market, the centrally planned economy, and the mixed or dualistic economy — judges each against the three questions, and then places South Africa precisely: a mixed economy, and a dualistic one.

## Subtopic: The Three Questions and the Test They Set

The three economic questions arise directly from scarcity, which Grade 10 established: unlimited wants, limited resources, so choices are unavoidable.

WHAT to produce, and in what quantities? A country cannot build both the maximum housing and the maximum weaponry. HOW to produce it? Labour-intensive methods that employ many people, or capital-intensive methods that use machines and few workers — a live question in a country with high unemployment. FOR WHOM? Once output exists, who gets it, and on what basis — ability to pay, need, effort, or political rank?

Systems differ on one underlying variable: who owns and controls the MEANS OF PRODUCTION — the land, mines, factories, machines and infrastructure with which everything is made. Private individuals and companies, or the state on behalf of all? Ownership determines who has the right to decide, and the right to decide is what answers the three questions.

From that root grow the features you must be able to compare across systems: the ownership of the means of production; the mechanism that co-ordinates decisions, whether prices or plans; the role of the profit motive; the degree of competition; the extent of consumer choice; and the scope of government.

One clarification prevents a common error. An economic system is not the same thing as a political system. Economic systems are classified by ownership and co-ordination, political systems by how power is held and transferred. The two often travel together, but they are separate axes and an examiner will reward you for saying so.

Take this section's questions now — state the three questions in the correct form, and identify ownership of the means of production as the variable that separates one system from another.

## Subtopic: The Free Market Economy

The FREE MARKET system — capitalism — rests on private ownership of the means of production. Its defining features: private property protected by law; freedom of enterprise and freedom of choice; the profit motive as the driver of activity; competition among many buyers and sellers; and a minimal, umpire-like state confined to law, order and defence.

Its co-ordinating mechanism is the PRICE MECHANISM, and this is what a top answer explains rather than merely names. Nobody instructs the economy. A rise in demand for a good raises its price; the higher price raises profit; higher profit attracts producers and resources into that industry; supply expands and price settles again. Prices simultaneously carry information — what is scarce — and incentive — what is worth producing. That is why the mechanism is called the invisible hand.

Now the three questions. WHAT is decided by consumers, through their spending: consumer sovereignty, with each rand acting as a vote. HOW is decided by producers seeking least cost, so they choose whichever combination of labour and capital is cheapest. FOR WHOM is decided by income: those with the most purchasing power obtain the most output.

Strengths: efficiency, because loss-making producers are eliminated; innovation, because new products earn profit; wide consumer choice; and rapid, automatic response to changing wants, with no bureaucracy required.

Weaknesses, and each must be stated as a mechanism. INEQUALITY: "for whom" is answered by ability to pay, so those born without assets or skills receive little, and the outcome can be efficient and unjust at the same time. PUBLIC GOODS: street lighting and defence are non-excludable, so nobody will pay for them voluntarily and the market under-supplies them. EXTERNALITIES: a factory that pollutes a river does not pay for the damage, so the price is wrong and too much is produced. MARKET POWER: successful firms grow, competition weakens and monopolies exploit consumers. And INSTABILITY: unregulated markets generate booms and recessions.

The questions for this section are with you now — explain the price mechanism as a process, and answer all three economic questions in free-market terms.

## Subtopic: The Centrally Planned Economy

The CENTRALLY PLANNED system — variously called command, socialist or communist — rests on state ownership of the means of production. Its features: land, mines and factories owned collectively; a central planning authority that sets output targets, often in multi-year plans; prices administered by the state rather than discovered in markets; production for use rather than for profit; and employment and basic provision guaranteed.

The three questions are answered by the same body. WHAT is decided by the planning authority according to its assessment of social need. HOW is decided by the plan, which allocates the labour, machinery and raw materials each enterprise may use. FOR WHOM is decided by the state, in principle according to need, distributed through wages, rations and free or heavily subsidised services.

Strengths, stated fairly: income and wealth can be distributed far more equally; unemployment can in principle be eliminated because the state is the employer; basic needs such as housing, health care and education can be guaranteed regardless of income; national resources can be mobilised very rapidly toward a chosen objective, which is why planned economies have achieved fast industrialisation; and there is no wasteful duplication of advertising and competing brands.

Weaknesses, and these too are mechanisms rather than opinions. The INFORMATION problem is the deepest: no central office can know the millions of local facts about wants and costs that prices summarise automatically, so plans are always partly wrong. The INCENTIVE problem: with no profit and no risk of failure, there is little reward for effort, quality or invention. SHORTAGES AND SURPLUSES follow, because administered prices cannot adjust — hence queues for some goods and warehouses of unwanted others. Consumer choice narrows to what the plan produced. And the bureaucracy needed to run the plan is itself costly and slow to correct errors.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — set the planned answers against the market answers question by question, and give one strength and one weakness with its mechanism attached.

## Subtopic: The Mixed and Dualistic Economy — South Africa

No economy is purely one or the other. Every real economy is MIXED: private ownership and enterprise operate alongside state ownership, regulation and provision.

In a mixed economy the three questions receive divided answers. WHAT is decided mainly by consumers through markets, but government intervenes where markets fail — supplying public goods such as roads and policing, and merit goods such as basic education and health care that would otherwise be under-consumed. HOW is decided mainly by firms pursuing least cost, but within rules: labour legislation, environmental regulation, competition law and health and safety standards. FOR WHOM begins with income earned in the market, and is then modified by redistribution — progressive personal income tax, social grants, free basic water and electricity allowances, no-fee schools and public health care.

South Africa fits precisely. The Constitution protects property while also entrenching socio-economic rights. Most production is by private firms, yet major state-owned enterprises operate in electricity, rail, ports, broadcasting and postal services. Markets are regulated by bodies including the Competition Commission, the National Credit Regulator and the sector regulators for energy and communications. And redistribution is substantial: a progressive tax system funds one of the largest social grant systems in the developing world.

Then the second word, and Grade 11 must handle it. South Africa is also DUALISTIC: two economies coexist inside one country. The FIRST is modern, formal, capital-intensive and globally connected — listed companies, deep-level mines, sophisticated banks, commercial farms, high-skill services. The SECOND is informal and survivalist — spaza shops, street trading, subsistence plots, casual piece work, unregistered services — with low productivity, no written contracts, no access to bank credit and little capital per worker.

Explain the dualism rather than merely describing it. Its roots are structural: land dispossession and the migrant labour system concentrated ownership and skills; apartheid spatial planning placed the majority far from economic centres; and the education system delivered unequal skills, so entry to the first economy requires qualifications the second economy could not obtain. The result is an economy that is sophisticated and impoverished at once, and every South African policy debate — from land reform to small-business support to public transport — is an argument about the bridge between the two.

The final questions of this part are with you now — the mixed answers to the three questions, the South African institutions that deliver them, and dualism explained by its causes rather than listed as a feature.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same three systems from a school kitchen, where the same three questions get asked every single morning.

## Subtopic: Three Ways to Run the Kitchen

One school, one kitchen, hungry learners. Three questions have to be answered before anyone eats. What do we cook? How do we cook it — twenty volunteers with knives, or one big machine? And who gets a plate?

Kitchen number one is the tuckshop. Nobody is in charge. Learners buy what they feel like, the seller notices vetkoek sells out by nine and pies do not, so tomorrow there are more vetkoek and fewer pies. Nobody sent a memo — the till did the talking. Learners with money eat well; learners without money watch. That is the free market: prices carry the message, profit carries the motivation, and your wallet decides your plate.

Kitchen number two is the feeding scheme. One official decides the menu for the whole term, one budget buys the ingredients, one recipe is followed, and every learner gets the same plate whether they have money or not. Nobody goes hungry. But if the official's menu is samp on Tuesdays and everybody hates samp, you eat samp all term — because there is no till to tell anyone otherwise. That is the centrally planned economy: fairer plates, worse information.

Kitchen number three is what almost every real school actually does: a feeding scheme for everyone AND a tuckshop next door. That is the mixed economy, and South Africa is one.

Questions on the three kitchens are coming to you now — match each kitchen to its system, and say who decides the menu in each one.

## Subtopic: What the Till Knows and the Memo Doesn't

Let us stay in the kitchen, because the deepest idea in this topic hides there.

A price is a message. When vetkoek sells out by nine, that sold-out counter is telling the seller something nobody had to write down: people want more of this. When the pies go stale, that is a message too. Multiply that by every shop in the country and prices are carrying millions of little messages every day, for free, about what is wanted and what is scarce.

The planning office has no till. It has to guess what forty million people want, and it has to guess again for every product. Some of the guesses are good. Many are not — and when the guess is wrong, the price cannot fix it, because the price was fixed by the office. So you get queues for the thing everyone wants and warehouses full of the thing nobody wants. Not because the planners were foolish, but because no office can know what a million tills know.

Now be just as fair the other way. The till is a brilliant listener with one cruel habit: it only hears money. A hungry learner with no coins sends no message at all, so the tuckshop does not cook for her. The market is not being unkind — it genuinely cannot hear a want that has no rand behind it. That single sentence explains why every country on earth keeps a feeding scheme alongside its tuckshop.

Your questions on messages and memos are up now — say what a price does that a plan cannot, and what a plan does that a price will not.

## Subtopic: Two Economies in One Country

Last piece, and it is the one that makes South Africa South Africa.

Walk out of a glass office tower in Sandton and drive an hour. Inside the tower: computers, contracts, medical aid, a pension, a bank that knows your name. An hour away: a woman selling airtime and sweets from a table, no contract, no medical aid, no bank loan available to her at any price, and R400 of stock as her entire capital.

Same country. Same currency. Same laws on paper. Two completely different economies — and that is what dualistic means: a modern formal economy and a survivalist informal one, living side by side.

The tempting explanation is effort, and it is wrong. Look at what actually separates the two. Land and ownership were taken and concentrated over generations. People were made to live far from where the work is, so getting to a job costs a big slice of a small income. Schools were deliberately unequal, so the qualification the tower asks for was never available. And banks lend against collateral, so the person with nothing to pledge cannot borrow the very thing that would let her grow. Four structural walls, none of them about how hard anyone tries.

Which is why South Africa runs a mixed system on purpose. The market builds the tower. Grants, no-fee schools, free basic services, land reform, small-business finance and public transport are the attempts at a door between the two economies. Whether those doors work is the argument in every election — but now you can name what the argument is actually about.

Here come the last questions of the lesson — mixed and dualistic, side by side. Land these and you can classify any country in the world in three sentences, and explain your own in five.
