**Bianca:** Let's take the class questions before we finish! First up: a learner's uncle says the price of cooking oil "was put up by the shops being greedy". After today's lesson, how should we think about a claim like that?

**Tutor:** Test it against the price mechanism, because the mechanism gives you a better explanation and a way to check it. Greed is constant — shopkeepers did not become greedier the month oil went up — so a constant cannot explain a change. What does change is scarcity, and the price is the messenger that reports it. Suppose a poor sunflower harvest or a disruption to imported oil cuts supply: the same number of buyers now chases fewer bottles, the price climbs, and two useful things happen at once. Consumers get the information — use less, substitute where you can — and producers get the incentive — crushing sunflower seed now pays better, so plant more, import more, enter the industry. The high price is already organising its own cure. Here is the check that separates the explanations: if greed were the cause, the price would stay high forever, since greed never subsides; if scarcity is the cause, the price should ease as new supply arrives — and that is what we in fact observe, season after season. One caution to keep your answer honest: where competition is weak — one dominant supplier, or firms colluding — prices can indeed be held above what scarcity justifies, and that is exactly why a mixed economy fields a Competition Commission. So the sophisticated answer is: in a competitive market, blame scarcity and expect correction; where competition has died, suspect market power and call the referee.

**Bianca:** Next question: the textbook lists "no wasteful duplication of advertising and competing brands" as a strength of planning. But we also said competition is what disciplines producers. Which is it? Are five brands of washing powder a waste or not?

**Tutor:** Both claims are true, and holding them together is exactly the kind of thinking this topic rewards. Look at what the five brands cost: five marketing budgets, five packaging lines, five head offices — real resources that a single planned factory would not spend. Counted that way, duplication is genuine waste, and the planners' point stands. Now look at what the five brands buy: each firm knows you can defect to a rival tomorrow, so each must hold its quality up and its price down, and each is hunting for the improvement that will win your loyalty — which is where better products come from. The single planned factory spends nothing on marketing and answers to nobody; if its washing powder is mediocre, you buy mediocre washing powder, this year and next. So the honest comparison is not waste versus no waste; it is the cost of duplication against the value of discipline and discovery. History's verdict is fairly clear: the discipline is usually worth more than the duplication costs, but not always and not everywhere — which is why even strongly market-oriented economies deliver some things through single public providers, like water pipes, where duplication truly would be absurd. Answer with that trade-off, name both sides, and you have turned a trick question into a distinction-level paragraph.

**Bianca:** Third one, and it is the classic mix-up: half the class keeps writing "South Africa is dualistic" when the question asks about the mixed economy, and the other half does the reverse. Give us the cure.

**Tutor:** The cure is to attach each word to the question it answers. MIXED answers: who decides? It is a statement about the system's machinery — private markets and the price mechanism making most decisions, with the state owning some enterprises, regulating the rest and redistributing income. Evidence for mixed is institutional: the Constitution protecting property alongside socio-economic rights, state-owned utilities beside private firms, the Competition Commission, the grant system. DUALISTIC answers a different question: what does the economy look like on the ground? It is a statement about structure — a formal, capital-intensive economy and an informal, survivalist one running in parallel. Evidence for dualistic is observational: the fintech office and the fruit crate, contracts versus no contracts, credit versus the collateral wall. Now the test that ends the confusion: could a country be one without the other? Yes — a rich European economy is thoroughly mixed but barely dualistic, since its informal sector is tiny. So the words cannot be synonyms. When a question mentions ownership, regulation, redistribution or state enterprises, it is asking about the mix. When it mentions informality, survivalist trade, credit exclusion or two economies, it is asking about dualism. Underline the cue words in the question before you write and the two essays will never swap bodies again.

**Bianca:** Last one: a learner asked whether the informal sector is actually a problem at all — her mother's chisa nyama supports the whole family. Should the goal really be to make the second economy disappear?

**Tutor:** That question deserves a careful answer, because the informal sector is simultaneously an achievement and a symptom, and policy has to respect both halves. The achievement is real: her mother's chisa nyama is entrepreneurship under the hardest possible conditions — it feeds a family, serves a community, and does it without subsidies, and millions of livelihoods stand on exactly such businesses. Nobody sensible wants that energy to disappear. The symptom is also real: most informal businesses are not informal by preference. The chisa nyama likely cannot get a loan to buy a bigger fridge, cannot bid for a catering contract without registration, cannot survive one theft or one illness, and earns its owner no pension. Informality is what an economy produces when the formal side's doors — credit, premises, registration, markets — are too heavy to push open. So the goal is not to make the second economy disappear; it is to make the WALLS disappear, so that a survivalist business can become a growing one on its own terms: registration that costs little and takes a morning, lending that accepts a track record instead of title deeds, trading spaces with power and water, and municipal rules that treat a trader as an economic asset rather than a nuisance. Judge any policy by that standard — does it formalise opportunity, or just police informality? — and you will reason better than most of the debate does.
