# Part 1 — Expert

This session maps the economic structure of South Africa: the three sectors that produce everything the country makes, the way those sectors have shifted over a century, and the infrastructure that carries all of it — the power lines, railways, ports and pipes without which no sector can work. By the end you will be able to classify any business into its sector, read South Africa's structure from the data, and argue, the way examiners want, why infrastructure and access to services decide who gets to participate in the economy at all.

## Subtopic: Three Sectors, One Economy

Every act of production in South Africa belongs to one of three sectors, sorted by how close the activity stands to nature.

The primary sector takes raw materials directly from nature. Mining gold on the West Rand, growing maize in the Free State, catching hake off the West Coast, felling timber in Mpumalanga — if it comes straight out of the ground, the sea or the soil, it is primary.

The secondary sector transforms those raw materials into made goods. A factory in Rosslyn assembling bakkies, a mill turning wheat into flour, a plant near Vanderbijlpark rolling steel, and construction — building houses, roads and stadiums — all of this is secondary. Manufacturing is its heart.

The tertiary sector produces services rather than physical goods. Banking in Sandton, a taxi ride to work, a spaza shop selling airtime, a teacher teaching, a nurse nursing, a call centre in Cape Town answering queries from London — nothing you can drop on your foot, yet all of it is production, because it satisfies wants and earns income.

Now the South African numbers, in round figures you can quote. The tertiary sector is the giant: it contributes roughly two thirds of our gross domestic product — finance and business services alone are the single largest slice, followed by trade, transport and government services. The secondary sector contributes roughly a fifth, with manufacturing at about thirteen percent of GDP. The primary sector, for all its fame, is now the smallest: mining contributes around six percent and agriculture around three percent of GDP.

Hold on to one warning: contribution to GDP and contribution to jobs are different questions. Agriculture produces about three percent of GDP but employs a noticeably larger share of workers, many in rural areas with few alternatives. A sector can be small in rands and large in livelihoods, and strong exam answers keep those two measures apart.

The questions for this section are with you now: each activity sorted into its sector, and the rough GDP shares of the three sectors placed in the right order.

## Subtopic: How the Sectors Link and How the Structure Has Shifted

The three sectors are not rivals standing in separate rooms; they are links in one chain. Follow a single orange. A farmer in Limpopo grows it — primary. A factory in Nelspruit squeezes it into juice and seals it in a carton — secondary. A supermarket chills it, advertises it and sells it to you, while a bank processes the payment and a truck company moves it — tertiary, three times over. Every product you can name travels this chain, gathering value at each link. That is why economists say the sectors are interdependent: a drought that hits the farmer eventually empties the factory shift and quietens the till.

Now put time into the picture, because South Africa's structure has changed profoundly. A century ago the economy stood on mining and farming — the primary sector dominated. Through the middle of the twentieth century, manufacturing grew behind tariff walls and cheap electricity until the secondary sector carried the economy. Since the 1980s the weight has moved decisively to services: finance, retail, communications, government. Economists call this pattern — primary to secondary to tertiary — structural transformation, and most economies walk it as they mature.

But South Africa's version carries a complication worth marks: our manufacturing share has been shrinking, from over twenty percent of GDP in the early 1990s to about thirteen percent today. Some economists call this premature deindustrialisation — the factory stage fading before it absorbed the workers the mines and farms released. It matters because manufacturing historically soaks up semi-skilled labour in large numbers, exactly what an economy with an unemployment rate above thirty percent needs. Services create jobs too, but many demand qualifications that excluded workers do not yet hold.

So when a question asks you to describe South Africa's economic structure, give three moves: name the tertiary sector as dominant, trace the historical shift from primary through secondary to tertiary, and note the concern that the secondary sector weakened too early.

Take this section's questions now: the orange's chain rebuilt link by link, the historical shift ordered correctly, and the deindustrialisation concern stated with its reason.

## Subtopic: Infrastructure — the Economy's Skeleton

Infrastructure is the collective name for the large, shared, long-lasting installations on which all production rides. None of it is bought for its own sake — nobody wants a power station the way they want a phone — yet without it, no sector functions.

Split it in two, because the split earns marks. Economic infrastructure serves production directly: power stations and the electricity grid, railways and roads, harbours at Durban, Richards Bay, Ngqura and Cape Town, airports, water schemes and dams, and telecommunications networks. Social infrastructure serves people directly and production indirectly: schools, hospitals and clinics, housing, sanitation. A healthy, educated worker is more productive — that is the indirect route from a clinic to GDP.

South Africa's endowment is genuinely impressive on paper: the densest rail network in Africa, world-class ports, a power system built to be among the cheapest anywhere, and near-universal mobile phone coverage. The exam word for what this should deliver is competitiveness — good infrastructure lowers every firm's costs at once.

But the recent story is one of strain, and honest answers say so. Electricity is the sharpest case: years of load shedding — scheduled power cuts, peaking in 2023 when cuts occurred on most days of the year — forced firms to buy generators and solar systems, raising costs and cutting output. Freight rail and ports tell a similar story: coal and ore exporters have lost sales not because the minerals ran out but because trains and cranes could not move the volumes. Economists estimate these bottlenecks have shaved percentage points off growth each year.

Here is the analytical sentence to carry into any essay: infrastructure failure acts like a tax on every producer simultaneously — it raises costs in all three sectors at once, which is precisely why fixing it is called the cheapest growth strategy the country has.

This section's questions are ready for you: economic and social infrastructure separated, South Africa's strengths and bottlenecks both named, and the tax-on-everyone logic applied.

## Subtopic: Service Provisioning, Access and Economic Opportunity

The final piece of the structure is about people: who actually receives services, and what access does to opportunity.

Start with what delivery has achieved. Since 1994, access has widened dramatically: roughly ninety percent of households are connected to electricity, close to ninety percent have access to piped water in the dwelling or yard or nearby, and mobile networks reach almost everyone. These are real numbers to quote, and they represent one of the larger service roll-outs any middle-income country has managed.

Now the unevenness, because averages hide it. Access is weakest exactly where poverty is deepest: rural former homeland areas and informal settlements. A household in a Limpopo village may have an electricity connection but face long walks for water; an informal settlement in Ekurhuleni may have water points but no waterborne sanitation or tarred roads. And beyond household services sits the digital divide: most South Africans reach the internet only through relatively expensive mobile data, so poorer learners and job-seekers pay the most, per rand of income, to participate online.

Why does this belong in economics and not just in politics? Because access to services is access to economic opportunity. Without electricity, a welder cannot run a machine and a learner cannot study after dark. Without transport, a job twenty kilometres away might as well be in another country — and commuting costs can consume a large share of a low wage. Without connectivity, you cannot see job adverts, compare prices or bank cheaply. Economists summarise it this way: poor service access raises the cost of participating in the economy, and it raises that cost most for the poor. That is how infrastructure gaps reproduce inequality from one generation to the next.

For the exam, structure the argument in three steps: state the achievement with a number, state the gap with a location, and finish with the mechanism — access converts infrastructure into opportunity, so unequal access means unequal opportunity.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the delivery numbers quoted, the gaps located, and the access-to-opportunity mechanism argued in full sentences.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same structure through one street you already know: a taxi rank, a chicken shop, and the wires and pipes that decide whether any of it works.

## Subtopic: From the Ground, Through the Factory, Over the Counter

Think about the last plate of chicken and pap you ate, and run it backwards.

The mealies for the pap grew on a farm — someone planted, watered and harvested them. The chicken was raised on a poultry farm. That is the primary sector: taking things directly from nature. Farms, mines, fishing boats, forests. If the work happens where nature keeps the raw material, it is primary.

But you did not eat raw mealies in a field. A mill ground them into maize meal and packed it into that printed plastic bag. An abattoir and a processing plant turned the live chicken into neat pieces. That is the secondary sector: factories taking raw stuff and making it into products. Making, building, assembling — if the job is transforming materials into goods, it is secondary.

And you still could not eat until somebody sold it to you. The shop that fried the chicken, the truck that delivered the maize meal, the bank app the shop owner uses, the municipality billing for the shop's lights — those are all the tertiary sector: services. Nothing you can hold, but without them the food never reaches the plate.

Here is the surprise about South Africa. Ask people what our economy is, and they say gold and mines. But out of every hundred rand the country produces, roughly sixty-five come from services, about twenty from factories and construction, and under ten from mines and farms together. We are a services economy with famous mines, not a mining economy with a few shops.

One trap to dodge: small in rands does not mean unimportant. Farming makes only about three rand in every hundred, but it feeds the country and gives work to many people in places where there are no other jobs. Money share and job share are two different scoreboards.

Your questions for this section are with you now — for each business named, ask one thing: does it take from nature, make into goods, or serve people?

## Subtopic: One Street, All Three Sectors Holding Hands

Stand at the taxi rank and watch the three sectors work as a team.

The vetkoek seller's flour started as wheat on a farm — primary. It became flour at a mill — secondary. She fries it and sells it, the taxi carries her to the rank, and the wholesaler supplied her oil — tertiary, three services deep. One vetkoek, three sectors, no sector able to work alone. If the wheat harvest fails, the mill runs short, the price of flour jumps, and the vetkoek gets smaller or costs more. Trouble in one sector always travels down the chain.

Now rewind South Africa like a movie. Your great-grandparents' economy was mostly primary: mines and farms employed the nation. Then came the factory years — steel, clothing, car plants — when the secondary sector grew strong. And in your lifetime, the services years: malls, banks, cellphone networks, government offices. Primary first, then secondary, then tertiary — economies tend to grow up in that order, like a person moving from crawling to walking to running.

But here is South Africa's sore point, said simply: our factories started fading before enough people had found work in them. The clothing factories that once filled whole towns in the Western Cape and KwaZulu-Natal shrank when cheaper imports arrived. Factory jobs matter because they suit people who have skills and willingness but not university degrees — exactly the workers our economy has most of. Services jobs grew instead, but many of them ask for matric plus more. That mismatch is one reason so many willing workers cannot find work.

So the honest one-line summary of our structure: a big services head, a shrinking factory body, and small but precious roots in the ground.

Take this section's questions now — follow the chain, order the eras, and say in your own words why the fading factories matter.

## Subtopic: The Wires and Pipes That Decide Who Gets to Play

Everything above assumed something invisible: that the lights were on, the taps ran and the roads existed. Infrastructure is the name for all of that shared equipment — power stations, roads, railways, ports, dams, pipes, cell towers — plus the people-serving kind: schools, clinics, houses.

Feel what it does through one day of load shedding. The lights go out at the chicken shop: the fryers stop, the fridge warms, stock spoils, and the card machine dies so customers with no cash walk away. The welder next door misses a deadline. The salon cancels appointments. Nobody's skill changed, nobody got lazy — one failure in shared infrastructure taxed every business on the street at the same time. Now imagine the same day repeated across the whole country, most days of the year, which is roughly what 2023 looked like. That is why economists say fixing power, rail and ports is the cheapest way to grow: repair one shared thing and every producer breathes easier at once.

Then the question of who has access, because the pipes do not reach everyone equally. Around ninety in every hundred households have electricity, and close to ninety have piped water reasonably close — a huge improvement over thirty years, worth stating proudly. But walk into a deep rural village or an informal settlement and the averages fall away: water is far, sanitation is shared, data is the most expensive thing in the monthly budget.

And that is the real economics of it: services are the entry ticket to the economy. No electricity means no studying after dark and no machine to run. No affordable transport means the far-away job does not pay once the taxi fare is subtracted. No data means job adverts happen without you. When access is unequal, opportunity is unequal — the gap in the pipes becomes a gap in people's chances.

And here come the last questions of the lesson: one about the blackout day, one about the access numbers, and one asking you to explain, in your own words, how a missing pipe becomes a missing opportunity.
