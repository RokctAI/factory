**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! First one, and it comes up every single year: is a mine that processes its own ore into refined metal primary or secondary? The class is split down the middle.

**Tutor:** The class is split because the firm genuinely straddles the line, and the trick is to classify the activity, not the company. Digging the ore out of the ground is primary — the material is being taken directly from nature. Smelting and refining that ore into metal is secondary — a raw material is being transformed into a made product. A large mining house does both under one company name, and the national accounts handle it exactly as you should in an exam: they split the firm's activities and record each part in its own sector. So if a question describes the extraction stage, answer primary; if it describes the smelter or refinery, answer secondary; and if it describes the company's marketing office selling the metal to buyers overseas, that is tertiary. One company, three sectors, no contradiction. The classification question is always about what is being done in the sentence in front of you, never about the logo on the gate.

**Bianca:** Next one: if the tertiary sector is two thirds of the economy, why does everyone — including the news — still talk about mining as if it carries South Africa?

**Tutor:** Because mining punches far above its GDP weight in three specific ways, and naming them is a high-mark answer. First, exports: minerals and metals remain a very large share of what South Africa sells to the world, so mining earns the foreign currency that pays for imported oil, medicines and machinery, even while it is only around six percent of GDP. Second, linkages: a mine buys explosives, steel, engineering services, transport and banking, so a rand of mining output pulls production out of the secondary and tertiary sectors too — economists call these backward linkages. Third, geography: entire towns in the North West, Mpumalanga and Limpopo exist around a single shaft or smelter, so mining's local importance in those places is total even when its national share looks modest. The clean exam formulation is this: the tertiary sector dominates production, but mining retains outsized importance through export earnings, linkages and regional employment. Both statements are true at once, and strong answers hold them together instead of choosing one.

**Bianca:** This one caused an argument: someone said load shedding proves state-owned infrastructure always fails, and the fix is to privatise everything. How should we handle that in an essay?

**Tutor:** Handle it the way economists handle every sweeping claim: separate the evidence from the conclusion, and give the conditions under which each side holds. The evidence is real — Eskom's generation failures and Transnet's rail and port bottlenecks imposed enormous costs, and the lesson is honest about that. But the sweeping conclusion does not follow automatically, and examiners reward the nuance. Infrastructure is typically a natural monopoly: one grid, one rail network, one set of pipes, because duplicating them would be wasteful. That means simple competition cannot always discipline the provider, whether it is public or private, and a private monopoly with no regulation can overcharge just as a public one can decay. What the recent South African reforms actually do is more precise: they keep the shared network but open parts of the system to competition — private generators may now sell electricity into the grid, and private operators are being invited onto rail routes. So the strong essay answer says: the failures show the cost of poor governance and under-investment; the remedy being pursued is a mix of regulated networks, private participation and competition where it is feasible; and the test of any arrangement, public or private, is whether it delivers reliable services at low cost.

**Bianca:** Last one, a practical one: essays keep asking us to link infrastructure to economic opportunity, and our answers keep coming back marked "too vague". What does a full-marks version of that link actually look like?

**Tutor:** Vague answers say "infrastructure helps the economy" and stop; full-marks answers trace a mechanism from a specific service to a specific person's productive capacity. Build the answer in three moves. Move one, state the achievement with a number: around ninety percent of households now have electricity access, a major post-1994 delivery success. Move two, locate the gap: access remains weakest in rural former homelands and informal settlements, and data costs put the internet largely out of reach of the poorest. Move three — the one markers look for — walk one concrete chain from service to opportunity: a learner without electricity cannot study after dark, so she enters matric exams disadvantaged; a job-seeker without affordable transport cannot accept work whose taxi fare eats the wage; a seamstress without reliable power cannot promise delivery dates, so she loses customers to bigger firms. Each chain ends the same way: the missing service raises that person's cost of participating in the economy, so unequal access becomes unequal opportunity, which reproduces inequality. Write one chain in full rather than three half-chains, attach a number and a place, and the "too vague" comment disappears.
