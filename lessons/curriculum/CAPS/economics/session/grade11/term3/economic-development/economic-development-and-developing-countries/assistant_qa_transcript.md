**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! First one: if growth and development usually travel together, why does the exam keep forcing us to separate them? It feels like splitting hairs.

**Tutor:** It feels that way until you meet the cases where the two split in real life — and those cases are precisely what the question is hunting for. Hold two countries in mind. An oil state can post spectacular growth for decades while its children stay out of school and its clinics stay empty: the pot grows, the lives do not — growth without development. Meanwhile a modest-income country that pours its resources into schooling and primary healthcare can push life expectancy and literacy far above its income level: lives improving faster than the pot — development outrunning growth. South Africa gives the exam its favourite middle case: upper-middle income by production, yet a Human Development Index rank well below that, because our health and education outcomes drag beneath what our income predicts. If growth and development were truly the same thing, none of those three cases could exist. So the separation is not hair-splitting; it is the whole diagnostic. When a question shows you a country, your first move is exactly this: check the output numbers, check the life numbers, and say which is ahead of the other — because the policy advice differs completely depending on the answer.

**Bianca:** Next: the six characteristics feel designed for lists, but essay questions ask us to apply them to South Africa. What does 'fits with a twist' actually look like in a written answer?

**Tutor:** It looks like three sentences per characteristic instead of one, and the middle sentence does the work. Sentence one states the characteristic and its mechanism: developing countries typically show low standards of living, meaning low average incomes with hunger and poor housing at the bottom. Sentence two applies it honestly to South Africa — and here is where the twist lives: South Africa's average income is upper-middle by world standards, so we do not fit the low-average picture, but our distribution is so unequal that millions still live below the food poverty line beneath that comfortable-looking average. Sentence three draws the conclusion the twist forces: for South Africa the standard-of-living problem is a distribution problem more than an average problem, so remedies must target the spread, not just the total. Do the same for unemployment, where we are not typical but extreme — above thirty percent official, above forty expanded — and for primary dependence, where production is service-led but exports still lean on minerals. Fits, fails, or fits-with-a-twist, argued with one number each: that structure turns a memory list into the analysis level of the mark scheme, and it is exactly the honesty examiners reward.

**Bianca:** Third: RDP, GEAR, NDP — the letters blur together in everyone's notes. Is there a way to remember what each one actually stood for without memorising documents?

**Tutor:** Remember them as three answers to one question — what should come first? — because each plan is a different answer. The RDP answered: needs first. Deliver houses, water, electricity and clinics directly, and development is the delivery itself. Its fingerprint on the country is physical — you can still point at RDP houses. GEAR answered: stability first. By 1996 the finances were fragile, so the plan disciplined debt and inflation, betting that a stable economy would attract investment and the growth would create the jobs. The stabilising succeeded; the jobs bet disappointed — that pairing of achievement and shortfall is the evaluation the exam wants. The NDP answered: capability first. By 2012 the lesson learned was that neither delivery nor stability suffices without a capable state, skilled people and working infrastructure, so it set 2030 targets — income poverty eliminated, unemployment down to single digits — and named the builders: education, infrastructure, a professional public service. Needs first, stability first, capability first. Attach one delivery instrument to the story — public works programmes for temporary jobs, special economic zones for factories, grants holding the floor — and one honest verdict: services largely delivered, growth and jobs repeatedly missed. That is the full plans question answered from a three-word skeleton.

**Bianca:** Last one: some of the class feel IKS is in the syllabus for sentimental reasons, not economic ones. Convince us it belongs in an economics exam.

**Tutor:** Gladly — with numbers first, then a mechanism, because that is how economics convinces anyone of anything. The numbers: stokvels and burial societies move tens of billions of rands every year through voluntary monthly contributions among people the formal banking system historically ignored. That is savings mobilisation and insurance — two core functions of a financial system — delivered at national scale with almost no infrastructure except trust. Any institution doing that much financial work is an economic fact, whatever else it is. The mechanism: development interventions succeed or fail on whether people adopt them, and people adopt what plugs into institutions they already trust. A bank product that ignores how stokvels work loses to the stokvel; a health campaign that dismisses healers loses the majority of patients who consult them; a drought strategy that overlooks sorghum and millet plants thirstier crops into a drying climate. Ignoring IKS is therefore not neutral — it is a predictable cause of policy failure, and predicting policy outcomes is economics. Finally, property: the hoodia case showed indigenous knowledge being taken and patented without consent — biopiracy — and the benefit-sharing agreements that followed, including rooibos, established that such knowledge is an asset that can earn income for its holders. Assets, institutions, adoption, income: every one of those is an economics word. That is why IKS sits in the syllabus, and why treating it seriously in an essay reads as competence, not sentiment.
