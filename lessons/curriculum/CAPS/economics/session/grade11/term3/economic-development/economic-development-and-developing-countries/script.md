# Part 1 — Expert

This session moves from how much an economy produces to what that production does for human lives: economic development. We define development against growth, learn how it is measured, work through the six classic characteristics of developing countries with South Africa held up to each one, survey the strategies countries use to develop — including South Africa's own succession of plans — and finish with a source of development thinking the exam increasingly rewards: Indigenous Knowledge Systems.

## Subtopic: Development versus Growth — and How Development Is Measured

Economic growth is a quantity statement: real GDP rose. Economic development is a quality statement: the lives people lead improved — healthier, better educated, longer, freer, with poverty and inequality falling. The two usually travel together, but not always, and the gap between them is where exam questions live. An oil-rich country can grow spectacularly while its people stay unschooled and unwell: growth without development. A country can also stretch modest growth into major gains in schooling and health: development racing ahead of growth.

So how is development measured? Since money alone will not do, the flagship measure is the Human Development Index, the HDI, which combines three dimensions: income — gross national income per capita adjusted for what money actually buys locally; health — life expectancy at birth; and education — years of schooling. Each country scores between zero and one. The pattern to remember for South Africa: our HDI ranking sits noticeably below our income ranking, in the middle of the world table despite an upper-middle income level — because life expectancy and learning outcomes drag below what our production would predict. That single sentence — richer than we are healthy or educated — is the exam's favourite South African development fact.

Below the averages sit the poverty measures. Absolute poverty means falling under a fixed minimum: the World Bank's extreme line of a few US dollars a day, or South Africa's own food poverty line — the rand amount needed for minimum daily nutrition, which around a quarter of South Africans fall beneath. Relative poverty means falling far below the standards of your own society. And because deprivation is more than money, statisticians also track multidimensional poverty — counting households short of schooling, services, housing and work at the same time.

Keep the trio distinct: growth counts output, development tracks the quality of lives, and poverty measures count who is being left beneath the floor.

The questions for this section are with you now: growth separated from development with an example, the HDI's three dimensions named, and South Africa's income-versus-HDI gap explained.

## Subtopic: The Six Signs — Common Characteristics of Developing Countries

Developing countries differ enormously, yet they tend to share a recognisable cluster of characteristics. The syllabus names six. Learn them as a diagnostic checklist, and for each, hold South Africa up to the light — because our country fits some perfectly and breaks others, and examiners love that nuance.

One: low standard of living. Low average incomes, and — crucially — wide inequality around the average, with hunger and poor housing at the bottom. South Africa fits with a twist: average income is upper-middle by world standards, but distribution is so skewed that millions live in absolute poverty beneath a comfortable-looking mean.

Two: low productivity. Output per worker is low, because workers lack machines, training and healthy conditions. A farmer with a hoe produces a fraction of what a farmer with a tractor manages; a clerk without connectivity processes a fraction of the transactions. Low productivity means low wages, which means low savings, which means little investment in the tools that would raise productivity — the first of several vicious circles.

Three: high population growth and high dependency burdens. Developing countries have young, fast-growing populations, so each working-age adult supports many dependants — children now, and increasingly the elderly too. Income that might have been saved and invested is consumed by immediate needs. South Africa's population growth, at around one and a half percent, is slower than much of Africa but still ahead of our economic growth — the shrinking-plate arithmetic from the previous lesson.

Four: high unemployment and underemployment. Not enough formal jobs, and many people surviving in informal work far below their capacity. Here South Africa is not typical but extreme: an official unemployment rate above thirty percent, and over forty percent on the expanded definition that counts those too discouraged to keep searching — among the very highest rates on Earth.

Five: dependence on the primary sector and on exporting raw materials. Many developing economies live off unprocessed exports — minerals, crops, oil — whose world prices swing wildly, making national income a hostage to markets they do not control. South Africa half-fits: our production is service-dominated, yet our exports still lean heavily on minerals and metals, so the old vulnerability survives in our trade accounts.

Six: deficient infrastructure and incomplete markets. Missing roads, unreliable power, scarce credit for the poor, weak information. You met South Africa's version in Term 1: world-class infrastructure in places, load shedding and broken freight rail elsewhere, and rural areas the networks barely reach.

Run the checklist over any country a question gives you, and say of each item: fits, does not fit, or fits with a twist. That structure alone lifts an answer a level.

Work this section's questions now: the six characteristics named, each matched to its mechanism, and South Africa diagnosed honestly against at least three.

## Subtopic: Development Strategies — and South Africa's Succession of Plans

How does a country deliberately develop? Strategies cluster around a few big choices, and then South Africa's own plans put flesh on them.

Choice one: what leads — the state or the market? State-led strategies use planning, public investment and state enterprises to push development; market-led strategies free prices, trade and private investment to pull it. Nearly every success story mixes the two, but the mix is contested in every parliament on Earth, including ours.

Choice two: which direction — inward or outward? Inward-looking strategies protect home industries with tariffs so a country builds its own manufacturing — import substitution. Outward-looking strategies chase export markets, forcing industries to meet world standards. The East Asian economies that developed fastest in living memory chose exports — and their example haunts every development debate.

Choice three: what is the engine — things or people? Big-push strategies bet on physical capital: dams, industrial zones, infrastructure corridors. Human-capital strategies bet on education, health and nutrition, arguing that skilled, healthy people are the engine everything else needs. The evidence says the second cannot be skipped: no country has developed with its people unschooled and unwell.

Now South Africa's succession since 1994 — learn it as a story, not a list of letters. The Reconstruction and Development Programme, the RDP, led with basic needs: houses, water, electricity, clinics — development delivered directly. It was followed in 1996 by GEAR — Growth, Employment and Redistribution — a market-friendly turn that stabilised debt and inflation and aimed for growth-led development; it steadied the finances, but jobs lagged. Later came programmes targeting infrastructure and skills, and since 2012 the umbrella has been the National Development Plan, the NDP 2030, with its headline targets of eliminating income poverty and cutting unemployment to single digits by 2030 through infrastructure, education and a capable state. Alongside it run delivery instruments you should be able to name: the Expanded Public Works Programme creating temporary work, special economic zones courting industrial investment, and the social grant system holding the poverty floor.

The honest evaluation, which examiners reward: real delivery on services and grants; repeated misses on growth and jobs — because plans on paper kept colliding with electricity shortages, skills gaps and weak implementation. A plan is a strategy; development is a delivery record.

Attend to this section's questions before the final piece: the three strategic choices stated, the South African plans placed in order with their emphases, and one honest evaluation sentence formed.

## Subtopic: Indigenous Knowledge Systems — Development from Within

The final strategy source is the oldest: Indigenous Knowledge Systems, IKS — the knowledge, practices and institutions that communities have developed over generations, passed on culturally rather than through formal science. The syllabus asks two things: what IKS contributes to development, and how it must be protected.

Start with contributions, concretely. Indigenous finance: the stokvel — the rotating savings club in which members contribute monthly and take turns receiving the pool — mobilises tens of billions of rands a year among people the formal banks long ignored; burial societies insure millions the same way. That is financial development, built entirely from within. Indigenous agriculture: drought-resistant crops like sorghum and millet, intercropping methods, and centuries of livestock knowledge suited to local conditions — increasingly relevant as the climate dries. Indigenous medicine: most South Africans consult traditional healers, now formally recognised by law, and indigenous plants carry real pharmaceutical value. Indigenous governance: communal land management and customary institutions that organise resources where formal institutions are thin.

Why does this matter for strategy? Because development that ignores what people already know, trust and use tends to fail — a savings product that fights the stokvel loses to it; a clinic that dismisses the healer loses the patient; a drought plan that ignores sorghum plants the wrong crop. Development from within builds on existing institutions instead of bulldozing them.

Now protection, through the case the exam loves: hoodia. The San people used the hoodia plant for generations to suppress hunger on long hunts. When that knowledge was patented for slimming products without consent or payment, it took years of struggle before a benefit-sharing agreement recognised the San as the knowledge holders. The general problem is called biopiracy — profiting from indigenous knowledge without consent or compensation — and the policy answer is legal protection: South Africa's IKS legislation, benefit-sharing requirements, and moves to record knowledge so patents cannot pretend it was never known. Rooibos tea tells the happier ending: after a long campaign, the industry now pays a share to Khoi and San communities recognised as the plant's traditional knowledge holders.

Hold the balance for essays: IKS is neither a museum piece nor a replacement for modern science — it is a development asset, to be used where it works and protected from theft while it does.

The final questions of this part are with you now: IKS defined, two contributions argued with examples, and the hoodia-to-benefit-sharing story told as the case for protection.

## Subtopic: Taller or Better? — What Development Actually Asks

Think of a growing child. Growth is the tape measure against the door frame: taller this year than last. Development is everything the tape measure cannot see: is the child healthier, learning, becoming capable and confident?

Countries work the same way. Growth is the tape measure: more production this year — the pot from the last lesson getting bigger. Development asks the door-frame-cannot-see questions: are people living longer? Can they read, and finish school? Is there a clinic that works, a tap that runs, a real chance of a job? A country, like a child, can get taller without getting better.

You can see the difference in one street. A new mall opens nearby: production, jobs, rands changing hands — the tape measure moves. But if the street's children still share one distant clinic, if the school still has sixty to a class, if the taps still run dry — the street grew without developing. Now flip it: a working clinic opens, the school gets teachers and textbooks, a bursary lands. Even before incomes move much, life got better — development arriving ahead of growth.

Because money alone cannot measure this, the world uses a three-part score called the Human Development Index: money — income per person; health — how long people live; learning — how long children stay in school and how much adults were schooled. Three dimensions, one score between zero and one.

And here is South Africa's uncomfortable score-sheet in one sentence: by money we rank in the world's upper-middle, but our full score sits lower, because our people die younger and learn less than our income says they should. Richer than we are healthy or educated. Carry that sentence into any exam — it is the whole growth-versus-development distinction wearing South African clothes.

Your questions for this section are with you now — each describes a change in a country or a street, and asks: did the tape measure move, did life get better, or both?

## Subtopic: The Six Signs, Walked Down One Street

The lesson's six characteristics of developing countries can feel like a list to memorise — until you walk them down a street you know. Come walk.

Sign one: low standard of living. Not just low income — crowded housing, meals that stretch, school shoes that must last. And in South Africa, always with the twist: a comfortable suburb twenty minutes away, because our problem is the spread as much as the average.

Sign two: low productivity. Watch a man dig a trench with a spade next to a machine that could do it in an hour. He is not lazy — he is under-equipped. Few tools, little training: each hour of work produces little, so each hour pays little. And because pay is little, nobody can save for tools — the circle that keeps itself going.

Sign three: many mouths per earner. In the developing world's young households, one pay packet or one gogo's pension feeds many people — children, cousins, the unemployed uncle. Economists call it the dependency burden: money that could have been saved is eaten tonight, and rightly so — but then there is nothing to invest tomorrow.

Sign four: work that is missing or too small. The street's saddest sign: matriculants with certificates and nowhere to take them. South Africa's unemployment — three in ten by the strict count, four in ten counting those who gave up — is the worst version of this sign anywhere.

Sign five: selling raw, buying made. The country digs ore and ships it out, then buys back phones and machines made from it — earning the smallest slice of its own resources, at prices the world decides. When the metal price falls, the whole street feels it.

Sign six: broken basics. The power that cuts, the train that never comes, the loan the bank will not give. You cannot run a business on infrastructure that keeps letting go of your hand.

Six signs, one street. Memorise them as the walk, and no list question can shake you.

Take this section's questions now — each shows you one scene from the street, and asks which sign you are looking at.

## Subtopic: Plans on Paper, Roots in the Ground

How does the street actually develop? Two sources: plans from above, and roots from below — and South Africa teaches both.

Plans from above, in one story. After 1994 the RDP built the basics — houses, taps, electricity, clinics — development delivered straight to the street. GEAR then steadied the country's money — debts and inflation under control — hoping growth would follow and hire the street; the steadying worked, the hiring disappointed. Since 2012 the big plan has been the NDP 2030: kill income poverty, shrink unemployment to single digits, fix infrastructure and schools. Beside it run the workhorses: public works programmes offering temporary jobs, special zones wooing factories, grants holding the floor. The honest report card, worth full marks for its honesty: services delivered, floor held — growth and jobs repeatedly missed, because load shedding, skills gaps and weak delivery kept tripping the plans.

Now the roots — because the street never waited for plans. The stokvel turns neighbours into a bank: everyone contributes monthly, each takes a turn to receive the pool — school fees get paid, stock gets bought, funerals get covered. Tens of billions of rands move this way every year, among people banks overlooked. The garden that survives drought grows sorghum because the grandmothers knew what the rain would do before the forecasts did. The healer sees patients the clinic queue cannot reach. This is Indigenous Knowledge Systems — IKS — knowledge grown over generations, and it is not folklore; it is working infrastructure made of trust.

Smart development joins the two: savings products that ride on stokvels instead of fighting them; clinics that work with healers; drought plans that plant what the grandmothers planted. And the law must guard the roots: when the San people's hoodia knowledge was patented for profit without consent — the crime called biopiracy — it took years to win recognition and a share; rooibos communities fought the same fight and now earn theirs. Knowledge is property too, and the street owns some.

And here come the last questions of the lesson: the plans in order with one honest verdict, the stokvel explained as indigenous finance, and the hoodia story told as the reason protection matters.
