# Part 1 — Expert

This session tackles the two questions every economy must answer together: how do we make the total bigger, and how is the total shared? That is economic growth on one side, and the distribution of income and wealth on the other — with the Lorenz curve and the Gini coefficient as the measuring instruments, and redistribution methods as the policy response. South Africa is the world's sharpest case study of all four, and the exam knows it.

## Subtopic: Economic Growth — Meaning, Measurement and South Africa's Record

Economic growth is the increase in a country's production of goods and services over time, measured as the percentage change in real gross domestic product from one year to the next. Two words in that definition carry the marks.

First, real. Nominal GDP can rise simply because prices rose — that is inflation wearing a growth costume. Real GDP strips out price changes by valuing output at constant prices, so a real growth rate reflects genuinely more goods and services. If nominal GDP rises six percent while prices rise five percent, real growth is roughly one percent — the honest number.

Second, per capita. Total real GDP can grow while the population grows faster, leaving each person on average with less. Real GDP per capita — total output divided by population — is the growth number that speaks to living standards. Hold this test: an economy only becomes better off on average when output grows faster than the number of people sharing it.

Now South Africa's record, in figures worth quoting. Over the past decade, real GDP growth has averaged only around one percent a year — with load shedding, failing freight rail and policy uncertainty each taking their bite — while population growth has run at roughly one and a half percent. Do the subtraction: real income per person has been shrinking slowly for years. That is the quiet fact behind much of the country's frustration, and it is why the National Development Plan's target of over five percent growth matters: at one percent, an economy doubles in about seventy years; at five percent, in about fourteen.

Why does growth matter so much? More output means more jobs and higher incomes; a bigger tax base that funds schools, clinics and grants without higher tax rates; and the resources to tackle poverty without taking from anyone. Growth is not everything — this lesson's whole second half is about what it misses — but nothing else replaces it.

The questions for this section are with you now: real separated from nominal, per capita applied honestly, and South Africa's decade summarised with both numbers.

## Subtopic: Measuring Inequality — the Lorenz Curve and the Gini Coefficient

A country can grow while most of its people stay poor, so economists built instruments to see how income is shared. The main one is a picture — the Lorenz curve — and since we are working in sound, we will draw it in words, carefully.

Take a square diagram. Along the horizontal axis, line up the whole population from poorest to richest, measured cumulatively in percentages: the poorest twenty percent, then forty, sixty, eighty, one hundred. Up the vertical axis, the cumulative percentage of total income those people receive. Now anchor the benchmark: if income were perfectly equally shared, any twenty percent of the people would hold exactly twenty percent of the income, any sixty percent would hold sixty — so perfect equality traces the straight diagonal line from the bottom-left corner to the top-right corner. That diagonal is called the line of perfect equality.

The Lorenz curve is the country's actual line. It starts at the same bottom-left corner and ends at the same top-right corner — zero people hold zero income, all the people hold all the income — but in between it sags below the diagonal, like a slack washing line. Read one point on South Africa's: the poorest sixty percent of the population receive only around ten percent of all income, so the curve passes through sixty on the horizontal but only about ten on the vertical — deep below the diagonal's sixty. The bigger the sag, the greater the inequality.

The Gini coefficient turns that sag into a single number. Take the area of the gap between the diagonal and the Lorenz curve, and divide it by the entire area under the diagonal. Perfect equality: no gap, Gini of zero. Perfect inequality — one person holds everything — the curve hugs the floor and the far right wall, the gap is the whole triangle, Gini of one. Every real country sits between. Scandinavian countries sit near zero comma two five to zero comma three. South Africa's Gini is around zero comma six three — the highest of any major economy on Earth, our most uncomfortable world record.

And one distinction before the questions: income is a flow — what you earn this year; wealth is a stock — what you own, accumulated over time and over generations. Wealth is always more unequally held than income; in South Africa the richest ten percent are estimated to own over eighty percent of all wealth. History explains much of it: decades in which the majority were legally blocked from owning land, businesses and skills leave a stock that no single year's earnings can rebalance.

Work this section's questions now: both axes labelled cumulatively, the sag read at a given point, the Gini defined as a ratio of areas, and income kept apart from wealth.

## Subtopic: Redistribution — the Methods Governments Actually Use

If the market's distribution is judged too unequal, government can redistribute — deliberately move income and opportunity across the Lorenz curve. South Africa runs one of the most extensive redistribution programmes in the developing world, and the exam wants the methods named and explained.

Method one: progressive taxation. A tax is progressive when the rich pay a higher percentage of their income than the poor, not merely more rands. South African personal income tax climbs through brackets from eighteen percent up to forty-five percent on the top slice of high incomes, and a small minority of high earners contribute the large majority of the total. The opposite design — regressive taxation — takes a bigger share from the poor; VAT tends that way, which is why staple foods like maize meal and brown bread are zero-rated.

Method two: cash transfers — the social grants. Around twenty-eight million South Africans receive a grant monthly: the older persons grant at over two thousand rand, the child support grant at over five hundred, disability, foster care, and the social relief of distress grant. Grants are the single most direct redistribution instrument we have, and the evidence is solid that they cut extreme poverty and hunger.

Method three: free and subsidised services — redistribution in kind. Free basic water and electricity allocations for poor households, no-fee schools, school feeding schemes, RDP housing, subsidised clinics. Every service a household receives free is income it did not have to spend.

Method four: labour-market and asset measures. The national minimum wage sets a floor under the working poor. Broad-Based Black Economic Empowerment and employment equity aim to redistribute ownership and opportunity, land reform aims to redistribute the land asset itself, and public spending on township and rural education aims at the deepest lever of all — redistributing skills, tomorrow's earning power.

The tension to argue in essays: redistribution needs revenue, and revenue needs growth — tax too heavily and investment may flee; grow without sharing and the Gini stays at zero comma six three. The exam phrase is that growth and redistribution must be complements, not substitutes: growth funds redistribution; redistribution, by building skills and demand, can feed growth.

Attend to this section's questions before the final piece: each method named with its example, progressive defined by percentage not rands, and the tension argued both ways.

## Subtopic: Achieving Growth, the Constraints in the Way, and Standard of Living

Close the loop: if growth is the engine that funds everything else, how does a country actually get it — and what is blocking South Africa's?

The methods of growth come down to using more inputs and using inputs better. More capital: investment in machines, factories and infrastructure, funded by savings — domestic or foreign. More and better labour: education, training, health. Better technology and methods: innovation, and openness to trade that forces efficiency and grows markets — exports let a country sell to the world, not just to itself. And underneath all of it, an environment that makes investment worth the risk: reliable electricity and transport, safety, stable policy, honest administration.

Now hold South Africa's constraints against that list, because every one maps onto it. Electricity and logistics: years of load shedding and failing freight rail have acted as a ceiling on output — factories cannot run on promises. Skills: the education system still sends too few learners out with the mathematics, technical and digital skills employers need, so firms report vacancies while unemployment stands above thirty percent. Low savings and investment: South Africa saves too little to fund its own investment needs, making us dependent on attracting foreign capital — which is exactly what policy uncertainty, corruption and crime frighten away. Each constraint is a growth method with a padlock on it — and that sentence is an essay plan in miniature.

Finally, standard of living — the human question hiding behind the statistics. Real GDP per capita is the workhorse measure, but it has famous blind spots: it says nothing about how income is shared — a rising average can coexist with rising poverty; it ignores unpaid work at home and much informal activity; it counts pollution-driven production as gain while ignoring the damage; and it measures money, not health or learning. That is why broader gauges exist, led by the Human Development Index, which combines income with life expectancy and education. South Africa's ranking on the HDI sits well below its ranking by income alone — our health and education outcomes drag below what our production would predict — and that single comparison, growth without matching development, is the bridge to the next topic in this term.

The final questions of this part are with you now: growth methods matched to South African constraints, the padlock sentence applied, and GDP per capita's blind spots listed against the HDI.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same machinery around one image you already own: a pot of food at a family gathering, and the queue that forms when it is served.

## Subtopic: The Pot and the People — Growth Done Honestly

Picture the economy as the family pot at a Sunday gathering. Economic growth means one thing: this year's pot holds more food than last year's. More goods, more services, more actually produced.

But two cheats can fake a bigger pot, and honest measurement blocks both.

Cheat one: prices rose. If the pot holds the same stew but every plate now costs more rands, the money value of the pot grew while the food did not. That is why economists insist on real growth — measure the pot at last year's prices, so only extra food counts, never extra price tags. When you hear "GDP grew six percent" while inflation was five, the honest growth was about one.

Cheat two: more people at the table. Suppose the pot really did grow two percent — but the family gathering grew three percent. Each person's plate got smaller even though the pot got bigger. Dividing the pot by the people gives output per person — per capita — and that is the number that decides whether the average person is actually better off.

Now South Africa's uncomfortable table. For about a decade our pot has grown around one percent a year, while the number of people at the table has grown around one and a half percent. Bigger pot, smaller plates. That arithmetic — growth below population growth — is the single most important fact in this topic, and you can now explain it to anyone in two sentences.

Why fight for a bigger pot at all? Because almost everything else is bought from it: jobs come from expanding production, and the taxes that pay for schools, clinics and grants are scooped from the pot. A country that wants to help its poor without a growing pot can only take from one plate to fill another — which works, but has limits. Grow the pot and you can fill plates without emptying any.

Your questions for this section are with you now — each one hides either a price-tag cheat or a more-people cheat, and your job is to catch it.

## Subtopic: The Queue of One Hundred — Seeing Inequality Without a Graph

Now the sharing. Here is the Lorenz curve rebuilt as a scene you can hold in your head.

Line up one hundred people who represent everyone in South Africa, arranged from the emptiest pockets on the left to the fullest on the right. All the income earned this year is one hundred coins.

If sharing were perfectly equal, the counting would be boring: walk past twenty people, count twenty coins; past sixty people, sixty coins. Every step adds exactly one coin. That boring steady count is the straight diagonal line economists draw as perfect equality.

Now count the real South African queue. Walk past the first twenty people — the poorest fifth — and your count reaches barely two or three coins. Keep going to person sixty and you have only around ten coins, though you have passed more than half the country. The count stays low, crawls, and then explodes at the end of the queue: the last ten people are holding roughly sixty-five of the hundred coins between them. Plotted, that lazy crawl followed by a late explosion is a curve sagging deep below the straight diagonal — the Lorenz curve, and the sag is the inequality.

One number summarises the sag: the Gini coefficient, which is just the size of the gap between the boring straight count and the real crawling count, expressed as a share of the biggest gap possible. Zero means no gap — perfect equality. One means the biggest gap possible — one person holds every coin. South Africa scores about zero comma six three, the highest of any major economy: our sag is the world's deepest.

Last piece: coins earned this year are income, but stuff already owned — houses, land, shares, savings — is wealth, and wealth is shared far more unequally, because it piles up over generations. In a country where, within living memory, most people were legally barred from owning land or building businesses, the wealth queue is even more lopsided than the income queue — history is stored in it.

Take this section's questions now — walk the queue, read the count at twenty and at sixty, and say what the sag and its number mean.

## Subtopic: Moving Coins Along the Queue — and Growing the Pot at the Same Time

If the queue is that lopsided, what can be done? Redistribution: deliberately moving coins — and chances — from the full-pocket end toward the empty-pocket end. South Africa does this at massive scale, through four kinds of moves.

Move one: take proportionally more from the full pockets. Income tax here works in steps — the more you earn, the higher the percentage on your top slice, up to forty-five cents in the rand. The word is progressive: the rich pay a bigger share, not just more rands. And notice the flip side: a tax everyone pays at the same rate on spending, like VAT, quietly bites the poor hardest — which is exactly why maize meal and brown bread carry no VAT at all.

Move two: put coins directly into empty pockets. Social grants reach around twenty-eight million people every month — pensions for the elderly at over two thousand rand, child support at over five hundred, disability and relief grants besides. It is the most direct anti-poverty tool the country has, and it measurably cuts hunger.

Move three: give things instead of coins. Free basic water and electricity, no-fee schools, feeding schemes, RDP houses, subsidised clinics. Every rand a poor household does not have to spend is a rand redistributed, even though no cash changed hands.

Move four: change what people can earn tomorrow. Minimum wages lift the floor under the working poor; empowerment and land policies move ownership; and spending on township and rural schools moves the deepest thing of all — skills, which set the size of a person's future coins.

Now the tension you must be able to argue from both sides. Redistribution is paid for out of the pot, so it needs the pot growing — squeeze the pot too hard and the cooks stop cooking: investment leaves, jobs with it. But a queue this lopsided also starves the pot — people without food, schooling or transport cannot work, buy or build. So the honest answer is never "growth or sharing"; it is that each feeds the other, and policy's job is to keep both moving.

And here come the last questions of the lesson: the four moves named with examples, progressive explained in percentages, and the pot-and-queue tension argued in both directions.
