**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! First one: the news said the economy grew last quarter, but the lesson says South Africans have been getting poorer per person for years. Both cannot be true — can they?

**Tutor:** They can both be true at once, and separating them is exactly the skill this topic examines. "The economy grew" is a statement about total real GDP — the whole pot. "People are poorer on average" is a statement about real GDP per capita — the pot divided by the people. When the pot grows around one percent a year while the number of people grows around one and a half percent, both headlines are honest: total output is at a record high, and the average plate is slowly shrinking. This is why every serious answer states which measure it is using before quoting a number. And add the second filter while you are at it: check that the growth figure is real, not nominal. A six percent rise in nominal GDP during five percent inflation is only about one percent of actual extra production. So run every growth headline through two questions — real or nominal, total or per person — and you will never be caught praising a boom that the arithmetic says is a slow decline, or dismissing genuine growth because prices muddied the number.

**Bianca:** Next one: in the exam, if we are asked to draw or describe the Lorenz curve, what are the exact features the marker is ticking off?

**Tutor:** There is a checklist, and it has six ticks. One: both axes labelled cumulatively — cumulative percentage of the population, poorest first, along the horizontal, and cumulative percentage of income up the vertical. The word cumulative is itself a tick; leaving it out is the most common lost mark. Two: the diagonal line from corner to corner, labelled as the line of perfect equality, with a one-line reason — any given percentage of people receives exactly that percentage of income. Three: the actual curve starting at the same bottom-left corner and ending at the same top-right corner — it must touch both corners, because zero people earn zero income and all people earn all income. Four: the curve sagging below the diagonal, never above it and never crossing it. Five: an interpretation sentence — the greater the sag, the greater the inequality — ideally anchored with a real reading, such as the poorest sixty percent receiving only around ten percent of income in South Africa. Six: the link to the Gini coefficient — the area of the gap divided by the whole area under the diagonal, giving zero for perfect equality, one for perfect inequality, and about zero comma six three for South Africa. Six ticks, maybe eight marks, and not one of them requires artistic talent — the labels and sentences carry the marks, not the beauty of the curve.

**Bianca:** Third one, and it got political: someone said grants make people dependent and the money should go to job creation instead. Someone else said grants are the only thing standing between millions and hunger. How do we write about that without ranting?

**Tutor:** By doing what economists are paid to do: state what the evidence supports, concede what is genuinely uncertain, and end on the condition that decides the matter. Start with the strong evidence: South Africa's grants measurably reduce extreme poverty and hunger — child nutrition and school attendance improve in grant-receiving households — and grant money is spent immediately in local shops, so it supports township economies rather than vanishing. Concede the real concern honestly: grants alone do not create the jobs that end the need for grants, and a grants bill that grows faster than the economy that funds it is not sustainable arithmetic — the pot must grow for the transfers to keep flowing. Then refuse the false choice, because it is false: the actual policy question is not grants or jobs, it is whether grants are paired with the things that build earning power — schooling, skills, transport to work, a growing economy that hires. Write it as a balanced conclusion: grants are a necessary floor under the poorest and an effective one, but they are a floor, not a ladder; the ladder is education, skills and employment, and the budget has to build both. That structure — evidence, concession, condition — turns a shouting match into a top-band essay answer.

**Bianca:** Last one: growth and redistribution keep being described as both partners and enemies. For an essay verdict, which is it?

**Tutor:** The examinable verdict is: potentially either, and policy design decides which — then you argue one direction at a time. The enemies case: redistribution is financed from the pot, so pushing tax rates too high, or making policy unpredictable, can frighten off the very investment that growth needs; if the pot stalls while the claims on it grow, both projects fail together. The partners case: South Africa's inequality is itself a brake on growth — people without nutrition, schooling or transport cannot produce, and an economy where most people have thin wallets has weak demand for its own businesses; redistribution that builds skills, health and purchasing power is therefore an investment in future growth, not merely a cost. Then the synthesis, which is where the top marks sit: the relationship depends on the kind of redistribution and the state of the economy. Transfers that feed children and school fees that vanish tend to pay growth back; redistribution that funds consumption while infrastructure crumbles does not. So conclude: growth and redistribution are complements when redistribution builds capacity and growth funds it — and substitutes only when either is done badly. One sentence of verdict, two argued directions, one condition. That is the full-mark shape.
