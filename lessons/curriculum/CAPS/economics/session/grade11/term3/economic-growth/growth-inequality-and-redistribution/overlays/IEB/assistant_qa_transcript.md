**Bianca:** Before we finish, the class wants a few things untangled. First: the Gini coefficient gets quoted everywhere, but does one number actually tell us anything useful, or is it just a scary headline?

**Tutor:** It tells you one precise thing, and knowing exactly what that thing is keeps you from misusing it. The Gini compresses the whole Lorenz picture — the entire walk down the queue — into the size of the droop, expressed as a fraction of the largest droop possible. That makes it superb for comparison: across countries, where South Africa's zero comma six three stands against the Nordic zero comma two five to zero comma three, and across time, where you can ask whether twenty years of policy moved our number at all. But compression discards information, and two of the discards matter. The Gini cannot tell you where in the queue the inequality lives — a country whose middle is hollowed out can score the same as a country whose very top ran away — and it says nothing about absolute living standards: a poor country where everyone is equally poor scores beautifully. So the disciplined move in any answer is to quote the Gini for the comparison, then add one queue reading to show where the sag sits — the poorest sixty percent holding about ten percent of income — and one absolute measure, like the food poverty line, for the level. Three numbers, three different jobs. Used that way the Gini is not a scary headline; it is the sharpest single instrument in the topic.

***

**Bianca:** Second: a few of us argued about this at break — if the rich already pay forty-five percent on the top slice, how can anyone claim the tax system still is not doing enough?

**Tutor:** Because the forty-five percent answers a different question from the one the critics are asking, and an economist keeps the questions separate. Question one: is the personal income tax progressive? Emphatically yes — the rates step from eighteen to forty-five percent, and a small minority of high earners supplies most of the revenue. Question two: is the tax system as a whole progressive? Weaker, because personal income tax is only one instrument in the mix. VAT sits beside it, taking the same rate from every spender, which claims a bigger fraction of a poor household's budget — the zero-rating of maize meal and brown bread softens that but does not remove it. Question three, and the one the argument is really about: can taxation alone fix a Gini of zero comma six three? No instrument can, because this year's tax operates on this year's income — a flow — while the deepest inequality sits in the stock of wealth, where the top tenth holds over eighty percent, accumulated across generations when the law barred the majority from accumulating anything. That is why the lesson lists four redistribution methods, not one, and why the skills method — changing what people can earn tomorrow — reaches where the tax tables cannot. So the honest verdict: the income tax is genuinely progressive, and it is still nowhere near the whole answer.

***

**Bianca:** Third: everyone says growth is the priority, but the lesson spent half its time on sharing. If we could only fix one, which one actually comes first?

**Tutor:** The question feels forced to choose, and the strongest answers refuse the choice with reasons. Watch what happens if you pick either one alone. Growth first, sharing never: the pot expands, but with our queue, the expansion pools at the full-pocket end — and an economy where sixty people share ten coins has too few buyers for what the pot produces and too few skilled workers to keep it growing; the boom chokes on the very inequality it ignored. Sharing first, growth never: the coins move, poverty eases for a while — grants genuinely do that — but every moved coin was ladled from a pot that is not growing, and the arithmetic runs out; you cannot redistribute your way to prosperity from a static total. Now look at the successful escapes from poverty around the world and you find the two moving together: growth generating the revenue, and spending on health, schooling and transfers building the workers and customers of the next round of growth. That is what the lesson's phrase means — complements, not substitutes. If a question forces a sequence on you, the defensible version is: pursue growth as the engine while protecting the redistribution that keeps the engine supplied — and always argue why the pair beats either alone.

**Bianca:** Last one: for the essay, is it enough to write that South Africa is 'very unequal', or do we need the actual numbers memorised?

**Tutor:** You need a small set of numbers, because in this topic the numbers are the argument. Written without them, your essay says something every newspaper says; written with them, it demonstrates measurement — which is the skill being assessed. Here is the pocket card, seven items. Growth: real GDP averaging about one percent a decade long, against population growth of one and a half — per capita decline in one subtraction. Doubling: one percent doubles in seventy years, five percent in fourteen — the case for the growth target in one contrast. The queue: poorest sixty percent, about ten percent of income; top ten percent, roughly sixty-five. The summary: Gini about zero comma six three, deepest of any major economy. The response: grants to about twenty-eight million people, tax steps from eighteen to forty-five percent. And the stock behind it all: the top tenth holding over eighty percent of wealth. Seven numbers, each attached to a concept, each usable as evidence in a different paragraph. Rehearse them until they surface without effort, and every essay in this topic starts with its spine already built.
