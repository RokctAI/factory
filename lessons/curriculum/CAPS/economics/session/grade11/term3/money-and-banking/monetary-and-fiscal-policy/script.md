# Part 1 — Expert

This session builds a working command of the two steering wheels of the South African economy: monetary policy, driven by the South African Reserve Bank, and fiscal policy, driven by the Minister of Finance. The case study is one the headlines supply regularly: inflation has risen above the target band. Which instrument does the SARB reach for, how does its effect travel through the economy, and how does that differ from what the finance minister could do?

## Subtopic: Two Policies, Two Drivers

First, get the division of labour absolutely straight, because mixing up who does what is the most common — and most expensive — error in this topic.

Monetary policy is the work of the South African Reserve Bank, the SARB — our central bank. It manages the *money side* of the economy: the cost of borrowing money, the amount of money and credit flowing through the banking system. Its headline instrument is the repo rate. The SARB is independent — it does not take orders from politicians — and its constitutional mandate is to protect the value of the currency, which in practice means keeping inflation inside the target band of three to six percent.

Fiscal policy is the work of the government itself, announced by the Minister of Finance in the annual Budget speech. It manages the *government's own money*: how much the state spends, and how much it collects in taxes. Its two big instruments are government spending and taxation.

A quick test you can apply to any instrument you ever meet: ask "whose hand is on it?" If the answer is the Reserve Bank, it is monetary policy. If the answer is the government and its budget, it is fiscal policy. The repo rate — SARB — monetary. VAT, income tax, spending on schools and grants — the minister — fiscal.

One more pair of labels before we work our scenario. A policy that speeds the economy up — more spending, easier borrowing — is called expansionary. A policy that deliberately slows the economy down is contractionary, or restrictive. Keep both words ready; the exam will ask you to classify.

The questions for this section are with you now: the two drivers matched to their instruments, the SARB's mandate and target band stated precisely, and expansionary distinguished from contractionary.

## Subtopic: The SARB's Move — the Repo Rate

Our scenario: inflation has climbed above six percent, outside the target band. The SARB must respond. Which instrument is it most likely to use?

The answer: it raises the repo rate. The repo rate is the interest rate at which the SARB lends money to the commercial banks — Absa, Standard Bank, FNB, Nedbank and the rest — when they need funds. It is the price the banks themselves pay for money, and everything else is built on top of it.

Now the transmission — the chain through which one interest-rate decision in Pretoria reaches every till point in the country. Follow it link by link.

Link one: the repo rate rises, so borrowing from the SARB costs the commercial banks more.

Link two: the banks pass that cost on by raising their prime lending rate — the rate they charge their own customers. Repo up, prime up, almost immediately.

Link three: loans throughout the economy become more expensive. Home loan instalments rise. Car finance costs more. Credit-card and store-card interest climbs. New borrowing looks less attractive, and households with existing debt have less left over each month.

Link four: households and firms cut back. Consumers postpone the new couch and the bigger car; firms shelve expansion plans because the loan to fund them now eats the expected profit.

Link five: total spending in the economy — aggregate demand — slows. With less money chasing goods and services, the upward pressure on prices eases, and inflation drifts back toward the target band.

That is a contractionary monetary policy: deliberately cooling spending to fight inflation. Notice the honest trade-off, because strong answers mention it: the same medicine that cools prices also cools growth and can cost jobs in the short run. Policy is choice under pain, not magic.

Work this section's questions before the fiscal side opens: the repo rate defined exactly, the five links of the transmission set in order, and the trade-off named honestly.

## Subtopic: The Minister's Toolbox — Two Fiscal Instruments

Now contrast this with what the Minister of Finance controls. The scenario asks for two fiscal instruments — here they are.

Instrument one: government spending. The state is an enormous spender — teachers' and nurses' salaries, social grants, roads, hospitals, school buildings. If the government wants to fight inflation, it can *reduce* the growth of its spending: fewer new projects, tighter budgets. Less government money flowing into the economy means less total demand, which relieves pressure on prices. Cutting spending to cool the economy is contractionary fiscal policy.

Instrument two: taxation. The government sets personal income tax, company tax, VAT and excise duties. To fight inflation it can *raise* taxes: higher income tax leaves households with less disposable income; higher VAT makes spending itself dearer. Less money in pockets, less spending, less inflationary pressure. Raising taxes is likewise contractionary.

And the reverse gear exists on both instruments. In a recession, with the economy limping, the minister can *increase* spending — public works programmes, bigger grants — and *cut* taxes, leaving more money in pockets. Both moves pump demand into the economy: expansionary fiscal policy.

Now classify our whole scenario, as the exam demands. SARB raising the repo rate: contractionary monetary policy. Government cutting spending: contractionary fiscal policy. Government raising taxes: contractionary fiscal policy. All three lean against inflation — same direction, different drivers, different instruments.

Watch for the classic trap question: "The Minister of Finance raises the repo rate to fight inflation — discuss." That sentence is broken before the discussion starts: the minister does not control the repo rate; the SARB does. Spotting the wrong hand on the instrument *is* the answer.

This section's questions are in front of you now — both fiscal instruments named, each one classified in its contractionary and its expansionary direction, and the misattributed-instrument trap recognised on sight.

## Subtopic: Comparing the Two Policies — and Answering Like an Economist

Let us line the two policies up side by side, because comparison questions are where the marks concentrate.

Speed of decision: monetary policy is nimble — the SARB's Monetary Policy Committee meets every two months and can move the repo rate the same day. Fiscal policy is slow — the Budget comes once a year, and big changes need political approval.

Breadth of impact: the repo rate is a blunt, economy-wide lever — it hits every borrower in the country, whether they were overspending or not. Fiscal instruments can be aimed: a tax on luxury goods, spending directed at infrastructure in one province. Blunt but fast, versus targeted but slow — that contrast is worth memorising as a sentence.

Who feels it first: repo-rate changes bite borrowers — mortgage holders, indebted firms — before anyone else. Tax changes bite whoever the tax touches; spending cuts bite whoever depended on the programmes. Every policy has a distributional edge, and examiners reward you for noticing who carries it.

And the two can pull together or apart. Fighting inflation works best when both are contractionary — the SARB tightening while the treasury restrains spending. If the SARB tightens while the government spends freely, the policies work against each other, and the SARB usually has to tighten even harder.

Your self-check routine for any policy question. One: name the problem — inflation above the band, or recession? Two: name the driver — SARB or Minister of Finance? Three: name the instrument — repo rate, spending, or taxes. Four: state the direction and classify it — expansionary or contractionary. Five: walk the transmission chain step by step to the effect on spending and prices. Five steps, and no policy question in Grade 11 or Grade 12 — business cycles, growth, inflation — can surprise you.

The final questions of this part are with you now: speed against reach, the distributional edge of each instrument, and the five-step routine applied to a policy scenario you have not seen before.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same two policies from a household in Tembisa — one hand on the price of borrowing, another hand on the household budget, and a month that has to balance either way.

## Subtopic: Two Drivers, Two Different Steering Wheels

Two completely different people are steering this economy, and almost every mark in this topic depends on not mixing them up.

The first is the South African Reserve Bank — the SARB. Think of it as the one who sets the price of borrowing money in this country. It does not spend your taxes and it does not build anything. Its job is to look after what your money is worth, which in practice means keeping inflation inside the band of three to six percent, and its main tool is one number called the repo rate. Importantly, it is independent: no politician can phone it and order a change. That is monetary policy.

The second is the government, speaking through the Minister of Finance in the Budget every year. This is the household-budget side of the country: what the state spends, and what it collects. Salaries for teachers and nurses, social grants, clinics, roads, RDP housing — that is the spending. Income tax, VAT, company tax, the duty on fuel — that is the collecting. That is fiscal policy.

So here is the one question that sorts any instrument you will ever be shown: whose hand is on it? Repo rate — the Reserve Bank's hand — monetary. VAT, grants, teachers' salaries, a new hospital — the Minister's hand, through the Budget — fiscal. Ask it every single time, because examiners write sentences designed to catch people who do not.

And two labels to keep in your pocket. Think of the economy as a balloon full of spending. Anything that blows air in — more government spending, lower taxes, cheaper loans — is expansionary. Anything that lets air out — spending cuts, higher taxes, dearer loans — is contractionary. For any move at all, ask whether it puts spending power into people's hands or takes it out.

Your questions for this section are with you now — for each one, just find the hand on the lever and say whether air is going in or coming out.

## Subtopic: How One Number in Pretoria Reaches Your Street

Now the scenario: prices are rising too fast, inflation has pushed above six percent, and the Reserve Bank has to act. It raises the repo rate. Here is how a decision in Pretoria ends up in your household, one step at a time.

Step one: the repo rate is what the Reserve Bank charges the big commercial banks — Absa, Standard Bank, FNB, Nedbank and the rest — when they borrow from it. Raise it, and money has just become more expensive for the banks themselves.

Step two: banks do not absorb that. They raise the rate they charge you, called the prime lending rate. Repo goes up, prime goes up, almost immediately.

Step three: now every loan in the country costs more. The bond repayment on the house goes up. The car instalment goes up. The store card and the credit card get more expensive. Someone who was planning to borrow decides to wait.

Step four: households and businesses pull in. The new lounge suite waits another year. The panel-beating shop postpones the second workshop, because the loan for it now swallows the profit it was supposed to make.

Step five: with everyone spending less, the total amount of money chasing goods in the shops falls. Sellers who were comfortably raising prices every few months find that customers have stopped following, so price increases slow down, and inflation drifts back toward the band.

That is a contractionary monetary policy, and it is worth being honest about what it costs. The same medicine that cools prices also cools the economy: growth slows, and some jobs are lost in the short run. The person with a bond feels it long before the person with none. This is why the Reserve Bank does not raise rates lightly — it is choosing between two kinds of pain, not performing magic.

Take the questions for this section now, and try telling the chain to yourself in your own words first — repo, prime, loans, spending, prices — because it is a story where each step causes the next, not a list to memorise.

## Subtopic: The Budget's Two Taps — Fast Tool, Slow Tool

The Minister of Finance cannot touch the repo rate. What the Minister has is two taps on the country's budget.

Tap one is government spending. The state is the biggest spender in the economy, so turning that tap down — fewer new projects, tighter departmental budgets, slower hiring — means less money flowing out into the country, which takes pressure off prices. Turning it up in a recession — public works, bigger grants, building programmes — pumps money in and gets the economy moving.

Tap two is taxation. Raise income tax or VAT and there is simply less left in people's pockets at the end of the month, so they spend less and prices calm down. Cut taxes and the opposite happens.

So put the whole scenario together and classify it. The Reserve Bank raising the repo rate: contractionary monetary policy. The government cutting back its spending: contractionary fiscal policy. The government raising taxes: contractionary fiscal policy too. Three different levers, two different hands, all leaning the same way against inflation — all letting air out of the balloon.

Watch for the sentence that is broken before you even start answering it: "The Minister of Finance raises the repo rate to fight inflation." The Minister does not control the repo rate; the Reserve Bank does. Spotting the wrong hand on the lever is itself the answer to that question.

Now the comparison that examiners love. Monetary policy is fast but blunt. Fast, because the Reserve Bank's committee meets through the year and can change the rate on the day it decides. Blunt, because a rate change hits every borrower in the country at once — the family whose bond was already tight gets the same increase as a company that was borrowing recklessly. Fiscal policy is slow but aimed. Slow, because the Budget comes round once a year and needs political agreement. Aimed, because a tax can be placed on luxury goods specifically, and spending can be directed at one province, one programme, one road.

And the two hands can pull together or against each other. Inflation comes down fastest when the Reserve Bank tightens and the Treasury restrains spending at the same time. If the Bank tightens while the government keeps spending freely, they are working against each other, and the Bank usually ends up having to tighten harder — which means ordinary borrowers pay more than they otherwise would have.

Here come the last questions of the lesson: the two taps, the two hands, and fast-but-blunt against slow-but-aimed. Land these and no policy question in Grade 11 or Grade 12 can catch you out.
