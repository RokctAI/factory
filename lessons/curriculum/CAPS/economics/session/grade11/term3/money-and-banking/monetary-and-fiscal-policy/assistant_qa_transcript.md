**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! First one, and it is bothering a lot of people: how can making loans MORE expensive bring prices DOWN? Surely if banks charge more, everything ends up costing more?

**Tutor:** That objection is a good one, and it fails for a reason worth understanding. You are watching the cost side; the repo rate works on the spending side. Yes, a higher rate raises the instalment on a bond, a car and a store card, and that is precisely the mechanism, not a side effect. Money that would have bought furniture or a weekend away now goes to the bank instead. Repeat that across millions of households and firms and the total amount of money being spent in the economy falls. Inflation is what happens when too much spending chases too few goods, so when the chasing slows, sellers who were comfortably raising prices every few months discover that customers no longer follow, and the increases stop sticking. Picture demand and supply for the whole country: the demand side pulls back while the supply of goods is unchanged, so the pressure pushing prices up eases. There is a real cost, and strong answers say so — the same medicine cools growth and can cost jobs — which is exactly why the Reserve Bank does not use it lightly.

***

**Bianca:** Next: under pressure, people blank on which policy is which. Is there a way to keep monetary and fiscal straight in a test without memorising two long lists?

**Tutor:** One question does it: whose hand is on the lever? The repo rate is pulled by the Reserve Bank, so it is monetary policy. Anything that lives in the Budget — income tax, VAT, fuel levies, social grants, teachers' salaries, a new clinic, a road — is pulled by the Minister of Finance, so it is fiscal policy. That single question is enough, because there is no instrument in this syllabus that belongs to both. It also happens to be the question the trap items are built to dodge. A paper will write "the Minister raised the repo rate to curb inflation" and rely on you reading straight past the mistake and starting to discuss inflation instead. If you have the habit, you notice the wrong hand immediately, and saying so IS the answer worth the marks. Build the habit outside the exam: whenever an economics headline crosses your path, name three things about it — the instrument, the hand, and the policy type. It takes seconds and it makes the reflex automatic.

***

**Bianca:** This one turned into a debate in class: if fiscal policy can also fight inflation, why lean on the repo rate at all? Raising it seems to punish ordinary people with home loans.

**Tutor:** You have walked into a genuine argument among economists, and the honest starting point is that there is no painless tool. Fiscal tightening hurts as well: cutting spending means fewer projects, slower hiring and thinner programmes for the people who depend on them, and raising taxes takes money directly out of every pocket it touches. So the choice is not pain against no pain — it is which pain, applied how quickly, and to whom. Monetary policy usually does the frontline work for two practical reasons. It is fast: the Reserve Bank's committee meets through the year and can change the rate on the day it decides, while the Budget arrives once a year and needs political agreement behind it. And the Bank is independent, so it can make an unpopular decision that elected politicians might avoid in a difficult year. The cost of that speed is bluntness — a rate change hits every borrower at once, whether or not they were part of the problem — while fiscal instruments can be aimed at a particular tax, programme or province. Fast but blunt, against slower but aimed: that trade-off is the heart of most comparison questions in this topic.

***

**Bianca:** Last one: the words themselves are causing trouble. What do "expansionary" and "contractionary" actually mean, and how do we apply them without second-guessing ourselves?

**Tutor:** Anchor both words to one image: the economy as a balloon full of spending. Expansionary means blowing air in — more government spending, lower taxes, cheaper loans — so more spending power reaches people's hands and the economy inflates. Contractionary means letting air out — spending cuts, higher taxes, dearer loans — so spending power is withdrawn and the economy deliberately deflates a little to cool prices. Then apply one test to any move at all: does this put spending power into people's hands, or take it out? In, expansionary; out, contractionary. Note that the labels are about direction, not about which hand pulled the lever, so both policies come in both directions: a repo cut is expansionary monetary policy, a repo rise is contractionary monetary policy, a tax cut is expansionary fiscal policy, a tax rise is contractionary fiscal policy. Our whole inflation scenario is air coming out from three directions at once — the rate up, spending restrained and taxes raised — three contractionary moves leaning together on the same overheated balloon. Name the direction and the driver separately in your answer, and full marks follow.
