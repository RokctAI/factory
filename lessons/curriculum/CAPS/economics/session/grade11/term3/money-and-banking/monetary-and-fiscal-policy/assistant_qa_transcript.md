**Student:** Why does making loans MORE expensive bring prices DOWN? Surely if banks charge more, everything costs more?

**Bianca:** Ooh, this is such a good confusion to clear up — you're looking at the cost side, but the repo rate works on the *spending* side. Yes, your loan instalment goes up, and that's exactly the point: money that would have bought a new TV now goes to the bank instead. Multiply that across millions of households and firms, and total spending in the economy drops. Inflation happens when too much spending chases too few goods — so less chasing means sellers can't keep hiking prices. The pain in your instalment *is* the medicine working, which is also why the SARB doesn't do it lightly.

**Student:** How do I keep monetary and fiscal policy straight in a test? Under pressure I just blank.

**Bianca:** One question saves you every time: "whose hand is on the lever?" Repo rate → the Reserve Bank's hand → monetary. Anything to do with taxes, VAT, grants, teachers' salaries, roads → the Budget → the Minister of Finance's hand → fiscal. Trick questions rely on you not asking it — they'll write "the Minister raised the repo rate" and hope you sail past. Circle the instrument, name the hand, and the trap becomes free marks. Practise on any economics headline you see this week: instrument, hand, policy type. Three labels, done.

**Student:** If both policies can fight inflation, why don't we just use the fiscal ones? Raising the repo rate seems to hurt ordinary people with home loans.

**Bianca:** You've hit a real debate that economists genuinely have! Fiscal tightening hurts too, though — cutting spending means fewer projects and smaller programmes, and raising taxes takes money straight out of pockets. There's no painless option. In practice the repo rate does the frontline work because the SARB can move fast — its committee meets every two months — while the Budget comes once a year and needs political approval. And the SARB is independent, so it can make the unpopular call that politicians might dodge. Fast-but-blunt versus slow-but-targeted: usually the fast tool moves first.

**Student:** What does "expansionary" and "contractionary" actually mean? The words confuse me more than the ideas.

**Bianca:** Then let's anchor the words to a balloon! Expansionary = blowing air in: more government spending, lower taxes, cheaper loans — the economy inflates with extra spending. Contractionary = letting air out: spending cuts, higher taxes, dearer loans — the economy deliberately deflates a little to cool prices. So for any policy move, ask: does this put spending power *into* people's hands or take it *out*? Into = expansionary, out = contractionary. Our whole inflation scenario — repo up, spending restrained, taxes up — is all air *out*: three contractionary moves leaning on the same overheated balloon.
