# Part 1 — Expert

This session treats the environment the way economists must: as scarce, valuable and dangerously easy to destroy for free. We diagnose why markets, left alone, deteriorate the environment; open the toolbox governments use to protect it; define sustainability properly; and then bring it all home — because South Africa is simultaneously a coal-dependent polluter, a climate victim of droughts and floods, and the site of one of the world's most watched energy transitions.

## Subtopic: Why Markets Fail the Environment

Start with the economics, because "people are greedy" is not an answer that earns marks. The environment deteriorates through a precise market failure, and its name is externalities.

An externality is a cost or benefit that lands on someone who was not part of the transaction. When a factory burns coal, the buyer pays for electricity and the seller pays for coal — but the neighbour breathing the smoke pays too, in health, and no price tag ever reached her. Her cost is external to the market. The result is mispricing: because the polluter does not pay the full cost of production, the product is too cheap, so too much of it gets made — the market oversupplies exactly the activities that damage most. Pollution is not the market breaking its rules; it is the market obeying prices that lie.

The second failure: many environmental goods are common resources — nobody owns them, everybody can use them. A fish stock, a river, clean air, groundwater. Each user gains the full benefit of taking one more fish while the cost of the shrinking stock is shared by everyone — so each rational user takes more, and the resource collapses. This is the famous tragedy of the commons: what belongs to everyone is cared for by no one. It explains overfishing off our coasts, overgrazed communal land, and water pumped from shared aquifers faster than rain refills them.

Third: future generations are not in the room. Markets record the demands of today's buyers; people born in 2060 place no orders. Costs that arrive decades later — a warming climate, an exhausted mine, a poisoned river — are discounted or ignored by decisions taken for this quarter's profit.

South Africa's ledger of these failures is concrete: acid mine drainage seeping from a century of gold mining into Gauteng's water; among the world's most polluted air on the Mpumalanga Highveld, where coal power stations cluster; overfished West Coast stocks; and one of the world's highest carbon-emission rates per person, because our electricity is overwhelmingly coal.

The questions for this section are with you now: externality defined with the smoke example, the commons tragedy reasoned step by step, and each South African case matched to its failure.

## Subtopic: Protecting the Environment — the Policy Toolbox

If the diagnosis is prices that lie and property that nobody holds, the remedies follow logically: fix the prices, set the rules, or fix the ownership. Governments use four families of tools, and the exam wants all four with examples.

Family one: command and control — direct regulation. The state sets legal limits and punishes breaches: emission standards for power stations, minimum water quality, environmental impact assessments before mines and developments may proceed, fishing quotas and licences, protected areas where extraction is banned outright. This is South Africa's backbone: the constitutional right to an environment not harmful to health, given teeth by environmental legislation. Strength: certainty — the limit is the limit. Weakness: it needs inspectors, and a rule that is not enforced is a suggestion.

Family two: market-based instruments — make the polluter pay. Environmental taxes attach the missing price tag: South Africa's carbon tax, a pioneer for a developing country, charges companies per ton of greenhouse gas; the plastic bag levy and the tyre levy work the same logic at the till. Do you see the elegance? The externality was a cost with no price — the tax gives it a price, so the polluter finally faces the true cost, and pollution becomes expensive to produce rather than free. The mirror image is subsidies for clean choices: solar water heater programmes, rebates for renewable energy. And between tax and rule sits the tradable permit system: cap total pollution, issue permits, let firms trade them — those who can cut cheaply sell to those who cannot, so the cap is met at the lowest total cost.

Family three: property rights and community management. Where the commons collapses because nobody owns it, one remedy is to create owners: fishing quotas that belong to communities, communal conservancies where villages own and manage wildlife and keep tourism revenue. Owners harvest sustainably because the future value belongs to them.

Family four: international agreements — because carbon and oceans ignore borders. One country cutting alone changes little and pays much; agreements like the Paris Agreement, which South Africa signed, commit countries to emission targets together, easing the temptation to free-ride on everyone else's efforts.

For evaluation marks: rules give certainty but need policing; taxes harness self-interest but hit the poor hardest unless revenue is recycled; permits find the cheapest cuts but need honest measurement; agreements bind the world but enforce weakly. No tool is complete — real policy mixes them.

Work this section's questions now: each tool family named with a South African example, the carbon tax argued through the externality logic, and one strength-weakness pair evaluated.

## Subtopic: Sustainability — Living Off the Interest, Not the Capital

Now the organising idea of the whole topic. The classic definition, from the Brundtland Commission, you should quote: sustainable development is development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs.

Translate it into the economics of a household. Natural resources are capital — a stock that can generate income forever if maintained. A fish stock breeds; a forest regrows; soil renews; the sun shines for free. Living sustainably means living off the interest — harvesting no faster than the stock regenerates. Deterioration is eating the capital: catching fish faster than they breed, mining soil fertility, burning in decades fossil fuels that took millions of years to form. The bank balance runs down, and the account belongs to your grandchildren.

Sharpen it with the resource distinction. Renewable resources — fish, forests, water, soil — regenerate, but only below a threshold rate of use: fished gently, a stock lasts forever; fished hard, it collapses and the renewable becomes non-renewable in practice. Non-renewable resources — coal, oil, gold, platinum — do not regenerate on any human timescale: every ton used is gone, so sustainability there means using the proceeds to build other assets — skills, infrastructure — that replace the depleted wealth, rather than consuming it all today.

Sustainability stands on three legs, and the exam loves the tripod: environmental — keep the stocks alive; economic — keep production and livelihoods going; social — share the costs and benefits fairly. Pull any leg away and the stool falls: an economy that destroys its water destroys itself; a conservation plan that starves the village next to the park will be ignored; growth that poisons the poor's air is not development.

South Africa's hardest version of the tripod is the just transition: our electricity is overwhelmingly coal, coal regions like Mpumalanga live from coal wages, yet the climate and our trading partners demand we decarbonise. Transition too slowly and we cook, and our exports get taxed at green borders; transition carelessly and we throw mining towns onto the street. Justice here means moving to clean energy while retraining workers and rebuilding regional economies — all three legs at once.

Attend to this section's questions before the final piece: the Brundtland definition quoted, interest-versus-capital applied to a renewable and a non-renewable, and the tripod tested on the just transition.

## Subtopic: The Global and Local Impact on South Africa

Close with the impacts, because the syllabus asks for them and because they are no longer predictions — they are recent memory.

The global problem first, in one paragraph of mechanism. Burning fossil fuels releases greenhouse gases; the gases trap heat; the planet warms — it has already warmed more than one degree above pre-industrial levels; and warming loads the dice for extreme weather everywhere: harder droughts, heavier floods, rising seas. The injustice at the heart of global negotiations: the North built its wealth on two centuries of emissions, yet the South — with the thinnest budgets and driest lands — takes the earliest and hardest hits. Africa emits a few percent of the world's greenhouse gases and endures a share of the damage many times larger.

Now South Africa's own recent ledger. Water: Cape Town's Day Zero drought, when the city came within weeks of shutting its taps, previewed a permanently drier south-west; South Africa is already a water-scarce country, with rainfall well below the world average. Floods: the Durban floods of 2022 killed over four hundred people and destroyed billions of rands of homes, roads and factory stock — the heavier-rain end of the same loaded dice. Agriculture: shifting rainfall menaces maize country and stresses fruit and wine exports; drought years already cut harvests and spike food prices, and the poorest households — spending the largest share of income on food — absorb the worst of it. Health and heat: rising temperatures hit outdoor workers and informal settlements hardest, again the poor first.

And the economic exposure runs through trade: as Northern markets introduce carbon border taxes, exports made with coal-fired electricity — our steel, our aluminium, eventually more — face penalties at the border. Our coal dependence is becoming a tariff on everything we sell.

Against this, the response you should be able to cite: South Africa's Paris commitments; the carbon tax; renewable energy auctions that have built some of the world's cheapest wind and solar; and the Just Energy Transition Partnership, in which wealthy nations pledged billions to help finance our move from coal — the world's test case for whether climate justice can be engineered. The verdict sentence for essays: South Africa is triply exposed — as polluter, as victim and as coal-dependent exporter — which is precisely why sustainability here is not green sentiment but economic survival.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the warming mechanism chained, two local impacts cited with evidence, and the triple exposure argued as one sentence.

## Subtopic: The Smoke Nobody Pays For

Picture a small factory beside a river, upstream from a village. The factory makes bricks; brick buyers pay for bricks; the owner pays for clay and wages. Everyone in the deal is happy. But the kiln smoke drifts over the village, and the wash-water runs into the river where children swim and gogos draw water for gardens. The village coughs and the gardens sicken — and the village was never part of the deal. They pay a cost that appears on nobody's invoice.

That invisible bill is what economists call an externality — a cost dumped on outsiders. And here is the trap: because the brickmaker never pays that bill, bricks are priced too cheap, so the market cheerfully orders more bricks — and more smoke — than it would if the full cost showed on the tag. The pollution is not the market malfunctioning; it is the market working perfectly on prices that lie.

Second scene, same village: the river itself, and the fish in it. The river belongs to nobody and everybody. Each fisherman reasons: if I take one more fish, I get the whole fish, and the emptier river is everyone's problem. Every fisherman reasons the same way — because each one is being individually sensible — and the river empties. What belongs to everyone is cared for by no one: the tragedy of the commons, and it is the story of our overfished coasts, overgrazed shared land, and boreholes pumping shared water dry.

Third scene: who is missing from both stories? The children not yet born. Markets hear today's buyers; the girl who will need this river in 2060 places no orders today. Her interests carry no price — unless somebody puts one on deliberately.

Scale the village up and you have South Africa: coal smoke over the Mpumalanga Highveld, mine acid in Gauteng's water, one of the world's highest pollution rates per person. Same stories, bigger river.

Your questions for this section are with you now — each shows a deal, and asks: who is paying without being in the deal, and why does that make the market order too much?

## Subtopic: Putting a Price on the Smoke

If the problem is bills that reach nobody, the fixes almost write themselves: send the bill, set the rules, or give the river an owner.

Fix one: rules with teeth. The law says: this much smoke and no more; no mine without an environmental study first; this fishing season, this quota, and no more. South Africa's constitution itself promises an environment not harmful to health, and laws stack on that promise. Rules are clear — but a rule without an inspector is a suggestion, and enforcement is where rules live or die.

Fix two: make the polluter pay — the clever one. Remember the missing invoice? The state can write it. You have paid this fix at the till: the plastic bag levy — those cents exist so the bag stops being free and starts being a choice. The carbon tax does the same to big polluters: every ton of greenhouse gas now costs money, so cutting pollution stops being charity and becomes saving. The moment the smoke has a price, cleaning up becomes profitable — the market's own greed is turned around and harnessed to fix what greed broke. The state can also pay people to go clean: solar rebates and renewable subsidies are the same tool in reverse.

Fix three: give it an owner. The unowned river empties, so create owners: a fishing quota that belongs to this village forever, a conservancy where the community owns the wildlife and keeps the tourism money. Watch how behaviour flips: an owner who will still be here next year harvests gently, because next year's fish are already hers.

Fix four: get the neighbours to sign. Smoke drifts across borders; my country cutting alone helps little and costs much — so countries sign together, as South Africa did in the Paris Agreement, promising targets side by side so no one free-rides for long.

No fix is perfect: rules need police, taxes pinch the poor unless the money is returned wisely, ownership must be assigned fairly, and world agreements are promises more than laws. Real protection stacks all four.

Take this section's questions now — each names a tool, and asks which broken piece of the market it repairs.

## Subtopic: Eating the Seed Mealies

Every farming family has always known the rule this whole topic stands on: eat the harvest, never the seed. Eat your seed mealies this winter, and next season there is no crop, no matter how hungry you were.

That rule, scaled to a planet, is sustainability. The famous definition — worth quoting word for word — says: meet the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs. Nature is the seed stock. Fish breed, forests regrow, rain refills dams — use them slower than they renew, and they feed you forever; use them faster, and the renewable dies like a non-renewable. Coal, gold and platinum never regrow at all — every ton spent is spent once, so the only honest use is turning that once-off wealth into things that last: skills, railways, working towns. Digging up the family gold to buy groceries is eating seed.

Sustainability has to stand on three legs at once: nature kept alive, the economy kept working, people treated fairly. Kick any leg out and it falls: kill the rivers and the economy dies with them; save the park but starve the village beside it, and the village will not save the park; grow rich while the poor breathe the smoke, and you have not developed — you have just moved the bill.

South Africa is living the three-legged test in real time — and it is not theory here. Cape Town counted down to Day Zero, days from dry taps. The 2022 Durban floods took over four hundred lives and drowned billions of rands of homes and workplaces. Our fields are getting thirstier while food prices climb — hitting hardest the households that spend most of their money on food. Meanwhile almost all our electricity still comes from coal — which warms the same climate that is flooding us, and which our trading partners have begun to tax at their borders: soon, goods made with dirty power pay extra to enter rich markets. Coal is becoming a tax on everything we sell.

So the country is trying the hardest sums in the topic: close the coal era without abandoning the coal towns — clean energy built fast, workers retrained, Mpumalanga given a future — with wealthy nations pledging billions to help pay, since their smoke built their wealth first. The world is watching whether we manage it, and your generation will run the attempt.

And here come the last questions of the lesson: seed versus harvest applied to a fish stock and a coal seam, the three legs tested on a real dilemma, and Day Zero and the floods cited as the evidence that this topic stopped being theory.
