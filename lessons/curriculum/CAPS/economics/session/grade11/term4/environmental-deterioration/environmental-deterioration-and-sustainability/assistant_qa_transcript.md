**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! First one: if pollution is really the market 'working perfectly on prices that lie', why not just let the market fix itself? Markets fix shortages and surpluses on their own, after all.

**Tutor:** Because the self-fixing mechanism runs on price signals, and this is the one failure where the signal itself is broken. Think about how a shortage fixes itself: buyers bid the price up, the higher price recruits more supply and rations demand, and the gap closes — the price carried the information. Now run pollution through that machinery. The neighbour's damaged lungs and the poisoned garden generate no price at all: no invoice reaches the brickmaker, so no signal tells the market to produce fewer bricks. The market cannot correct an error it cannot see, and prices are its only eyes. Worse, the feedback that does exist points the wrong way — the polluter's costs are lower than the truth, so competition actually rewards the dirtiest producer with the biggest market share. That is why economists, who normally trust markets deeply, treat externalities as a case for intervention: not to overrule the price system but to repair it, by writing the missing invoice through a tax, a rule or an owner. Keep this framing for essays — the environmental argument is not markets versus government; it is that government must fix the prices so the market can finally do its job on true information.

**Bianca:** Next: the carbon tax and the plastic bag levy both get accused of being just another way to squeeze money out of ordinary people. Is that criticism fair, and how do we handle it in an exam answer?

**Tutor:** It is partly fair, and the exam rewards you for saying exactly which part. The unfair part first: the point of these taxes is not revenue but behaviour. The levy exists so that a bag is no longer free, and the carbon tax exists so that a ton of emissions is no longer free — each one restores the missing cost so that polluting less becomes financially attractive. Judged as environmental instruments, they are working with the market's own logic, and that is their genius. The fair part: any tax on consumption or energy tends to be regressive — the poor spend a larger share of their income on the taxed things, so the pinch lands hardest at the bottom, and a carbon tax that raises electricity costs touches every poor household's hotplate. The answer to that is not to abandon the instrument but to recycle the revenue deliberately: use the collections to fund free basic electricity, public transport, or cash transfers, so the poor are compensated while the price signal keeps steering everyone away from pollution. Economists call this a revenue-neutral or recycled design. So the full-marks response has three moves: state the purpose — pricing the externality to change behaviour; concede the equity problem — regressive impact on the poor; and give the design solution — recycle the revenue toward poor households. Purpose, problem, design. That structure converts an angry accusation into an evaluation answer.

**Bianca:** Third: sustainability sounds like it means stopping growth, and in a country with our unemployment that feels impossible to defend. Are we being asked to choose between jobs and the planet?

**Tutor:** No — and the strongest answers show why that choice is mostly an illusion, in both directions. Direction one: environmental destruction is itself an economic destroyer, on evidence from our own recent history. Cape Town nearly ran out of water — and a city without water is a city without tourism, factories or jobs. The Durban floods took over four hundred lives and billions of rands of homes, roads and stock in days. Drought years cut harvests and spike the food prices that poor households feel most. Deterioration does not trade off against the economy; it eats the economy. Direction two: the transition is not only a cost — it is also an investment programme. Building wind and solar plants, manufacturing components, insulating homes, restoring land and water systems: these are jobs, many of them in exactly the regions coal will leave behind, and our renewable auctions have already produced some of the world's cheapest new electricity. What is true — and you must concede it — is that the costs and benefits land on different people: a Mpumalanga coal worker loses first, while the new industries hire elsewhere and later. That is why the syllabus and the country insist on the word just in just transition: retraining, regional rebuilding, and outside finance from the wealthy nations whose historical smoke created the problem. So the exam answer is: not growth versus planet, but unmanaged deterioration versus a managed transition — and the second is the only one with jobs at the end of it.

**Bianca:** Last one: for the essay on protecting the environment, there are four tool families and we always run out of time. What is the highest-scoring way to organise it?

**Tutor:** Organise by the failure each tool repairs, because that shows understanding rather than memory — and it happens to be faster to write. Open with one diagnostic sentence: the environment deteriorates because costs go unpriced, resources go unowned, and countries free-ride on each other. Now each tool family gets a paragraph with the same internal shape — name, mechanism, South African example, one-line evaluation. Regulation: the state sets limits directly — emission standards, quotas, impact assessments; certain in effect but only as strong as its inspectors. Market instruments: the state prices the externality — the carbon tax and bag levy make pollution cost money, subsidies pay for clean choices, tradable permits find the cheapest cuts; efficient, but regressive unless revenue is recycled. Property rights: the state creates owners for the commons — community quotas and conservancies; owners protect future value, but the initial allocation must be fair. International agreements: countries commit together — Paris — because carbon ignores borders; broad but weakly enforced. Close with the sentence that earns the synthesis mark: no single tool repairs all three failures, so effective protection stacks them — rules for the worst harms, prices for the rest, owners for the commons, and treaties for the planet. Four paragraphs, one diagnostic open, one synthesis close. Practise that skeleton once tonight and the essay writes itself under exam pressure.
