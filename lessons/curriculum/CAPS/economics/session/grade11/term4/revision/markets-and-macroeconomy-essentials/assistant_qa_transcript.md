**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! First: if the free market is so efficient, why doesn't South Africa just deregulate everything and let the till decide? One of us argued exactly that.

**Tutor:** Then let us test the argument with the tools this year gave you, because it half-works — and knowing which half is the skill. The till genuinely is a brilliant information machine: no committee can learn what millions of price signals reveal daily, which is why planned economies produced gluts beside shortages. Deregulation where markets work well — many sellers, informed buyers, no spillovers — is usually good economics. But this year also handed you the systematic list of where the till fails, and South Africa scores high on every entry. Public goods: no firm profitably builds street lighting or a court system, so pure deregulation simply goes without them. Externalities: the till records the price of coal power, not the cost of the smoke. Inequality: the market feeds those who can pay — and in the most unequal major economy on Earth, the till alone would leave millions outside the shop entirely. Market power: deregulate and you do not always get competition; sometimes you get one dominant firm setting prices — count the sellers, as you learned. And the dual economy sharpens all of it: the informal side lacks the titles, credit and infrastructure that make markets work even on their own terms. So the examinable answer is not free market good or bad — it is: markets where competition and information are strong, state action where the failures are structural, and the whole judged case by case. That is what mixed economy means when it is a decision rule instead of a label.

**Bianca:** Next, a calculation panic: in elasticity questions, some of us keep getting answers like minus two and freezing. Is negative elastic or inelastic?

**Tutor:** Freeze no more — here is the whole procedure, and the sign question dissolved. Price and quantity demanded move in opposite directions by the law of demand, so the raw division is negative almost every time; economists therefore read the ABSOLUTE size and quietly drop the sign. Minus two means size two: elastic. Minus nought comma four means size nought comma four: inelastic. The marker wants the magnitude compared to one, and most memos accept the answer with or without the minus, provided your interpretation matches the size. Now the full drill, because the sign is only step three of five. Step one: identify which variable changed first — price — and compute its percentage change from the ORIGINAL value. Step two: compute the percentage change in quantity from ITS original value. Step three: divide quantity's percentage by price's percentage — order matters; upside-down fractions are the commonest error in this question type. Step four: interpret the size against one — above one, buyers answered hard, elastic; below one, they shrugged, inelastic; exactly one, unitary. Step five — where the real marks hide: connect to revenue. Size two with a price rise means revenue falls; size nought comma four with a price rise means revenue rises. Practise until the five steps run without thought, and this question becomes the most reliable four marks on the paper.

**Bianca:** Third: in the cost tables, why do we stop where marginal revenue equals marginal cost instead of where average cost is lowest? Producing at the cheapest average sounds smarter.

**Tutor:** It sounds smarter and it is a genuine trap — so let us dismantle it with one honest example. Lowest average cost answers the question: at which output is each plate cheapest to make? But the firm is not collecting cheapness; it is collecting PROFIT, and profit asks a different question: does the NEXT plate add more to revenue than to cost? Suppose the cheapest average sits at forty plates, each averaging eighteen rand against a thirty-rand price. Tempted to stop? Interrogate plate forty-one: it costs, say, twenty-two rand marginally — above average, pulling the average up — but it EARNS thirty. Nine rand of pure extra profit, abandoned if you stop at the prettiest average. Plate forty-two costs twenty-six: still four rand of profit. Plate forty-three costs thirty-one: now the next plate eats profit, and THERE you stop — at marginal revenue equals marginal cost, beyond the lowest-average point. The average tells you how the past units behaved; the margin tells you what the next decision does — and decisions live at the margin. In the exam's table, run one finger down the MC column against the constant price, expand while MC sits below price, stop at equality, and then — the finishing touch markers love — state the profit as total revenue minus total cost at that output to prove the rule delivered. Cheapest is a fact about units; profitable is a fact about choices. Choose.

**Bianca:** Last one: when the repo rate goes up, my family feels the bond payment within a month. Why would the Reserve Bank deliberately hurt households like that?

**Tutor:** Because it is choosing the smaller hurt — and understanding that trade is understanding the whole of monetary policy. Start with what the Bank is defending you from. Inflation is a tax nobody legislates: at high inflation the grocery money loses value in the taxi on the way to the shop, and it lands hardest on exactly the households with no assets that grow — the poor hold cash and grants, the wealthy hold property and shares. Runaway inflation has wrecked economies within living memory, and once entrenched, expectations of inflation cause inflation, a spiral brutal to break. Now the mechanism you named: raising the repo makes borrowing dearer — your bond, car finance, shop credit — so households and firms spend less, demand cools, and sellers lose the power to keep raising prices. Your family's heavier bond payment IS the medicine working, not a side effect — the Bank is deliberately cooling total spending, and it has no scalpel that reaches only someone else's spending. The honest ledger: the cost is real — slower growth, postponed hiring, strained bonds — and the benefit is a currency that still buys next year what it roughly buys today, the foundation under every wage negotiation, savings account and grant. That is also why the mandate lives with an independent central bank: an election-season government would always choose the painless month over the stable year. And notice the exam link: this is the two-drivers comparison in real life — the fast pedal pressed on your own street. Feel it, name the mechanism, weigh both sides: that is the full-marks answer, and the grown-up one.
