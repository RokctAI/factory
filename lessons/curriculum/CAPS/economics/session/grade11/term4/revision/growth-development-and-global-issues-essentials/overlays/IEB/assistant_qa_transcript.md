**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! First: the Lorenz curve keeps coming up as a describe-and-interpret question, and people either draw it wrong or describe it without numbers. What is the safe method?

**Tutor:** Build it in four sentences that never change, then attach the numbers last. Sentence one — the axes: the horizontal carries the population accumulated from poorest to richest; the vertical carries their accumulated share of total income. Sentence two — the benchmark: perfect equality is the straight diagonal, because any twenty percent of people would hold exactly twenty percent of income. Sentence three — the reality: the actual curve bows below the diagonal, and the deeper the bow, the worse the inequality. Sentence four — the score: the Gini coefficient compresses that bow into one number between zero and one. Now the numbers that turn description into interpretation: South Africa's Gini sits around nought comma six five, among the highest ever recorded, and on our curve you can pass sixty percent of the population while accumulating barely a tenth of the income. Two figures, correctly placed, are worth more than a paragraph of adjectives. The classic drawing errors, so you can avoid all three: a curve above the diagonal, which is impossible; a curve that fails to start at the origin or end at the top corner, which breaks the definition of accumulation; and axes labelled income and people without the word cumulative, which markers read as not understanding the instrument. Four fixed sentences, two placed numbers, three errors avoided — that is full marks on sight.

**Bianca:** Next: for the growth-versus-development distinction, is there a way to make sure we do more than just define the two words? Apparently the marks sit in the application.

**Tutor:** They do, and the reliable move is to run one country through both lenses until the lenses visibly disagree — because the disagreement is the understanding. Take a mineral-rich economy in a commodity boom: pit output soars, real GDP jumps — the growth lens flashes green. Now the development lens: are lives longer, classrooms fuller, choices wider? If the mineral revenue leaves through foreign shareholders, or funds consumption in one city while clinics run dry elsewhere, the development lens stays red — and there is your growth-without-development case, argued rather than asserted. Then reverse it with the HDI's logic: a middle-income country that spends deliberately on health and schooling can outrank a richer one on the index while trailing it on GDP — development running ahead of growth. Close by anchoring at home, both ways: South Africa's growth has crawled behind population growth for a decade, so the growth lens itself flashes red; and yet grant coverage, electrification and school access expanded through that same decade, so parts of the development picture moved even while income per person stood still. One country, two lenses, lenses disagreeing in both directions — write that and the marker cannot mistake you for someone who memorised two definitions. The trap to avoid: treating the HDI as a synonym for development. It is an index of three dimensions — health, education, income — a measurement instrument, not the concept itself; freedoms, safety and dignity belong to development even though no index line captures them.

**Bianca:** Third: the strategies question — RDP, GEAR, AsgiSA, NDP — feels like pure memory. Is there an argument hiding in the list, or do we just recite the initials?

**Tutor:** There is an argument, and reciting without it wastes the question. The initials are four answers to one standing dilemma: when needs are urgent and money is short, do you spend first or stabilise first? Read the sequence through that dilemma and it becomes a story. The RDP answered spend first — houses, water, clinics, basic needs led the budget. Within two years the finances strained, and GEAR answered stabilise first — discipline the deficit, court investment, let growth fund the social agenda later; stability arrived, but growth and jobs undershot the promise. AsgiSA then narrowed the question: if growth will not come broadly, attack the specific binding constraints — skills, infrastructure, logistics. And the NDP widened it again into a two-decade map where infrastructure, education and employment carry both goals at once. Now the evaluation, which is where the top marks sit: the recurring South African finding is strong diagnosis, weak delivery — each plan read the problem well and stumbled in execution, so the binding constraint has been the state's capacity to implement, not the quality of its documents. Deliver that as your closing line with one piece of evidence — an infrastructure target missed, a skills pipeline that never scaled — and the memory list becomes an argued answer. The sequence is the skeleton; the dilemma and the verdict are the flesh; markers pay for flesh.

**Bianca:** Last one: with four huge topics in one revision session, how should we split our remaining study time — equally, or somewhere strategic?

**Tutor:** Strategically, and the strategy comes from how these four topics actually behave in questions. Notice what today kept revealing: they are not four separate topics — they are one argument wearing four coats. Inequality is the reason redistribution exists; redistribution needs growth; growth pulls in globalisation's gains and losses; globalisation's losses and the environment's failures are both stories of costs landing on people outside the deal; and sustainability is the growth question asked on behalf of the future. So spend your first hour not inside any topic but on the connections — write the four names on one page and draw every arrow you can defend, each arrow labelled with its mechanism. That single page is the highest-yield hour available to you, because integrated questions are exactly where most answers collapse into topic-by-topic recitation. Then split the remaining time by your weakest mechanism, not your least-favourite topic: if you cannot narrate the Lorenz curve, that is a mechanism gap; if you can list globalisation's causes but not close the circuit from container to value chain, that is another. Drill mechanisms, because evidence attaches to mechanisms in memory — Day-to-day facts like the Gini near nought comma six five, the plans in order, the 2008 contagion, the maize imports, will hold once they have a mechanism to hang on. Last discipline: for each topic, rehearse one evaluation sentence aloud until it is reflex — grow and share; strong diagnosis, weak delivery; grows the total, moves the shares; bill the smoke, guard the river, save the seed. Walk in with four mechanisms, four evidence sets and four verdict sentences, and there is no long question in this half of the syllabus you cannot build on the spot.
