# Part 1 — Expert

This session is the first of two revision sweeps for the year's examination — the systems, factors, national numbers and market machinery of terms one and two, plus the two great policies, rebuilt as four exam-day structures: economic systems and the four factors; measuring and mapping the economy; elasticity, costs and revenue; and connected markets under the two policy steering wheels. Nothing new arrives today; everything arrives sharpened.

## Subtopic: Economic Systems and the Four Factors

Every economy answers three questions — WHAT to produce, HOW, and FOR WHOM — and the systems differ by WHO answers. In the FREE MARKET economy, prices answer: millions of buying decisions signal what is wanted, profit steers resources toward it, and the state stands back. Strengths: efficiency, innovation, choice. Weaknesses: inequality, public goods undersupplied, externalities ignored. In the CENTRALLY PLANNED economy, the state answers: planners set outputs and prices. Strengths on paper: equity and full employment. Weaknesses in practice: no price signals, so shortages beside surpluses, weak incentives, slow innovation. Every real economy is MIXED — markets allocating most goods with the state taxing, regulating and providing — and South Africa's mixture is also DUALISTIC: a first-world financial and industrial economy operating beside a large informal and subsistence economy, two economies sharing one country.

The factors of production power whichever system runs, and each factor earns its own reward. NATURAL RESOURCES — everything provided by nature — earn RENT. LABOUR — human effort, physical and mental — earns WAGES, and its quality is human capital, raised by education, training and health. CAPITAL — man-made means of production: machines, buildings, tools — earns INTEREST; capital is not money itself but what money buys for production. ENTREPRENEURSHIP — the factor that combines the other three, takes the risk and makes the decisions — earns PROFIT, the RESIDUAL: whatever remains after rent, wages and interest are paid, which can be negative. That residual position is why profit is the reward for risk.

The South African angle the paper loves: local economic planning, access to resources and empowerment procurement decide who actually gets to use the factors — a licence, a stand, a tender or a loan is the difference between owning a factor and earning from it.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: systems compared through the three questions, the dualistic label justified, and each factor married to its reward with profit as the residual.

## Subtopic: Measuring and Mapping the Economy — Aggregates, Sectors and Infrastructure

One economy, three measurements that must agree. The PRODUCTION method sums value added by every industry — GVA — then adds taxes on products and subtracts subsidies to reach GDP at market prices. The INCOME method sums what the factors earned: wages plus rent plus interest plus profit. The EXPENDITURE method sums spending on final goods: C plus I plus G plus X minus M. The components carry their own marks: C is household consumption, the economy's largest slice; G is government consumption — salaries of nurses and teachers, not grants; I is GROSS FIXED CAPITAL FORMATION — spending that builds tomorrow's capacity: factories, roads, machines; X minus M corrects for the border, because imports satisfy demand without domestic production. GNI then follows factor income home: GDP plus what residents earn abroad minus what foreigners earn here.

Now map the economy that generates those numbers. Three SECTORS: PRIMARY takes from nature — agriculture and mining; SECONDARY transforms — manufacturing, construction, electricity; TERTIARY serves — trade, transport, finance, government. South Africa's structural story: the primary share of GDP has fallen, manufacturing has stagnated — premature deindustrialisation — and the tertiary sector now produces roughly two-thirds of GDP. The sectors link in chains: iron ore to steel to construction; maize to milling to retail — so a failure upstream cascades downstream.

Underneath everything lies INFRASTRUCTURE, the economy's skeleton: electricity, transport, water, communications. When the skeleton weakens, every sector limps — load-shedding is the national case study, cutting production, closing small firms and deterring investment. Infrastructure also decides ACCESS: the household with electricity, water, transport and connectivity can produce, learn and trade; the household without them is fenced out of the economy before it starts.

The questions for this section are with you now: three methods reconciled, each expenditure component classified correctly, the sectoral shift told with its direction, and infrastructure argued as both engine and gate.

## Subtopic: Markets at Work — Elasticity, Costs and Revenue

PRICE ELASTICITY OF DEMAND measures how strongly quantity demanded answers a price change: the percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in price. Read the number in five categories: ELASTIC, greater than one — quantity answers hard; INELASTIC, less than one — quantity shrugs; UNITARY at exactly one; and the textbook endpoints, perfectly elastic and perfectly inelastic. In words for the curves: an elastic demand curve lies flatter — a small price rise sweeps away much quantity; an inelastic curve stands steeper — price moves, quantity barely stirs. The DETERMINANTS decide which you face: availability of substitutes above all, necessity against luxury, the share of income the item takes, and time — demand grows more elastic as buyers find alternatives.

The payoff is TOTAL REVENUE, price times quantity. Facing INELASTIC demand, raise the price: the quantity lost is small, revenue rises — bread, petrol, electricity. Facing ELASTIC demand, a price rise bleeds revenue away, while a price CUT can raise it — fizzy drinks with five rivals on the shelf. One sentence carries the logic: elasticity tells you which way revenue moves when price moves.

Costs next, for the firm's side. FIXED costs do not change with output — rent is owed at zero plates cooked; VARIABLE costs rise with every unit. From totals come AVERAGE cost per unit and MARGINAL cost — the cost of the NEXT unit, the number decisions actually use. A small firm in a competitive market is a PRICE TAKER: it sells every unit at the market price, so marginal revenue equals price. The profit rule: expand while marginal revenue exceeds marginal cost, stop where MARGINAL REVENUE EQUALS MARGINAL COST — before that point each unit adds profit, beyond it each unit eats profit. Verify in any table by comparing the MR and MC columns row by row, and state profit as total revenue minus total cost at that output.

Stop for this section's questions now — the elasticity number calculated and classified, determinants applied, the revenue payoff run both ways, and the MR-equals-MC rule found and verified in a table.

## Subtopic: Connected Markets and the Two Policies

Markets talk to each other through RELATIVE prices — buyers compare. SUBSTITUTES compete: beef against chicken; when beef's price rises, chicken's DEMAND curve shifts right. COMPLEMENTS partner: cars and petrol; when petrol's price rises, the demand for cars — especially thirsty ones — shifts left. State the mechanism, not just the label: the price change in one market shifts the demand CURVE in the other.

PRODUCT markets and FACTOR markets form a loop: every job hides inside something somebody buys, because the demand for labour is derived from the demand for products — when clothing imports displace local clothing, the factory's workers feel the product market's verdict in the factor market. And markets differ by structure — count the sellers: perfect competition with many, monopolistic competition with many differentiated, oligopoly with a few large, monopoly with one — from the taxi rank's many operators to the electricity grid's single supplier.

Above all the markets stand two macro steering wheels. MONETARY policy belongs to the South African Reserve Bank, whose primary mandate is price stability — inflation targeted between three and six percent. Its instrument is the REPO RATE, the price at which banks borrow from the central bank. Raise it and the transmission runs: banks raise prime, loans and bonds cost more, households and firms borrow and spend less, demand cools, inflation eases — at the cost of slower growth. Cut it and the chain runs in reverse. FISCAL policy belongs to the government through the annual budget: GOVERNMENT SPENDING and TAXATION. Spending injects demand directly; taxation withdraws it and redistributes; the balance between them is a deficit financed by borrowing, which accumulates as public debt with its own interest bill crowding the budget.

Compare them as the exam does: different drivers — independent central bank against elected government; different instruments — interest rates against spending and taxes; different speeds — the repo rate moves in an afternoon, budgets move yearly; different targets — inflation against growth, jobs and redistribution. A full answer names the mix: both hands on the same economy, ideally pulling the same way.

The final questions of this part are with you now: substitutes and complements argued through curve shifts, the product-factor loop closed, the four structures counted, and the two policies compared driver by driver.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same machinery through three things from an ordinary week: a kitchen, a plate of food, and a till slip.

## Subtopic: Three Kitchens and Four Pay Packets

Picture three kitchens. Kitchen one: everyone cooks whatever sells — the cook watches the till, and the till decides the menu. That is the free market: fast, inventive, ruthless — and the family that cannot pay does not eat. Kitchen two: head office faxes the menu — someone far away decides how many loaves this town bakes. That is the planned economy: fair on paper, but the memo does not know what the till knows, so one shelf overflows while another stands empty. Kitchen three: the till decides most things, but the state inspects the kitchen, taxes the takings, and cooks for those who cannot pay. That is the mixed economy — every real country, ours included. And South Africa runs two kitchens at once: a gleaming restaurant economy in Sandton and a paraffin-stove economy in the informal settlement — one country, dual economy. Name that in the exam: mixed AND dualistic.

Now, who gets paid in any kitchen? Four pay packets for four contributions. The LAND under the kitchen — nature's contribution — earns RENT. The HANDS that cook — labour — earn WAGES, and better-trained hands earn more, which is the whole case for education. The STOVE and the building — capital, things made to make other things — earn INTEREST. And the OWNER who rented the land, hired the hands, bought the stove and risked the savings? The owner earns PROFIT — whatever is LEFT OVER after the other three packets are paid. Some months that leftover is nothing; some months it is negative. Profit is the only pay packet that can be empty, and that is exactly why it exists: it is the price of carrying the risk.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on this section are with you right now: three kitchens told apart by who reads the till, and four pay packets matched to four contributions, with profit as the leftovers.

## Subtopic: Rubber Bands, Planks and the Next Plate

Some prices you can dodge, and some you cannot. Cooldrink goes up three rand — you switch brands, buy the rival, drink water: your demand stretched like a RUBBER BAND. Bread goes up three rand — you grumble and buy the bread: your demand is a PLANK, it barely bends. That stretch has a number: percentage change in quantity over percentage change in price. Rubber bands score above one — elastic; planks score below one — inelastic. What makes a plank? No substitutes, a necessity, a small slice of your budget. What makes a rubber band? Five rivals on the shelf and time to find them.

Here is why shopkeepers should care: the stretch decides what a price change does to the money in the till. Raise the price of a PLANK and the till fills — customers barely leave. Raise the price of a RUBBER BAND and the till empties — customers bounce away faster than the higher price compensates; with rubber bands, it is the price CUT that can fill the till. One test — how much do they bounce? — answers every revenue question in the paper.

Now step behind the counter of a kota stand and decide how many plates to cook. Two kinds of costs sit in the books: the stand's rent, owed even on a day with zero plates — fixed; and the bread, polony and chips that walk out with every plate — variable. The market pays thirty rand a plate, every plate — the stand is too small to move the price. So interrogate the NEXT plate, and only the next plate: what does it cost to make? While the next plate costs less than thirty rand, cook it — it adds profit. The moment the next plate costs MORE than thirty rand — the small pan overcrowded, the helper overtime — stop. The stopping rule is the whole theory: produce until the next one earns exactly what it costs. Marginal revenue equals marginal cost, spoken in plates.

Take this section's questions now — rubber bands and planks diagnosed, the till predicted before the price moves, and the next plate interrogated to the stopping point.

## Subtopic: The Till Slip and the Two Drivers

How do you measure a whole country? The same way you read a till slip. Add up everything the country produced and sold — final items only, or the flour inside the loaf gets counted twice. You can add the slip three ways and must reach one total: add what every business ADDED at its step; add what every factor EARNED — all the wages, rents, interest and profits; or add what everyone SPENT — households' groceries, firms' new machines, government's nurses and teachers, and foreigners' purchases of our exports, minus the imports that came from other countries' tills. Three adding machines, one economy, one answer.

And the slip has three departments. Out of the ground: farms and mines — the primary sector. Through the factory: bakers, builders, manufacturers — secondary. Over the counter: shops, taxis, banks, teachers — tertiary. Our country's slip has changed: the counter section has grown to about two-thirds of the total while the factory section thinned — and everything on the slip depends on the wiring and plumbing behind the shop: electricity, roads, water, network signal. Cut the power and watch the whole slip shrink — the country has run that experiment for you.

Finally, the two drivers with hands on the economy's speed. Driver one sits at the Reserve Bank with one pedal: the REPO RATE. Push it up and every loan in the country — bonds, car payments, shop credit — gets heavier within weeks; spending cools, prices calm. Ease it down and borrowing cheapens, tills ring again. Driver one's job description is one line: keep inflation between three and six percent. Driver two sits in the Treasury with the budget: choose what to SPEND on clinics, grants, roads — and what to TAX from payslips, purchases, profits. Spend more than you tax and you borrow the gap, and the debt's interest bill quietly eats future budgets. The repo pedal moves in an afternoon; the budget turns once a year like a ship. Fast pedal, slow ship — both steering the same economy, and the exam's favourite question is simply: which driver, which tool, which target?

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: the till slip added three ways, the three departments with their shift, and the two drivers matched to pedal, ship, speed and target. Take the structures in — the paper opens to them like a door you already have the key for.
