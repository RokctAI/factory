**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! First: the three GDP methods keep tangling people up — production, income, expenditure. Why are there three at all, and which mistakes actually lose the marks?

**Tutor:** There are three because one flow of economic activity can be photographed from three angles, and the deepest revision insight is that they are the same photograph. When a bakery sells a fifteen-rand loaf, that fifteen rand is simultaneously value produced, income earned and money spent — production, income and expenditure are one event wearing three hats, which is exactly why the three totals must reconcile. Now the mark-losing mistakes, in descending order of frequency. Mistake one: putting social grants inside G. Grants are transfers — money moving between pockets with nothing produced — while G counts government consumption: the salaries of nurses, teachers and police. Mistake two: treating I as any spending by firms. I is gross fixed capital formation — machines, plants, roads, capacity for tomorrow — not firms' wage bills or stock purchases. Mistake three: forgetting that M is subtracted, and why: imports satisfy local demand without local production, so leaving them in overstates what we made. Mistake four: counting the flour and the loaf — intermediate goods double-count, which is the whole reason the production method works in value ADDED. And the refinement that signals a top answer: GNI follows the income home — GDP plus what residents earn abroad, minus what foreigners earn here. Photograph, three angles, four traps. Check your last practice answer against those four and you will probably find one of them.

**Bianca:** Next: elasticity questions with calculations — people get the number right and then the interpretation wrong, or the other way around. Is there a fixed drill?

**Tutor:** There is, and it has exactly four steps: compute, band, explain, predict. Step one, compute: percentage change in quantity over percentage change in price, and keep the habit of showing both percentage calculations, because method marks survive arithmetic slips. Step two, band the number: above one elastic, below one inelastic, exactly one unitary — and say the band aloud in words, since a naked number earns less than a classified one. Step three, explain WHY the good sits in that band using the determinants: the chips are elastic because rivals crowd the same shelf; the taxi fare is inelastic because school is compulsory and there is no substitute ride; and remember time — demand stretches as buyers get the chance to hunt alternatives. Step four, predict the till: inelastic plus a price rise means revenue climbs, because the riders still ride; elastic plus a price rise means revenue falls, because the snackers scatter — and the twist worth marks, with elastic demand a price CUT can raise revenue, because volume returns faster than the price fell. The commonest interpretation error is collapsing steps two and four — announcing that elastic means revenue falls, full stop. It does not: elastic means quantity reacts strongly, and what happens to revenue depends on which direction price moved. Keep the four steps separate and that error becomes impossible. Compute, band, explain, predict — write those four words in the margin before you touch any elasticity question.

**Bianca:** Third: in the cost tables, why do we only ever interrogate the NEXT unit? Surely the stall owner cares about the whole morning's costs, not one vetkoek at a time.

**Tutor:** She cares about the whole morning when she reports profit — but she cannot DECIDE with morning totals, and seeing why is seeing the whole theory. A decision is always about the margin: the question in front of her is never 'was today good?' but 'should I fry one more?' — and yesterday's stand fee has no vote in that question. The stand fee is owed whether she fries zero or two hundred; it is a sunk, fixed cost, so letting it influence the next-unit decision is the classic error economists warn against. The only numbers with a vote are the next vetkoek's cost — flour, oil, mince, maybe overtime — and the next vetkoek's revenue, which for a price taker is simply the market's fifteen rand. While the next one costs less than fifteen, frying it adds to profit no matter how the morning has gone; the moment it costs more, frying it subtracts, again regardless of the totals. Follow that logic unit by unit and it delivers you automatically to the stopping point: produce until marginal revenue equals marginal cost. Then — and only then — the totals return for the reporting job: profit equals total revenue minus total cost at that chosen output, and if the stand fee is so high that even the best output loses money, that is a different decision — whether to keep the stall at all. In a table question, run your finger down the MR and MC columns, mark the last row where MR still covers MC, and state profit from the totals at that row. Decide at the margin, report with the totals — one sentence, full method.

**Bianca:** Last one: for the monetary-versus-fiscal comparison, what is the cleanest structure that still reaches the top marks?

**Tutor:** A four-row comparison with a mechanism inside each row, closed by one sentence on the mix. Row one, the drivers: monetary policy sits with the Reserve Bank, deliberately independent so that painful decisions survive politics; fiscal policy sits with the elected government, deliberately accountable because taxing and spending are choices about whose money goes where. Row two, the levers: one interest rate against a thousand budget lines — the repo rate against spending and taxation. And here you must show a transmission chain, because this is where mechanism marks live: repo up, banks raise prime, credit and bond repayments heavier, households and firms spend less, demand cools, inflation eases — six links, recite them till they are furniture. Row three, the speeds: the repo can move the day the committee meets and bites within weeks; the budget is annual, and its projects take years — quick pedal, slow ship. Row four, the targets: price stability, three to six percent, against growth, jobs and redistribution. Then the closing sentence that lifts the answer: the two are strongest pulling together and weakest in contradiction — a treasury spending heavily while the central bank fights inflation forces rates higher than either wanted, so the mix, not either policy alone, steers the economy. Four rows, one chain, one mix sentence. And a warning for the comparison questions generally: never write two separate essays about two policies — the marks sit in the rows where they touch.
