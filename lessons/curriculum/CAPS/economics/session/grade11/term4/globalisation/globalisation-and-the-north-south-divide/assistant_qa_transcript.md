**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! First one: causes of globalisation questions keep appearing for eight marks, and our lists keep earning four. What separates a four-mark list from an eight-mark answer?

**Tutor:** Mechanisms separate them — the marker is paying for the word "because", not for nouns. A four-mark answer writes: containers, the internet, the WTO, multinationals. An eight-mark answer takes fewer causes and completes each circuit. Containerisation: a standard steel box moves from ship to truck to train without unpacking, which collapsed the cost of moving goods, which made it profitable to manufacture far from customers — that is a mechanism, three links long. Communication technology: undersea cables and the internet moved information instantly and freely, which let firms coordinate production across continents in real time, which made the global value chain possible — design in one country, chips in another, assembly in a third. Then switch families and signal that you are switching: technology made globalisation possible, policy made it permitted. Tariffs negotiated down over decades, producing the WTO in 1995; exchange controls relaxed so finance could cross borders; China, the former Soviet bloc and post-1994 South Africa rejoining the world market with billions of workers and consumers. Two families, two or three causes each, every cause argued to its consequence. That is the eight-mark shape — and it is also faster to write than a long list, because you stop hunting for a seventh noun.

**Bianca:** Next: the class wants a ruling. When an essay asks us to evaluate globalisation's consequences, are we allowed to conclude that it is simply bad? Some feel the Newcastle story proves it.

**Tutor:** You are allowed any conclusion you can defend — but "simply bad" is nearly impossible to defend, and the Newcastle story alone will not carry it, because the same evidence set contains Rosslyn and East London. Here is the defensible structure. Concede the gains with numbers: world-scale car plants supporting over a hundred thousand South African jobs; consumer prices far below what a closed economy could manage — cheap imports acting as an unannounced raise on every wage; and, globally, hundreds of millions lifted from extreme poverty through export-led growth. Then present the losses with equal honesty: tens of thousands of clothing jobs destroyed when tariffs fell; contagion, as in 2008, when a foreign housing collapse cost South African jobs within a year; and gains distributed unevenly, with the mobile and skilled collecting most. Now notice what the evidence actually shows: globalisation raised the total while redistributing the shares. That finding supports a conditional verdict, not a simple one — something like: globalisation benefits South Africa on balance only when policy captures the gains and cushions the losses through skills, safety nets and, where justified, temporary protection. Examiners score that far above either cheerleading or condemnation, because it is the only conclusion the full evidence permits. The skill being tested is not opinion; it is holding both columns of the scorecard at once.

**Bianca:** Third: is the North/South divide still real if China is now an economic giant and BRICS exists? One of us argued the whole concept is outdated.

**Tutor:** That argument deserves a proper answer, because it is half right, and the half-right answers are where the best marks live. What has genuinely changed: China's rise is the largest poverty reduction in human history, India and Brazil have shifted the world's economic centre of gravity southward, and Southern groupings like BRICS — South Africa included — now demand a voice the South never had when the divide was named. A static picture of a rich North and a uniformly poor South is indeed outdated. What has not changed: the structural mechanisms. Most of Africa still sells raw and buys made, which keeps it on the terms-of-trade treadmill; Southern debt is still owed in foreign currency, so a rate rise in Washington still squeezes clinics in Lusaka; and voting power in the IMF and World Bank still tilts heavily North. Extreme poverty remains concentrated in the South, and the patents, research and finance remain concentrated in the North. So the examinable formulation is: the divide is real but no longer simple — it has narrowed dramatically at some points, chiefly in Asia, while persisting or deepening at others, chiefly in Africa, and the mechanisms that created it still operate wherever countries remain raw-material exporters. Concept retained, nuance added, evidence on both sides. That is how you handle any "is this concept outdated" question in economics.

**Bianca:** Last one, close to home: should South Africa protect industries like clothing and poultry with tariffs, or does that just make everything expensive? We know both sides get angry about this one.

**Tutor:** Then answer it the way the angry sides cannot: costs, benefits, conditions. The case for protection: an industry flattened by the world's cheapest producers does not die alone — towns, skills and supplier networks die with it, as Newcastle showed, and some capabilities, once lost, are very costly to rebuild. A tariff or local-content rule buys time and preserves jobs that the economy, at over thirty percent unemployment, desperately cannot spare. The case against: a tariff is paid by every consumer — including the poor — through higher prices, so protecting one industry's workers quietly taxes everyone else's groceries; and protection that becomes permanent removes the pressure to improve, leaving an industry that needs the tariff forever. Now the conditions that decide it, which is what the examiner is really asking for: protection defends the economy's interests when it is temporary, tied to a genuine plan to restore competitiveness, and aimed at industries with a real prospect of standing again — and it fails when it is open-ended, plan-free, or shields firms that will never compete. Current South African policy on clothing and poultry attempts exactly that conditional model: tariffs plus local-procurement commitments plus industry master plans. Conclude that the tool is legitimate and dangerous at once, and that the design, not the slogan, decides which. Costs, benefits, conditions — that frame turns every angry policy debate in this topic into marks.
