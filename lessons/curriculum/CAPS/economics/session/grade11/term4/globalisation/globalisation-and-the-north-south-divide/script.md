# Part 1 — Expert

This session tackles globalisation: what it means, the forces that caused it, the consequences it delivers — good and bad — and the deep divide it has not healed: the gap between the global North and the global South. South Africa sits inside every part of this story, as an open economy on the southern side of the divide, so the examples come home fast and so do the exam questions.

## Subtopic: What Globalisation Is — and the Forces That Caused It

Globalisation is the process by which the world's economies become increasingly interconnected and interdependent — goods, services, money, people, information and ideas flowing across borders in ever greater volumes, so that national economies function more and more as parts of a single world economy. The working test: decisions taken on one continent change prices, jobs and incomes on another, faster and more powerfully than a generation ago.

The causes come in two families: technology made globalisation possible, and policy made it permitted.

Technology first. Transport: the humble shipping container, standardised in the mid-twentieth century, collapsed the cost of moving goods — one steel box, lifted from ship to truck to train without unpacking, made distance cheap. Communication: undersea fibre cables, satellites and the internet made information free to move; a design drawn in one country is manufactured on another continent the same day. Computing lets a firm coordinate factories, warehouses and finances across a dozen time zones in real time. Result: for the first time in history, a company can slice production into stages and place each stage wherever it is done cheapest — the global value chain, globalisation's signature invention.

Policy second. From the late 1940s, countries negotiated tariffs downward, a process that produced the World Trade Organisation in 1995, policing rules for most of world trade. Exchange controls were relaxed, letting finance cross borders at the speed of a keystroke. State-dominated economies — China from 1978, the former Soviet bloc after 1989, and South Africa itself rejoining world markets after 1994 — entered the trading system, adding billions of workers and consumers. Multinational corporations wove it all together, producing and selling in dozens of countries at once.

Hold the two-family structure for essays: containers, cables and computers made it possible; trade deals, financial liberalisation and reopening economies made it allowed.

The questions for this section are with you now: globalisation defined with its interdependence test, and the causes sorted into their technology and policy families.

## Subtopic: The Consequences — a Two-Sided Scorecard

Globalisation's consequences are genuinely two-sided, and the exam rewards a scorecard, not a verdict shouted from one side.

The gains column. Trade raises efficiency: countries specialise where they produce best, and consumers everywhere harvest lower prices and wider choice — the clothing, electronics and food on South African shelves would cost far more in a closed economy. Investment flows: foreign direct investment brings factories, capital and skills to countries short of savings; South Africa's motor industry exists at world scale because global carmakers built plants here to serve world markets, supporting over one hundred thousand jobs. Technology spreads faster: vaccines, solar panels and mobile banking reached the South quicker because knowledge now travels. And on the grandest scale, hundreds of millions of people — most visibly in Asia — worked their way out of extreme poverty through export-led growth. Globalisation built that road.

The losses column. Competition destroys as well as creates: when cheap imported clothing flooded in after tariffs fell, South African textile towns — Newcastle, parts of KwaZulu-Natal and the Western Cape — lost tens of thousands of jobs; efficient for the world, devastating for those streets. Volatility travels too: a financial crisis in one country now spreads through the whole system, as 2008 proved when a housing collapse in the United States threw South Africans out of work within a year — contagion is globalisation's fever. Power tilts toward the mobile: multinationals can shift profits to low-tax countries and play governments against each other, weakening the tax base, while workers — far less mobile — lose bargaining power, a dynamic called the race to the bottom. And the gains have been shared unevenly, between countries and within them: globalisation has coincided with widening internal inequality in most open economies, ours included.

The balanced verdict examiners want: globalisation raises the world's total income while redistributing opportunity roughly toward the efficient and the mobile — so the policy question is never simply for or against, but how to capture the gains while protecting and re-skilling those the losses land on.

Work this section's questions now: two gains and two losses argued with South African evidence, contagion explained through 2008, and the balanced verdict stated in one sentence.

## Subtopic: The North/South Divide

Now the deep structure underneath the scorecard. The North/South divide is the name for the persistent gap between the developed economies — the global North: North America, Western Europe, Japan, Australia, plus other high-income economies — and the developing economies of the global South: most of Africa, Latin America and much of Asia. The labels are economic shorthand, not strict geography — Australia sits in the South's oceans and the North's economy.

The divide shows in every statistic that matters: the North holds a minority of the world's people but a large majority of its income, wealth, research and patents; the South holds most of humanity, most of the world's poverty, and the youngest populations. But describe it in mechanisms, not just numbers, because mechanisms earn the marks.

Mechanism one: the terms of trade. Many Southern economies still export primary products — ores, crops, oil — and import manufactured goods and services. Primary prices swing wildly and, over long periods, have tended to buy fewer and fewer manufactured imports per ton exported. Selling raw and buying made is a treadmill: you run harder each year to afford the same tractor.

Mechanism two: debt and finance. Southern countries borrow in foreign currency; when world interest rates rise or their currencies fall, repayments balloon, and spending on schools and clinics is squeezed to service debt — a cycle Africa has lived through repeatedly, and which several countries are living through now.

Mechanism three: voice. The rules of the world economy are written in institutions — the International Monetary Fund, the World Bank, the WTO — where voting power and agendas have historically tilted North. The South trades in a game whose rules it had little hand in writing; tariffs on processed goods, for instance, have long been set higher than on raw materials, quietly discouraging the South from industrialising its own resources.

Yet the divide is not fixed. China, India, Brazil and others have shifted the world's economic centre of gravity southward; the BRICS grouping — with South Africa a member — presses for a louder Southern voice; and the African Continental Free Trade Area aims to grow the South's own internal market. The divide is real, deep and slowly — unevenly — narrowing at some points while widening at others.

Attend to this section's questions before the final piece: the divide defined as economics rather than geography, the three mechanisms explained, and one force changing the divide named.

## Subtopic: South Africa Inside the Global Economy

Finish where the exam finishes: South Africa's own position, argued with evidence.

Measure our openness first. Exports plus imports amount to well over half of South Africa's GDP — we are among the more open large economies, and it shows in daily life: the petrol price tracks world oil and the rand; the maize price tracks world grain markets; the fortunes of mining towns track Chinese construction. When China sneezes, the platinum belt catches cold — that is interdependence, lived.

Our global links run through trade agreements and blocs worth naming. The African Continental Free Trade Area opens the continent's market to South African manufactures — the great opportunity, since Africa buys our made goods, not just our ores. Preferential access to American and European markets supports our farm and car exports. BRICS membership links us to the big Southern economies and their development bank. And membership of the WTO sets the rules underneath it all.

Now the two-sided experience, honestly. Globalisation built our world-scale motor industry, feeds our fruit and wine into Northern supermarkets and our platinum into the world's vehicles, and finances our chronic shortfall of savings with foreign capital. The same openness let cheap imports gut our clothing and footwear industries, exposes the rand to violent swings whenever global sentiment turns, and leaves our exports hostage to commodity prices we do not set. South Africa is the North/South divide in one country: Northern-style finance in Sandton, Southern-style raw-export dependence in the trade accounts.

For essays, keep the policy debate two-handed. One hand: deeper integration — chase export markets, court investment, use AfCFTA to industrialise. Other hand: strategic protection — tariffs and local-content rules to shelter jobs while industries rebuild, as current policy attempts with clothing and poultry. The mature conclusion mirrors globalisation's own scorecard: openness grows the pie but redistributes the slices, so integration must come paired with the skills, infrastructure and safety nets that let ordinary South Africans survive the losses and claim the gains.

The final questions of this part are with you now: openness evidenced with a number, one agreement matched to its purpose, and the two-handed policy debate argued to a conclusion.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the whole topic through two objects you can hold: the phone in your pocket, and a t-shirt on a Newcastle sewing table.

## Subtopic: The World in Your Pocket

Take out your phone and read it like a map. The design might come from America or Korea. The chip: Taiwan. The screen: Korea or Japan. The cobalt in the battery: dug in the DRC, our neighbourhood. The assembly: China or Vietnam. The software updates: pulled down through an undersea cable that lands near Mtunzini on the KwaZulu-Natal coast. The airtime you buy from a spaza: sold by a company listed in Johannesburg and operating across Africa. One object, six countries minimum — that is globalisation, and you carry it daily.

How did the world get like this? Two doors had to open.

Door one: it had to become cheap to move things and free to move information. The shipping container did the first — a standard steel box that stacks from ship to truck to train without unpacking, making it cost cents to move a shirt across an ocean. Undersea cables and the internet did the second — a design, an order, a payment crossing the planet in a second. Once moving stuff is cheap and moving information is instant, a company can slice production like a loaf: design here, chips there, assembly somewhere else, each slice wherever it is done best and cheapest.

Door two: countries had to allow it. For decades, governments taxed imports heavily at the border and blocked money from crossing. Then, deal by deal, tariffs came down; a world referee — the World Trade Organisation — was set up for the rules; money was freed to move; and giant economies that had locked themselves away, China above all, walked back into the world market. South Africa itself rejoined the world's shops and investors after 1994.

Technology opened what was possible; policy opened what was permitted. Both doors, both families — and any exam question about causes is asking for both.

Your questions for this section are with you now — sort each cause behind the right door: could-move, or allowed-to-move.

## Subtopic: One Shop Now — Who Won and Who Lost on Our Streets

When the whole world becomes one shop, two South African stories happen at once. Both are true. Learn both.

Story one, the gain: East London and Rosslyn. Global carmakers built plants in South Africa not for our small market but for the world's — bakkies and sedans roll out of our factories into Europe and beyond, and over a hundred thousand families eat from that industry. Add the shopper's gain: because the world is one shop, the clothes, phones and food on our shelves cost far less than they would if South Africa made everything alone. Cheap imports are a raise nobody announces: the same wage buys more.

Story two, the loss: Newcastle. When clothing tariffs fell, imported t-shirts arrived at prices our factories could not touch. The sewing machines went quiet, tens of thousands of jobs went with them, and towns built on textiles have never fully recovered. Tell it honestly: the world got its shirts cheaper, and specific streets in KwaZulu-Natal paid for it.

And one more consequence with no factory in it: the fever that travels. In 2008, banks in America collapsed over housing loans that had nothing to do with us — and within a year, South African factories were on short time and hundreds of thousands of jobs were gone, because our customers' economies had stopped buying. Connected means exposed: one shop, one flu.

So refuse the playground question "is globalisation good or bad?" and answer like an economist: it grows the total and moves the shares. The world as a whole gets richer; the gains land on the efficient, the skilled and the mobile; the losses land on whoever competes head-on with the world's cheapest — and policy's job is to spread the first and cushion the second, with skills, safety nets and, sometimes, temporary protection while an industry gets back on its feet.

Take this section's questions now — each gives you a winner or a loser from these stories, and asks you to explain the mechanism that picked them.

## Subtopic: Top of the Map, Bottom of the Map

Colour the world map by income and a pattern jumps out: the rich lands cluster in the top half — North America, Europe, Japan — and most of the poor lands sit below — Africa, Latin America, much of Asia. Economists call it the North/South divide. It is shorthand about money, not strict geography — Australia is southern on the map and Northern in the wallet.

Why does the gap persist? Watch one cup of coffee. The beans grow in Ethiopia or Uganda — the South. They are sold raw, for cents. The roasting, branding, packaging and café selling happen in the North — where the cup fetches fifty rand. The South did the growing and got the cents; the North did the finishing and got the rands. Selling raw and buying made is the divide's engine room: the finishing is where the money lives, and the finishing happens up north. Worse, raw prices bounce around and, over the decades, a ton of beans has tended to buy fewer and fewer machines back.

Add two more weights. Debt: Southern countries borrow in dollars, and when world interest rates climb or their currency slips, repayments swell until schools and clinics are squeezed to pay bankers. Voice: the rulebooks of world money and trade are written in institutions where the North historically held the votes — and the rules show it, like import taxes that stay low on raw beans but climb on roasted coffee, quietly telling the South: keep sending it raw.

But the map is moving. China and India have hauled hundreds of millions out of poverty and pulled the world's economic centre southward. BRICS — with South Africa in it — demands a louder Southern voice. And the African free trade agreement is the South's most direct answer: let Africa roast, sew, assemble and sell to Africa, so the finishing — and the rands — stay on this side of the map.

South Africa lives on both sides at once: Sandton banks like the North, while our exports still lean on ores like the South. Which side wins our future depends on whether we learn to finish what we dig and grow.

And here come the last questions of the lesson: the coffee cup traced from cents to rands, the three weights on the South named, and the forces moving the map argued with one example each.
