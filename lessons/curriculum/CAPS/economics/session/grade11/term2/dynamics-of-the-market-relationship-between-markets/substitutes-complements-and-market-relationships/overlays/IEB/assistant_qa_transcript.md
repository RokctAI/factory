**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! First one: shifts versus movements along the curve. Half the room still cannot say when a demand curve slides along itself and when the whole thing jumps sideways. Is there a test we can run in two seconds?

**Tutor:** There is, and it never fails. Ask one question: whose price changed? If the price that changed belongs to the good on the diagram's own axis, you travel along its existing demand curve — nothing about the curve itself is different; you have simply moved to a new point on it. If the price that changed belongs to anything else — a substitute, a complement, or if the change is in income, tastes or expectations — the whole curve relocates, because the quantity demanded is now different at every single price on the axis. Run our margarine case through the test. Butter jumps from sixty to eighty-five rand. You are drawing the margarine market, and butter's price is not on margarine's axis — so margarine's entire demand curve steps to the right. But if margarine itself went on special, you would slide down margarine's existing curve to a bigger quantity. One more habit protects you in written answers: name the axis before you answer. Say "on the margarine diagram, butter's price is an outside force" and the marker can see your reasoning even before the diagram appears. Whose price changed — the good on the axis, or something outside it? Two seconds, every time.

***

**Bianca:** Next: substitutes and complements. The definitions are easy, but in questions people freeze — is peanut butter a rival of jam or a partner of bread? How do we decide fast under pressure?

**Tutor:** Use the two jobs test, and let the sentence structure do the thinking. Ask: do these two goods do the same job, or do they do one job together? Rice and maize meal both fill the pot with starch — same job, so they are rivals, substitutes, and one can stand in for the other. Bread and polony build one sandwich together — one job, two ingredients, so they are partners, complements. Your peanut butter example is lovely because it is both, depending on the pairing: against jam, peanut butter competes for the same spot on the slice — same job, substitutes. Alongside bread, it can only be enjoyed with its partner — one job together, complements. That is worth saying in an exam answer, because it shows the relationship belongs to the pair, not to the good alone. Then attach the direction rule: rivals move together — the rival's price and my demand rise together; partners move apart — the partner's price rises and my demand falls. If you can build the sentence "when the price of X rises, buyers of Y..." and finish it honestly, the classification and the shift direction both fall out on their own.

**Bianca:** Third one: derived demand made a few of us uneasy. If the demand for electricians is borrowed from the demand for powered homes, does that mean workers have no say at all in what happens to their wages?

**Tutor:** It means something more precise, and actually more useful: the ceiling over any wage is set in the product market. Workers and their skills matter enormously on the supply side — a scarce, accredited installer can bargain hard, and that scarcity is why solar wages got bid up so quickly in our story. But no amount of bargaining conjures wages out of a product nobody buys. If households stopped installing solar tomorrow, the demand curve for installers would march leftward no matter how skilled they are, because the demand for their labour was never freestanding — it was borrowed from the demand for the systems they fit. This is why economists watch product markets to predict job markets. When you hear that clothing imports are squeezing local factories, you can forecast the next headline about textile jobs before it is printed; when logistics volumes boom, demand for drivers follows as surely as a shadow. And the loop closes on itself: those wages become spending, which feeds product demand somewhere else in the economy. So workers have a real say — through skills, scarcity and bargaining — but they exercise it inside a boundary drawn by what buyers in product markets are willing to pay for.

**Bianca:** Last one, exam strategy: if a question hands us some unfamiliar market — say, a town with three cement suppliers — how do we classify its structure without panicking?

**Tutor:** Run the two sorting questions in order, then confirm with the behaviour you would expect. Question one: how many sellers? Very many, many, a few, or one — that single count nearly finishes the job. Question two: how hard is the entry door? Free entry keeps you in the competitive structures; blocked or expensive entry pushes you toward oligopoly and monopoly. Your three cement suppliers: a few sellers, and cement plants cost a fortune, so entry is hard — oligopoly, almost certainly. Now confirm with behaviour, because that is where the extra marks live: expect interdependence — each supplier watching the others before quoting a price — sticky prices, competition through service and delivery rather than open price wars, and a standing temptation to collude, which is illegal and exactly what the Competition Commission polices. If the products had been differentiated and the sellers numerous — salons, takeaways — you would have landed on monopolistic competition; identical products with a crowd of sellers lands on perfect competition; one seller behind a blocked door is monopoly. Finish by placing the market on the perfect-versus-imperfect line with a reason: any structure where sellers hold power over price is imperfect. Count, test the door, describe the behaviour — that sequence classifies any market a question can invent.
