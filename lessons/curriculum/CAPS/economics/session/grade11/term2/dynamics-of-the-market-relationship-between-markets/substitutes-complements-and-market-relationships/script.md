# Part 1 — Expert

This session takes the demand and supply machinery you already own and points it at a bigger truth: markets talk to each other. A price change in one market moves demand in another; what happens in the shops decides what happens to jobs; and the number of sellers in a market decides how prices get made at all. Three relationships — substitutes and complements, product markets and factor markets, and the four market structures — and each one is a reliable source of exam marks.

## Subtopic: Relative Prices — the Signal Buyers Actually Read

Start with a distinction that unlocks everything else: the difference between a price and a relative price.

The money price of chicken might be seventy rand a kilogram. But no shopper judges that number in isolation. The question every buyer silently asks is: seventy rand compared to what? Compared to beef at one hundred and forty rand a kilogram, chicken is half the price — that comparison, the price of one good expressed in terms of another, is the relative price, and it is the signal that actually steers spending.

Prove it with a thought experiment. Suppose every price in the economy doubled overnight — chicken to one hundred and forty, beef to two hundred and eighty, and your income doubled too. The money prices all changed; the relative price did not — chicken still costs half of what beef costs — and a rational shopper's choice between them would not change either. Now instead suppose only beef doubles while chicken stays at seventy. Chicken's money price never moved, yet chicken just became relatively cheaper, and shoppers will swing toward it.

That is the deep point: markets coordinate behaviour through relative prices. When economists say prices are signals, they mean relative prices — a good becoming relatively cheaper draws buyers in and warns suppliers of its rival; a good becoming relatively dearer pushes buyers away and invites producers to supply more of it.

Keep one piece of housekeeping from Grade 10 sharp, because the whole lesson leans on it. A change in the price of the good itself moves you along its demand curve. A change in anything else — including the price of a related good — shifts the entire demand curve to a new position. Along versus shift: the examiner tests it every single year.

The questions for this section are with you now: relative price computed and interpreted, the doubling thought experiment reasoned through, and movement along a curve separated from a shift of the curve.

## Subtopic: Substitutes and Complements — and Their Graphs in Words

Now the first relationship between markets: related goods. Two goods are substitutes when they satisfy the same want, so one can replace the other — chicken and beef, butter and margarine, taxi rides and bus rides. Two goods are complements when they are consumed together, so they need each other — a cellphone and mobile data, a braai pack and charcoal, a car and petrol.

Take substitutes first, and picture the graph as I describe it. Draw the market for beef: price on the vertical axis, quantity on the horizontal axis, the demand curve sloping downward from top left to bottom right. Now let the price of chicken — the substitute — rise sharply, say from seventy to ninety-five rand a kilogram after a bird-flu outbreak. Shoppers standing at the fridge compare relative prices and swing toward beef. In the beef market this is not a movement along the curve — beef's own price has not changed yet. It is a rightward shift of the entire beef demand curve: at every possible beef price, more beef is now demanded than before. The new curve sits to the right of the old one, and the market slides to a new equilibrium with a higher beef price and a larger beef quantity. Say the rule in one breath: a rise in the price of a substitute shifts demand for the other good to the right.

Now complements, same axes, new market: mobile data. Let the price of smartphones fall as cheap handsets flood in. Phones and data are consumed together, so cheaper phones mean more phone users, and every new user needs data. The demand curve for data shifts to the right — at every data price, more gigabytes are demanded. Run it the other way for the exam's favourite: if the price of cars rises, fewer cars are bought, and the demand curve for petrol shifts to the left. The rule: a rise in the price of a complement shifts demand for its partner to the left; a fall shifts it to the right.

Notice the elegant symmetry worth two easy marks: for substitutes, the price of the related good and the demand for our good move in the same direction; for complements, they move in opposite directions.

Work this section's questions now: substitutes and complements identified, each shift drawn in words with its direction, and the symmetry rule applied to a pair you have not seen before.

## Subtopic: Product Markets and Factor Markets — the Loop That Connects Shops to Jobs

The second relationship is bigger: entire categories of markets feeding each other. Product markets are where goods and services are sold — the shops, the online stores, the taxi ranks. Factor markets are where the factors of production are hired — the labour market where wages are set, the capital market where loans are priced, the property market where rent is negotiated.

The bridge between them is one of the most examinable ideas in Grade 11: derived demand. Nobody hires a bricklayer for the pleasure of it; the demand for bricklayers is derived from the demand for houses. Nobody demands truck drivers except because goods must move to buyers. Demand in a factor market is always derived from demand in some product market.

Now run the loop forward with a South African case. Suppose demand for new housing in Gauteng surges. In the product market, house prices rise and construction firms plan more projects. That surge travels straight into the factor markets: firms demand more bricklayers, more cement, more scaffolding, more finance. Wages for scarce artisans get bid up; cement orders climb; banks price more building loans. The product market boom became factor market demand.

The loop also runs backward. Those higher wages and material costs are production costs, and rising costs shift supply curves in product markets leftward — pushing house prices higher still, or squeezing the boom. And remember the circular flow from Term 1: the wages paid in factor markets become household income, which becomes spending in product markets. Shops and jobs are not two separate worlds; they are two halves of one loop, and a shock to either half travels around the circle.

For the exam, master both directions in one sentence each. Forward: stronger product demand raises derived demand for factors, lifting wages and factor prices. Backward: higher factor prices raise costs, shifting product supply leftward and raising product prices. Practise until each direction takes you five seconds.

Attend to this section's questions before the final piece: derived demand defined and illustrated, the forward loop traced from houses to bricklayers, and the backward loop traced from wages to prices.

## Subtopic: The Four Market Structures — a First Map

The third relationship asks a different question: within a single market, how many sellers are there, and how much power does each hold over price? The answer sorts every market in the economy into one of four structures. Grade 12 will spend a year on their graphs; today you master the map itself.

Structure one: perfect competition. Very many small sellers, identical products, buyers and sellers fully informed, and no barriers to entry — new firms can walk in freely. No single seller can influence the price; each is a price taker. Fresh produce markets come close: one tomato farmer among thousands sells at the going price or not at all.

Structure two: monopolistic competition. Still many sellers and easy entry, but now products are differentiated — similar, not identical. Every hair salon, takeaway and clothing brand offers its own version, so each seller has a small degree of price power built on loyalty and location, but competition stays close and fierce.

Structure three: oligopoly. A few large sellers dominate, and entry is difficult — think of South Africa's big banks, the mobile networks, or the fuel retail majors. The defining feature is interdependence: each firm must anticipate its rivals' reactions before moving price, which is why oligopoly prices are often sticky and competition shifts to advertising, features and loyalty programmes. The ever-present temptation is collusion — agreeing not to compete — which is illegal and policed by the Competition Commission; the bread price-fixing case remains the famous South African example.

Structure four: monopoly. One seller, a product with no close substitute, and entry blocked — by law, by licence, by ownership of a resource, or by the sheer cost of duplicating a network. Eskom in electricity transmission is the standard local example. A monopolist is a price maker: it can set price by choosing how much to supply, though even it is disciplined by what buyers can afford.

The classification line the exam loves: perfect competition is the only perfect market; the other three are imperfect markets, because sellers there hold some power over price. And the two questions that sort any market in seconds: how many sellers, and how hard is entry?

The final questions of this part are with you now: the four structures ranked by number of sellers and entry, each matched to a South African example, and perfect separated from imperfect with the reason stated.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same three relationships from ground level: a fridge in a supermarket, a building site, and the difference between a taxi rank and the electricity grid.

## Subtopic: The Fridge Decides — Chicken, Beef and the Compared Price

Stand at the meat fridge with two hundred rand and watch how your own mind works.

You never ask "is chicken expensive?" in mid-air. You ask "is chicken expensive compared to beef?" If chicken is seventy rand a kilo and beef is one-forty, chicken is the value play. That compared price — one good measured against the other — is what economists call the relative price, and it is the only price your feet actually obey.

Test it. If every price in the shop doubled tonight, and your pay doubled too, tomorrow's shopping would feel strange but your choices would land in the same basket — nothing got cheaper compared to anything else. But if only beef doubles while chicken sits still, chicken suddenly looks like a bargain even though its tag never changed. Cheaper compared-to is what pulls the trolley.

Now name the two kinds of teammates goods can be. Chicken and beef do the same job — filling the pot with protein — so they are substitutes: one can stand in for the other. Braai pack and charcoal do their job only together — so they are complements: partners, not rivals.

And here is how one market whispers to another. Bird flu hits and chicken jumps to ninety-five. In the beef market, something odd happens: beef's own price hasn't moved, yet more people want beef this week than last week. Picture beef's demand curve — price up the side, quantity along the bottom, the curve sloping down. It does not slide along itself; the whole curve steps to the right, because at every beef price there are now more willing buyers. Rival's price up, my demand up — that is the substitute signature.

Partners work the other way. If braai packs get pricey, fewer braais happen, and charcoal sales sink even though charcoal's price never moved — charcoal's whole demand curve steps to the left. Partner's price up, my demand down.

Your questions for this section are with you now — for each pair, first ask: same job, or job done together? Rivals move together; partners move apart.

## Subtopic: Every Job Is Hiding Inside Something Somebody Buys

Here is a question that sounds philosophical but is pure economics: why does a bricklayer have a job?

Not because anyone loves bricklaying. A bricklayer has a job because somebody, somewhere, wants a house. The want for the house comes first; the want for the bricklayer is borrowed from it. Economists say the demand for labour is derived — second-hand demand, passed down from the product to the people and materials that make it. Every job you can name is hiding inside something somebody buys: the truck driver inside your delivery, the barista inside your coffee, the miner inside a battery on another continent.

Once you see that, you can watch shops and jobs pull each other like two children holding hands. Suppose Gauteng goes on a house-building spree. First the product market heats: houses sell fast, prices climb, builders take on new projects. Then — automatically — the factor markets heat too: sites need bricklayers, so artisan wages get bid up; sites need cement, so cement orders surge; sites need money, so banks write more loans. Nobody planned that second wave. Demand for houses became demand for hands, bags and loans all by itself.

But the hands pull back. Those higher wages and pricier cement are costs to the builders, and higher costs mean builders supply less eagerly at any given price — which nudges house prices up further. And there is a third pull, the one you met in the circular flow: the bricklayer's fatter pay packet goes home and becomes spending at the shops, warming product markets all over town.

So when the news says "construction is booming" or "mines are cutting jobs", you now hear it properly: product markets and factor markets are two halves of one loop. Whatever happens to the things people buy happens next to the people who make them.

Take this section's questions now — each one asks you to find the product hiding behind a job, or the jobs hiding behind a product.

## Subtopic: Count the Sellers — From the Taxi Rank to the Grid

Last relationship: how the number of sellers in a market decides who gets to set the price. Walk through four South African scenes, counting sellers as you go.

Scene one: a street market with fifty tomato sellers, all selling the same tomatoes. Try charging five rand above everyone else and watch your customers take three steps to the next table. With many sellers and identical goods and nothing stopping newcomers, no one controls the price — everyone takes it. That is perfect competition, and it is the only market we call perfect.

Scene two: the takeaways along one taxi route. Many sellers again, easy to open another — but now each is a little different: this one's chips are famous, that one is next to the rank, the other gives extra chakalaka. Each shop can charge a rand or two more than its rivals because of that difference, but only a rand or two. Many sellers, similar-but-not-identical products: monopolistic competition.

Scene three: cellphone networks. Count the sellers: a handful. Starting a new network would cost billions, so newcomers stay out. And notice how the few behave — before one moves a price, it thinks hard about how the others will answer, the way taxi associations watch each other on a contested route. Few sellers, hard entry, everyone watching everyone: oligopoly. The dangerous shortcut is agreeing not to compete — collusion — which is illegal, and the bread price-fixing scandal shows it happens close to home.

Scene four: the wires that bring electricity to your street. One seller. No substitute you can switch to, and no chance of a rival building a second grid past your house. One seller, blocked entry: monopoly — the price maker, which is exactly why monopolies get watched and regulated.

Four scenes, one ladder: as sellers get fewer and entry gets harder, price power grows — from taking the price, to nudging it, to watching rivals, to making it. Every market you will ever meet sits on one rung of that ladder.

And here come the last questions of the lesson: count the sellers, test the entry door, place the market on its rung — and keep rivals-move-together, partners-move-apart in your pocket for the first two.
