**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! First one: when the price of chicken rises and people buy more beef, half of us drew a movement along the beef demand curve and lost the marks. Why is it a shift and not a movement?

**Tutor:** Because of whose price changed, and that single check settles every question of this type forever. A movement along the beef demand curve is caused by one thing only: a change in the price of beef itself. In our story, beef's price did not move an inch at the moment the shift happened — chicken's price moved. Chicken's price is, from the beef market's point of view, an outside condition, like income or taste, and any outside condition changes the quantity demanded at every possible beef price simultaneously. If beef costs one hundred and forty rand, more people now choose it than yesterday at that same one-forty; if it costs one-twenty, more choose it than yesterday at one-twenty. When the quantity demanded rises at every price at once, the whole curve has to relocate to the right — no single point on the old curve can tell that story. The routine to burn in: read the question, find whose price changed. Own price — slide along the curve. Anything else, including a related good's price — pick the whole curve up and move it. Then, and only then, let the market slide along the new curve to its new equilibrium.

**Bianca:** Next one: how do we tell substitutes from complements when the pair is unfamiliar? Bread and polony started a genuine debate — some said partners, some said rivals.

**Tutor:** Good, because bread and polony expose the fact that the test is behaviour, not category. Ask the one diagnostic question: when the price of the first good rises, does the second good sell more or less? Rivals do the same job, so a dearer first good pushes buyers toward the second — substitutes make the partner's sales rise. True partners are consumed together, so a dearer first good drags the second down with it — complements make the partner's sales fall. Now apply it to bread and polony honestly: for most households they are eaten together as polony sandwiches, so expensive bread means fewer sandwiches and less polony — complements. But notice the deeper lesson your debate uncovered: the relationship lives in how consumers actually use the goods, not in the goods themselves. Brown bread and white bread are substitutes for each other while each is a complement of polony. In an exam, if the pairing is unusual, state your assumption in one clause — "assuming these are consumed together" — then run the price test. The marker is checking the logic chain, and a stated assumption plus correct logic earns full marks even where households differ.

**Bianca:** Third one: derived demand feels obvious for bricklayers, but someone asked — is there any demand that is NOT derived? Teachers? Nurses? Where does it stop?

**Tutor:** Sharp question, and the answer draws the exact line the concept needs. Consumer demand in product markets is original demand: you want the bread, the haircut, the taxi ride for the satisfaction it gives you directly. Factor demand is derived: no firm wants labour, land or capital for its own sake — it wants them only because they produce something consumers want. So the test is not the job title but who is doing the demanding and why. A hospital hires nurses because patients demand healthcare: derived. A province hires teachers because households and the nation demand education: derived, with the state acting as the buyer on citizens' behalf. Even the demand for a delivery truck is derived from the parcels households ordered. What is never derived is the final consumer's own want at the end of the chain — that is the original spark every other demand borrows from. This is also why factor markets crash when product markets crash: when consumers stopped travelling during hard times, nobody needed as many flights, so demand for pilots and airport staff fell within months, even though those workers were exactly as skilled as before. Derived demand means the fortunes of every factor are chained to the product it helps make.

**Bianca:** Last one, exam strategy: if a question gives us a strange market and asks which structure it is, what is the fastest reliable method under time pressure?

**Tutor:** Use a three-question ladder, always in the same order, and let the answers sort the market for you. Question one: how many sellers — very many, many, a few, or one? That alone eliminates half the options. Question two: what is the product like — identical across sellers, differentiated, or without any close substitute? Question three: how hard is entry — free, easy, difficult, or blocked? Now read the ladder. Very many sellers, identical product, free entry: perfect competition. Many sellers, differentiated products, easy entry: monopolistic competition. A few sellers, entry difficult, firms reacting to each other: oligopoly — and the word "interdependence" belongs in your answer, because it is the structure's fingerprint. One seller, no close substitute, blocked entry: monopoly, and name the barrier if the question shows one, whether a licence, a patent or a network nobody can duplicate. Finish with the classification sentence that examiners reserve a mark for: only perfect competition is a perfect market; the other three are imperfect because sellers hold some influence over price. Three questions, one sentence — under a minute, and nothing about the strangeness of the market can throw you, because the ladder never changes.
