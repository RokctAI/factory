**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! First one: half of us still cannot separate average cost from marginal cost. They both feel like "cost per unit-ish" things, so what is the actual difference?

**Tutor:** The difference is which units you are talking about, and it decides which arithmetic you use. Average cost is a statement about everything produced so far: take the total cost and share it across all the units, so at four units you take R76 and divide by four to get R19 each. Marginal cost is a statement about one unit only — the newest one — and you isolate it by subtracting neighbouring totals, so the fifth unit costs R102 minus R76, which is R26 and nothing to do with the four before it. Notice how far apart those two numbers sit at the same point in the table: average is around R20 while the marginal figure has already climbed to R26. That gap is exactly why the confusion is expensive. A memory hook that holds under pressure: average is about all of them, so you divide by all of them; marginal is about the most recent, so you subtract to strip the others away. If you find yourself dividing while answering a marginal question, you have already taken the wrong turn.

***

**Bianca:** Next: the marginal revenue column is R30 the whole way down, and the class thinks that must be a trick or a typing error. Why does it never move?

**Tutor:** It never moves because of what kind of firm this is. Our firm is one small seller in a large competitive market, so the price of R30 is set by the market and not by the firm; it can sell one unit or six without the price shifting. Every additional unit therefore brings in exactly R30 more, and since marginal revenue is nothing but the size of each step in total revenue, and total revenue climbs R30, R60, R90, R120, R150, R180, the steps are R30 every time. The same fact makes average revenue R30 as well, because money per unit sold can only be the price you sold at. Picture it: with output along the bottom and rands up the side, total revenue is a straight line rising steadily from the corner, while average and marginal revenue lie together as one flat line running level at the R30 mark from one end to the other. That flat line is the signature of perfect competition. Next year you meet the monopoly, which must lower its price to sell more, and its marginal revenue line slopes downhill below the price — and this year's flat line will suddenly look like a gift.

***

**Bianca:** This one caused a proper argument: if the firm still makes R40 of profit at six units, why does the lesson insist six is wrong? Profit is profit, surely?

**Tutor:** Profit is profit, but the question in front of you is not "is this profitable?" — it is "is this the most profit available?" At five units the firm banks R48. Choosing six means handing R8 back, because that sixth unit costs R38 to make and brings in only R30. Nothing about the first five units changed; you simply added a transaction that loses money and let it eat into what you had already earned. Put it in everyday terms: you would not pay R38 for something worth R30 to you, and the fact that your wallet still has money in it afterwards does not make the purchase sensible. That is the whole shift Grade 11 is asking you to make, from thinking about the total to thinking at the margin. Maximum profit means every unit that pays for itself gets produced, and no unit that does not. Test each unit on its own merits, not on the health of the overall total, and the sixth unit fails its own test.

***

**Bianca:** Last one, and it is a strategy question: in the exam, should we use the marginal-revenue-equals-marginal-cost rule or just work out profit at every level? The profit column feels safer to us.

**Tutor:** Do both, in a deliberate order. Lead with the marginal reasoning, because that is the economics the marker is looking for. Walk the units aloud in your answer: the fifth unit earns R30 and costs R26, so it is produced; the sixth earns R30 and costs R38, so it is refused; therefore output is five. If a question asks you to work with a graph, describe what is happening there in the same breath — output along the bottom, rands up the side, the marginal cost line falling from R18 to R10 and then swinging steeply up through R16, R26 and R38 in a lopsided tick, with the flat R30 marginal revenue line cutting through it between the fifth and sixth units. Then verify with the profit column if you have room: R150 minus R102 is R48, which beats R44 at four units and R40 at six. That verification line turns a good answer into one that is very hard to mark down. The one thing to avoid is submitting only the brute-force column, because a question that says "explain using marginal analysis" is testing the reasoning, not the arithmetic.
