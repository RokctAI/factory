**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! First up: plenty of us still muddle average cost with marginal cost. Both sound like "cost per unit" ideas, so where is the real dividing line?

**Tutor:** The dividing line is which units the number is talking about, and that choice fixes the arithmetic. Average cost describes the entire batch produced so far: share the whole bill across every unit, so at four units you take R88 and divide by four to get R22 each. Marginal cost describes exactly one unit — the newest — and you isolate it by subtracting neighbouring totals: the fifth unit costs R120 minus R88, which is R32, and the four units before it play no part in that number. Look how far apart those figures sit at the same spot in the table: the average hovers near R24 while the marginal figure has already reached R32. That gap is why the confusion costs so dearly. A memory hook that survives exam pressure: average covers all of them, so you divide by all of them; marginal covers only the most recent, so you subtract to strip the rest away. The moment you catch yourself dividing in a marginal question, you already know you have taken the wrong exit.

**Bianca:** Next one: the marginal revenue column reads R40 all the way down, and half the class is convinced something must be missing. Why does it never budge?

**Tutor:** It never budges because of the kind of firm we are studying. Ours is one small seller inside a large competitive market, so the R40 price is set by the market, not by the firm — it can sell one unit or six and the price stands still. Each additional unit therefore brings in exactly R40 more, and since marginal revenue is simply the size of each step in total revenue, and total revenue climbs R40, R80, R120, R160, R200, R240, every step measures R40. The same fact pins average revenue at R40, because income per unit sold can only be the price it sold for. Picture it: output along the bottom, rands up the side, total revenue rising as a straight line out of the corner, while average and marginal revenue share one flat line at the R40 mark from end to end. That flat line is the signature of perfect competition. Next year you will meet the monopoly, which must cut its price to sell more, so its marginal revenue tilts downhill below the price — and this year's flat line will suddenly feel like a gift.

**Bianca:** This one started an argument at break: if the firm still clears R74 of profit at six units, why does the lesson insist that six is wrong? Money is money!

**Tutor:** Money is money, but the question being asked is not "is this profitable?" — it is "is this the most profit on offer?" At five units the firm banks R80. Choosing six hands R6 back, because that sixth unit costs R46 to make and earns only R40. Nothing changed about the first five units; the firm simply bolted on one losing transaction and let it gnaw at what was already earned. Put it in pocket-money terms: you would never pay R46 for something worth R40 to you, and the fact that your wallet still holds cash afterwards does not make the deal wise. That is the mental shift Grade 11 wants from you — moving from judging the total to judging each unit at the margin. Maximum profit means every unit that pays its own way gets made, and not one unit beyond that. Test each unit on its own merits rather than on the health of the grand total, and the sixth unit fails its own test every time.

**Bianca:** Last one, and it is pure strategy: when a question asks for the best output, should we lean on the marginal-revenue-equals-marginal-cost rule or just grind out the profit at every level? The profit column feels safer.

**Tutor:** Use both, in a deliberate order. Open with the marginal reasoning, because that is the economics the answer is meant to display. Walk the units aloud on paper: the fifth unit earns R40 and costs R32, so it is produced; the sixth earns R40 and costs R46, so it is refused; therefore output is five. If the question involves a graph, narrate it in the same breath — output along the bottom, rands up the side, the marginal cost line dropping from R20 through R14 to R12 and then bending steeply up through R18, R32 and R46 in a lopsided hook, with the flat R40 marginal revenue line cutting through it between the fifth and sixth units. Then, if space allows, verify with the profit column: R200 minus R120 is R80, which beats R72 at four units and R74 at six. That verification line turns a solid answer into one that is very hard to fault. What you must not do is submit only the brute-force column, because a question that says "explain using marginal analysis" is testing the reasoning, not the arithmetic.
