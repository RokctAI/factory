# Part 1 — Expert

This session builds the working method behind every market-structure question that Grade 12 will ask: cost and revenue analysis. It takes a plain table of output and total cost, derives average and marginal cost from it, builds the revenue side at a market price of R30, and then answers the question every firm asks: how much should I produce to maximise profit?

## Subtopic: The Cost Table and the Three Cost Concepts

Here is our firm's cost schedule for output levels zero to six units. Total cost: at zero units, R20. At one unit, R38. At two units, R50. At three units, R60. At four units, R76. At five units, R102. At six units, R140.

Stop at the first row, because it already teaches something deep. The firm produces nothing, yet costs are R20. How? Fixed costs — rent, insurance, the security contract — must be paid whether the machines run or not. That R20 at zero output *is* the fixed cost, and it hides inside every later total. The rest of total cost — the part that grows as output grows — is variable cost: raw materials, casual wages, electricity for the machines.

Now the two derived measures. Average cost is total cost divided by output — the cost per unit. Compute it row by row. One unit: 38 divided by 1 is R38. Two units: 50 divided by 2 is R25. Three units: 60 divided by 3 is R20. Four units: 76 divided by 4 is R19. Five units: 102 divided by 5 is R20,40. Six units: 140 divided by 6 is about R23,33. Watch the shape: average cost falls, bottoms out at four units, then rises — the famous U-shape. It falls at first because the R20 of fixed cost is being spread over more and more units; it rises later as the firm strains its capacity.

Marginal cost is different, and it is the star of this lesson: the *extra* cost of producing *one more* unit. You get it by subtracting neighbouring totals. From zero to one unit: 38 minus 20 is R18. From one to two: 50 minus 38 is R12. Two to three: 60 minus 50 is R10. Three to four: 76 minus 60 is R16. Four to five: 102 minus 76 is R26. Five to six: 140 minus 102 is R38. Marginal cost dips, then climbs steeply.

A common pitfall: dividing when you should subtract. Average asks "per unit so far" — divide. Marginal asks "what did the *last* unit add" — subtract. Keep those verbs straight and half the battle is won.

The questions for this section are with you now: fixed cost read off the zero-output row, average cost computed by division, and marginal cost computed by subtracting neighbouring totals.

## Subtopic: Building the Revenue Side at a Market Price of R30

Now the money coming in. Our firm sells in a competitive market at a given price of R30 per unit — it is a price taker; nothing it does changes that R30.

Total revenue is price times quantity. Zero units: R0. One unit: R30. Two: R60. Three: R90. Four: R120. Five: R150. Six: R180. A steady march upward in equal steps of R30.

Average revenue is total revenue divided by output. One unit: 30 over 1 is R30. Two units: 60 over 2 is R30. Three: 90 over 3 — R30 again. Do you see it? Average revenue *is* the price. That is always true: revenue per unit sold can only be the price you sell at.

Marginal revenue is the extra revenue from selling one more unit — subtract neighbouring totals, exactly as we did for cost. From one to two units: 60 minus 30 is R30. Two to three: 90 minus 60 is R30. Every step: R30. For a price-taking firm, marginal revenue equals the price, because each extra unit fetches exactly R30 without disturbing the market.

So the revenue side of our table is beautifully simple: total revenue climbs by R30 a step, and average revenue and marginal revenue are both flat at R30. On a graph, that flat line at R30 is the firm's demand line — horizontal, the signature of perfect competition. Grade 12 will contrast this with the monopolist, whose marginal revenue falls — but the *method* you are learning today carries over unchanged.

Attend to this section's questions before the decision is made: total revenue at each output, average revenue recognised as the price itself, and marginal revenue held flat at R30 for a price taker.

## Subtopic: Finding the Profit-Maximising Output

Now the decision. The firm wants maximum profit, and profit is total revenue minus total cost. Brute force first — compute it at every output level, because seeing the whole landscape builds trust in the cleverer method to come.

Zero units: 0 minus 20 — a loss of R20. One unit: 30 minus 38 — a loss of R8. Two units: 60 minus 50 — profit R10. Three units: 90 minus 60 — profit R30. Four units: 120 minus 76 — profit R44. Five units: 150 minus 102 — profit R48. Six units: 180 minus 140 — profit R40.

Read down that profit column: minus 20, minus 8, 10, 30, 44, 48, 40. Profit climbs, peaks at five units with R48, then falls. The answer: the profit-maximising output is five units.

But now the economist's method — the one exams reward. Think unit by unit, at the margin. Each extra unit brings in marginal revenue of R30. Each extra unit costs its marginal cost. The rule: produce every unit whose marginal revenue is at least its marginal cost, and stop before the first unit where marginal cost overtakes marginal revenue.

Walk the table. Unit one: brings R30, costs R18 — produce it, R12 ahead. Unit two: R30 versus R12 — produce, R18 ahead. Unit three: R30 versus R10 — produce. Unit four: R30 versus R16 — produce. Unit five: R30 versus R26 — still R4 ahead — produce. Unit six: R30 versus R38 — this unit *destroys* R8 of profit. Refuse it.

Stop at five. Exactly where the brute-force column peaked. The rule "produce until marginal revenue equals marginal cost" — MR equals MC — found the answer without computing a single profit figure. That is why examiners love it: it shows you understand *why* five is best, not just that it is.

This section's questions are in front of you now — the profit column read correctly, the marginal walk taken unit by unit, and the sixth unit refused with a reason rather than an instinct.

## Subtopic: Verifying, Graphing and the Habits That Earn Marks

Let us verify from both sides, the way you should in any exam answer. At five units, was the last unit worth it? Marginal cost R26, marginal revenue R30 — yes, it added R4 of profit. Would a sixth unit pay? Marginal cost R38 against R30 — no, it would cost R8 of profit. Confirmed from both directions: five units. And the profit there: total revenue R150 minus total cost R102 gives R48 — matching our profit column exactly. When two independent methods agree, you can trust the answer.

Notice also *why* the sixth unit is refused even though the firm still makes R40 of profit producing six. R40 is profit — but it is R8 *less* than R48. Maximising profit means squeezing out every profitable unit and not one unit more. "Still profitable overall" is not the test; "is the *next unit* profitable" is.

If the question asks for a graph, plot output on the horizontal axis and rands on the vertical. Marginal cost traces its tick shape — down from 18 to 10, then up through 16, 26, 38. Marginal revenue is the flat line at R30. Their crossing, between five and six units, marks the profit-maximising zone — and you read the answer as the last whole unit before marginal cost breaks above the line: five.

Your self-check routine for any cost-revenue table. One: does total cost at zero output equal fixed cost? Two: average equals total divided by quantity — *divide*; marginal equals the change in total — *subtract neighbours*. Three: for a given market price, average revenue and marginal revenue both equal that price. Four: find where marginal cost crosses marginal revenue from below and stop there. Five: verify with the profit column if time allows.

Master this table today. In Grade 12, perfect markets, monopolies and oligopolies will all be interrogated with exactly these six columns — only the shape of the revenue side changes. The method is yours for good.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the answer defended from both sides, the shape of the marginal cost curve against the flat revenue line, and the five-step self-check applied to a table you have not seen before.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same six columns from a vetkoek stand outside a taxi rank — a gas bottle, a bag of flour, and one honest question asked over and over.

## Subtopic: Costs That Sleep and Costs That Work

Picture a vetkoek stand. Before a single vetkoek is fried, money has already left your pocket: the table hire, the gas bottle, the licence for the spot. Those bills arrive whether you sell a hundred vetkoek or none at all. In our lesson's firm that sleeping cost is R20 — look at the row where output is zero and total cost is still R20. Nothing was made, and R20 was still owed. Those are the fixed costs.

Then there are the costs that only wake up when you work: flour, oil, mince, the cousin you pay to help on a busy morning. The more you fry, the more of those you buy. Those are the variable costs, and they are the reason total cost climbs as output rises — from R20 at zero, to R38, R50, R60, R76, R102 and R140 at six units.

Now two different questions people confuse, and the whole topic sits on the difference.

The first question is "what is each one costing me on average?" You answer that by sharing the total bill across everything you made — you divide. At three units, total cost is R60, so R60 shared over three units is R20 each. At four units, R76 over four is R19 each. At five, R102 over five is R20,40. Notice it drops and then climbs again, and there is a homely reason: the sleeping R20 gets split between more and more vetkoek, so each one carries a smaller slice of it — until you start straining, working the pan too hard and paying for help, and costs push back up.

The second question is completely different: "what did the last one cost me?" Not the average — just the newest one on its own. You answer that by subtracting: this row's total minus the row before it. Going from three units to four, total cost goes from R60 to R76, so the fourth unit cost R16 by itself. The fifth: R102 minus R76 is R26. The sixth: R140 minus R102 is R38. That is marginal cost, and see how it dips low and then climbs steeply as the stand gets stretched.

Two questions, two different verbs. Average asks about everything so far, so you divide. Marginal asks about the last one only, so you subtract. Mix up the verbs and every number afterwards is wrong.

Your questions for this section are with you now — for each one, decide first whether it is asking about everything so far or only about the last one, and the right verb follows.

## Subtopic: Thirty Rand a Plate, Every Single Plate

The money coming in is the easy side, and it is easy for a real reason.

Our stand sells at the going rate: R30 a plate. It is one small stand among many at the rank, so it cannot charge R40 and it has no need to charge R20 — the price is simply what the market is paying. Economists call that being a price taker.

So the takings are pure multiplication. One plate, R30. Two, R60. Three, R90. Four, R120. Five, R150. Six, R180. That is total revenue, climbing in equal steps.

Now ask our two questions again on this side of the table. What is each plate bringing in on average? Divide: R90 over three plates is R30. R150 over five is R30. It is R30 every time, and it could not be anything else, because the money per plate is simply the price you charge. Average revenue is the price.

And what did the last plate bring in? Subtract: R60 minus R30 is R30; R90 minus R60 is R30. Also R30, every step of the way. Marginal revenue is the price too, because selling one more plate at this stand does not force the price down for anybody.

Picture the money side as a picture. Put the number of plates along the bottom and rands up the side. Total revenue is a straight line climbing steadily from the corner, gaining R30 for every plate. Average revenue and marginal revenue together are one flat line lying at the R30 mark, running level from left to right, never sagging. That flat line is the giveaway for a small firm in a competitive market — and next year, when you meet a monopoly that must drop its price to sell more, its marginal revenue line will slope downhill instead, and today's flat one will suddenly look precious.

Take the questions for this section now — check you can say why both R30 lines are flat, in your own words, before you carry on.

## Subtopic: Is the Next One Worth Making?

Here is the decision, and you already make it in ordinary life.

The blunt way first: work out the profit at every level, since profit is money in minus money out. At zero, nothing in and R20 out: a loss of R20. One plate: R30 in, R38 out — a loss of R8. Two: R60 in, R50 out — R10 profit. Three: R90 against R60 — R30. Four: R120 against R76 — R44. Five: R150 against R102 — R48. Six: R180 against R140 — R40. The profit climbs, tops out at five plates with R48, and then slips back.

Now the smarter way, which is really just the way you think about a taxi fare or one more slice of pizza. Ask of each plate on its own: is this one worth making? Every plate brings in R30. Each plate costs what it costs.

Plate one brings R30 and costs R18 — make it, you are R12 up. Plate two brings R30 and costs R12 — make it. Plate three brings R30 and costs R10 — make it. Plate four brings R30 and costs R16 — make it. Plate five brings R30 and costs R26 — still R4 up, so make it. Plate six brings R30 and costs R38 — that one takes R8 out of your pocket. Refuse it.

Stop at five. Exactly where the blunt method peaked, reached without working out a single profit figure. Economists write it as "produce until marginal revenue equals marginal cost", but the idea underneath is just: keep going while the next one pays, and stop when it stops paying.

And notice the trap, because this is the bit that separates a good answer from a great one. At six plates the stand still makes R40 — real money, genuinely positive. But R40 is R8 less than R48. "Am I making a profit?" is the wrong question. "Is the next one worth it?" is the right one.

Picture this side too. Along the bottom, the number of plates; up the side, rands. The marginal cost line is not straight: it drops from R18 down to R10 at three plates, then swings sharply upward through R16, R26 and R38, so it makes a lopsided tick or swoosh shape, low in the middle and rising steeply at the right. The marginal revenue line lies flat across it at R30. The two cross between the fifth and sixth plates, and the answer is the last whole plate before the cost line breaks up through R30 — the fifth.

And here come the last questions of the lesson: divide or subtract, flat lines or rising ones, and the one question a firm asks about every next unit. Land these and Grade 12's market structures will only be changing the shape of the revenue side, never the method.
