**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! First, the calculation itself: the same numbers keep producing different answers around the room. Where exactly does the working fall apart?

**Tutor:** Almost always at one of two spots, and both can be repaired tonight. Spot one: the wrong number on the bottom of the percentage change. Percentage change is the change divided by the original value — the number things started at — times one hundred. When the bus pass climbs from five hundred to five hundred and seventy-five rand, the change is seventy-five and the original is five hundred, so seventy-five over five hundred gives fifteen percent. Divide by five hundred and seventy-five instead and you get about thirteen percent, and everything after that is contaminated. Spot two: dividing the two percentages upside down. Elasticity is the quantity response over the price push — percentage change in quantity demanded on top, percentage change in price on the bottom. Six percent over fifteen percent gives zero comma four; flip the fraction and you get two comma five, which throws the good into the wrong category and reverses every conclusion about revenue. A three-second sanity check: does the answer match the story? Passengers barely moved when the fare jumped, so the elasticity must be small, comfortably below one. If your calculator announces two comma five, the story itself is telling you the fraction is inverted.

***

**Bianca:** Next: the minus sign. Some worked answers keep it, some drop it, and the class wants one safe rule to follow under exam pressure.

**Tutor:** Here is the rule, and the reason behind it, because the reason is what protects the marks. The law of demand guarantees that price and quantity demanded move in opposite directions, so one of the two percentage changes is always negative and the strict answer is always negative — minus zero comma four for our bus route. That minus sign carries no fresh information; it only restates the law of demand. So economists conventionally take the absolute value and compare sizes: zero comma four against one comma six. In your written answer, do this: calculate honestly, and when the result is negative, add one clause — "the negative sign reflects the inverse relationship between price and quantity demanded; we interpret the absolute value" — then interpret the size. That sentence shows you command both the mathematics and the convention, which is worth more than silently discarding a sign. And a caution for the road ahead: when income elasticity arrives next year, the sign genuinely matters, because a negative there flags an inferior good. So the habit to build is not "ignore minus signs" but "know what each sign means before deciding whether it matters".

**Bianca:** Third: someone found a straight demand curve in the textbook labelled elastic near the top and inelastic near the bottom, and now the steep-means-inelastic shortcut feels broken. Which version is true?

**Tutor:** Both are true once each sits in its proper place. The shortcut compares two different curves passing through the same neighbourhood of prices: if one is much steeper than the other around the going price, the steeper one responds less to a price change there, so relatively steep suggests relatively inelastic — a perfectly respectable sketching habit. The textbook label describes travelling along one straight curve, where elasticity genuinely changes as you move, because elasticity is built from percentages rather than raw amounts. Near the top of the curve, the price is high and the quantity small — so a one-rand price cut is a small percentage of the big price, while the extra units sold are a large percentage of the tiny quantity: large quantity response over small price change, elasticity above one. Near the bottom, that same one-rand cut is a big percentage of the now-low price, while the extra units barely register against the already-large quantity: elasticity below one. Identical slope everywhere, different percentages everywhere. The exam-safe wording: say relatively elastic or relatively inelastic when comparing curves, calculate whenever a number is requested, and keep "elastic at the top, inelastic at the bottom" for describing a single straight line.

**Bianca:** Last one, the essay favourite: when a question asks whether a business should raise or cut its price, how do we build the answer?

**Tutor:** Use a four-step frame and let elasticity referee every step. Step one, classify the good's demand through the determinants: hunt for the ways out. A commuter bus route where no other transport runs — few substitutes, a necessity, short-run setting: inelastic. A car wash surrounded by rival washes and a bucket at home: elastic. Step two, state the rulebook line that applies: inelastic demand means price and total revenue travel together; elastic demand means they travel apart. Step three, prove it with the arithmetic when numbers are supplied, showing both revenue totals: five hundred rand times two thousand passengers is one million; five hundred and seventy-five times one thousand eight hundred and eighty is one million and eighty-one thousand, so revenue rose. Numbers turn a claim into a demonstration. Step four, add the qualifications that mark out a top answer: revenue is not profit — extra sales bring extra costs, so a complete decision needs the cost side too; and when the question has a policy angle, note the fairness point that taxes on inelastic necessities weigh heaviest on the poor, which is why the heavy duties chase cigarettes and alcohol rather than staple foods. Classify, state the rule, prove with numbers, then qualify — the same frame answers every pricing question this topic can throw at you.
