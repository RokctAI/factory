# Part 1 — Expert

This session builds the one tool that turns the law of demand from a direction into a number: price elasticity of demand. The law tells you that when price rises, quantity demanded falls. Elasticity tells you by how much — and that "how much" decides whether a spaza raises or drops its prices, why the Minister of Finance taxes cigarettes and not bread, and a reliable eight-to-twelve marks in every Grade 11 paper.

## Subtopic: The Elasticity Number — Definition and Calculation

Price elasticity of demand measures how responsive quantity demanded is to a change in the good's own price. As a formula in words: elasticity equals the percentage change in quantity demanded, divided by the percentage change in price. Both changes as percentages — that is what makes the number fair to compare across goods, whether we are pricing bread or BMWs.

Learn the percentage-change step first, because most lost marks die there. Percentage change equals the change, divided by the original value, times one hundred. Original value on the bottom — always.

Now a full worked example, slowly. A taxi association raises the fare on a local route from twenty rand to twenty-four rand. Daily riders fall from one thousand to nine hundred and fifty. Step one, the price change: four rand on an original twenty is four over twenty times one hundred — a twenty percent increase. Step two, the quantity change: fifty fewer riders on an original one thousand is fifty over a thousand times one hundred — a five percent decrease. Step three, divide: five percent over twenty percent gives zero comma two five. Riders barely flinched: a big price push produced a small quantity response.

One convention before we interpret. Because demand slopes downward, one change is always negative — strictly the answer is minus zero comma two five. Economists usually drop the minus sign and speak of the absolute value, and so will we: what matters is the size of the response, and the exam expects you to say so rather than silently ignore the sign.

Second example for contrast. A tuck shop raises a chocolate from twenty-five rand to thirty rand — a twenty percent increase. Weekly sales fall from four hundred bars to two hundred and eighty — a thirty percent decrease. Thirty over twenty: elasticity one comma five. Same size of price push, triple the flight of buyers.

Two goods, two numbers, one scale: zero comma two five versus one comma five. The rest of the lesson is learning to read that scale.

## Subtopic: Reading the Number — Five Categories and Their Graphs in Words

The elasticity scale has a centre at one, and every demand curve you will ever meet falls into one of five zones.

Elasticity greater than one: elastic demand. Buyers respond more than proportionally — our chocolate at one comma five. These are goods people can walk away from or replace. Picture the graph: price on the vertical axis, quantity on the horizontal; an elastic demand curve is relatively flat, because a small vertical step down in price triggers a long horizontal stride out in quantity.

Elasticity less than one: inelastic demand. Buyers respond less than proportionally — our taxi route at zero comma two five. Goods people feel they must have. The curve is relatively steep: even a tall vertical jump in price produces only a short horizontal shuffle in quantity.

Elasticity exactly one: unitary elasticity. A ten percent price rise brings exactly a ten percent quantity fall — the boundary case, and, as we will prove shortly, the point where total revenue stands still.

Then the two textbook extremes. Perfectly inelastic demand, elasticity of zero: quantity does not respond at all, no matter the price. The curve is a vertical line standing straight up from the quantity axis — the same quantity bought at any price. A life-saving medicine with no alternative comes close. Perfectly elastic demand, elasticity of infinity: at one particular price buyers will take any amount, but raise the price by one cent and quantity collapses to zero. The curve is a horizontal line lying flat at that price — and you have met it before as the demand line facing a single small farmer in a perfectly competitive market.

A warning that earns marks: slope and elasticity are cousins, not twins. Elasticity changes along a straight demand curve — elastic near the top where prices are high and quantities small, inelastic near the bottom. So never say "this curve is elastic" from shape alone; say "relatively elastic" or, better, calculate. Steep-means-inelastic and flat-means-elastic is a useful sketching habit, not a law.

## Subtopic: What Makes Demand Elastic or Inelastic — the Determinants

Why is taxi demand stubborn at zero comma two five while chocolate flees at one comma five? Five determinants decide, and the exam asks for them constantly.

First and mightiest: the availability of substitutes. If close alternatives exist, buyers escape a price rise easily, so demand is elastic — one brand of cooldrink among many is hyper-elastic, because another brand is one shelf away. If nothing else does the job — a commuter with no bus or train on that route — demand is inelastic. Almost every other determinant works through this one.

Second: necessity versus luxury. Necessities — bread, maize meal, electricity for the basics, the ride to work — keep being bought when prices rise: inelastic. Luxuries — restaurant meals, designer sneakers — are postponed or abandoned: elastic.

Third: the share of income the good claims. Goods that take a sliver of the budget — a box of matches, salt — are barely worth reacting over: inelastic. Goods that swallow a big slice — rent, a car, a smartphone — force buyers to respond: elastic.

Fourth: time. In the week after a petrol price jump, motorists mostly pay — demand is inelastic in the short run. Give households a year and they car-pool, move closer to work, or buy smaller cars: demand becomes more elastic in the long run. Elasticity grows with time to adjust.

Fifth: habit and addiction. Goods that hook their users — cigarettes, alcohol, gambling, arguably data — hold their buyers through price rises: inelastic. This is precisely why Treasury leans so hard on so-called sin taxes: the smokers mostly keep smoking, and the revenue mostly keeps flowing.

Also worth a line: how broadly the good is defined. Food as a whole is deeply inelastic — nobody stops eating — but one specific brand of bread is highly elastic. The narrower the definition, the more substitutes exist, the higher the elasticity.

For essays, do not just list these — apply them in pairs: name the determinant, then attach the good in the question to it with a because-sentence.

## Subtopic: Elasticity and Total Revenue — the Payoff

Here is why businesses and Treasury care about this number. Total revenue is price times quantity, and a price change pulls those two in opposite directions: raise the price and you earn more per unit but sell fewer units. Which force wins? Elasticity is the referee.

Run the taxi numbers. Before: twenty rand times one thousand riders — twenty thousand rand a day. After the increase: twenty-four rand times nine hundred and fifty — twenty-two thousand eight hundred rand. Revenue rose by two thousand eight hundred rand. With inelastic demand, the riders who stay pay so much more that they outweigh the few who leave: price up, revenue up.

Now the chocolate. Before: twenty-five rand times four hundred bars — ten thousand rand a week. After: thirty rand times two hundred and eighty — eight thousand four hundred. Revenue fell by one thousand six hundred rand. With elastic demand, the fleeing buyers outweigh the higher price on each bar: price up, revenue down.

The rulebook, worth memorising as three lines. When demand is inelastic, price and total revenue move in the same direction — so raise the price. When demand is elastic, price and total revenue move in opposite directions — so a price cut grows revenue. When elasticity is exactly one, small price changes leave revenue unchanged — revenue is at its peak.

This rulebook explains pricing all around you. Sports fans meet rising ticket and subscription prices because loyal demand is inelastic. Airlines drop off-season fares because leisure travel is elastic — the discount fills seats faster than it cheapens them. And the Minister of Finance taxes cigarettes, alcohol and fuel because inelastic demand means the tax raises revenue reliably instead of destroying the market — while the same logic warns that steep VAT on necessities lands hardest on the poor, who cannot cut back.

One caution to close: revenue is not profit. Selling more units also costs more to supply, and the full cost-and-revenue machinery from earlier this term handles that. Elasticity answers the revenue question — and in the exam, that is exactly the question it will be asked with.

The final questions of this part are with you now: both revenue calculations reproduced, the three-line rulebook applied to fresh numbers, and one policy example argued through elasticity.

## Subtopic: The Shrug Test — Ouch Alone Is Not the Question

Here is the mistake almost everyone makes on day one: thinking elasticity asks "does the price rise hurt?" It does not. It asks: when the price rises, what do buyers do?

Watch two price rises in the same shop. Bread goes up by two rand. Everybody complains — and nearly everybody buys the bread anyway, because supper is supper. Big ouch, small change in behaviour. Now the fizzy drinks go up by two rand. Less complaining, but something quieter happens: hands drift to the cheaper brand, or to tap water, and sales sink fast. Small ouch, big change in behaviour.

Economists ignore the complaining and measure the behaviour. Demand that barely changes when price rises is inelastic — stiff, like a plank: push it, and it hardly bends. Demand that changes a lot is elastic — stretchy, like a rubber band: the same push and it gives completely. Bread is a plank. Fizzy drink brands are rubber bands.

To make it a number, compare two percentages: by what percent did the price move, and by what percent did the buying move? Divide the buying-change by the price-change. If the answer is smaller than one, buyers moved less than the price did — plank, inelastic. Bigger than one — rubber band, elastic. Exactly one is the tipping point.

Try it. Price up twenty percent, purchases down five percent: five over twenty is a quarter — zero comma two five, a proper plank. Price up twenty percent, purchases down thirty percent: thirty over twenty is one comma five — rubber band. One habit protects the calculation: percentage change is always the change divided by the original number, the number things started at.

Your questions for this section are with you now — for each good, skip the complaints, watch the behaviour, and divide the buying-change by the price-change.

## Subtopic: Why Bread Is a Plank and Cooldrink Is a Rubber Band

Why do goods differ this way? Ask four everyday questions about any good, and its elasticity explains itself.

Question one: is there an escape route? That is the big one. When one cooldrink brand gets pricey, the escape route is on the same shelf — another brand, or water. Escape routes everywhere: rubber band. When the taxi fare rises and there is no bus, no train and work is eighteen kilometres away — no escape route: plank. Almost everything else comes back to this question of substitutes.

Question two: need or nice-to-have? Maize meal, bread, the ride to work, electricity for the fridge — needs stay bought when prices rise: plank. Takeaways, sneakers, concert tickets — nice-to-haves get postponed: rubber band.

Question three: how big a bite of your budget? A box of matches doubling in price steals fifty cents — nobody re-plans their life: plank. Rent going up ten percent moves hundreds of rands — everybody responds, even if it means moving: rubber band. Small bite, small reaction; big bite, big reaction.

Question four: how much time have buyers had? The week petrol jumps, everyone just pays — plank for now. A year later, lift clubs have formed and smaller cars have been bought — the rubber band stretches slowly. Time turns planks into rubber bands.

And one sneaky extra: habits hold people. Cigarettes keep selling through every price rise because addiction blocks the escape route from inside. That is exactly why the Budget raises tobacco and alcohol taxes every single year — the buyers stay, so the tax money comes.

Run any good through the four questions — escape route, need, budget bite, time — and you can predict plank or rubber band before touching a calculator. That prediction, written in because-sentences, is exactly what essay questions want.

## Subtopic: The Shopkeeper's Dilemma — When Raising the Price Loses You Money

Now the payoff, through one week at Mama Dee's tuck shop. She sells airtime vouchers and chocolate bars, and she wants more revenue — more total money through the till. Price times quantity. Her instinct says: raise prices. Her instinct is half right.

Monday, she raises voucher prices ten percent. Learners grumble — but airtime is a need with no escape route at school: a plank. Hardly anyone stops buying. She now earns ten percent more on almost every sale: the till total rises. For plank goods, price up means revenue up.

Wednesday, emboldened, she raises the chocolate thirty rand from twenty-five. Chocolate is a treat with escape routes everywhere — chips, biscuits, the shop across the road: a rubber band. Sales collapse from four hundred bars to two hundred and eighty. Till total on chocolate: it was twenty-five times four hundred — ten thousand rand. Now thirty times two hundred and eighty — eight thousand four hundred. She raised the price and lost one thousand six hundred rand. For rubber-band goods, price up means revenue down — and, run in reverse, a price cut on a rubber-band good can grow revenue, because the flood of new buyers outweighs the discount.

That is the whole rulebook in one shop. Plank: push price up, revenue follows. Rubber band: pull price down, revenue follows. Tipping point at one: revenue stands still.

And once you own the rulebook, you see it everywhere. Data bundles priced boldly because commuters and students must connect. End-of-season sales slashing clothing prices because fashion is pure rubber band. Treasury taxing cigarettes, alcohol and petrol — plank, plank, plank — because the buyers stay and the revenue arrives, and the same logic explaining why VAT stays off brown bread and maize meal: taxing the poor's planks would squeeze hardest exactly where nobody can let go.

One last honesty check: the till total is revenue, not profit — costs still wait in the wings, and earlier this term taught you their machinery.
