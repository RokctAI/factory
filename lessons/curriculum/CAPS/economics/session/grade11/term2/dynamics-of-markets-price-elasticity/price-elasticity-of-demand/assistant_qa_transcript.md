**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! First one, the calculation itself: people keep getting different answers from the same numbers. Where exactly does the working go wrong?

**Tutor:** In ninety percent of cases it dies at one of two spots, and both are fixable tonight. Spot one: the wrong number on the bottom of the percentage change. Percentage change is always the change divided by the original value — the number things started at — times one hundred. When the fare goes from twenty to twenty-four rand, the change is four and the original is twenty, so four over twenty gives twenty percent. Learners who divide by twenty-four get sixteen comma seven percent and everything downstream is poisoned. Spot two: dividing the percentages the wrong way around. Elasticity is the quantity response over the price push — percentage change in quantity demanded on top, percentage change in price on the bottom. Five percent over twenty percent gives zero comma two five; flip it and you get four, which places the good in the wrong category and flips every conclusion about revenue. A self-check that takes three seconds: ask whether your answer matches the story. Riders barely moved when the fare jumped, so the elasticity must be a small number, well below one. If your calculator says four, the story itself is telling you the fraction is upside down.

***

**Bianca:** Next: the minus sign. Some worked examples keep it, some throw it away, and the class wants one rule they can follow in the exam without losing marks.

**Tutor:** Here is the rule, plus the reason, and the reason is what protects the marks. The law of demand guarantees that price and quantity demanded move in opposite directions, so one of the two percentage changes is always negative, and the strict answer is always a negative number — minus zero comma two five for our taxi route. Because that minus sign carries no extra information — it merely repeats the law of demand — economists conventionally work with the absolute value and compare sizes: zero comma two five against one comma five. In the exam, do this: calculate honestly, and if the answer is negative, write one clause — "the negative sign reflects the inverse relationship between price and quantity demanded; we interpret the absolute value" — and then interpret the size. That sentence shows the marker you understand both the mathematics and the convention, which is worth more than silently dropping the sign. And one caution for later topics: when you meet income elasticity next year, the sign genuinely matters, because a negative there identifies an inferior good. So the habit to build is not "ignore minus signs" but "know what each sign means before deciding whether it matters".

**Bianca:** Third: someone found a straight demand curve in the textbook labelled elastic at the top and inelastic at the bottom, and now the steep-means-inelastic shortcut feels like a lie. Which is true?

**Tutor:** Both, once you put each statement in its proper place. The shortcut compares two different curves through the same neighbourhood of prices: if one curve is much steeper than another around the going price, the steeper one responds less to a price change there, so relatively steep suggests relatively inelastic, and that is a perfectly good sketching habit. The textbook's label is about travelling along one straight curve, and there the elasticity genuinely changes as you move. The reason is that elasticity is built from percentages, not raw amounts. Near the top of the curve, price is high and quantity is small — so a one-rand price cut is a small percentage of the big price, while the units gained are a large percentage of the tiny quantity: big quantity response over small price change, elasticity above one. Near the bottom, the same one-rand cut is a huge percentage of the now-low price, while the extra units barely dent the already-large quantity: elasticity below one. Same slope everywhere, different percentages everywhere. So the exam-safe language is: call a curve relatively elastic or relatively inelastic when comparing curves, calculate when a number is asked for, and reserve "elastic at the top, inelastic at the bottom" for describing a single straight line.

**Bianca:** Last one, and it is the essay favourite: how do we structure the answer when a question asks whether a business should raise or cut its price?

**Tutor:** Use a four-step frame, and let elasticity referee the whole thing. Step one, classify the good's demand using determinants: name the escape routes. A township taxi route with no bus alternative — few substitutes, a necessity, short-run setting: inelastic. A chocolate bar surrounded by chips, biscuits and a rival shop: elastic. Step two, state the rulebook line that applies: inelastic demand means price and total revenue move in the same direction; elastic demand means they move in opposite directions. Step three, do the arithmetic if numbers are given, and show both revenue totals: twenty rand times a thousand riders is twenty thousand; twenty-four times nine hundred and fifty is twenty-two thousand eight hundred, so revenue rose. Numbers turn an assertion into a proof. Step four, add the caution that separates top answers: revenue is not profit — extra sales carry extra costs, and a full decision needs the cost side too; and if the question has a policy flavour, note the equity point that taxes on inelastic necessities burden the poor hardest, which is why the sin-tax logic targets cigarettes and alcohol rather than bread. Classify, state the rule, prove with numbers, then qualify. Four steps, and the same frame answers every pricing question this topic can generate.
