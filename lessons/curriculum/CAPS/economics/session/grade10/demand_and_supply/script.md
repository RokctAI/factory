Welcome, student. Today we learn the two forces that set every price you have ever paid: demand and supply. By the end of this lesson you will state both laws, draw the market diagram, shift the correct curve when conditions change, and find the new equilibrium. Our case study is painfully South African: what happens to the price of maize meal after a drought.

## Subtopic: The Law of Demand

Demand is the quantity of a good that buyers are willing and able to buy at each price. Mark both words: willing and able. Wanting a car is not demand; wanting it with money to pay for it is.

The law of demand says: the higher the price, the lower the quantity demanded; the lower the price, the higher the quantity demanded — other things remaining equal. Price up, quantity demanded down. It matches daily experience: when airtime is expensive, people buy less of it.

On a graph we put price on the vertical axis and quantity on the horizontal axis. Plotting the price-quantity pairs gives the demand curve, sloping downward from left to right. Label it D.

Now a distinction that decides marks in every exam: movement along the curve versus a shift of the whole curve. If the price of maize meal itself changes, we slide along the same demand curve to a new point — that is a change in quantity demanded. Nothing about the buyers changed; only the price did.

But if something other than the price changes — consumer income rises, the price of bread (a substitute) changes, tastes change, the population grows — then buyers behave differently at every price. The entire curve shifts: rightward for an increase in demand, leftward for a decrease. Keep this rule ready: own price moves you along the curve; anything else shifts it.

In our drought story, note carefully: consumer income is unchanged. The question tells us so. That is a signal that the demand curve will stay exactly where it is. Hold that thought.

## Subtopic: The Law of Supply

Now the other side of the market: the sellers. Supply is the quantity of a good that producers are willing and able to offer for sale at each price.

The law of supply says: the higher the price, the higher the quantity supplied; the lower the price, the lower the quantity supplied — other things equal. Price up, quantity supplied up. Why the same direction? Because for producers, price is the reward. A higher maize price makes it worth planting more hectares, hiring more workers, running the mill longer. The supply curve therefore slopes upward from left to right. Label it S.

The same distinction applies on this side. A change in the good's own price moves us along the supply curve — a change in quantity supplied. But when something other than price changes — the cost of fertiliser, technology, taxes and subsidies, the number of producers, and crucially natural conditions like weather — the whole supply curve shifts. Better conditions or lower costs shift supply rightward: more offered at every price. Worse conditions or higher costs shift supply leftward: less offered at every price.

Now our drought. The question says the drought halves the maize harvest. Ask the two exam questions. First: which side of the market is affected — buyers or sellers? The drought struck the farms, the producers. Supply. Second: did the good's own price cause this change? No — weather did. So we are shifting the whole supply curve, not sliding along it. And in which direction? At every possible price, farmers now have less maize to offer. The supply curve shifts leftward, from S to S1.

That is the complete diagnosis: demand curve untouched, supply curve shifted left. Half the marks in a market-dynamics question sit in exactly this reasoning.

## Subtopic: Equilibrium — Where the Market Settles

Put the two curves on one set of axes and they cross. That crossing point is the market equilibrium, and it is the most important point in microeconomics.

At the equilibrium price, the quantity buyers want to buy exactly equals the quantity sellers want to sell. The market clears — no maize left unsold, no buyers turned away. We label the equilibrium price P (or P e) and the equilibrium quantity Q on the axes.

Why does the market settle there and nowhere else? Test the two alternatives. Suppose the price sits above equilibrium. Sellers offer plenty — the high price attracts them — but buyers hold back. Quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded: a surplus. Unsold bags pile up, and sellers cut prices to move stock. The price falls toward equilibrium.

Now suppose the price sits below equilibrium. Buyers rush in, but sellers offer little. Quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied: a shortage. Empty shelves, queues — and buyers willing to pay more bid the price up. The price rises toward equilibrium.

So from above, price falls; from below, price rises. Equilibrium is the only resting place — like a marble rolling to the bottom of a bowl. No official sets this price; the pushing and pulling of surplus and shortage does the work. That is what economists mean by price formation through market forces.

## Subtopic: The Drought Diagram — Working the Full Example

Now we draw the full answer to our case study, step by step, exactly as you would in the exam.

Step one: axes. Price of maize meal on the vertical axis, quantity of maize meal on the horizontal axis. Label them.

Step two: the original market. Draw the downward-sloping demand curve D and the upward-sloping supply curve S. Mark their intersection: original equilibrium, price P and quantity Q, with dotted lines to both axes.

Step three: apply the shock. The drought halves the harvest — a supply-side event, not caused by the maize price. Draw a new supply curve S1 to the left of S. An arrow from S to S1 makes your reasoning visible to the marker.

Step four: consumer income is unchanged — the demand curve does not move. Write that down; stating why the other curve stays put earns reasoning marks.

Step five: read off the new equilibrium. S1 crosses D at a new point: the new equilibrium price P1 and quantity Q1. Dotted lines again. Compare: P1 is higher than P, and Q1 is smaller than Q.

Step six: tell the story in words, because the diagram alone is only half the answer. At the old price, the halved harvest leaves quantity demanded exceeding quantity supplied — a shortage. Buyers compete for scarce maize meal and bid the price up. As the price rises, two things happen at once: some buyers reduce purchases — a movement along the demand curve — and farmers stretch to supply what they can — a movement along the new supply curve S1. The price keeps rising until the shortage disappears at the new, higher equilibrium price P1, with less maize traded than before.

Verify with common sense, your final self-check: a drought making maize meal scarcer and more expensive matches exactly what South Africans see at the shops in drought years. When your diagram, your words and reality all agree, you have the full method. Practise it until the six steps run automatically.
