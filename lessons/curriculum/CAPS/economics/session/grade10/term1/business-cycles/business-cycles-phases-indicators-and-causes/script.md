# Part 1 — Expert

The circular flow can expand and contract — the last lessons showed why. Today we study the pattern those movements make over time: BUSINESS CYCLES, the recurring rise and fall of economic activity around its long-term path. The syllabus wants the phenomenon described in its four phases, the indicators that reveal it, the composition of the time series that displays it, the exogenous and endogenous explanations for it, and its effects. South Africa's cycles, dated officially by the Reserve Bank, give every part of this topic a local face.

## Subtopic: The Phenomenon — Four Phases

Plot a country's real production year by year and two shapes appear at once: a long upward TREND — the economy's growth path over decades — and a WAVE that rises above and sinks below that path, repeating irregularly. The wave is the business cycle, and its anatomy has four named phases.

The UPSWING, or expansion: production rising, firms hiring, incomes growing, spending feeding back into more production — the injections outrunning the leakages. The PEAK: the upper turning point — activity at its fullest; factories near capacity, jobs plentiful, but also prices pressing upward and shortages of skills appearing; the economy has, for now, nowhere higher to go. The DOWNSWING — contraction, and when deep and prolonged, recession: production falling, retrenchments, incomes and spending shrinking, the contracting spiral you met in the circular flow. And the TROUGH: the lower turning point — activity at its weakest; but also the point where the seeds of recovery lie: costs low, interest rates typically eased, replacement demand accumulating — and the cycle turns upward again.

Two disciplines. First, the cycle repeats but not on a timetable — phases vary in length and depth; "cycle" means recurring shape, not clockwork. Second, the formal definition of recession used in practice: two consecutive quarters of declining real GDP — a definition worth quoting exactly.

## Subtopic: Indicators — the Economy's Instruments

The cycle cannot be seen directly; it is read from INDICATORS — measurable series that move with activity — and the syllabus classifies them by their timing.

LEADING indicators move BEFORE the economy turns: they warn. New vehicle sales, building plans passed, new orders placed with factories, share prices, business confidence surveys — decisions made today about tomorrow's activity, so they bend first. COINCIDENT indicators move WITH the cycle: they confirm where we are now — total production itself, retail sales, employment in the current month. LAGGING indicators turn AFTER the economy has turned: they confirm history — unemployment (firms retrench late and rehire late), inventories relative to sales, outstanding loans. The order matters and examines well: leading warns, coincident confirms the present, lagging certifies the past.

In South Africa, the RESERVE BANK compiles composite leading, coincident and lagging indicators — bundles of many series combined — and officially DATES the country's cycle: it announces, after careful lag, when an upswing or downswing began and ended. South Africa's history shows long downswings and hard-won upswings tied to commodity prices, droughts, global conditions and domestic confidence — and the composite leading indicator is among the most watched numbers in the financial press, precisely because it bends before the economy does.

## Subtopic: Decomposing the Time Series

A plotted economic series — monthly production, sales, employment — mixes several movements at once, and the syllabus requires you to name and separate FOUR.

The TREND: the long-term direction over decades — for most economies, gently upward, driven by population, capital accumulation and technology. The CYCLICAL movement: the business cycle itself — multi-year waves around the trend, the subject of this topic. The SEASONAL movement: regular within-one-year patterns tied to the calendar — retail leaping every December, agriculture following harvests, tourism following holidays; so regular that statisticians publish "seasonally adjusted" figures with the pattern removed, precisely so the cycle underneath can be seen. And IRREGULAR (random) movements: one-off shocks with no pattern — a flood, a strike, a pandemic month — noise that decomposition sets aside.

Why decompose? Because raw figures mislead. December retail sales always jump — reading that jump as an upswing confuses season with cycle. A drought year's collapse in farm output is irregular, not necessarily cyclical. The analytical habit: before declaring "the economy is turning," ask WHICH movement you are looking at — trend, cycle, season, or shock. Exam questions hand you exactly such scenarios to classify, and the four names, cleanly applied, are the marks.

## Subtopic: Causes and Effects

Why do cycles happen? Explanations divide by where the push comes from.

EXOGENOUS causes originate OUTSIDE the economic system: weather and drought — devastating for an agricultural economy; wars and political shocks; pandemics; and for South Africa especially, swings in world commodity prices — when platinum and coal prices fall abroad, our mines, exports and incomes follow, through no domestic decision. These theories see the economy as calm water disturbed by outside stones.

ENDOGENOUS causes arise INSIDE the system's own workings: waves of business optimism and pessimism that feed on themselves — confident firms invest, hiring spreads confidence, until expectations overreach; credit expanding in good times — banks lend freely, spending swells — then tightening sharply when caution returns; and the simple mechanics you already own: injections and leakages chasing each other around the circular flow, over- and under-shooting equilibrium. Real cycles typically mix both: an outside shock lands on an inside amplifier.

The EFFECTS make the topic matter. Downswings: unemployment rising — with retrenchments falling hardest on the least skilled; incomes and profits shrinking; government tax revenue falling exactly when grant needs rise; poverty and inequality deepening; investment postponed, which weakens the NEXT upswing. Upswings: jobs, incomes and revenue recovering — but late in the boom, rising prices and interest rates. Knowing the phase is therefore practical intelligence: for a household deciding on debt, a firm timing expansion, a government budgeting — and for a matric learner reading the news like an economist.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same topic from the taxi rank — because everyone who has lived through a good year and a bad year in the same town has FELT a business cycle; today it gets its names.

## Subtopic: The Economy Breathes

Think of a year you remember when things were good: the rank buzzing, shops hiring, everyone's cousin finding work, month-end crowds thick. And a year when things were tight: quiet tills, short shifts, "we're not taking anyone now." Same town, same people — different YEARS. That difference, repeating over the years, is the business cycle: the economy breathing in and breathing out.

The breath has four moments, and you've lived them all. Breathing IN — the upswing: month by month, a little more work, a little more spending, and each feeds the other: more customers means more hiring means more customers. FULL LUNGS — the peak: everything running flat out; work easy to find, but prices creeping and everything strained — full is full. Breathing OUT — the downswing: orders slow, shifts shorten, retrenchments start, and each cut spends less at the next shop — the bad news passes itself along. EMPTY — the trough: the hardest stretch; but notice what quietly gathers there: things are cheap, borrowing gets easier, worn things need replacing — and one day the orders start again.

Two street truths to keep. The breath repeats but never on schedule — nobody can tell you the month the turn comes. And the official name for a serious breathing-out: two quarters in a row of shrinking production — that's a recession, and when the news says it, that's the definition they're using.

## Subtopic: Headlights, Speedometer, Rearview Mirror

How do you know which phase you're in — before it's obvious? The same way a taxi driver knows the road: three instruments, looking three directions.

HEADLIGHTS look ahead — the leading indicators. Building plans passed: someone's planning to build next year. New vehicle orders: someone's confident about tomorrow's business. Share prices, confidence surveys — all decisions about the FUTURE, made today, so they bend before the economy does. When the headlights dim — plans withdrawn, orders cancelled — the turn is coming, even while today still feels fine.

The SPEEDOMETER reads right now — the coincident indicators: this month's production, this month's retail sales, who's employed today. It confirms where you actually are.

The REARVIEW MIRROR shows where you've been — the lagging indicators. Unemployment is the classic: bosses retrench only after the bad months have piled up, and rehire only after the good ones have — so joblessness peaks AFTER the trough. When the mirror looks terrible, the worst may already be behind you.

And who watches all three for the country? The Reserve Bank — it bundles dozens of series into official leading, coincident and lagging indicators and, later, stamps the dates: the downswing began here, the upswing there. When the news says "the leading indicator declined for a third month" — you now know exactly what that sentence is: the country's headlights, dimming.

## Subtopic: Why the Wave Comes — and Who Gets Wet

Last piece: what pushes the breath — and who feels it hardest.

Some pushes come from OUTSIDE the economy's own machinery. Drought — no harvest, no farm wages, no spending in the farm towns. A war far away raising oil prices at our pumps. A pandemic closing the world. And South Africa's special weather: WORLD PRICES for what we dig — when platinum or coal prices fall overseas, mine towns feel it within months, and nobody local did anything wrong. Outside stones, thrown into our pond.

Other pushes grow INSIDE the machinery itself. Mood is a real economic force: when businesses feel confident they invest and hire, and the hiring spreads the confidence — until everyone has overbuilt and the mood snaps. Credit swells the same way: banks lend easily in good times, tighten hard in bad — pumping the wave higher, cutting the trough deeper. Most real waves are both at once: an outside stone landing in a pond already sloshing.

And who gets wet? Never everyone equally. The downswing reaches the shop floor before the boardroom: retrenchments start with the newest hired and least skilled; the township feels the quiet months before the suburbs do. Government gets squeezed from both sides at once — less tax coming in exactly as more families need help. And a hard trough steals from the future too: the machines not bought and skills not trained in the bad years weaken the next good ones.

So the cycle is not just a graph — it's the shape of ordinary lives over time, and reading it is self-defence: pay down debt near the peak, be patient near the trough, and never mistake a season's jump or a drought's dip for the tide itself.
