# Part 1 — Expert

The circular flow drew the economy's plumbing; today we attach the meter. If money circles endlessly between households, firms, government and the world, how large IS the circle? The syllabus's quantitative elements answer with two national measures — Gross Domestic Product and Gross National Income — and the skill is threefold: define each precisely, distinguish them sharply, and know what the big numbers can and cannot tell you. These measures anchor every macroeconomic discussion you will meet through matric and beyond.

## Subtopic: Measuring the Whole Economy

Why measure at all? Because every macro question — is the economy growing, can the state afford more clinics, are we better off than a decade ago — needs a NUMBER for "the economy," and opinions cannot settle what measurement can. The circular flow itself shows the three possible measuring points, and they must all agree: measure PRODUCTION — the value of everything firms make; measure INCOME — the wages, rent, interest and profit paid to households for the factors; or measure SPENDING — what is spent buying the output. Production creates income, income funds spending, spending buys production: three gauges on one pipe, and in principle one total.

The headline measure is GROSS DOMESTIC PRODUCT — GDP: the total market value of all FINAL goods and services produced WITHIN a country's borders in a given period, usually one year. Statistics South Africa compiles it; the news reports its growth rate quarterly; budgets, debt ratios and international comparisons all stand on it.

Each phrase in the definition works for its living, and the examiner tests each: market value — counted in prices, so unlike things (haircuts and coal) can be added; final goods and services — sold to the end user; within the borders — geography decides, not nationality; in a given period — a flow per year, not a stockpile. Learn the definition in full sentences; a definition missing "final" or "within the borders" is a definition missing marks.

## Subtopic: GDP's Fine Print — Final Goods and the Border

Two phrases in the definition carry traps; open both.

FINAL goods only — because counting everything sold would count the same value repeatedly. Follow bread: the farmer sells wheat to the miller for R4; the miller sells flour to the baker for R7; the baker sells the loaf to you for R18. Add all three sales and you get R29 — but only R18 of value exists; the wheat and flour are INTERMEDIATE goods, already inside the loaf's price. GDP counts the final R18 once — or, equivalently, adds each stage's VALUE ADDED: the farmer's 4, the miller's 3, the baker's 11 — R18 again. Double counting is the error; "final goods only" is the guard.

WITHIN THE BORDERS — geography, strictly. The output of a foreign-owned mine on South African soil counts in SOUTH AFRICAN GDP: produced here, counted here, whoever owns it. A South African engineer working in Dubai produces there — her output is Dubai's GDP, not ours. The border rule makes GDP a measure of the domestic production MACHINE: what this territory's farms, mines, factories and offices generated, regardless of whose passport the owners hold.

Also excluded from GDP, and asked often: second-hand sales (the car was counted when new; reselling it creates no new production — though the dealer's SERVICE fee is new and counts); pure financial transfers (buying shares moves ownership, produces nothing); social grants (transfers, not payments for production); and most unpaid and informal work — the gogo's childcare for the family, subsistence vegetables — real value, invisible to the tills the statisticians read. Hold that last exclusion; it returns as a limitation.

Stop for this section's questions now — the bread chain without double counting, the border rule applied both directions, and the excluded list with reasons.

## Subtopic: GNI — Following the Factors Home

The second measure changes one thing only: the rule of belonging. GROSS NATIONAL INCOME — GNI — measures the total income earned by a country's PERMANENT RESIDENTS' factors of production in a period, WHEREVER in the world that production happened. GDP asks WHERE was it produced; GNI asks WHO earned the income. Geography against ownership — that single contrast is the exam's favourite.

The bridge between them is factor income crossing the border. Start with GDP; SUBTRACT the factor incomes produced here but paid to foreign owners — the dividends of the foreign-owned mine sailing to shareholders abroad, the foreign engineer's salary remitted home; ADD the incomes South African factors earn abroad — profits of South African companies' operations elsewhere, wages our citizens earn overseas and send back. GDP minus factor payments to the rest of the world, plus factor income received from it: GNI.

The two numbers tell one country's story by their GAP. In South Africa, with substantial foreign ownership in mining and industry, more factor income LEAVES than arrives — so our GNI runs somewhat below our GDP: the machine on our soil produces more than our residents get to keep. A country like Lesotho, with many citizens working abroad and remitting wages, can show GNI ABOVE GDP: its people earn more than its territory produces. Neither gap is shameful; each describes a structure — but reading the gap is exactly the comprehension examiners reward.

## Subtopic: Per Capita, Comparisons and the Limits of the Number

Totals mislead when populations differ; hence PER CAPITA measures: GDP divided by population — output per person. A large economy with a huge population can be poorer per person than a small rich one; per capita is the first correction for any welfare comparison. The second correction is PRICES: comparing years needs the inflation effect stripped out — real versus nominal, formalised in Grade 11; for now, know that a bigger number is not better if prices simply rose.

Then the honest limits, because the syllabus asks what GDP does NOT measure. Distribution: GDP per capita is an average — one billionaire and ninety-nine unemployed average out respectably; the number is silent on who holds the income. Unpaid and informal work: the caregiving, home-growing, and much township trade that never crosses a formal till — real production, uncounted, which understates economies like ours especially. Well-being: GDP counts production, not health, safety, leisure or education quality — a country can grow while its people's lives worsen. And the environment: GDP counts the timber sold but not the forest lost; disasters can even RAISE GDP through rebuilding. The measure is a speedometer, not a report card: essential for what it reads, silent on what it doesn't.

Exam technique: definitions verbatim with every working phrase; the GDP-GNI contrast in one sentence — where produced versus who earned; the bridge direction correct — subtract what leaves, add what arrives; and limitations as a listed, explained set. This topic is a definitions-and-distinctions bank — precision collects it.

The final questions of this part are with you now: per capita corrections, the four limits with examples, and the definitions at full precision.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same two measures from the stoep — because counting a country turns out to be the same job as counting a household's year, with the same two rules and the same blind spots.

## Subtopic: Counting Everything the Street Made

Imagine your street decides to count everything it produced this year — one big number for the whole street's work. The tills give it to you: the spaza's sales, the salon's braids, the mechanic's repairs, the gogo's vetkoek — everything MADE and SOLD on the street, added up in rands. That's the street's GDP: the value of what this place produced.

But count carefully, because one mistake doubles everything. The mechanic buys parts for R300 and fixes a car for R900. Count the parts AND the repair and you've counted the parts twice — they're inside the R900. So the rule: count only what reaches the FINAL buyer, or count each person's ADDED bit — the mechanic added R600 of skill to R300 of parts. Same total, no doubling. And some money moving isn't making: uncle selling his old bakkie to the neighbour — the bakkie was counted the year it was built; today it just changed hands. Granny's grant arriving — support, not production. Money can move without anything new being made, and the count wants only the NEW.

One more rule before the number is honest: the street counts what was made ON the street — even the shop owned by the man from another town. His shop stands here, employs here, produces here: it counts here. That's the border rule, street-sized.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the street count are coming to you right now: what counts, what doubles, what merely moves.

## Subtopic: The Where-Rule and the Who-Rule

Now the two national measures, as two different questions about the same street.

Question one — the WHERE-rule: what was produced ON this street? That's GDP. The outsider's shop counts, because it stands here. The street's daughter working in Sandton doesn't count — her work happens there. GDP is about the PLACE: this soil, these premises, this year. It measures the street as a MACHINE — how much this place, as a place, can produce.

Question two — the WHO-rule: what did the street's PEOPLE earn, wherever they earned it? That's GNI. Now the daughter in Sandton counts — she's ours, and her salary comes home. And the outsider's shop partly UNcounts — the shop's profit leaves with him to his own town. GNI is about the PEOPLE: our residents' earnings, wherever the work stood.

And the gap between the two numbers tells the street's story. If outsiders own the street's biggest businesses, the place produces plenty (big GDP) but the profits ride out at closing time (smaller GNI) — the machine is here, the keeping is elsewhere. That's roughly South Africa's shape: foreign-owned mines and firms mean some of what our soil produces is kept abroad. Another street sends its sons and daughters out to work and their wages come home — small production on the street, big earnings by its people: GNI above GDP. That's Lesotho's shape. Two rules, two numbers — and the GAP between them is the real story.

Your questions for this part are up now: where-rule or who-rule, case by case — and read the gap for each street.

## Subtopic: What the Big Number Can't See

Last piece — and it keeps you honest whenever a politician waves a GDP figure: know what the number is blind to.

Blind spot one: WHO got it. The street's count says R2 million produced — but if one house took R1,8 million of it, the average tells you nothing about the other houses. GDP is a total, not a sharing; averages hide the split.

Blind spot two: the work with no till. The grandmother minding four children every day so their parents can work — enormous value, no receipt, uncounted. The vegetables grown and eaten at home; the neighbour fixing the neighbour's roof for a plate of food. Streets like ours run on uncounted work — so the official number always UNDERSELLS what places like this actually produce and do.

Blind spot three: how life FEELS. The count rises when more is sold — it cannot see whether the clinic queue got shorter, whether the street got safer, whether children learn. A street can produce more and live worse.

Blind spot four: what got used up. Sell the big tree for timber and the count goes UP — the tree's absence appears nowhere. Production that eats tomorrow looks identical, in the number, to production that doesn't.

So hold both truths like an economist: the number matters — growth funds wages, taxes, clinics; a shrinking count is real pain. AND the number is a speedometer, not a report card — it tells you how fast, never how well, and never for whom. Reading it with both truths is what this topic was for.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: the four blind spots, each with a street example — and one balanced sentence on why the number still matters. Count wisely; question the count. That's quantitative economics, begun.
