**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! First: you said production, income and spending are three gauges that should read the same total. But surely people don't spend everything they earn — so how can the spending gauge ever match?

**Tutor:** Beautifully spotted — and the answer is that the accountants define spending more widely than a household does. Your instinct is right that wage-earners save part of their income; but in the national accounts, that saved money finances investment spending — firms buying machinery and buildings — which sits inside the spending gauge too. Unsold stock is handled the same way: goods produced but still on the shelf at year-end are counted as inventory investment, as though the firm bought its own output. Add exports, and every rand of production finds a matching category of expenditure — consumption, investment, government spending, or net exports. So the three gauges agree by construction: production creates exactly that much income, and every use of that income — even 'not spending it' — lands in some expenditure bin. In practice the three measurements differ slightly because data is imperfect, and statisticians publish a small discrepancy item openly. The deep point survives: production, income and spending are the same circle read at three points, not three separate quantities.

***

**Thandi:** Next: the border rule feels unfair to me. The Australian mine digs OUR minerals, but the dividends leave — and we still get to brag about the GDP? Whose number is it really?

**Tutor:** You have just discovered, on your own, exactly why TWO measures exist — hold onto that indignation, because it is analytically correct. GDP deliberately ignores your question: it is a reading of the production machine on this territory — output, jobs, activity — and for some purposes that is the right lens: the mine employs South Africans, buys South African electricity and services, and pays South African taxes, all real benefits that happen here. But your instinct that "some of that isn't really ours" is precisely what GNI captures: subtract the dividends sailing to Australian shareholders, add what South African firms and workers earn abroad, and you get the income our residents actually keep. That is why the GDP-GNI gap is the story, not either number alone: a country like ours, with deep foreign ownership, produces more than it keeps — and a policy debate about foreign investment is really a debate about that gap: foreign capital builds machines and jobs here today, at the price of factor income flowing out for years. Neither bragging nor despairing is analysis; reading both numbers together is.

***

**Thandi:** Third: if the informal sector and gran's childcare never get counted, isn't our GDP just wrong? Why does anyone respect a number with holes that big?

**Tutor:** Distinguish "wrong" from "incomplete with known edges" — the difference matters. Statisticians are not naive about the holes: Statistics South Africa runs surveys designed to estimate informal trading and includes an imputation for it, so the formal tills are not the whole story even in the official number. What genuinely stays outside is unpaid household and care work — gran's childcare — excluded not by oversight but by a definitional line: GDP counts production exchanged in markets, because market prices are what let unlike things be added at all. Put a rand value on every hour of care and you must invent prices no transaction ever set — a defensible project, and some countries publish exactly such satellite accounts alongside GDP, but a different measure than the market one. The respectable position is the lesson's two-truths one: the number is reliable for what it measures — market production, comparable across years and countries — and systematically silent on what it excludes, which in an economy like ours is a large, mostly female, workforce of unpaid producers. Use the number; footnote the holes; never let a speaker who quotes GDP pretend the footnote doesn't exist.

***

**Thandi:** Last one: politicians always celebrate when GDP grows. After today I'm suspicious — should we cheer growth or not?

**Tutor:** Cheer with your eyes open — the trained answer is conditional, and you now own every condition. First ask: real or nominal? If prices rose five percent and the number rose five percent, nothing grew but the price tags. Then: faster than the population? Two percent growth with three percent more people means each person, on average, got poorer. Then: who received it? Growth that pools in one suburb changes no lives on your street — distribution decides whether growth is felt. And: at what cost to the stock we draw on tomorrow — was this the kind of growth that builds capacity, or the kind that sells the jacaranda? When growth passes those tests — real, per-capita positive, broadly shared, not eating the future — then yes, cheer honestly: that growth is new clinics, new jobs and new tax revenue, and its absence is genuine hardship, as any downswing year proves. The skill this topic gives you is not cynicism about the number; it is the four-question habit that turns a headline into an assessment. Politicians quote the speedometer. You now know to ask where the car is going.
