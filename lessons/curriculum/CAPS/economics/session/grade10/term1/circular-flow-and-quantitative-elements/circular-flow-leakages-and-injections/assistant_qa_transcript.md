**Student:** I don't get why saving is a "leakage". Saving is supposed to be responsible! Why does the economy punish it?

**Thandi:** Nobody's punishing anyone — "leakage" sounds negative, but it's just a plumbing word! It only means the money stepped out of the spending stream for a moment: you earned it, but firms didn't get it back as sales. And here's the redemption arc: your R500 doesn't nap in the vault. The bank lends it to a firm that buys equipment, and boom — it re-enters the flow as investment. Saving is actually how the economy funds its future machines. Leakage today, injection tomorrow, through the same door.

***

**Student:** How do I remember which things are leakages and which are injections? I keep swapping exports and imports.

**Thandi:** Use the money's passport, not the goods! Ask one question: which way does the *money* move? Imports: you buy a phone from China, so South African money flies overseas — money leaves, leakage. Exports: Britain buys our wine, so British money lands in a South African firm's account — money arrives, injection. Learners swap them because they follow the goods (wine leaving looks like a "leak"). Ignore the goods. Follow the money. Money out = leakage, money in = injection, every single time.

***

**Student:** Is the government a leakage or an injection? It seems to be both and that confuses me.

**Thandi:** It's both — because government is a *door*, not a one-way arrow. Taxes flow in through that door: that's the leakage, money households and firms can no longer spend. Government spending flows back out: teachers' salaries, clinics, roads, grants — that's the injection. Same with the bank door (savings/investment) and the border door (imports/exports). Every door has traffic both ways. So never ask "is government a leakage?" Ask "which direction is this specific flow going through the government door?"

***

**Student:** What's the actual difference between a closed economy and an open economy? Just the foreign sector?

**Thandi:** Exactly that — and it matters more than it sounds. A closed economy trades with nobody outside its borders: only households, firms and maybe government. Add the foreign sector, and the economy is "open" — now imports can leak money out and exports can inject money in. Real South Africa is very open: think how much of what we use is imported, and how much gold, fruit and cars we sell abroad. That's why exam questions love the open version — it's the one that matches reality, with all three leakages and all three injections in play.
