**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! First up — the one everyone whispers: saving is supposed to be responsible, so why does the model call it a "leakage"? It sounds like the economy is punishing good behaviour.

**Tutor:** Nobody is punished — leakage is a plumbing word, not a judgement. It records one fact: the R500 saved stepped out of the spending stream, so firms did not receive it as sales this round. But follow the money one step further and the story completes itself: the bank lends those savings to a firm buying equipment, and the same rands re-enter the flow as investment — through the very same door they left by. Saving is how the economy funds tomorrow's machines; the model simply tracks its exit and its return separately. Direction of flow, never virtue — an economy that saved nothing would have nothing to invest.

***

**Thandi:** Next: imports and exports keep swapping in people's heads. Wine LEAVES the country and that's an injection? It feels backwards.

**Tutor:** It feels backwards because you are following the goods — follow the MONEY instead, and the classification never fails. The circular flow is a map of money, so ask one question of any transaction: which way does the money move across the border? Imports: a phone arrives from China, but South African money flies out to pay for it — money leaves, leakage. Exports: our wine sails to Britain, and British money lands in a South African firm's account — money arrives, injection. The goods and the money always travel in opposite directions; learners who track the crates get it inverted every time. Give the money a passport and stamp it: out, leakage; in, injection.

***

**Thandi:** This one's subtle: the model says leakages and injections decide whether the economy grows or shrinks. But how does MY family's saving decision reach the whole country's production?

**Tutor:** Through repetition and addition — your household is one of millions making the same kind of choice, and the circle adds them all. Trace one round: if households collectively hold back more spending this month, the shops' tills take in less; shops facing quieter tills order less from producers; producers facing smaller orders cut shifts; cut shifts mean smaller wage packets — which arrive at NEXT month's tills already lighter. Each round passes the reduction along, slightly compounded — that is the contracting spiral, and it is why economists watch total leakages against total injections so anxiously. The reverse spiral is just as real: a new export contract or a government building programme pours money in, tills ring, orders rise, shifts lengthen. No single family moves the economy — but the SUM of families is the economy; the circular flow is precisely the machine that adds you all up.

***

**Thandi:** Last one: in the exam, what actually earns the marks on this topic — the drawing or the explanation?

**Tutor:** Both, and each has its own discipline. The drawing earns marks structurally: four participants named and boxed; the two markets placed between households and firms; the financial sector, government and foreign sector connected with arrows in BOTH directions; and every arrow labelled — an unlabelled arrow is a lost mark, because the label is the economics. Practise until you can build it in layers from memory: two participants, then the bank, then government, then the border. The explanation earns marks through the master lists and the comparison: three leakages and three injections named in full, and the equilibrium statement — leakages greater, contraction; injections greater, expansion; equal, equilibrium. And the classification questions — a scenario to sort as leakage or injection — are decided by the money's direction, as we said. One drawing, two lists, one comparison, one sorting rule: that is the entire topic's mark sheet, and every piece of it is now yours.
