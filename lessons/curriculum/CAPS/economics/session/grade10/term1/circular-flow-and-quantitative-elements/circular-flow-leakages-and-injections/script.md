# Part 1 — Expert

Today we draw the single most important diagram in all of Economics: the circular flow. By the end of this session you will build the flow for an open economy with four participants — households, firms, government and the foreign sector — and you will classify savings, taxes and imports as leakages, and investment, government spending and exports as injections. The diagram is the examination's favourite model; build it in layers and it can never overwhelm you.

## Subtopic: The Two Original Participants — Households and Firms

Every economy, no matter how complicated, begins with two participants: households and firms.

Households are the owners of the factors of production. Remember them from earlier work: natural resources, labour, capital and entrepreneurship. Every factor ultimately belongs to someone sitting in a household — your family supplies labour when someone goes to work; a family that owns land supplies natural resources; savings that fund machines are capital; the aunt who runs her own business supplies entrepreneurship.

Firms are the production units. They take those factors and combine them to produce the goods and services we consume.

Now watch the circle form. Households supply factors of production to firms through the factor market. In return, firms pay factor incomes: wages for labour, rent for natural resources, interest for capital, and profit for entrepreneurship. That is the first loop: factors flow one way, income flows back the other way.

But households do not eat wages — they spend them. Households use their income to buy goods and services from firms through the goods market. That spending becomes the firms' revenue, which firms use to pay for factors again... and the circle turns.

Notice there are two kinds of flow travelling in opposite directions. Real flows are the physical things: factors of production moving to firms, goods and services moving to households. Money flows are the payments travelling the opposite way: income to households, spending to firms. Every real flow has a money flow running against it. This two-participant model is called a simple closed economy, and if you can draw it with arrows labelled correctly, you have the skeleton for everything that follows.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: the two participants, the two markets, and real flows against money flows. Answer with the arrows in mind.

## Subtopic: Leakages and Injections — Why the Circle Shrinks and Grows

If households spent every cent they earned, the circle would spin at a constant size forever. But they do not — and here the model becomes realistic.

Suppose households save part of their income at the bank. That money leaves the spending stream — it is income earned but not passed back to firms. We call any such escape a leakage, or a withdrawal. Savings is our first leakage.

Does saved money vanish? No — and this is the beautiful part. Banks and other financial institutions lend those savings to firms, which use them to buy machinery, buildings and equipment. That spending on capital goods is called investment, and it pumps money back into the flow. Any such re-entry is called an injection. Savings leak out; investment injects back in — connected through the financial sector.

Why do leakages and injections matter so much? Because they decide whether the whole economy grows or shrinks. If total leakages are greater than total injections, less money returns to firms each round: production falls, incomes fall, the circle contracts. If injections exceed leakages, the circle expands — more spending, more production, more income. When they are equal, the economy is in equilibrium. Hold onto that comparison; it returns in Grade 11 and Grade 12 macroeconomics again and again.

A common pitfall: learners think a leakage is "bad" and an injection is "good". Not so — savings finance tomorrow's investment. Leakage and injection describe direction of flow, not virtue.

Stop for this section's questions now — savings, investment, and the equilibrium comparison: classify each flow by its direction, never by its virtue.

## Subtopic: Adding Government — Taxes In, Spending Out

Now bring in the third participant: the government. The government interacts with both households and firms, and it brings one leakage and one injection of its own.

First, the leakage: taxes. Households pay personal income tax; firms pay company tax. That money is taken out of the spending stream — households and firms cannot spend what SARS has collected. Taxes are therefore a leakage, exactly like savings.

But government does not sit on the money. It flows back through government spending: paying teachers and nurses, building roads and schools, buying goods and services from firms, and paying social grants to households. Government spending is an injection — money pumped back into the circle.

Government also supplies public goods and services — things like policing, street lighting and public healthcare — to both households and firms. So on your diagram, the government sits between households and firms with arrows both ways: taxes flowing in from both, spending and services flowing out to both.

Pause and take stock. We now have two leakages — savings and taxes — and two injections — investment and government spending. An economy with households, firms and government, but no dealings with other countries, is still called a closed economy. One participant remains to open it up.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — the government's arrows, both directions, and the running tally of leakages and injections so far.

## Subtopic: The Foreign Sector — Opening the Economy

The fourth participant is the foreign sector — all the countries South Africa trades with. Adding it turns our closed economy into an open economy, which is what South Africa really is.

Think about what happens when South Africans buy imported goods — cellphones from China, cars from Germany. Money earned inside South Africa is paid to firms outside South Africa. That spending leaks out of our circular flow. Imports are a leakage.

Now reverse it. When other countries buy South African goods — our gold, our fruit, our wine — foreign money flows into South African firms. Exports are an injection.

Let us now assemble the complete picture, and this is the exam skill: the full open-economy circular flow has four participants — households, firms, government, and the foreign sector — connected through three markets: the factor market, the goods market, and the financial sector standing between savers and investors.

And the master lists you must be able to produce instantly. The three leakages: savings, taxes, imports. The three injections: investment, government spending, exports. Here is a check to see if you truly understand rather than memorise: every leakage has a matching injection through the same channel. Savings leak out through the financial sector; investment returns through it. Taxes leak out through government; government spending returns. Import payments leave through the foreign sector; export earnings return. Three doors, each with traffic in both directions.

Verify your classification skill with this quick test. A household buys a Japanese television — leakage, an import. The government builds a new clinic — injection, government spending. A firm ships wine to Britain — injection, an export. A family puts R500 into a savings account — leakage, savings. If you got all four, you are ready — for this part's questions, which are with you now: the complete open-economy model, the master lists, and the three doors with traffic both ways.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same diagram from the street — because the circular flow isn't really a diagram at all; it's your own neighbourhood on a payday Friday, drawn with arrows.

## Subtopic: Friday in the Neighbourhood

Forget the textbook for a minute and watch a payday Friday. Someone in your house worked all month at a firm — hands and hours supplied. Friday, the wage lands. By Saturday, that wage is at the spaza, the taxi rank, the clothing shop — and those shops use the takings to pay THEIR workers, who spend at other shops, whose takings pay other wages... Do you see it? The money never stops; it CIRCLES. Work flows one way, money flows back, spending sends it round again.

That circle is the whole model. Households — that's every family — supply the working hands, the rented rooms, the saved money, the small businesses. Firms — every shop, workshop, factory, and yes, the gogo's vetkoek table — combine those into things to sell. Two meeting places connect them: the place where work is hired (the factor market — think of it as the hiring side of life) and the place where things are bought (the goods market — the till side of life).

And notice the two-lane traffic on every road: the REAL lane carries actual things — labour going to work, bread coming home; the MONEY lane runs the opposite way — wages coming home, spending going out. Every real thing has money travelling against it. Draw Friday with arrows and you have drawn the simple circular flow — two participants, two markets, two lanes.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on Friday's circle are coming to you right now: who supplies what, which market is which, and which lane each arrow drives in.

## Subtopic: Three Drains and Three Taps

Now picture the circling money as water in a round channel — and notice the channel isn't sealed. It has three drains, and three taps.

Drain one: the BANK. The R500 auntie saves this month steps out of the spending stream — the shops never see it. But look above the channel: the bank is also tap one, because that same R500 gets lent to a business buying a new oven — and back in it flows, as investment. Drain two: SARS. The tax taken from the wage never reaches the spaza — out it drains. But the government is tap two: teachers' salaries, the new clinic, the road crew — government spending pours back in. Drain three: the BORDER. Buy a phone made in China and your money leaves the country's channel entirely — imports drain it. But when Britain buys our wine, British money pours IN — exports are tap three.

Three drains: savings, taxes, imports. Three taps: investment, government spending, exports. And here's the trick that beats all memorising: each drain and its tap share ONE door. Bank door: savings out, investment in. Government door: taxes out, spending in. Border door: imports out, exports in. Learn three doors, not six facts.

One warning from the lesson worth repeating in street language: drains aren't villains. The money auntie saves is exactly what funds the oven the bakery buys. Drains and taps describe which WAY the water moves — never whether it's good.

Your questions for this part are up now: name the drain or the tap for each story — and the door it uses.

## Subtopic: Reading the Water Level

Last picture: why anyone bothers with this diagram at all. Watch the water LEVEL.

If the three drains together pull out more than the three taps pour in, the channel's level drops: less spending reaches the shops, so shops order less, so firms produce less, so wages shrink — and next month even less circles. That's an economy contracting, and you can feel it on the street: quiet tills, shorter shifts. If the taps together beat the drains, the level rises: more spending, more production, more hiring — the good spiral. And when drains equal taps, the level holds steady — economists call it equilibrium; the street calls it "things are stable."

That's why the news talks endlessly about savings rates, government budgets, imports and exports — they're reading the drains and taps, trying to guess next month's water level. When you hear "government announces infrastructure spending" — that's a tap opening wider. "Consumers under pressure, saving more" — a drain pulling harder. You can now read the same news like an economist: every headline is a drain or a tap.

And the classification game the exam plays is exactly the game you've just learned: a family buys a German car — border drain, import. A mine sells platinum abroad — border tap, export. SARS collects; the clinic gets built — government door, both directions. R500 into the stokvel's bank account — bank drain; the loan it funds — bank tap.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: read the level — drains against taps — and classify each headline through its door. Get the doors right, and you own the most important diagram in the subject.
