# Part 1 — Expert

Welcome to the discipline itself. Before supply curves and business cycles, Economics must be defined: what it studies, how it is organised into branches, what methods it uses, and where it stands among the sciences. Examiners open Paper 1 with exactly these foundations, and candidates who can define precisely — in the syllabus's own terms — collect the opening marks that set a paper's tone. Today: the elements of economics, its branches, its methods, and its scientific character.

## Subtopic: The Elements — What Economics Is Made Of

Economics is the social science that studies how people, businesses and governments use LIMITED resources to satisfy UNLIMITED needs and wants. Every word of that definition earns its place, so take it apart.

The ELEMENTS of economic activity are the building blocks the subject watches. NEEDS: the essentials of survival and dignity — food, water, shelter, clothing, healthcare. WANTS: the desires beyond necessity — a newer phone, a bigger house — and here is the engine of the whole subject: wants are unlimited, renewing themselves as fast as they are satisfied. RESOURCES — the factors of production that satisfy them: natural resources (land and what it yields), labour (human effort, physical and mental), capital (man-made means of production — machines, buildings, tools), and entrepreneurship (the organiser who combines the other three and carries the risk). Each factor earns its own reward: rent for natural resources, wages for labour, interest for capital, profit for entrepreneurship — a pairing the examination tests directly.

And the three PROCESSES that connect resources to satisfaction: PRODUCTION — combining factors to create goods and services; EXCHANGE — trading what is produced, through markets and money; CONSUMPTION — using goods and services to satisfy the needs and wants, which is the end purpose of the whole chain. Production, exchange, consumption: the syllabus calls these the basic processes, and every economy on earth, however simple or advanced, runs all three.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: elements defined, factors paired with rewards, and the three processes in order.

## Subtopic: The Branches — How the Subject Divides

Economics organises itself into branches, and the first division is the one every later grade leans on.

MICROECONOMICS studies the individual units: one household deciding what to buy, one firm setting a price, one market finding equilibrium. Its questions are close-up: how does the price of maize form? Why does a firm hire another worker? MACROECONOMICS studies the economy as a WHOLE: total production, general unemployment, inflation across all prices, national income. Its questions are aerial: is the economy growing? Why is unemployment rising? The classic test: one shop's price is micro; the general price level is macro. Term two's market dynamics live in micro; term one's circular flow and GDP live in macro — the syllabus itself alternates between the two lenses.

Beyond the great division, the syllabus names working branches. ECONOMIC THEORY builds the explanations — the laws and models. APPLIED ECONOMICS takes the theory to real problems: transport economics, agricultural economics, labour economics. ECONOMIC HISTORY studies how economies developed — term three's journey from barter to industry. DEVELOPMENT ECONOMICS focuses on improving poor economies' welfare — deeply relevant to South Africa. And NORMATIVE versus POSITIVE economics splits the subject's sentences: positive statements describe what IS and can be tested — "unemployment is 32 percent"; normative statements judge what OUGHT to be — "government should create jobs." One is measurable; the other is opinion, however informed. Exams love asking you to classify statements across that line.

Stop for this section's questions now — micro against macro, the working branches matched to their questions, and positive sorted from normative.

## Subtopic: The Methods — How Economists Work

Economics calls itself a science because of its METHOD, not its subject matter. The method is the familiar scientific loop, adapted to society.

Step one: OBSERVATION — economists watch the real economy: prices, employment, spending, production, gathered as data. Step two: HYPOTHESIS — a proposed explanation: "when the price of a good rises, the quantity demanded falls." Step three: TESTING — the hypothesis meets the data; in economics the laboratory is history and statistics, since one cannot rerun a country under controlled conditions. Step four: LAW or THEORY — explanations that survive testing repeatedly earn the name of law — the law of demand — always with the guard clause CETERIS PARIBUS: "all other things being equal," because in a society everything moves at once, and a law isolates one relationship by holding the rest still in thought.

The tools of the method: DATA — collected by Statistics South Africa, the Reserve Bank, international bodies; INDICES — single numbers summarising many, like the consumer price index; MODELS — deliberate simplifications, like the circular flow diagram waiting in the next topic: false in detail, true in structure, useful precisely because reality is too complicated to think about raw. GRAPHS — the subject's favourite language, where relationships become pictures.

And the honest limits: economics studies PEOPLE, who learn, adapt and react to predictions themselves — so economic laws are tendencies, not certainties; forecasts carry error; and disagreement among economists is normal science, not failure. State the limits when asked — examiners reward the candidate who knows what the method can and cannot promise.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — the loop in order, the tools named, ceteris paribus explained, and the limits stated honestly.

## Subtopic: Economics Among the Sciences

Where does Economics stand in the family of knowledge? The syllabus wants the classification argued, not just asserted.

Economics is a SOCIAL science: its subject is human behaviour in society — choices, trades, institutions. It shares that family with sociology, psychology and political science, and it shares their fundamental difficulty: the subject matter has free will. But within the social family, economics is the most QUANTITATIVE member: it measures relentlessly — prices, quantities, rates, indices — and builds mathematical models, which gives it a family resemblance to the natural sciences: hypotheses, data, testing, laws.

Contrast it with a NATURAL science like physics or chemistry: their laboratories control conditions and repeat experiments; their laws hold without exception — water boils at the same temperature every time the conditions repeat. Economics can rarely experiment on whole economies, cannot hold society still, and its regularities are tendencies. Hence the honest classification: a social science that USES scientific method — empirical, quantitative, model-building — while studying subject matter that will never sit still like a molecule.

And why it MATTERS that economics sits among the sciences at all: because its claims are tested against evidence, not taste. When a minister claims a policy will create jobs, economics asks for the data, the mechanism, the comparison. That evidentiary habit is what the subject offers every citizen — the equipment to test economic claims rather than merely believe them. Grade 10 economics is, among other things, training in not being fooled.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the classification argued both ways — what economics shares with the natural sciences, what it can never share, and why the difference is a strength to state, not a weakness to hide.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same foundations from the street — because you have been doing economics since you first held pocket money; today just names what you already practise.

## Subtopic: The Science of Not Enough

Here is the whole subject in one sentence: everybody wants more than there is — now what?

Start with yourself on a Saturday with R50. The WANTS line up instantly: airtime, chips, that cap, taxi money to a friend. The R50 cannot cover them all — and notice, when one want is satisfied, another steps forward. That endless queue is what economists mean by unlimited wants. The R50 — and behind it, the world's land, workers, machines and organisers — is what they mean by limited resources. Economics is simply the study of that gap: not enough, for everyone, forever — and all the clever arrangements people invent to live with it.

Every economy — your pocket, the spaza, South Africa — runs the same three-step engine on that gap. Somebody MAKES things: production — the gogo baking vetkoek combines her flour (resources), her hands (labour), her stove (capital), and her decision to bake at all (entrepreneurship — she took the risk). People SWAP things: exchange — vetkoek for money at the taxi rank. And people USE things: consumption — someone eats the vetkoek, and a want dies happy... until lunchtime, when it's reborn. Make, swap, use. Production, exchange, consumption. The fancy names describe your street perfectly.

And each helper in the making gets its own kind of pay — remember it as four rewards for four contributions: the land is rented, the hands are waged, the stove-money earns interest, and the risk-taker keeps the profit.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the not-enough problem are coming to you right now: wants against resources, the three-step engine, and who earns which reward.

## Subtopic: Close-Up Lens, Drone Shot

Economics looks at the world through two lenses, and once you see them, you'll never confuse them again.

The CLOSE-UP lens: one shop, one buyer, one price. Why did the spaza raise the price of bread by a rand? Why do people buy more airtime at month-end? That's microeconomics — the economics of somebody, of one market you could stand inside.

The DRONE SHOT: pull the camera up until the whole country fits in frame. Not one shop's price but ALL prices rising together — inflation. Not one person job-hunting but millions — unemployment. Not one bakery's output but everything the nation produced this year — that's macroeconomics, the economics of everybody at once.

The test that always works: could you point at it on your street? One taxi fare — micro. The cost of living — macro. One firm hiring — micro. The unemployment rate — macro.

And one more sorting trick the exam pays for: some sentences MEASURE and some sentences WISH. "Petrol went up by ninety cents" — measurable, checkable, positive economics. "Petrol is too expensive" — that's a judgement; reasonable people can differ; normative economics. Neither is wrong — but confusing them is how arguments go bad: one person quoting measurements, the other expressing wishes, both thinking they disagree about facts. Economics trains you to label the sentence before fighting about it.

Your questions for this part are up now: lens by lens and sentence by sentence — close-up or drone, measurement or wish.

## Subtopic: Testing Like a Scientist, Watching People Like a Neighbour

Last piece: what makes this a SCIENCE — and what kind of science it can honestly be.

The method is one you already use. You notice the taxi rank is quiet on rainy mornings — observation. You guess: people stay home when it rains — hypothesis. Next three rainy days, you watch — testing. It holds — you've got yourself a small law of rank life. That loop — notice, guess, test, keep what survives — is the entire scientific method, and economists run it on prices, jobs and spending instead of taxis and rain.

But economists add a magic phrase you should learn to love: CETERIS PARIBUS — "if nothing else changes." Why? Because in real life everything changes at once. Prices rise AND payday arrives AND a new shop opens — so which caused what? The phrase lets a law isolate one thread: IF only the price moves, people buy less. It's not cheating; it's how you think clearly about a tangled world — one thread at a time.

And the honest difference from physics: water never DECIDES to boil differently because it heard the forecast. People do exactly that — save because they fear job losses, buy because they expect price rises. Economics studies subjects who talk back. So its laws are strong tendencies, not certainties; its predictions carry humility; and when two economists disagree on the news, that's the sound of a young science working on the hardest material there is: us.

That's your subject: the science of not-enough, run on people. Everything this year — markets, cycles, flows, wages — is that one problem, studied with that one method.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: run the loop on a street observation, deploy ceteris paribus properly, and say — in your own words — why a science of people must stay humble. Welcome to Economics.
