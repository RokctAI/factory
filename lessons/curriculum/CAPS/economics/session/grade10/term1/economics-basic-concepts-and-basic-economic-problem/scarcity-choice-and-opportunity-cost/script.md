# Part 1 — Expert

The first lesson defined the discipline; this one confronts its founding problem. SCARCITY — limited resources against unlimited wants — is called the basic economic problem because every other topic in the subject is a response to it. Today: scarcity distinguished precisely from shortage, the choices scarcity forces, opportunity cost as the true price of every decision, the three questions every economy must answer, and the syllabus's ethical thread — how economic activity promotes or violates human rights and the environment.

## Subtopic: Scarcity — the Permanent Condition

Define with precision, because the examination punishes loose language here. SCARCITY is the permanent condition in which resources are insufficient to satisfy all wants. It is universal — rich and poor economies alike face it, because wants expand with means — and it is permanent: no growth abolishes it. Distinguish it from a SHORTAGE: a temporary market situation where, at the going price, quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied — bread sold out before noon, fuel queues in a supply crisis. Shortages can be fixed — prices adjust, supplies arrive; scarcity cannot. A millionaire faces no shortages worth mentioning and still faces scarcity: even unlimited money cannot buy unlimited time. Confusing the two is the topic's classic lost mark.

Because resources are scarce, every economic actor must CHOOSE. The household chooses between the school shoes and the winter jacket; the firm chooses between a new machine and a marketing campaign; the state chooses between another clinic and another kilometre of road. Scarcity does not merely limit us — it FORCES ranking: which want gets satisfied first, which waits, which never.

And choice has a shadow that follows it everywhere: whatever was NOT chosen. That shadow gets the next section to itself, because it is the single most useful concept in the subject.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: scarcity against shortage, defined and distinguished, and why even wealth cannot escape the condition.

## Subtopic: Opportunity Cost — the True Price

OPPORTUNITY COST is the value of the NEXT BEST ALTERNATIVE sacrificed when a choice is made. Not all alternatives forgone — only the best one you would otherwise have taken. The learner with R120 who buys a calculator, whose next-best use was a data bundle, has an opportunity cost of one data bundle — not the movie ticket ranked third, not everything else in the shop.

Run the concept up the levels. The HOUSEHOLD: an evening spent on part-time work earns R150; its opportunity cost is the studying not done — and the true cost of the shift is measured there, not in money. The BUSINESS: Thabo's spare R6 000 buys a sewing machine; the next best use was stock earning the mark-up — the machine's real cost is that forgone trading profit. The STATE: a billion rand on a stadium is a billion not spent on its next-best use — clinics, schools — and every budget debate in a democracy is, properly understood, an argument about opportunity cost. Money prices tell you what you PAY; opportunity cost tells you what you GIVE UP — and economics insists the second is the true price.

Two disciplines for the exam. First: opportunity cost is expressed in REAL terms — the alternative itself: "one data bundle", "the forgone stock profit" — not in rands, because the rands were spent either way. Second: only the NEXT BEST alternative counts — rank first, then name the runner-up. Term two's production possibility curve will draw this very idea as a line; today it must live in words.

Stop for this section's questions now — opportunity cost defined, computed at all three levels, and expressed correctly as the next-best alternative in real terms.

## Subtopic: The Three Questions Every Economy Must Answer

Scarcity forces choices not just on individuals but on whole societies — and every society, whatever its politics, must answer the same three questions.

WHAT to produce? Resources committed to maize cannot grow flowers; a factory tooled for taxis is not making tractors. Every economy must decide the basket: how much food, housing, weaponry, entertainment. HOW to produce? Labour-intensive methods employ many hands; capital-intensive methods use machines and fewer workers — a choice with deep consequences in a country of high unemployment. FOR WHOM to produce? Who gets the output — decided by incomes and prices, by need, by policy: the distribution question, and the most politically charged of the three.

Societies answer through their ECONOMIC SYSTEMS. In a MARKET economy, prices answer all three: what sells profitably gets produced, the cheapest method wins, and output goes to those willing and able to pay. In a PLANNED (command) economy, the state answers: planners set production targets, methods and distribution. In a MIXED economy, markets answer most questions while the state corrects, redistributes and provides what markets neglect — and South Africa is firmly mixed: private shops and factories beside public schools, clinics, grants and state enterprises. Grade 10 needs the classification and the recognition that the pure forms are textbook poles; every real economy sits between them, differing in degree.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — the three questions with examples, and the three systems matched to who answers.

## Subtopic: Human Rights and the Environment

The syllabus closes this topic with its ethical thread: economic activity can PROMOTE human rights and the environment — or violate both — and the candidate must argue each direction with examples.

Promotion first. Production creates employment, and fair employment funds the rights the Constitution names: food, shelter, education, healthcare — earned income is the everyday engine of dignity. Exchange builds interdependence — markets that reward honesty and service. Taxation of economic activity funds the public provision of rights: schools, hospitals, grants. A growing economy widens what a society can afford its people.

Violation, equally real. Production can exploit: child labour, wages below subsistence, unsafe workplaces — the labour rights of term three exist because these violations recur. Markets can exclude: those without income are invisible to price signals — the FOR WHOM question answered brutally. And the environment: production consumes natural resources and emits waste, and where the costs land on third parties — the community breathing the smoke, the river downstream — economists call them EXTERNALITIES: costs real people pay that no market price collected. Unchecked, the pursuit of output degrades the very resource base future production needs — hence SUSTAINABILITY: meeting today's needs without robbing tomorrow's.

The exam shape: given a scenario — a factory employing a village but polluting its river — argue BOTH sides, then the balance: employment and incomes promoted; health and environmental rights violated; remedies in regulation, taxation of the externality, enforcement of labour law. Economics does not choose for society, but it names the trade-off precisely — and precision in naming trade-offs is this whole topic's gift.

The final questions of this part are with you now: promotion and violation argued with examples, externalities defined, and a scenario weighed on both sides.

## Subtopic: Not a Shortage — a Forever Thing

When the bread runs out at the spaza by ten, that's a SHORTAGE — annoying, temporary, fixed by lunchtime when the delivery van arrives. Scarcity is a different animal entirely, and the difference matters.

Scarcity is the forever-gap between what people want and what exists to give them. It never gets fixed, because wants refill. Get the phone — now you want the earbuds. The family gets the bigger room — now the furniture looks small. A rich family and a poor family both live inside this gap; the rich one's wants have simply moved upmarket — overseas holidays instead of taxi fare — and TIME stays scarce for everyone: even a billionaire gets twenty-four hours.

So the test to keep: can it be fixed by delivery, by price, by waiting? Shortage. Is it the permanent condition of wanting-more-than-there-is? Scarcity. The van fixes the bread; nothing fixes scarcity.

And because of the forever-gap, life is RANKING. The household with R3 000 after rent doesn't ask "what do we want?" — everything — it asks "what comes FIRST?" School shoes before the jacket. Electricity before airtime. Scarcity's real force isn't that it says no — it's that it makes you put your wants in a queue and defend the order.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the forever thing are coming to you right now: shortage or scarcity, case by case, and what the gap forces every household to do.

## Subtopic: The Price Tag Behind the Price Tag

Every choice wears two price tags. The first is the one printed — R120 for the calculator. The second is invisible and heavier: WHAT ELSE that R120 would have been. Economics calls the second one opportunity cost, and once you see it, you can't unsee it.

The rule for reading the hidden tag: name the NEXT BEST thing you'd have done — not everything in the world, just the runner-up. You buy the calculator; your genuine second choice was the data bundle. Then the true cost of the calculator is: one data bundle. Not R120 — the money left either way — but the bundle you'll now live without.

Run it around the yard. The learner who takes the Saturday shift earns R150 — but the hidden tag reads "one day of matric revision," and in exam season that tag can cost more than the money earned. The mother choosing the taxi to work daily versus a monthly train ticket. Thabo putting R6 000 into a machine when that money could have bought stock — his hidden tag is the profit the stock would have made. Even FREE things carry the tag: the free community event still costs the afternoon — and the afternoon had a next-best use.

That's why economists say the true price of anything is the next-best thing you give up. Money measures the front of the tag; life is lived on the back of it. People who read both tags make visibly better decisions — watch for it, in your house and in the news.

Your questions for this part are up now: flip the tags — for each choice, name the runner-up, in real things, not rands.

## Subtopic: People and the Planet in the Ledger

Last piece: the yard-level view of the syllabus's big ethical thread — because the economy isn't numbers somewhere; it's what happens to people and places you know.

The promoting side, visible on any street: the factory that opened means wages; wages mean school fees paid, clinic visits afforded, dignity funded by work. The shops trading mean choices and lower prices. The taxes flowing from all of it — that's where the school's roof and the clinic's medicines come from. When the economy works, rights stop being words on paper and become airtime in the phone and food in the pot.

The violating side, just as visible. The employer paying below what life costs. The job with no safety gear. And the cost that sneaks past every till: the factory whose smoke the neighbourhood breathes, whose waste the river carries to the next village. Notice the trick of it — the factory's PRICES don't include the coughing children; somebody pays that cost, but not at the counter. Economists call it an externality: a cost that lands on people who were never part of the deal. Unwatched, it means today's production quietly eats tomorrow's — fished-out waters, worn-out land — the opposite of sustainability, which just means: don't feed the family today by burning the table.

So when the exam hands you the classic scenario — the factory that employs the village AND poisons its river — refuse the one-sided answer. Count both columns like a bookkeeper: jobs, incomes, taxes on one side; health, water, tomorrow's resources on the other. Then name the fixes: rules enforced, the polluter made to pay the hidden cost, workers' rights upheld. Two columns, honestly weighed — that's economic thinking with its conscience attached.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: promotion and violation from your own street, the sneaky cost named, and the factory scenario weighed in both columns. The basic problem is yours now — everything else this year is society's attempts to answer it.
