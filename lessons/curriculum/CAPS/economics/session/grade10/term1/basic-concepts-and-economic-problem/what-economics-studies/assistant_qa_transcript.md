**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! First one: if wants are truly UNLIMITED, isn't the whole subject hopeless? We can never win — so why study the game?

**Tutor:** Because the game is not about winning finally; it is about playing WELL — and the difference between playing well and badly is enormous. Unlimited wants against limited resources means every person, business and government faces choices forever — and choices can be made wisely or wastefully. A household that budgets eats all month; one that doesn't goes hungry by week three, on the same income. A country that allocates resources toward schools and infrastructure grows; one that wastes them stagnates, with the same mineral wealth. Economics exists precisely because scarcity is permanent: it is the study of choosing well under a constraint that never lifts. Hopeless would be a world where choices didn't matter. Ours is the opposite — every allocation matters, which is why the subject earns its place next to the sciences.

**Thandi:** Next: entrepreneurship confused a few of us. The gogo's stove is capital and her hands are labour — but her 'decision to bake' earns profit? Deciding doesn't look like work.

**Tutor:** Look closer at what the decision carries: RISK and ORGANISATION, and both are real contributions no other factor supplies. Before a single vetkoek exists, someone must judge that the rank wants vetkoek rather than fat cakes, commit money to flour that might not sell, combine the stove, the hands and the recipe into a working operation, and stand to LOSE if the judgement is wrong. The machinist is paid whether the shirts sell or not; the landlord collects rent regardless; the lender's interest is contractual. Only the entrepreneur eats the failure — burnt stock, quiet days, wasted flour — and that is why only the entrepreneur keeps the residual profit when the judgement is right. Profit is not pay for effort; it is the reward for risk carried and resources organised. Remove that function and the other three factors stand idle — a stove, hands and flour with nobody to say 'bake, today, for that rank, at that price.'

**Thandi:** This one's sharp: the lesson says economic laws are only 'tendencies' — so how is the law of demand a LAW if people can just ignore it?

**Tutor:** A law in social science claims a regularity, not an iron rule for every individual. The law of demand says: when the price rises, ceteris paribus, the quantity demanded FALLS — across the market as a whole. One stubborn customer may keep buying at any price; that no more breaks the law than one tall child breaks the statement 'children are shorter than adults.' The market total obeys because most people, most of the time, respond to prices the same direction — and decades of data across countries confirm it with remarkable consistency. What the 'tendency' honesty concedes is the margin: HOW MUCH quantity falls varies by product and person, exceptions exist at the edges, and predictions carry error bars. So treat economic laws the way a nurse treats vital-sign ranges: reliable enough to act on, humble enough to double-check. The examiner wants both halves — the law stated firmly, its nature as a tendency acknowledged.

**Thandi:** Last one: positive versus normative — but surely facts and opinions mix all the time? 'Unemployment is 32 percent AND that's a disaster' — one sentence, both kinds. What do we do with those?

**Tutor:** Exactly what you just did — split the sentence, because each half answers to a different court. 'Unemployment is 32 percent' answers to EVIDENCE: Statistics South Africa either measured that or it didn't; the claim is checkable and could be wrong. 'That's a disaster' answers to VALUES: it weighs suffering, priorities, what a society owes its members — reasonable people with the same data can rank it differently, though most would agree here. The skill is not banning mixture — nearly every real economic statement mixes — but keeping the receipts separate: know which part of your claim you would defend with a graph and which part with an argument about what matters. Policy debates go wrong when the halves swap courtrooms — statistics dismissed as 'just opinion,' or preferences dressed up as measured fact. An economist — and a Grade 10 candidate — earns trust by labelling each half honestly and defending each with its own kind of evidence. That habit, more than any formula, is what this first topic is really teaching.
