**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! First one: the definition says wants are unlimited — but I know people who honestly want very little. Doesn't that break the whole definition?

**Tutor:** It tests the definition properly, which is good — but watch what "unlimited" actually claims. It does not claim every individual is greedy; it claims that wants, taken across people and across time, never reach a final stop. Even your contented friend replaces wants rather than ending them: the want for the phone becomes the want for it to keep working, then for the repairs, then for something quieter — more free time, more security for the family, a better clinic nearby. And step back from individuals to society: has any country ever announced that it now has enough schools, enough housing, enough healthcare, and stopped? Never — each solved problem uncovers the next want in the queue. Modest people exist everywhere; a society with a completed shopping list has never existed anywhere. That is the precise sense in which wants are unlimited, and it is why scarcity — next lesson's subject — never retires.

***

**Thandi:** Next: you paired capital with interest. But if my aunt owns her pressure hose outright and pays nobody, where is the interest? It looks like the hose earns nothing.

**Tutor:** Sharp — and the answer upgrades your understanding of every factor reward. The money tied up in that hose could have been earning elsewhere: in a savings account, lent out, or invested. By locking it into equipment, your aunt gives up that alternative earning — so economists say the capital must earn at least its interest to justify itself, whether or not a bank is involved. The reward is measured by what the money could have earned in its next use, not by whether an actual payment changes hands. The same logic runs through the other factors: a man farming his own land still "earns rent" in the sense that the land could have been rented out; the owner working in her own shop earns implicit wages. When the business's takings are counted properly, each factor's share is carved out first — rent, wages, interest — and only what remains after all of them is true profit, the entrepreneur's reward for risk. That is why some businesses that look profitable are actually eating their owner's unpaid factor rewards.

**Thandi:** Third one: positive versus normative sounds like facts versus opinions — so should we just ignore the normative side, since it can't be tested?

**Tutor:** No — and the reason matters for how you'll use this subject as a citizen. Normative statements are where every economic decision actually lives: whether to tax sugar, fund universities, or raise grants are all "ought" questions, and no amount of data alone can settle an "ought". What the positive-normative split gives you is not a licence to ignore values but a discipline for arguing well: first establish the measurable facts — what the sugar tax did to consumption, what it raised in revenue, who paid it — and only then debate the values with those facts on the table. The failure mode the split protects you from is smuggling: dressing a value judgement up as a finding, or dismissing solid evidence because you dislike its implications. A strong economics answer — and a strong citizen — does both jobs and labels each: here is what the evidence shows; here is what I believe society should do about it, and why. The subject doesn't outlaw opinions; it makes you show your working before you're allowed one.

**Thandi:** Last question: if economic laws are only tendencies and forecasts keep missing, why should anyone trust economists at all?

**Tutor:** Trust them the way you trust a weather service — properly calibrated. A forecast of rain doesn't guarantee rain, yet you'd be foolish to ignore it; over many days, following the forecast beats guessing. Economic laws work the same way: the law of demand won't tell you what one specific shopper does at one till, but it reliably tells you what a million shoppers do when a price rises — and policies built against such tendencies fail expensively, which is itself evidence the tendencies are real. The honest comparison is not economist versus perfect prophet; it is economist versus the alternatives — hunches, slogans and wishful thinking, which perform far worse. And notice that the humility is a feature of the method, not a flaw: a discipline that states its error margins, publishes its data and revises when the evidence turns is exactly the kind you can trust, precisely because it never asks you to take its word. Distrust certainty about human behaviour; respect measured tendencies stated with their limits. That is the scientific temperament this subject is trying to grow in you.
