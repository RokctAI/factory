**Thandi:** Class questions before we wrap! First: opportunity cost counts only the NEXT BEST alternative. But when Ayanda spends her R300, she loses EVERYTHING else R300 could buy — why does economics shrug off all the rest?

**Tutor:** Because she was never going to have them all — three hundred rand buys exactly one path, so exactly one path was truly available to lose. Lay out her honest ranking: boots first, gym month second, movie outing third. Taking the boots slams ONE real door — the one she would otherwise have walked through: the gym. The movie never stood a chance; it had already lost to the gym before the boots even entered the contest, so mourning it counts a sacrifice that was never on the table. Defining cost as the single best forgone alternative keeps the tool sharp and usable: it answers the only question a decision-maker needs — what am I actually trading away, here, now? Pile up everything forgone and every choice looks infinitely expensive, which guides nobody. One choice, one runner-up, one true cost — that is the whole discipline.

***

**Thandi:** Next one: the lesson says even FREE things carry an opportunity cost. So is "free" just a lie? The holiday soccer clinic charges nothing!

**Tutor:** Not a lie — an incomplete sentence, and economics teaches you to finish it. "Free" describes the money price only, and money is just one of the scarce things you spend. The clinic asks no rands, true — but it claims the whole afternoon, and afternoons are scarce, with their own queue of claimants: revision, the paid shift, sleep. Attending kills the runner-up afternoon, and that death is a real cost, settled in time rather than cash. This is the point of the old saying that there is no such thing as a free lunch: somebody's resources, somebody's hours, somebody's alternatives are consumed even when no till rings. The practical habit to build: whenever anything is offered free, ask what it charges in your other currencies — time, attention, energy. Plenty of free things pass that test brilliantly; the skill is running the test instead of letting the R0 on the label switch your brain off.

***

**Thandi:** Third — and it's a spicy one: the market answers FOR WHOM with "whoever can pay." Isn't that simply unjust? Why does the subject treat it as one respectable option?

**Tutor:** The subject treats it as one option among several precisely so that the justice debate can happen with clear eyes instead of slogans. State the market's genuine virtues first: it rations without queues or officials, it rewards producing what others value, and it signals scarcity instantly through price. Then state its cold edge, exactly as you did: need without money casts no vote — the market registers demand, never hunger. The planned alternative — officials allocate — can serve need directly, and carries its own documented failures: shortages, queues, favouritism wearing an official stamp. That is why nearly every real society, ours very deliberately, mixes the answers: markets allocate most goods, while the state answers FOR WHOM differently where the market's answer offends justice — social grants, public healthcare, no-fee schools. Where exactly that line belongs is a normative question — a values argument, and a worthy one. What economics contributes is the groundwork underneath the argument: what each mechanism actually does and what each actually costs, so the debate fights over real trade-offs rather than imaginary ones. Your question is not dodged by the subject — it is armed by it.

***

**Thandi:** Last one: "make the polluter pay the hidden cost" sounds tidy — but how do you put a rand value on a valley full of coughing people? Some harms seem beyond pricing.

**Tutor:** You have walked into one of the discipline's deepest rooms, and the honest answer has two floors. Floor one: much of an externality CAN be priced — roughly, but usefully. Clinic visits, medicine, workdays lost, water treatment, the fishing income a silted stream destroys: all estimable in rands, and even a rough price beats the current price, which is ZERO — today the brickworks pays nothing at all for its smoke, so it produces as though harm were free. A pollution levy or clean-up liability drags the hidden cost onto the balance sheet that created it, and suddenly the cleaner kiln becomes the cheaper kiln — the fix works even with imperfect numbers. Floor two: you are right that some things resist pricing on principle — a child's lungs, a river a community has drunk from for generations — and good economics says so out loud. There, REGULATION takes over from pricing: certain harms are not taxed into moderation but forbidden outright — emission ceilings, protected water sources, banned inputs — society drawing lines that are simply not for sale. The mature scenario answer wields both tools and explains the division of labour: price what can be priced, so hidden costs stop being free; prohibit what must never be priced at all. Sorting each harm to its proper tool is economic reasoning with its conscience engaged — and it is precisely what a top answer looks like.
