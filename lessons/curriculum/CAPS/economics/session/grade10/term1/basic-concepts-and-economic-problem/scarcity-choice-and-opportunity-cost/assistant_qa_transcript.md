**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! First: opportunity cost says only the NEXT BEST alternative counts. But when I spend R120, I give up EVERYTHING else R120 could buy — why does economics ignore all the rest?

**Tutor:** Because you could never have had them all anyway — the R120 buys exactly one path, so only one path was truly available to lose. Line up your genuine ranking: calculator first, data bundle second, movie third. Choosing the calculator closes ONE real door — the one you would actually have walked through otherwise: the bundle. The movie was never your fate; it lost to the bundle before the calculator entered the race, so counting it double-counts sacrifice that was already spent. Measuring cost as the single best forgone alternative keeps the concept honest and usable: it answers the only question that matters for the decision — 'what am I actually trading away?' Add everything forgone and every choice would look infinitely expensive, which tells a decision-maker nothing. One choice, one runner-up, one true cost.

***

**Thandi:** Next: the lesson says even free things have an opportunity cost. Doesn't that make 'free' meaningless? The community netball day costs nothing!

**Tutor:** It makes 'free' PRECISE rather than meaningless: free describes the money price only, and money is just one of the scarce things you spend. The netball day charges no rands — but it takes the afternoon, and the afternoon is a scarce resource with its own queue of claimants: revision, the part-time shift, rest. Attending means the runner-up afternoon dies — that is a real cost, paid in time. This is why economists needle the phrase 'free of charge': charged in money, no; costless, never — hence the old saying that there is no such thing as a free lunch: someone's resources, someone's time, someone's alternatives are always consumed. The practical habit: when anything is offered free, ask what it costs in your OTHER scarce currencies — time, attention, energy. Plenty of 'free' things are excellent value on that reckoning; the skill is doing the reckoning rather than letting the R0 on the front tag switch your thinking off.

**Thandi:** This one's political, so brace: the FOR WHOM question — the market answers 'those who can pay.' Isn't that just unfair, full stop? Why does economics treat it as one option among several?

**Tutor:** Economics treats it as one answer among several precisely so the fairness argument can be had with open eyes rather than slogans. The market's for-whom has real virtues worth stating: it rations without queues or officials, rewards production, and signals what people value. It also has the brutal edge you named: need without income goes unserved — the market cannot see hunger, only demand backed by money. The planned answer — officials allocate — can serve need directly, and history shows its own failures: shortages, queues, favouritism with a stamp. That is why almost every real society, ours emphatically, mixes: markets allocate most goods, while the state answers for-whom differently where the market's answer offends justice — grants, public healthcare, free basic education. Where exactly to draw that line is a NORMATIVE question — a values argument, and a legitimate one. What economics contributes is the positive groundwork: what each mechanism actually does, what each costs, so the fairness debate argues about real trade-offs instead of imagined ones. The subject doesn't dodge your question; it equips it.

**Thandi:** Last one: for the factory-and-river scenario, 'make the polluter pay the hidden cost' sounds neat — but how do you actually price coughing children? Some costs seem beyond rands.

**Tutor:** You are standing on one of the deepest problems in the discipline, and the honest answer has two layers. Layer one: much of the externality CAN be priced, imperfectly but usefully — clinic visits and medicines, workdays lost, water treatment, fishing income destroyed — and even an imperfect price beats the current one, which is ZERO: today the factory pays nothing at all for the smoke, so it produces as if the harm were free. A pollution tax or clean-up liability moves the hidden cost onto the balance sheet that caused it, and suddenly cleaner methods become the cheap option — the fix works even when the price is rough. Layer two: you are right that some things — a child's health, a river a community has fished for generations — resist pricing, and economics should say so plainly. That is where REGULATION rather than pricing takes over: some harms are not taxed into moderation but prohibited outright — emission limits, protected water sources, banned substances — society drawing lines that are not for sale. The mature answer to the scenario uses both tools and says why: price what can be priced so hidden costs stop being free, and forbid what should never be priced at all. Naming which harm belongs to which tool — that is economic thinking with its conscience attached, and it is exactly what the top of the mark scheme looks like.
