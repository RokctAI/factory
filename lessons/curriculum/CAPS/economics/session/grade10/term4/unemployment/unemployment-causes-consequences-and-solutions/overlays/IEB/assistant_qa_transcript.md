**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! First one, and half the group wanted it asked: if there are really three thousand applications for forty posts, why do we still hear people say the unemployed should just try harder?

**Tutor:** Because that claim is easier to say than to check — so let us check it, with the topic's own tools. Start with arithmetic: at that supermarket, effort could not change the outcome for two thousand nine hundred and sixty people. If every applicant doubled their effort — better CVs, earlier queues, more applications — the store would still hire forty. Individual effort decides WHO gets the forty posts; it cannot decide HOW MANY posts exist, and unemployment is a question about how many. Then check the definitions: the strict count includes only people ACTIVELY searching — every person in that statistic is, by definition, already trying, spending scarce taxi money and data to keep trying. Then check the structure: if effort were the missing ingredient, unemployment would sit evenly across skill levels — instead it collapses as qualifications rise, while the artisan post across town goes begging. That pattern is the signature of a mismatch between what the economy demands and what people have been enabled to supply — a system property, not a character flaw. None of this means effort is worthless; within the queue, preparation genuinely matters. But confusing the queue's existence with the queuers' attitude is a diagnostic error, and this topic is one long lesson in diagnosis. An economist's sentence for it: effort allocates the scarce posts; it does not create them. Creating them is what the engine, the bridge and the training system are for.

**Thandi:** Next: Lerato's story was called nothing broken, just a gap. But the gap has no income in it. If frictional unemployment is so harmless, why does it still feel like a crisis to the person inside it?

**Tutor:** A sharp question, because it separates two things the topic must keep apart: the SYSTEM'S health and the PERSON'S hardship. From the system's side, frictional unemployment is not just harmless — it is functional. An economy where every worker seized the first available post instantly would be an economy of terrible matches: engineers packing shelves, teachers guarding warehouses. Search time is how skills find the posts that use them, and better matches mean higher productivity and higher lifetime earnings, so a healthy labour market deliberately contains people mid-hop. But from the person's side, those months still need rent and food — and here is where your observation becomes real economics: whether a gap stays frictional depends on whether the person can AFFORD the search. A graduate with family support searches until the right post appears; a graduate with nothing takes anything within a fortnight — a mismatch that wastes her qualification — or exhausts her taxi money and drifts toward discouragement, where the gap quietly hardens into something structural. That is why the safety mechanisms matter even for the mildest type: the UIF for those with contribution histories, family networks, and faster matching platforms that shorten everyone's gap. So hold both truths: frictional unemployment is the system breathing normally, AND the breath is only comfortable for people with cushions. An answer that sees only the first is cold; only the second, confused. Both together are the full picture.

**Thandi:** Third one: the lesson said public works jobs are temporary and modest, and the UIF runs out. My cousin says that means these programmes are just painkillers, not medicine. Is he right?

**Tutor:** Your cousin has half of a good argument, and the half he is missing is the interesting part. He is right that neither programme cures the disease: a public works post ends, a UIF benefit expires, and structural unemployment stands exactly where it stood. If someone claimed these were the SOLUTION, the painkiller charge would land. But follow the medical picture properly: in real medicine, painkillers are not the opposite of treatment — they are what keeps the patient alive and functional long enough for treatment to work. Translate that: a household with zero income does not calmly retrain — it sells its assets, pulls children from school, and loses the very capacity that recovery would need. The bridge and the net prevent that collapse. They hold skills, health and school attendance intact while the slow medicine — training systems, labour-absorbing growth, small-business development — takes its years to act. And notice two things painkillers never do, which these programmes genuinely attempt: a public works post writes a FIRST LINE into an empty CV — and for a first-time seeker, that line is treatment, because inexperience was part of her disease; and UIF benefits keep spending alive in local shops during downswings, which shortens the downswing itself. The honest evaluation, then: judged as cures, both fail, and the lesson said so plainly. Judged as what they are — stabilisers that buy time and protect capacity while cures operate — they are indispensable. The exam-ready sentence: the bridge and the net do not solve unemployment; they make solving it possible. That is neither painkiller nor cure — it is the difference between a patient who can recover and one who cannot.

**Thandi:** Last one, and it is the one everyone actually wants answered: we are the young people in this lesson. Knowing everything we now know — what should a Grade Ten actually do?

**Tutor:** The most practical question of the term, and the topic answers it more concretely than you might expect. First: read the demand side before choosing your supply. The skill-sorted queue is the single most important fact in your economic life — joblessness collapses as scarce skills rise. So before locking in subjects, courses or a trade, look at what the economy is actually short of: artisans, technicians, health workers, agricultural specialists, anyone who can build, fix, code or care. A qualification chosen because the demand exists beats a more glamorous one chosen blind. Second: treat experience as a currency you can start earning now. The first-job loop — experience required, experience unavailable — is cruel, but it has cracks: holiday work, a stall, helping in a family business, volunteering at the clinic, a public programme later if needed. Every verifiable line in a CV is a rand in that currency, and the earlier you start minting, the sooner the loop breaks. Third: understand the geography. Distance from work is a cost you can plan around — in where you study, what transport a job would need, which towns hold the industries you aim at. Fourth, further out: remember that most new jobs anywhere are born in small firms — some of you should be planning to be the employer at the gate, not the applicant, and everything this year taught about markets, costs and support programmes is your toolkit for that. And last, as citizens: you now know which promises are real. When someone offers one simple cure for five different diseases, you can name what they are skipping. Vote, argue and plan like someone who can tell an engine from a bridge from a net — because the queue shortens one informed decision at a time, and yours start now.
