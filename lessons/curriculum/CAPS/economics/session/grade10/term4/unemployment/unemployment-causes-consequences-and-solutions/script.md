# Part 1 — Expert

Term three counted the labour force and watched the labour market set wages. This term opens with what that market fails to do for millions: UNEMPLOYMENT — South Africa's defining economic problem. Today: the nature and measurement of unemployment; the TYPES economists distinguish, because each has its own cause and its own cure; the consequences, personal and national; and the approaches to solving it — production growth, public works programmes and unemployment insurance. This topic examines as diagnosis and prescription: name the type, then match the remedy.

## Subtopic: The Nature and Measurement of Unemployment

Recall the definitions precisely, because everything rests on them. The STRICTLY unemployed: working-age people without work, AVAILABLE for work, and ACTIVELY SEEKING it. The EXPANDED definition adds DISCOURAGED work-seekers — those who want work but have stopped searching. The UNEMPLOYMENT RATE divides the unemployed by the LABOUR FORCE, never by the population.

South Africa's unemployment is severe by any international comparison, and the syllabus asks for its CHARACTERISTICS rather than perishable percentages. It is STRUCTURAL rather than merely cyclical: it persists through upswings, so it cannot be explained by the business cycle alone. It is YOUTH-CONCENTRATED: the youth rate runs far above the national rate, and first-time work-seekers wait longest. It is SKILL-DIVIDED: unemployment falls steeply as education and skill rise — the scarce-skill gate has queues of employers, the unskilled gate has queues of workers. It is SPATIALLY UNEVEN: heaviest in former homeland areas and townships distant from economic centres. It is LONG-TERM: a large share of the unemployed have been jobless for a year or more — which erodes skills, networks and confidence, making re-entry harder the longer it lasts. And it carries a large DISCOURAGEMENT margin, which is why the expanded rate exceeds the strict one by several percentage points.

Two working consequences of that profile. First, growth alone will not clear it: an economy can grow while unemployment stays high, if growth is capital-intensive or concentrated in skilled sectors. Second, the youth and long-term concentrations mean remedies must reach people the market is currently bypassing entirely — a point the solutions section will press.

## Subtopic: The Types of Unemployment

Economists sort unemployment by CAUSE, because the cure follows the cause. Grade 10 needs five types, each with its definition, an example, and its remedy.

FRICTIONAL unemployment: people between jobs — the graduate searching for a first post, the worker who resigned and is looking. It is short-term, voluntary in part, and exists in every healthy economy, because matching people to posts takes time. Its remedy is INFORMATION: better job-matching services, online vacancy platforms, career guidance — shortening the search, not abolishing it.

STRUCTURAL unemployment: a MISMATCH between the skills or locations workers have and the skills or locations employers need. The mineworker retrenched when a shaft closes and whose skills fit no growing sector; the unskilled work-seeker in an economy demanding technicians; the willing worker two hours from where the jobs are. This is South Africa's dominant type — long-term, stubborn, unmoved by ordinary growth — and its remedies are correspondingly structural: education and training, artisan and technical skills programmes, retraining for displaced workers, and measures that reduce the distance between people and jobs.

CYCLICAL unemployment: caused by the downswing — demand falls, firms retrench; term one's business cycle, seen from the shop floor. Its remedy is demand: policies that lift spending and production — the state's spending, lower interest rates, export growth — restoring the derived demand for labour.

SEASONAL unemployment: work that exists only in part of the year — harvest labour, holiday-season retail, tourism staff. Its remedies are diversification of local economies and off-season work or training.

And the type the syllabus names for our history: STRUCTURAL unemployment's cousin, TECHNOLOGICAL unemployment — machines replacing tasks, from mechanised mining to automated tills. Real, recurring, and answered by the same instrument as structural: retraining toward the work technology creates rather than the work it removes.

## Subtopic: Causes and Consequences

The CAUSES of South African unemployment stack in layers, and a good answer names several.

Structural and historical: an economy built around capital-intensive mining and manufacturing, employing relatively few per rand of output; an education system that has not produced the technical and artisan skills employers seek; and apartheid's spatial legacy placing people far from work. Demand-side: slow economic growth — the production boundary advancing too slowly to absorb the arriving cohorts; weak investment; and downswings destroying jobs faster than upswings create them. Supply-side: rapid growth of the working-age population from term three's demographic profile — every year a large new cohort arrives at the gate. And frictions: information gaps, transport costs that make job-searching itself unaffordable, and the loss of networks that long-term unemployment inflicts.

The CONSEQUENCES divide cleanly for the examination. For the INDIVIDUAL and household: lost income and poverty; dependence on grants and family; the erosion of skills and confidence; and documented effects on health, dignity and family stability — unemployment is not merely an absence of wages but a social condition. For the ECONOMY: output forgone — the country producing inside its production possibility curve, exactly as last term's diagram drew it; lower aggregate demand as the jobless spend less, weakening the circular flow; lost tax revenue while social spending needs rise, squeezing the budget from both sides; and skills decaying, which lowers future capacity. For SOCIETY: poverty and inequality deepening; social exclusion, especially among young people; and the associated strains on cohesion and safety that follow long-term joblessness in any country.

Note the self-reinforcing loop the examiner rewards: unemployment lowers spending, lower spending lowers production, lower production lowers hiring. Breaking such loops is precisely what the solutions attempt.

## Subtopic: Approaches to Solving Unemployment

The syllabus names three approaches; each attacks a different part of the problem, and none is sufficient alone.

GROWTH OF PRODUCTION. If firms produce more, they need more labour — derived demand doing its work. Policies: investment in productive capacity; infrastructure that lowers the cost of doing business; support for labour-intensive sectors — agriculture, tourism, construction, services — because the composition of growth matters as much as its rate; support for small and medium enterprises, which generate a large share of new jobs; and export promotion. The strength: it creates SUSTAINABLE, self-financing jobs. The limit: growth alone can bypass the unskilled, and capital-intensive growth may raise output without raising employment — which is why the type-diagnosis matters.

PUBLIC WORKS PROGRAMMES. The state employs people directly on labour-intensive public projects — roads, water infrastructure, environmental work, community services. South Africa's Expanded Public Works Programme is the standing example. Strengths: immediate income to households that have none; work experience and training for first-time seekers; and assets built that raise future productivity. Limits: the jobs are usually temporary and modestly paid; the programmes are funded from the budget — an opportunity cost; and they treat symptoms unless paired with training that leads onward. Judge them as a BRIDGE, not a destination.

UNEMPLOYMENT INSURANCE. The Unemployment Insurance Fund — funded by that one percent from employees and one percent from employers you costed in Accounting — pays temporary benefits to contributors who lose work, plus maternity, illness and dependants' benefits. Strengths: it cushions the income shock, prevents destitution during search, and stabilises spending in downswings — an automatic support to the circular flow. Limits: benefits are temporary and partial; and crucially, the UIF only helps those who WERE formally employed — it does not reach the never-employed young person, who is precisely South Africa's largest unemployed group.

The mature conclusion the examination wants: diagnose before prescribing. Cyclical unemployment needs demand; frictional needs information; structural — our dominant type — needs education, training and spatial remedies, supported by growth whose composition favours labour, with public works bridging and insurance cushioning. No single instrument solves a structural problem, and any answer offering one is incomplete.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the three approaches with strengths and limits, and remedies matched to diagnosed types.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same subject from the queue at the gate — because in this country, unemployment is not a chapter; it is somebody in nearly every family, and understanding it properly is a form of respect.

## Subtopic: The Queue That Doesn't Move

Stand at a factory gate at six in the morning. The queue is long, quiet, and mostly young — matric certificates in plastic sleeves, CVs printed at the internet café. The gate opens; two people go in; the rest go home and come back tomorrow.

That queue is what the statistics mean. And four things about it explain this whole topic. It is YOUNG: the front of every unemployment number in South Africa is people under thirty-five, many looking for a FIRST job — and the cruel loop of first jobs is that experience is required to get the experience. It is SPLIT BY SKILL: down the road at the engineering firm, the queue is three people long and the firm is phoning agencies — same town, opposite problem. It is SPREAD UNEVENLY: in some districts the nearest real gate is two taxis away, and the fare costs more than a day's food. And it is LONG-STANDING: many in the queue have been coming for over a year — and every month of standing there rusts a skill, cools a network, and thins the confidence you need to walk in the door.

One more thing about the queue, and it's the thing the news often misses: some people stopped coming. Not because they found work — because they stopped believing the gate opens. The strict count no longer sees them; the expanded count does. So when someone tells you the rate, ask which count — and remember the people who left the queue are the deepest part of the problem, not the smallest.

## Subtopic: Five Different Reasons for the Same Empty Hands

Here's the mistake that ruins most conversations about unemployment: treating it as ONE thing. It isn't. Empty hands have at least five different stories — and each one needs a different fix.

Story one: the graduate who finished in November and starts in February. Unemployed today — but only in the GAP between school and work. Every economy has these people; the fix is faster matching — better vacancy platforms, job centres, guidance. Nothing deeper is wrong.

Story two — and this is South Africa's big one: the man who spent twelve years underground, the shaft closed, and nothing else in the region needs what he can do. Or the young woman with a matric certificate in a town whose only vacancies say "artisan, three years' experience." The problem isn't laziness, or a bad month for the economy — it's a MISMATCH between what people can do and where, and what employers need and where. That's structural unemployment, and better job adverts won't touch it. It needs training, trades, and closing the distance between people and jobs.

Story three: the retrenchment letter that arrives when the whole country is in a downswing — orders dried up, so shifts vanished. That one lifts when the economy lifts; the fix is demand — spending, building, exporting.

Story four: the fruit-picker with work from January to April and nothing after — seasonal, predictable, needing something else in the quiet months.

Story five: the till operator replaced by the self-service machine, the mineworker replaced by the drilling rig. Machines take tasks; that's been true since the mill. The fix is the same as story two: learn what the machine needs a person for.

Diagnose before you prescribe — that's the whole skill. Anyone who offers one cure for all five stories is selling something.

## Subtopic: Bridges, Engines and Nets

Last piece: what actually helps — and the honest limits of each, because false hope is its own harm.

The ENGINE: growing the economy. More production genuinely means more jobs — that's the derived demand you already know: no orders, no shifts; more orders, more shifts. But be sharp about the KIND of growth: a mine that expands with machines might add billions in output and forty jobs; a tourism sector, a farm, a construction site, a small-business street adds far more hands per rand. So the question is never just "is the economy growing?" but "growing in what — and does it need people?" That's why supporting small businesses, farming, tourism and building matters so much here.

The BRIDGE: public works. The state hires people directly — fixing roads, clearing rivers, working in clinics and schools. It's real money into households with none, real experience for a first CV, and real assets left behind. But keep it honest: these jobs are usually temporary and modest, they're paid for out of taxes, and if the programme doesn't LEAD somewhere — a skill, a certificate, a next job — the bridge ends in the same river. A bridge, not a destination.

The NET: unemployment insurance. That one percent off your future payslip, matched by your employer, goes into a fund that pays you for a while if you lose your job — so a retrenchment doesn't become homelessness next month, and your spending doesn't vanish from the shops overnight. Vital — and note the gap in it: you can only claim from a job you HAD. The young person in the queue who has never been employed has paid nothing in and receives nothing out. The country's biggest group of unemployed people stands outside the net entirely.

So: an engine that must grow the right way, a bridge that must lead somewhere, and a net with a hole where the young stand. Anyone who says the answer is simple hasn't looked at the queue.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: engine, bridge and net matched to what each fixes and what each misses — and one honest paragraph on why no single answer is enough. Look after the queue; some of you will be the ones who shorten it.
