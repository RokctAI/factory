**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close the year! The first one always causes a stir: someone asks why redress is still needed when the discriminatory laws were repealed thirty years ago. What's the economic answer?

**Tutor:** The economic answer is about STOCKS, not just rules. A law is a rule about flows — who may buy, work, live where, from today onward. Repeal changes the flows immediately and completely. But an economy runs substantially on stocks: the land already titled, the business already capitalised, the qualification already earned, the network already built, the deposit already saved. Those stocks were accumulated under the old rules and were not redistributed by the repeal — they simply carried over, and they keep generating advantage under the new rules exactly as they did under the old. Worse, stocks reproduce: property secures the loan that starts the firm; the firm pays the school fees; the school builds the network that finds the first job. So two families obeying identical rules from 1994 still compound at different rates, because they started with different stocks. That is why redress targets the STOCKS — land, capital, skills, ownership — rather than merely guaranteeing equal rules. Fairness of rules and equality of position are different economic objects, and only the first was achieved by repeal.

**Thandi:** Next: some in class say redress programmes are unfair to people who personally did nothing wrong — young people today who inherited none of the decisions. How should we think about that?

**Tutor:** Take the objection seriously, because it is sincerely held and the answer is stronger for engaging it. Two distinctions do most of the work. First, redress addresses INHERITED POSITION, not personal guilt: nobody is being punished for what an ancestor did; the programmes adjust the distribution of assets and opportunities that the past produced. A young person who inherited advantage did nothing wrong either — but they did inherit, and inheritance is precisely what economics can measure and act on. Second, note who actually bears the cost: most redress instruments are not confiscations but reallocations at the margin — of new procurement, new appointments, new finance — funded largely from tax and directed at expanding opportunity. That said, the objection contains one point economists should concede: instruments that entrench a narrow beneficiary class serve neither justice nor efficiency — which is exactly the critique that produced the 'broad-based' in B-BBEE, and it is a fair test to apply to any programme: does this reach broadly, or does it reward a few repeatedly? The mature exam answer states the counter-argument honestly, then answers it on the position-versus-guilt distinction — and concedes the real design risk rather than pretending it away.

**Thandi:** This one's about land, which is the hottest topic of all: why has land reform been so slow, if everyone agrees the dispossession was wrong?

**Tutor:** Because agreeing on the wrong is the easy part; the machinery of transfer is genuinely hard, and four constraints bind at once. First, EVIDENCE: restitution requires establishing who held what, when, and how they were removed — decades later, with records often destroyed and claimants dispersed, each claim is a research project. Second, FINANCE: redistribution has largely proceeded by purchase, which requires budget on a scale that competes with schools, clinics and grants — the opportunity cost from term one, in its sharpest form. Third, CAPACITY: transferring land is the beginning, not the end — the state must supply title, extension support, finance and market linkage, and where that support has been thin, transferred farms have often failed, which then discredits the whole programme unfairly. Fourth, LEGAL and constitutional constraints on how property may be acquired, which have been argued over continuously. Add to those the plain arithmetic that many claimants prefer cash compensation to farming, and a picture emerges of a programme that is slow for structural reasons rather than mere neglect. The honest exam answer says both: the pace has fallen far short of the targets set, AND the reasons are structural and multiple — with the strongest single lesson being that transfer without support fails, which is why post-settlement support is now the reform most economists press for.

**Thandi:** Last question of the whole year: after everything — cycles, markets, unemployment, redress — is our economy actually going to be alright?

**Tutor:** The honest answer has to be conditional, and I think you have earned the full version rather than reassurance. What the analysis says plainly: the constraints are severe — an unemployment rate among the world's worst, inequality entrenched by a century of design, growth too slow to absorb the cohorts arriving each year, and institutions carrying more demand than capacity. Nothing in this year's economics permits a comfortable prediction. What the same analysis says just as plainly: none of those constraints is a law of nature. Unemployment is a mismatch between what people can do and what the economy demands — and both sides of that mismatch are alterable by policy: training pointed at real demand, growth weighted toward labour-absorbing sectors, small firms made easy to start, distance between people and work reduced. Inequality was built by decisions and can be unwound by decisions, though more slowly than it was built. Countries in comparable positions have transformed within a generation, and they did it with the combinations this year has described — not with a single clever policy, and never quickly. So the accurate sentence is: the outcome is not determined, and it is not automatic. It depends on choices made repeatedly over decades, by people who understand what the choices actually do — which is the entire reason economics is taught in schools rather than only in universities. You are now among the people who can read the arguments, test the claims, and tell a mechanism from a slogan. That is not nothing. That is, genuinely, where it starts.
