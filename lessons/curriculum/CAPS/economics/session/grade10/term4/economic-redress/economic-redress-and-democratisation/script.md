# Part 1 — Expert

The year closes on the question every previous topic has been circling: what does an economy do about a past that shaped who owns, who works and who was excluded? ECONOMIC REDRESS is the deliberate correction of historical economic injustice — and the syllabus asks you to identify the marginalised groups, understand redress through the FACTORS OF PRODUCTION, and describe the democratisation of economic decision-making: labour legislation, public participation, NEDLAC, self-regulating bodies and the macro-economic adaptations that frame them all.

## Subtopic: Economically Marginalised Groups

MARGINALISED means pushed to the edge of economic life — excluded from ownership, employment, skills, credit and decision-making. South Africa's marginalisation was not accidental or incidental: it was legislated, systematically, over decades, and its patterns persist because economic disadvantage transmits across generations even after the laws that created it are repealed.

The syllabus's groups, each with the mechanism of exclusion — because naming the mechanism is what turns a list into an answer. BLACK South Africans, dispossessed of land by law, restricted in where they could live, work, trade and own property, denied equal education, and channelled into low-wage migrant labour. WOMEN, and Black women in particular: excluded by law and custom from property, credit and many occupations; carrying unpaid care work that constrains labour force participation; and still concentrated in lower-paid sectors and positions. RURAL communities, in areas deliberately starved of infrastructure and investment — the spatial pattern behind term three's geography of unemployment. PEOPLE WITH DISABILITIES, facing physical and attitudinal barriers to workplaces and schooling. And YOUTH, who inherit disadvantage as the first-time work-seekers of a labour market with too few entry points.

Note the compounding: these categories overlap, and disadvantages multiply where they intersect — a young rural Black woman with a disability faces not five separate obstacles but one compounded exclusion. The examination rewards the candidate who names both the groups and the mechanisms, and who recognises the intersection.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: the groups, each with its mechanism of exclusion, and the compounding effect explained.

## Subtopic: Redress Through the Factors of Production

The syllabus frames redress around the FACTORS OF PRODUCTION — an elegant structure, because ownership of the factors is precisely what marginalisation denied. Work through all four.

NATURAL RESOURCES — land above all. Dispossession was the foundational injustice, so land reform is foundational redress, in three programmes: RESTITUTION — returning land, or compensating those dispossessed after 1913; REDISTRIBUTION — transferring land to those who had no access; and TENURE REFORM — securing the rights of people whose occupation of land was legally insecure. The economic point beyond justice: land without support fails — redress in this factor requires finance, training, equipment and market access alongside the title deed, or the transfer produces neither livelihood nor output.

LABOUR — redress through employment and skills. Employment equity legislation requires designated employers to remove barriers and take affirmative measures so that workforces at every level reflect the population — targeting not entry alone but MANAGEMENT and skilled occupations, where exclusion was sharpest. Alongside it, skills development: levies funding training, learnerships and apprenticeships that address the mismatch of term three by building the qualifications marginalised workers were denied.

CAPITAL — redress through ownership and access to finance. Broad-based Black Economic Empowerment addresses who OWNS productive capital: ownership stakes, management control, enterprise and supplier development, and preferential procurement that channels state purchasing toward transformed suppliers. Development finance institutions and small-business funds address the credit exclusion that kept entrepreneurship out of reach for those without collateral or banking history.

ENTREPRENEURSHIP — redress through enabling business ownership: small business support, incubation, simplified regulation, market access and procurement set-asides — because the capacity to start and run a firm was denied by law, by lack of capital and by exclusion from networks.

The analytical thread: for each factor, ask what was DENIED, what the redress instrument transfers or builds, and what SUPPORT the transfer needs to become productive. Answers that name an instrument without its support conditions miss the economics.

Stop for this section's questions now — the four factors with their denial, their instrument, and their support conditions.

## Subtopic: Democratising Economic Decisions

Redress concerns not only what people own but who DECIDES. Democratisation of economic procedures means opening economic decision-making beyond a narrow group — and the syllabus names the machinery.

LABOUR LEGISLATION, from term three, is itself democratisation: the right to organise, bargain and be consulted converts workers from subjects of decisions into parties to them. PUBLIC HEARINGS and participation: parliamentary committees take public submissions on economic legislation and budgets; municipal integrated development plans require community input; regulators hold hearings before major decisions on prices and licences. The principle is that those affected by economic decisions should have a channel into them.

NEDLAC — the National Economic Development and Labour Council — is the flagship institution and a favourite examination target. It brings FOUR constituencies to one table: GOVERNMENT, ORGANISED BUSINESS, ORGANISED LABOUR, and ORGANISED COMMUNITY (development organisations representing civic, youth, women's and rural interests). Its function: significant labour legislation and major economic policy are tabled and negotiated there BEFORE Parliament — social dialogue seeking consensus rather than imposition. Its strength: policy that emerges with buy-in from those who must live with it; its limit, honestly stated: consensus-seeking is slow, and constituencies that are not well organised are less well represented at the table.

SELF-REGULATING BODIES: professional and industry bodies that set and enforce their own standards — accountants, engineers, medical professionals, advertising, press. Democratisation here means the standards and disciplinary processes are set by practitioners within a legal framework, with public accountability — devolved regulation rather than every rule written by the state.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — the participation channels named, NEDLAC's four constituencies and function with its limits, and self-regulation defined.

## Subtopic: Macro-economic Adaptations and Judging Redress

South Africa's redress has run inside successive MACRO-ECONOMIC frameworks, and Grade 10 needs their shape rather than a policy history. The consistent aims: economic PERFORMANCE — growth sufficient to expand employment and fund social spending; EMPLOYMENT creation as an explicit target rather than a hoped-for by-product; equitable DISTRIBUTION through progressive taxation, social grants, and public provision of education, health, housing and basic services; ECONOMIC STABILITY — inflation controlled, public finances sustainable, because instability destroys savings and hits the poor hardest; and EXTERNAL stability — a manageable balance of payments and currency.

The permanent tension the examination wants named: these aims can pull against each other. Redistribution demands revenue, which demands growth; growth may demand investment incentives that appear to favour the already-advantaged; stability constrains the borrowing that ambitious social spending would need. Policy is the balancing of these, and reasonable people differ about the balance — a normative question resting on positive analysis, exactly as term one taught.

How, then, should redress be JUDGED? By evidence, and on both ledgers. Achievements are real and measurable: access to basic services, housing, education and social grants expanded enormously; a substantial Black middle class and professional class established; ownership and management demographics shifted; and a body of law and institutions built where none protected the majority before. Shortfalls are equally real: unemployment persistently high; wealth and income inequality among the world's most severe; land reform slower than intended and often unsupported after transfer; benefits sometimes concentrated among a narrow elite rather than broadly based — the "broad-based" in B-BBEE responding to exactly that critique. The examination's mature answer states both ledgers, identifies mechanisms rather than slogans, and distinguishes what evidence shows from what the candidate would prefer.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the macro-economic aims with their tensions, and redress judged on both ledgers with mechanisms named.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same subject from the family table — because redress is not an abstraction here; it is why one household starts a generation behind another that worked just as hard.

## Subtopic: Starting the Race at Different Lines

Two learners write the same matric exam. One studied in a room with electricity, in a school with a laboratory, with parents who could pay a registration fee. The other studied by candlelight after a two-hour taxi trip, in a school with no lab, in a family where nobody has ever had money left over at month-end. Same exam. Same effort, maybe. Different starting lines.

Now notice where the starting lines came from — because this is the part that matters economically. They were not natural, and not accidental. Laws decided who could own land, and took it from most people. Laws decided where families could live, and put them far from work. Laws decided which schools got money, and which got almost none. Laws decided who could hold which job. That is what "marginalised" means: pushed to the edge of the economy by design, not by chance.

And here's the hard economics: repealing those laws stopped the machine, but it did not undo what the machine built. Land already transferred stays transferred. A grandmother denied schooling still cannot help with homework. A family with no property still has no collateral for a business loan. Disadvantage travels down generations by inheritance — of assets, of education, of networks, of address. That is exactly why "the law is equal now" and "the economy is equal now" are two very different statements — and redress is the attempt to close the gap between them.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the starting lines are coming to you right now: who was pushed to the edge, by what mechanism, and why repeal alone did not restore equality.

## Subtopic: Four Things You Need to Build a Livelihood

Think about what anyone actually needs to make a living, and you'll rediscover the four factors — and every redress programme lands on one of them.

LAND, or a place to produce. If your family's land was taken, redress means getting land back — restitution — or getting access to land you never had — redistribution — or making your existing occupation legally secure — tenure. But hear the crucial follow-through: land alone is a field, not a farm. Without seed money, equipment, training and someone to sell to, a title deed feeds nobody. Every honest redress conversation says: transfer PLUS support, or the transfer fails.

SKILLS AND WORK. If the jobs were reserved and the schools starved, redress means opening the workplace — employment equity, so workforces at every level, including management, look like the country — and building the qualifications people were denied: learnerships, apprenticeships, trade training paid for by a skills levy. Notice it targets management deliberately, because that is where exclusion was hardest and where decisions get made.

MONEY TO WORK WITH — capital. If your family never owned productive assets, you start with nothing to invest and nothing to borrow against. So redress works on ownership — shares in firms, seats in management, procurement that sends state and corporate buying toward transformed suppliers — and on credit: development finance and small-business funds for people the banks would never have looked at.

THE CHANCE TO BUILD SOMETHING — entrepreneurship. Support to start and survive: incubators, simpler paperwork, guaranteed markets through set-asides, mentorship. Because "just start a business" is easy advice for someone with capital, contacts and a customer list, and a serious barrier for someone with none.

Four needs, four kinds of redress. And one test to apply to any of them: does it hand over a THING, or does it build a working LIVELIHOOD?

Your questions for this part are up now: each factor matched to what was denied and what redress must include to actually work.

## Subtopic: Who Sits at the Table

Last piece, and it's about power rather than property: WHO GETS TO DECIDE.

An economy where a few people make all the decisions is undemocratic even if the shops are full. So South Africa built channels for other voices. Workers got the right to organise, bargain and be consulted — that's term three's labour law doing democratic work: you cannot be retrenched by surprise memo; your union sits opposite management. Ordinary people got hearings: Parliament takes public submissions on economic laws and budgets; your municipality must ask the community what to build; regulators must listen before approving a price increase. If you have ever seen a notice inviting comment on a tariff, that is this principle in action.

And then the big table: NEDLAC. Four chairs — government, business, labour, and community organisations speaking for civic, youth, women's and rural interests. Major economic laws go THERE before Parliament, so the people who will live with a policy argue about it before it becomes law. It's slower than one minister deciding — deliberately. The honest catch: the better organised your constituency, the louder your chair; groups without strong organisation are quieter at that table than their numbers deserve.

There's also self-regulation: doctors, engineers, accountants setting and policing their own professional standards inside the law — decisions devolved to the people who understand the work, with public accountability attached.

And finally, the fair way to judge all of this — both columns, as always. Real gains: services, housing, schooling and grants reaching millions who had none; a substantial Black professional and middle class; ownership and management no longer looking like 1993. Real shortfalls: unemployment still brutal, inequality still among the world's worst, land reform slow and often unsupported, and benefits too often pooling among a few rather than spreading broadly. Both columns are true. Saying only one of them is how conversations about this country go wrong — and an economist's job is to say both, with the mechanisms attached.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: the tables and channels named, and redress judged on both ledgers. That closes Grade 10 Economics — you can now describe an economy, measure it, watch it cycle, read its markets, count its people, and argue honestly about its fairness.
