# Part 1 — Expert

This session is the second revision sweep for the year's examination — terms two, three and four gathered into the four structures the paper tests: the state's tools in the market; the long evolution of markets from barter to industry; population, the labour force and the rules of work; and the twin contemporary issues, unemployment and economic redress. Everything compressed to the exam-day version: definitions sharpened, diagrams described so you can rebuild them, and every list attached to the mechanism that makes it earn marks.

## Subtopic: The State in the Market — Taxes, Subsidies and Price Controls

The state intervenes where markets produce outcomes society rejects — unaffordable essentials, exploited workers, harmful goods — and the syllabus arms it with four instruments, each with a diagram you must command in words.

A TAX on a product raises sellers' costs, shifting the supply curve LEFT and up by the tax: the new equilibrium carries a higher price and a smaller quantity, with the burden shared between buyer and seller. A SUBSIDY is the mirror: the state pays part of the cost, supply shifts RIGHT and down, price falls, quantity rises — which is why basic foods like brown bread and maize meal are zero-rated for VAT while other goods carry the full fifteen percent.

A MAXIMUM PRICE — the ceiling — is set BELOW equilibrium to keep essentials affordable. Read the verbal diagram: at the ceiling price, quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied, so a permanent SHORTAGE opens. Consequences follow mechanically: queues, rationing, and black markets where the good resells above the legal price. A ceiling only binds below equilibrium; set above, it changes nothing.

A MINIMUM PRICE — the floor — is set ABOVE equilibrium to protect producers' incomes. At the floor, quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded: a permanent SURPLUS, which the state must buy up, store or export, at a cost to the budget. The MINIMUM WAGE is a floor in the labour market: it raises the incomes of workers who keep employment, while the exam expects the honest other half — at a wage above equilibrium, the quantity of labour demanded can fall, so the policy trades higher pay for some against fewer posts for others.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: each instrument's shift or line placed correctly, ceilings generating shortages and floors generating surpluses, and the minimum wage argued from both sides.

## Subtopic: From Barter to Industry — the Evolution of Markets

The exam tells this as a story in stages; know the stages and the problem each one solved.

Stage one: SELF-SUFFICIENCY. Households produce everything they consume — no surplus, no trade, no market. Stage two: SURPLUS and BARTER. Better tools produce more than the household needs, and the extra gets swapped. But barter carries a built-in brake: the DOUBLE COINCIDENCE OF WANTS — the maize farmer must find a fisherman who wants maize at the exact moment the farmer wants fish. Trade stays slow, local and lucky.

Stage three: MONEY — the invention that releases the brake. Anything generally accepted in exchange works, and the syllabus wants money's functions: a MEDIUM OF EXCHANGE, ending the double coincidence; a UNIT OF ACCOUNT, pricing everything in one measure; a STORE OF VALUE, letting surplus wait; and a STANDARD OF DEFERRED PAYMENT, making credit possible. With money, markets widen from the village to the region to the world.

Stage four: GOVERNMENTS ENTER. As markets grow, the state arrives to issue and protect the currency, enforce contracts, regulate weights, measures and quality, tax trade, and provide what markets will not — roads, courts, schooling. Markets and the state grow together, not in sequence.

Stage five: INDUSTRIAL DEVELOPMENT. Machines, factories and wage labour multiply production; people move from land to towns. South Africa's version runs through the mineral revolution — diamonds and gold pulling railways, capital and labour inland — building an economy around mining and, later, manufacturing, with the migrant labour system leaving scars the redress section returns to.

The questions for this section are with you now: the stages in order, the double coincidence of wants explained, money's four functions, and the state's entry justified.

## Subtopic: Population, Labour and the Rules of Work

First, who counts. The working-age population runs from fifteen to sixty-four. Inside it, the LABOUR FORCE — the economically active population — holds everyone employed plus everyone unemployed but actively seeking work. Outside it stand full-time learners, homemakers, the retired and those too discouraged to search. The labour market then prices work like any market: the DEMAND for labour comes from employers and is DERIVED demand — nobody hires for its own sake; hiring follows the demand for what workers produce. The SUPPLY of labour comes from households. The wage is the price at the gate where those two meet, which is why scarce skills command high wages while unskilled labour queues.

Second, the rules. Labour law exists because the individual worker and the employer meet with unequal power — one job against many applicants — so the law levels the ground. Three Acts anchor the topic. The BASIC CONDITIONS OF EMPLOYMENT ACT sets the floor beneath every contract: maximum ordinary hours, leave, notice, and protections against child labour. The LABOUR RELATIONS ACT governs the collective game: the right to join unions, to bargain collectively, to strike within procedure — and employers' right to lock out. The EMPLOYMENT EQUITY ACT addresses fairness in hiring and promotion, prohibiting unfair discrimination and requiring redress of past exclusion.

Third, when talks fail. COLLECTIVE BARGAINING between unions and employers — often in bargaining councils — sets wages and conditions sector by sector. Deadlocks travel a ladder: first CONCILIATION at the CCMA, where a commissioner helps the parties settle; then ARBITRATION, where the commissioner decides; and for matters of law, the Labour Court. Strikes that follow procedure are protected; those that skip it are not.

Stop for this section's questions now — the labour force fenced correctly, demand for labour derived not direct, the three Acts matched to their jobs, and the dispute ladder climbed in order.

## Subtopic: Unemployment and Economic Redress

Unemployment first, compressed to its skeleton. The STRICT definition counts those without work, available and actively seeking; the EXPANDED definition adds discouraged work-seekers; the rate divides the unemployed by the labour force, never the population. The TYPES carry the marks because each names its own cure: FRICTIONAL — between jobs, cured by information; STRUCTURAL — skills or location mismatched to what employers need, South Africa's dominant type, cured by education, training and spatial remedies; CYCLICAL — the downswing's retrenchments, cured by demand; SEASONAL — harvest and holiday work, cured by diversification; TECHNOLOGICAL — machines replacing tasks, cured by retraining. Consequences sort across three levels — individual, economy, society — and solutions across three instruments: growth of production for sustainable jobs, public works as a bridge of income and experience, and the UIF as a net that catches only those who were formally employed.

Redress second, and structure is generous here too. Apartheid excluded people from the economy by race, gender and location; redress works through the FOUR FACTORS OF PRODUCTION. Land: restitution for those dispossessed, redistribution, and secure tenure. Labour: employment equity and skills development. Capital: broad-based ownership schemes, preferential procurement, access to finance. Entrepreneurship: support for small business through training, funding and easier regulation. The evaluation mark lives in the pairing: an instrument alone is not a livelihood — a title deed without seed money, a learnership without a job at its end, a tender without working capital, each falls short unless the support arrives together.

Democratising economic decisions completes the topic: workers get a voice through unions and labour law; communities through public hearings; and at national level NEDLAC seats government, business, labour and community before major economic laws reach Parliament.

The final questions of this part are with you now: definitions and types diagnosed from scenarios, solutions matched to types, redress organised by factor, and the seats at the decision table named.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same material at street level: a referee with a whistle, a village that invented shopping, and a gate where the year's hardest topics stand in one queue.

## Subtopic: Referees, Ceilings and Floors

Markets are games, and games need referees. Left alone, the bread market prices some families out; the job market pays some workers almost nothing; the sugary-drink market sells all it can. So the state steps onto the field with four moves — and each move has a consequence the exam wants you to see coming.

Move one: make a thing more expensive on purpose. Tax it — the levy on sugary drinks, the tax on cigarettes — and the price rises, so less gets bought. Move two: make a thing cheaper on purpose. Subsidise it, or drop the VAT to zero the way brown bread and maize meal carry no VAT while most goods carry fifteen percent — and more families afford it.

Move three: the CEILING. The state pins a price BELOW where the market wanted it, so essentials stay affordable. But pin the price down and sellers offer less while buyers want more — and the gap between them becomes a queue. Ceilings make queues: rationing, first-come-first-served, and a back door where the desperate pay extra anyway. The referee stopped the price rising; the shortage found another way to charge.

Move four: the FLOOR. The state pins a price ABOVE the market's level so producers survive — and now sellers offer more than buyers take. Floors make piles: surpluses somebody must buy, store or ship out. The minimum wage is a floor under people instead of produce: real protection for the worker who keeps the job, and the honest sentence adds — at a higher price for labour, some employers hire fewer hands. Both halves, always.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on this section are with you right now: the four moves, why ceilings make queues and floors make piles, and the minimum wage in two honest halves.

## Subtopic: From Swapping Goats to Swiping Cards

Start in a village where every household does everything: grows its food, builds its hut, weaves its cloth. Nobody shops, because nobody has extra and nobody specialises. Then better tools arrive, and one season the maize family harvests more than it can eat. The extra wants to become fish, and the swapping begins.

But swapping has a catch that can kill it: you must find someone who has what you want AND wants what you have, on the same day. The maize family stands at the river while the fisherman shakes his head — he wants a goat, not maize. Economists call it the double coincidence of wants; the village calls it a wasted morning.

Then comes the invention that fixed everything: money. Anything everyone accepts — cattle once, gold later, printed notes, now a tap of a card. Money does four jobs at once: it is the go-between, so the fisherman takes coins and buys his goat elsewhere; the measuring stick, pricing maize, fish and goats on one scale; the storage jar, holding this season's surplus for next year; and the promise-keeper, letting you buy now and pay later. With those four jobs done, markets explode outward — village to town to country to world.

And as the markets grow, two more characters walk in. The chief — the state — arrives to guard the coins, enforce the promises, check the scales, take a share as tax, and build the roads the traders need. Then the machines arrive: factories multiplying what hands alone could make, pulling people off the land into towns — in South Africa's story, pulled inland by diamonds and gold, on railways built to reach them.

Take this section's questions now — the wasted morning at the river, money's four jobs, and the chief and the machines in the right order.

## Subtopic: The Gate, the Team and the Starting Line

One factory gate, early morning, and the whole term standing at it.

First, who is on the team? Count everyone fifteen to sixty-four who is working or genuinely looking. The learner in class, the grandmother at rest, the mother at home by choice — off the team, not unemployed, just not playing. The team is the labour force, and the wage at the gate is a price like any other: set where employers' need for hands meets workers' need for work. The employer's need is second-hand — nobody hires because hiring is nice; hiring happens because customers somewhere are buying what those hands will make.

Second, the rules at the gate. One worker against one employer is no contest, so the law evens it: a floor of basic conditions under every job — hours, leave, notice; the right to stand together in a union and bargain as one voice; and a rule against unfair discrimination in who gets hired and promoted. When the talking deadlocks, there is a ladder instead of a war: first a peacemaker helps both sides settle; if that fails, a referee decides; and points of law go to a court that handles only work.

Third, the queue at the gate is not one queue. In it stand five different people: the one between jobs, needing only a faster match; the one whose skills fit nothing nearby — our country's biggest group, needing training and transport, not adverts; the one retrenched in a downswing, waiting for demand; the seasonal worker in the quiet months; and the one a machine replaced, needing to learn what the machine cannot do.

And fourth — the starting line. Some at the gate began the race generations back; others were held behind the line by law: no land, thin schooling, businesses forbidden. Redress is the working answer: land returned or secured, skills built, ownership and finance opened, small businesses helped to start — and each instrument needs its partner, because a field without seed money is not yet a farm. At the national table, government, business, labour and community sit together before the big economic rules are written.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: the team fenced, the rules and the ladder, the five people in the queue diagnosed, and redress argued factor by factor. Walk into the paper knowing the gate — the paper is about the gate.
