**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! First one, and it costs marks every year: what exactly is the difference between a shortage and scarcity? Loadshedding feels like both.

**Tutor:** Loadshedding is the perfect test case, so let us run it. A SHORTAGE is a market condition at a moment: at the current price, quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied — and it is curable. Build the plants, fix the plants, raise the price, and the shortage closes. SCARCITY is the permanent backdrop behind every shortage and every abundance alike: even with every power station running perfectly, South Africa would still face the deeper question of whether the concrete, engineers and billions spent on electricity should instead have built hospitals, railways or schools. That question never closes, because resources are finite and wants are not. So the electricity crisis is a shortage sitting on top of scarcity: the crisis can end; the choosing cannot. In the exam, the marker watches for exactly this separation — a learner who writes that scarcity will be solved when the economy grows has confused the two, because growth moves the production possibility curve outward yet the curve still has an edge, and the edge is scarcity. One more precision worth marks: opportunity cost is scarcity's shadow. Wherever you find a scarce resource being used, the next best forgone use stands behind it — which is why the concept appears in every topic from the PPC to the government budget. Master the pair — permanent condition, curable symptom — and this question type never catches you.

***

**Thandi:** Next: when a question says the demand for chicken changed, how do we know whether to MOVE along the curve or SHIFT the whole thing? The class splits fifty-fifty every time.

**Tutor:** Then let us end the split with a single test: ask what changed FIRST. If the price of chicken itself is the thing that changed, nothing shifts — buyers simply respond along their existing willingness, and you write movement along the demand curve. If ANYTHING else changed first — income, the price of beef, a health scare, load-shedding closing the takeaways — the whole relationship between price and quantity has been rewritten, and the entire curve shifts. The reason learners split is that both cases end with a different quantity bought, so the outcome looks identical; the difference lives entirely in the cause. Here is the discipline for exam day. Read the scenario and underline the first event. Beef price rises — that is a related good, so chicken's demand curve shifts right, and only THEN does chicken's price rise as the market finds its new equilibrium. Chicken price rises because of a supply problem — that is chicken's own price changing, so demand does not shift; the market moves along the demand curve to the new crossing point. Notice the deeper logic: a curve is an entire table of if-then promises — if the price were ten rand, buyers would take this much; if twenty, that much. The product's own price selects a row from the table; everything else rewrites the table. Say it that way in your head — select a row, or rewrite the table — and the fifty-fifty split becomes a hundred percent.

**Thandi:** Third one: if GDP goes up, does that mean South Africans are actually better off? Some of us argued about this after the news.

**Tutor:** The argument you had is precisely the essay the examiner wants, so let me arm both sides and then settle it. The yes side: rising GDP means more final goods and services were produced inside our borders — generally more jobs, more income flowing through the circular flow, more tax revenue for clinics and grants. It is genuinely good news, and countries with persistently rising GDP do see poverty fall over time. The no side, in four blind spots. First, population: if GDP rises two percent while the population rises two percent, GDP per person — the number that tracks living standards — has not moved at all; always divide before celebrating. Second, distribution: GDP is a total, not a sharing rule; the total can rise while most households see none of it, and South Africa's inequality makes this blind spot especially wide here. Third, the uncounted economy: the grandmother's childcare, the vegetable garden, much informal trading — real production, invisible to the statisticians, so GDP understates what poorer communities actually produce. Fourth, costs booked as gains: if a factory raises output while polluting a river, the output enters GDP and the poisoned river enters nowhere. The mature conclusion: GDP measures the SIZE of production, not the QUALITY of life, so rising GDP is necessary good news but never sufficient — check per capita, check distribution, check what the number cannot see. That paragraph, structured exactly so, is full marks.

**Thandi:** Last one: the business cycle sounds automatic — recession comes, recovery comes. So why does anyone bother trying to fix the economy? Why not just wait?

**Tutor:** Because the wave is real but the waiting is not free — and both halves of that sentence earn marks. The cycle does have an internal rhythm: in prosperity, optimism stretches into over-borrowing and over-building; the excess corrects; the correction overshoots into recession; low prices and low interest rates eventually tempt spenders back, and recovery begins. In that sense, yes, upswings have always followed downswings. But look at what the waiting costs while the wave turns. Workers retrenched in a recession lose more than months of wages — skills rust, confidence erodes, and some never re-enter, which permanently lowers the economy's capacity; the production possibility curve you revised can actually creep inward if factories close and investment stalls. A short recession can therefore leave a long scar, and this is exactly why governments and the Reserve Bank lean against the cycle rather than watch it: lifting spending and cutting interest rates in the downswing to shorten it, cooling excess in the upswing to soften the next fall. The indicators are the reason timing is possible at all — headlights like building plans passed give warning before the turn, which is why the exam insists you classify leading, coincident and lagging correctly rather than merely list them. So the answer to why not just wait is: because people are not spectators of the wave, they are in the water — and policy exists to keep the deep parts short. Understanding that is understanding why the whole subject matters.
