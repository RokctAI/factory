# Part 1 — Expert

This session is the first of two revision sweeps for the year's examination — the whole of terms one and two, reorganised into the four structures the paper actually tests: the economic problem and the curve that draws it; the circular flow and the national numbers; demand, supply and equilibrium; and markets, value and the business cycle. Nothing here is new; everything here is the exam-day version — compressed, ordered, and rebuilt around the questions markers reward.

## Subtopic: Scarcity, Choice and the Economic Problem

Start where the subject starts. Economics is the study of how societies use SCARCE resources to satisfy UNLIMITED wants — and scarcity is a permanent condition, not a temporary shortage. A drought ends; scarcity never does, because wants always outrun means. From scarcity follows CHOICE, and from choice follows OPPORTUNITY COST: the value of the NEXT BEST alternative given up. Not everything given up — the single best thing. The learner who spends Saturday at soccer practice gives up many things, but the opportunity cost is only the most valued one, perhaps the shift at the shop that paid eighty rand.

Every economy must answer THREE questions: WHAT to produce, HOW to produce it, and FOR WHOM. Keep the branches straight too: MICROECONOMICS studies the individual unit — one household, one firm, one market — while MACROECONOMICS studies the totals: national production, unemployment, the general price level.

Now the diagram that draws all of this, described so you can rebuild it anywhere. The PRODUCTION POSSIBILITY CURVE: one axis carries one product, say maize; the other axis carries another, say clothing. The curve bows outward from one axis to the other, showing every MAXIMUM combination the economy can produce with its resources fully and efficiently used. A point ON the curve is efficient. A point INSIDE the curve is underutilisation — unemployed workers, idle factories, exactly South Africa's situation with unemployment above thirty percent. A point BEYOND the curve is unattainable today. And the whole curve SHIFTS outward when resources grow or technology improves — that outward shift is economic growth drawn in one movement. The downward slope of the curve IS opportunity cost: producing more maize means walking along the curve and surrendering clothing.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: scarcity distinguished from shortage, opportunity cost defined as the next best alternative, the three questions, and every position on and around the curve read correctly.

## Subtopic: The Circular Flow and the National Numbers

The circular flow begins with two participants. HOUSEHOLDS own the factors of production and sell them to FIRMS on the factor market, earning income; firms combine the factors into goods and services and sell them back to households on the product market. Money flows one way around the circle, goods and factors flow the other.

Then the circle springs LEAKS and gains INJECTIONS — and the exam wants three of each. Leakages, money leaving the flow: SAVINGS, TAXES, and spending on IMPORTS. Injections, money entering: INVESTMENT, GOVERNMENT SPENDING, and EXPORT earnings. When injections exceed leakages the flow grows; when leakages win, it shrinks. Adding GOVERNMENT brings taxes in and spending out; adding the FOREIGN SECTOR opens the economy, and South Africa is very open — the petrol price alone proves how directly the world reaches our circle.

The national numbers measure the flow. GDP — gross domestic product — is the total value of all FINAL goods and services produced WITHIN the country's borders in one year. Two traps live in that definition. Final goods only: counting the flour and the bread double-counts the flour. Within the borders: GDP obeys the WHERE-rule, so a foreign-owned mine in Limpopo counts in South Africa's GDP. GNI obeys the WHO-rule — it follows factor income home to the nation that owns the factors, adding what South Africans earn abroad and subtracting what foreigners earn here. Divide by the population for PER CAPITA figures, which allow comparison — but recall the limits of the number: it misses unpaid household work, the informal sector's full extent, the distribution of income, and the environment's costs.

The questions for this section are with you now: three leakages against three injections, the where-rule against the who-rule, and the big number's blind spots.

## Subtopic: Demand, Supply and Equilibrium

The LAW OF DEMAND: as price rises, quantity demanded falls, other things equal — pictured as a curve sloping DOWNWARD from top left to bottom right. The LAW OF SUPPLY: as price rises, quantity supplied rises — a curve sloping UPWARD from bottom left to top right. Where they cross is EQUILIBRIUM: the one price where quantity demanded equals quantity supplied, the market cleared, no queue and no pile.

Now the distinction that decides most marks: a MOVEMENT along a curve versus a SHIFT of the whole curve. Price changes cause movements ALONG; everything else — income, tastes, prices of related goods, number of buyers, costs, technology, weather — shifts the WHOLE curve. Say the sentence precisely: a change in price never shifts the curve of the same product.

Work the classic example verbally. A drought strikes maize. Supply shifts LEFT — at every price, less is offered. At the old price there is now excess demand: buyers want more than farmers can offer, and the shortage bids the price UP. The market slides along the demand curve to a new equilibrium: higher price, lower quantity. Every shock works this same four-step logic: name the curve that shifts, name the direction, name the excess at the old price, name the new equilibrium.

And when a table of prices and quantities appears, the table is the boss: equilibrium sits in the row where the two quantity columns are equal; above that price read off EXCESS SUPPLY as quantity supplied minus quantity demanded; below it, excess demand. State the units, state the row, and the marks follow.

Stop for this section's questions now — laws stated, movement against shift, the drought worked in four steps, and a table read row by row.

## Subtopic: Markets, Value and the Business Cycle

Three ideas the paper loves to blur. UTILITY is the satisfaction a good gives — subjective, personal. VALUE is the worth attached to it, driven by utility and scarcity together, which is why water — essential but abundant — usually carries a low price while diamonds — trivial but scarce — carry a high one. PRICE is value expressed in money on a market.

Markets themselves get classified. A PERFECT market has many buyers and sellers, identical products, full information and free entry, so no single participant sets the price — fresh produce markets come close. IMPERFECT markets break one or more conditions: a MONOPOLY has one seller, an OLIGOPOLY a few large ones, and monopolistic competition many sellers with differentiated products. Markets perform functions worth listing: they set prices, spread information, allocate resources, and link buyers to sellers across the world — electronics now let one world market operate from a phone in a pocket.

Then the economy's heartbeat. The BUSINESS CYCLE runs four phases in order: RECOVERY, when production and employment begin rising; PROSPERITY, the peak of activity; RECESSION, when spending, output and employment fall; and DEPRESSION, the deep trough. Indicators are the instruments that track it: LEADING indicators — like building plans passed — change before the economy turns, the headlights; COINCIDENT indicators move with it, the speedometer; LAGGING indicators — like unemployment — confirm the turn afterwards, the rearview mirror. Causes divide into forces inside the economy, like waves of optimism and spending, and shocks from outside, like world commodity prices — and for South Africa, the world's appetite for our minerals swings our cycle hard.

The final questions of this part are with you now: utility, value and price separated; perfect against imperfect markets; the four phases in order; and each indicator matched to its mirror, window or dial.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same year through things you can hold: a wallet at month-end, one street on a Friday, and a cooler box at a taxi rank.

## Subtopic: One Wallet, Three Questions

Month-end. The wallet holds four hundred rand and the list holds six hundred rands' worth of needs. That gap is the whole subject of economics — not a bad month, a permanent condition. There is never enough for everything; there is only choosing.

And every choice has a price tag behind the price tag. Spend the four hundred on new school shoes, and the real cost is not the number on the box — it is the BEST thing those rands would otherwise have done, maybe the data bundle and the taxi fares. Economists call that the opportunity cost, and the rule is strict: count only the next best thing given up, not the whole wish list. One choice, one sacrifice, the best one forgone.

A household answers three questions with that wallet: WHAT to buy, HOW to stretch it, WHO gets what. A country answers the same three with everything it has — what to produce, how, and for whom. Same problem, bigger wallet.

Now picture the country as one oven that can bake two things — bread and cakes. Bake nothing but bread, and cakes are zero. Every extra cake costs some bread: that trade-off, drawn as a curve bowing from the bread axis to the cake axis, is the production possibility curve. Baking ON the curve means the oven is fully used. Baking INSIDE it means racks stand empty — workers without work, machines standing still, the country producing less than it could. Points BEYOND the oven's limit are dream days — until you build a bigger oven: more resources, better technology, and the whole curve moves outward. That outward move has a name: growth.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on this section are coming to you right now: the gap in the wallet, the price tag behind the price tag, and the oven read inside, on and beyond.

## Subtopic: The Circle of Money on One Street

Friday afternoon on one street. The factory pays wages; workers walk to the spaza and the salon and spend; the spaza and the salon use the takings to pay their own workers and order stock; those workers spend again. Money moves in a circle — households give firms their work, firms give households wages, and the wages come straight back over the counters. That circle is the economy.

But watch the water level in the circle, because three drains empty it and three taps refill it. Drains: the rands SAVED under the mattress or in the bank, the rands TAXED away, and the rands spent on IMPORTED goods — all three leave the street. Taps: firms INVESTING in new equipment, GOVERNMENT spending on the clinic and the road, and EXPORT money arriving when the street sells to the world. Taps beating drains: the street gets busier. Drains beating taps: quieter shops, shorter shifts.

Now count what the street made in a year — every haircut, every loaf, every repair — at its selling price. Count only FINISHED things: the loaf counts, the flour inside it does not, or the flour is counted twice. That total, done for the whole country, is GDP: everything produced inside the borders, whoever owns the business. Follow the profits home instead — what our people earn anywhere, minus what foreign owners take out — and you have GNI. And remember what the big number never sees: the grandmother's unpaid childcare, who actually got the money, and the smoke over the factory.

Take this section's questions now — three drains, three taps, the finished-goods rule, and what the total misses.

## Subtopic: The Argument That Sets the Price, and the Economy That Breathes

A cooler box of cold drinks at the taxi rank on a hot day. Price them at thirty rand and they sit there sweating — buyers walk past, the pile grows, and the pile pushes the price down. Price them at five rand and they vanish before noon — the queue is the shortage, and the shortage pulls the price up. Somewhere between sits the price where the last drink meets the last thirsty customer: no pile, no queue. The market found equilibrium without a committee — the argument between buyers and sellers settled it.

Two different things can happen to that cooler box, and the exam lives on telling them apart. If the seller changes the PRICE, buyers simply move along their own willingness — that is movement ALONG the curve. But if the day turns cold, or a second seller arrives, or the wholesaler's costs jump, the whole situation shifts — the entire curve moves, and a new argument settles at a new price. Price moves you along; everything else moves the curve.

Now lift your eyes from the rank to the whole economy, and notice that it breathes. Some years everything inhales: shops hire, overtime returns, building sites multiply — recovery rising into prosperity. Then the exhale: orders thin, shifts shorten, retrenchment letters arrive — recession sinking toward depression, until the breath turns again. Four phases, always in order, and instruments to read them by: building plans passed are headlights showing the road ahead; today's sales are the speedometer; unemployment is the rearview mirror, confirming where the economy has already been.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: the cooler box priced too high and too low, movement against shift told apart in one sentence, and the four breaths of the economy matched to headlights, speedometer and mirror. Revise the structures, not the pages — the structures are what the paper pays.
