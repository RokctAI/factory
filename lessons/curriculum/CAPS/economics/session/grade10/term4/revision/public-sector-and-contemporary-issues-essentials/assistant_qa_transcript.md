**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! First: if ceilings cause queues and black markets, why does any government ever set a maximum price? It sounds like a policy that only breaks things.

**Tutor:** Because the alternative it prevents can be worse than the damage it causes — and the exam wants you weighing exactly that trade, not reciting one side. Think about when ceilings get reached for: a drought doubles the price of water, a crisis triples the price of paraffin, rents in a city climb beyond what nurses and teachers earn. Left alone, the market rations by price — whoever can pay, gets; whoever cannot, goes without — and for essentials, going without means real harm. A ceiling swaps rationing by price for rationing by queue, which at least does not exclude the poor automatically. That is the genuine case for it. Now the honest costs, which you already know: the shortage is permanent for as long as the ceiling binds, queues waste hours, black markets charge the desperate more than the market ever did, and — the subtle one — suppliers quietly exit or stop maintaining quality, so the problem deepens over time. So the mature evaluation runs: a ceiling is defensible as a SHORT-TERM shield for essentials during a shock, and it fails as a long-term policy because it treats the price while worsening the supply. The lasting fix is always on the supply side — more housing built, more water infrastructure, more competition — with the ceiling, if used at all, buying time. Costs, benefits, conditions: that frame converts every intervention question in this topic into marks.

**Thandi:** Next one: why does the syllabus make us learn the whole barter-to-money story? Nobody is swapping goats anymore — is it just history for history's sake?

**Tutor:** It reads as history, but it is actually the sharpest tool you own for understanding the present — because every stage of that story is still running somewhere around you. Start with the double coincidence of wants: the moment you see why barter fails, you understand what money IS — not notes and coins, but whatever solves that failure. And that definition is being tested right now, in your pocket: airtime transfers, bank apps, stokvel records — each is money to the exact degree that it does the four jobs: go-between, measuring stick, storage jar, promise-keeper. When inflation runs hot in a country, watch which job breaks first: money keeps working as a go-between long after it fails as a storage jar, and people flee to dollars or goods — the story explains crises the news cannot. The stages also explain development itself: a community stuck near self-sufficiency — growing its own food, trading little — is poor for reasons the story names precisely: no specialisation, no surplus, thin markets. That is not ancient history; that is parts of our own country today, and it is why building roads, connecting markets and banking the unbanked are development policy — they move communities along the stages. So when the exam asks for the evolution of markets, tell it as the syllabus wants — stages in order, problems solved — but understand you are describing the machinery of why some places prosper and others wait. That understanding is the difference between memorising the story and owning it.

**Thandi:** Third: my cousin says unions and all these labour laws are why firms won't hire. Is that true, or is it more complicated?

**Tutor:** It is more complicated, and the complication is where the marks — and the truth — live. Give the claim its due first: labour law does raise the cost and reduce the flexibility of hiring. A firm that cannot easily retrench in a downturn hires more cautiously in the upturn; a bargaining council wage set for large firms can price a small township workshop out of formal hiring altogether. Those effects are real, documented, and worth two honest sentences in any essay. But now test the claim against the counter-evidence. First, the law exists because the alternative was tested for a century and failed the workers utterly: unequal bargaining power is not a theory — one job, three hundred applicants makes the point arithmetically. Second, look at WHERE our unemployment sits: overwhelmingly among the young and unskilled who have never held a formal job. Their problem is not that labour law made their jobs expensive; it is that the economy, growing slowly and capital-intensively, generates too few entry-level posts — the structural diagnosis you revised. Scrapping the Basic Conditions Act would not conjure those posts; it would mainly strip protection from those already working. Third, international comparison: countries with strong labour law and low unemployment exist in numbers, which tells you the law is one variable among many, not the master switch. The defensible conclusion: labour regulation is a genuine cost worth designing carefully — especially for small firms — but South Africa's unemployment is primarily a growth and skills problem, and any answer blaming a single villain has misread the evidence. Hold both halves and you outscore both angry sides.

**Thandi:** Last one, and it's the one people whisper: is redress still needed this many years after apartheid, or should everything just be equal treatment from here?

**Tutor:** Answer it the way economics answers everything: with mechanisms, not slogans. The case for equal treatment from here rests on an assumption — that removing the discriminatory laws reset the race. So test the assumption against how economies actually transmit advantage. Assets compound: a family that could own land and a house in 1970 has collateral, inheritance and equity in 2026; a family forbidden to own transmits the zero forward, compounding just as faithfully. Networks hire: first jobs flow through contacts, and exclusion from industries for generations empties exactly those networks. Schools feed on local wealth: municipalities with thin rate bases fund thinner classrooms, so yesterday's spatial exclusion sets today's matric results. None of these mechanisms requires a single present-day act of discrimination to keep running — they are momentum, and momentum does not stop because the push stopped. That is the economic case that formally equal treatment, applied to an unequal starting grid, preserves the grid. Now the other half, which the syllabus itself demands: redress is judged by RESULTS, not intentions. An instrument that enriches a narrow few, or a programme that awards assets without the finance and skills to use them, fails the very people it names — the field without seed money. So the mature position is not redress forever, unexamined, nor equal treatment now, unexamined: it is redress targeted at the mechanisms — assets, skills, networks, geography — measured honestly, corrected when captured, and designed to make itself unnecessary. That is also, not by coincidence, exactly the answer that earns the evaluation marks.
