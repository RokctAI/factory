Welcome, student. Today we draw the single most important diagram in all of Economics: the circular flow. By the end of this lesson you will build the flow for an open economy with four participants — households, firms, government and the foreign sector — and you will classify savings, taxes and imports as leakages, and investment, government spending and exports as injections.

## Subtopic: The Two Original Participants — Households and Firms

Every economy, no matter how complicated, begins with two participants: households and firms.

Households are the owners of the factors of production. Remember them from earlier work: natural resources, labour, capital and entrepreneurship. Every factor ultimately belongs to someone sitting in a household — your family supplies labour when someone goes to work; a family that owns land supplies natural resources; savings that fund machines are capital; the aunt who runs her own business supplies entrepreneurship.

Firms are the production units. They take those factors and combine them to produce the goods and services we consume.

Now watch the circle form. Households supply factors of production to firms through the factor market. In return, firms pay factor incomes: wages for labour, rent for natural resources, interest for capital, and profit for entrepreneurship. That is the first loop: factors flow one way, income flows back the other way.

But households do not eat wages — they spend them. Households use their income to buy goods and services from firms through the goods market. That spending becomes the firms' revenue, which firms use to pay for factors again... and the circle turns.

Notice there are two kinds of flow travelling in opposite directions. Real flows are the physical things: factors of production moving to firms, goods and services moving to households. Money flows are the payments travelling the opposite way: income to households, spending to firms. Every real flow has a money flow running against it. This two-participant model is called a simple closed economy, and if you can draw it with arrows labelled correctly, you have the skeleton for everything that follows.

## Subtopic: Leakages and Injections — Why the Circle Shrinks and Grows

If households spent every cent they earned, the circle would spin at a constant size forever. But they do not — and here the model becomes realistic.

Suppose households save part of their income at the bank. That money leaves the spending stream — it is income earned but not passed back to firms. We call any such escape a leakage, or a withdrawal. Savings is our first leakage.

Does saved money vanish? No — and this is the beautiful part. Banks and other financial institutions lend those savings to firms, which use them to buy machinery, buildings and equipment. That spending on capital goods is called investment, and it pumps money back into the flow. Any such re-entry is called an injection. Savings leak out; investment injects back in — connected through the financial sector.

Why do leakages and injections matter so much? Because they decide whether the whole economy grows or shrinks. If total leakages are greater than total injections, less money returns to firms each round: production falls, incomes fall, the circle contracts. If injections exceed leakages, the circle expands — more spending, more production, more income. When they are equal, the economy is in equilibrium. Hold onto that comparison; it returns in Grade 11 and Grade 12 macroeconomics again and again.

A common pitfall: learners think a leakage is "bad" and an injection is "good". Not so — savings finance tomorrow's investment. Leakage and injection describe direction of flow, not virtue.

## Subtopic: Adding Government — Taxes In, Spending Out

Now bring in the third participant: the government. The government interacts with both households and firms, and it brings one leakage and one injection of its own.

First, the leakage: taxes. Households pay personal income tax; firms pay company tax. That money is taken out of the spending stream — households and firms cannot spend what SARS has collected. Taxes are therefore a leakage, exactly like savings.

But government does not sit on the money. It flows back through government spending: paying teachers and nurses, building roads and schools, buying goods and services from firms, and paying social grants to households. Government spending is an injection — money pumped back into the circle.

Government also supplies public goods and services — things like policing, street lighting and public healthcare — to both households and firms. So on your diagram, the government sits between households and firms with arrows both ways: taxes flowing in from both, spending and services flowing out to both.

Pause and take stock. We now have two leakages — savings and taxes — and two injections — investment and government spending. An economy with households, firms and government, but no dealings with other countries, is still called a closed economy. One participant remains to open it up.

## Subtopic: The Foreign Sector — Opening the Economy

The fourth participant is the foreign sector — all the countries South Africa trades with. Adding it turns our closed economy into an open economy, which is what South Africa really is.

Think about what happens when South Africans buy imported goods — cellphones from China, cars from Germany. Money earned inside South Africa is paid to firms outside South Africa. That spending leaks out of our circular flow. Imports are a leakage.

Now reverse it. When other countries buy South African goods — our gold, our fruit, our wine — foreign money flows into South African firms. Exports are an injection.

Let us now assemble the complete picture, and this is the exam skill: the full open-economy circular flow has four participants — households, firms, government, and the foreign sector — connected through three markets: the factor market, the goods market, and the financial sector standing between savers and investors.

And the master lists you must be able to produce instantly. The three leakages: savings, taxes, imports. The three injections: investment, government spending, exports. Here is a check to see if you truly understand rather than memorise: every leakage has a matching injection through the same channel. Savings leak out through the financial sector; investment returns through it. Taxes leak out through government; government spending returns. Import payments leave through the foreign sector; export earnings return. Three doors, each with traffic in both directions.

Verify your classification skill with this quick test. A household buys a Japanese television — leakage, an import. The government builds a new clinic — injection, government spending. A firm ships wine to Britain — injection, an export. A family puts R500 into a savings account — leakage, savings. If you got all four, you are ready for the practice questions — and ready for the Grade 11 quantitative version of this model, where these flows get numbers attached.
