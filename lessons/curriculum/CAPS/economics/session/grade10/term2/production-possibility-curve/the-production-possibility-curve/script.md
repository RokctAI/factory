# Part 1 — Expert

Term one defined scarcity and opportunity cost in words; today they get their picture. The PRODUCTION POSSIBILITY CURVE — the PPC — draws the boundary of what an economy can produce with everything it has, and in one diagram displays scarcity, choice, opportunity cost, efficiency, unemployment and growth. It is the syllabus's chosen model for making the basic economic problem visible — and a reliable source of diagram and interpretation marks in every examination that touches it.

## Subtopic: The Curve That Draws Scarcity

The model rests on deliberate simplifications — state them, because examiners ask for the assumptions. The economy produces only TWO goods; its RESOURCES are fixed in quantity and quality; TECHNOLOGY is fixed; and all resources are FULLY and efficiently employed. Under those assumptions, every possible division of the economy's effort between the two goods can be listed.

Take a farming economy dividing fixed land and labour between maize and vegetables. The schedule: option A — no maize, 50 tonnes of vegetables; B — 10 maize, 45 vegetables; C — 20 and 38; D — 30 and 28; E — 40 and 15; F — 50 maize, no vegetables. Plot vegetables on one axis, maize on the other; join the points, and the PRODUCTION POSSIBILITY CURVE appears: a boundary bowing outward from axis to axis.

Read what the curve IS: every point ON it is a maximum — the most of one good obtainable given the chosen amount of the other, with everything employed. The curve is scarcity drawn: the economy can choose any point on it, but nothing beyond it. And choice is drawn too: moving along the curve from B to C means MORE maize and FEWER vegetables — the gain on one axis is paid for on the other. There is no movement along the curve without giving something up; the diagram refuses free lunches by construction.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: the assumptions in full, the schedule plotted, and why every point on the curve is a maximum.

## Subtopic: Reading the Points — On, Inside, Beyond

Three zones, three meanings — the examination's favourite reading exercise.

ON the curve: EFFICIENT and attainable. All resources employed, none wasted; the economy is getting everything possible from what it has. Which point on the curve is "best" the model cannot say — that is society's choice among efficient options: more food or more manufactured goods, the WHAT question from term one, drawn as a position on a boundary.

INSIDE the curve: ATTAINABLE but INEFFICIENT. Producing, say, 20 maize and only 28 vegetables when the curve offers 38 at that maize level means resources stand idle or misused — workers unemployed, land fallow, machines silent. The economy is paying scarcity's price without collecting scarcity's output. Inside points are where recessions and unemployment live on this diagram.

BEYOND the curve: UNATTAINABLE — for now. No arrangement of current resources and technology reaches it. Desirable, but out of reach until the curve itself moves.

And along the curve, OPPORTUNITY COST becomes measurable. From A to B, gaining the first 10 maize costs 5 vegetables; from B to C, the next 10 maize costs 7; then 10, then 13, then 15. The cost of maize RISES as more is produced — INCREASING opportunity cost, and the reason the curve bows outward: resources are not equally suited to all uses. The first fields switched to maize are those best for maize; push further and you convert land and hands that grew vegetables brilliantly and grow maize poorly — each extra tonne of maize costs more vegetables than the last. A straight-line PPC would claim resources are perfectly interchangeable; the bowed curve tells the truer story.

Stop for this section's questions now — the three zones classified, and opportunity cost computed between points, with the bowing explained.

## Subtopic: What Moves the Curve

The curve is a boundary for a MOMENT — given today's resources and technology. Change those, and the boundary itself moves. The syllabus sorts the movers into internal and external factors.

INTERNAL factors — arising within the economy: the quantity of resources (population growth and labour force expansion; new capital accumulated through investment; new resource discoveries); the QUALITY of resources (education and training raising labour's skill; better management); TECHNOLOGY (new methods getting more output from the same inputs); and efficiency reforms that shift practice closer to best use. Improvements shift the PPC OUTWARD — the economy can now reach combinations that yesterday were unattainable. That outward shift IS economic growth, drawn: not a movement along the boundary, but the boundary itself advancing.

EXTERNAL factors — arriving from outside: weather and climate — rain expands the agricultural frontier, drought pulls it in; international conditions — access to world markets, world prices for exports, imported technology and capital; and shocks — pandemics, wars, disasters destroying resources. Favourable externals push the curve outward; destruction and loss drag it INWARD — a smaller boundary, fewer possibilities, the economy poorer in capacity.

Keep the two movements rigorously apart, because confusing them is the topic's classic error: a movement ALONG the curve is a reallocation — more maize, fewer vegetables, same capacity; a SHIFT of the curve is a change in capacity itself. Unemployment falling moves the economy from inside toward the curve — that is recovery, not growth; the curve advancing is growth. Three different improvements, three different pictures.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — internal and external movers sorted, outward against inward, and along-versus-shift-versus-toward kept strictly apart.

## Subtopic: Consequences of Inefficiencies

The syllabus ends the topic where South Africa lives: what it COSTS to produce inside the curve.

Name the gap first. An economy at an inside point produces less than its capacity — and the distance to the curve is measurable loss: output that could exist and does not. Every idle worker, unused machine and fallow field is a piece of that gap — and the gap is not abstract: it is houses unbuilt, food ungrown, services undelivered, with the resources to deliver them standing ready.

Then the consequences, chained. Lost output means lost INCOMES — the wages the unemployed do not earn, the profits idle capacity does not generate. Lost incomes mean lost SPENDING — the circular flow turning smaller. Lost spending and profit mean lost TAX REVENUE — the state poorer exactly where social needs are greatest. Persistent idleness ERODES the resources themselves: skills decay in long unemployment, machines rust, the discouraged stop seeking — today's inside point quietly pulls tomorrow's curve inward. And the social consequences the subject must name: poverty, inequality and exclusion concentrated among those the idle resources represent — with South Africa's unemployment rate standing as the country's visible inside-the-curve distance.

The policy reading follows: an economy inside its curve can grow output WITHOUT new resources — by employing what it has: absorbing the unemployed, using idle capacity, matching skills to work. That is recovery toward the boundary — the cheapest growth available. Reaching BEYOND today's curve then needs the shifters: investment, education, technology. Two tasks, in order: get to the curve; then move the curve.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the gap named, the chain of consequences traced, and the two policy tasks distinguished.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same curve from a bakery before dawn — one oven, two breads, and every big idea in the topic baked into a day's choices.

## Subtopic: One Oven, Two Breads

A bakery has one oven, two bakers, and one day. It can bake white loaves, brown loaves, or some of each — and the day's possibilities make a little menu. All white: 50 whites, no browns. Mostly white: 45 whites and 10 browns. Balanced-ish: 38 and 20. More brown: 28 and 30. Nearly all brown: 15 and 40. All brown: none and 50.

Notice the iron rule inside the menu: every extra brown loaf is PAID FOR in whites. The oven's day is fixed; the bakers' hands are fixed — push one bread up and the other must come down. That's scarcity, not as a lecture but as a menu: the day offers many combinations, but every one of them is a trade.

Draw the menu as a picture — whites up one side, browns along the bottom, each combination a dot, the dots joined — and you get a curved boundary line: the edge of the possible day. Everything the bakery can fully-bustle its way to lies ON that line. That line is the production possibility curve, and a whole country's version is the same idea with bigger ovens: fixed resources, two products for simplicity, and a boundary that says "this much, no more — choose your mix."

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the menu are coming to you right now: the fixed oven, the trades between breads, and what the boundary line means.

## Subtopic: Lazy Days and Dream Days

Now the three kinds of day the picture sorts — and you've seen all three in real life.

The FULL day: everything humming — ovens never cold, both bakers busy, flour flowing. That day lands ON the line: the most the bakery can do, in whatever mix it chose. No point on the line is "the right one" — more white or more brown is the owner's call; the line only promises each choice is a maximum.

The LAZY day: one baker off sick, the oven idle an hour between batches, a sack of flour forgotten. That day lands INSIDE the line — say 20 browns but only 28 whites when the full day offered 38. Nothing about the bakery shrank; it just didn't use what it had. Inside the line is waste's address — and for a country, inside the line is where unemployment lives: hands ready, work undone.

The DREAM day: 50 whites AND 40 browns — lovely, and impossible. That combination lies BEYOND the line: no honest arrangement of this oven and these hands reaches it. Not a failure — just tomorrow's target, waiting for the bakery itself to change.

And one more secret the menu whispers: the browns get more expensive as you bake more of them. The first few browns cost only 5 whites; the last batch costs 15. Why? The bakers and pans best suited to brown get used first; push further and you're converting white-bread specialists to brown-bread work they do slowly. Resources aren't all-purpose — that's why the boundary bows outward like a full sail instead of running straight.

Your questions for this part are up now: full, lazy and dream days placed on the picture, and the rising price of brown explained.

## Subtopic: Growing the Oven

Last piece: how dream days become possible — and what lazy days really cost.

The line moved for the bakery the day it bought a second oven. Suddenly every mix improved: more whites AND more browns within reach — yesterday's impossible combinations now on the menu. The boundary itself stepped outward. That's growth — and everything that grows a country works the same way: more workers and machines (a bigger oven), better-trained hands (faster bakers), better methods (a smarter recipe), and friendlier weather outside (the morning market buying everything). And the line can step BACKWARD too: the oven breaks, a baker emigrates, drought hits the wheat — the possible day shrinks, and no cleverness of mix can bake what capacity lost.

Keep the three improvements apart, because they look similar and are not: choosing a different MIX is walking along the line — no growth, just reallocation. Fixing a lazy day is walking from inside back TO the line — recovery: more of everything, using only what was always there. And the line itself advancing — that alone is growth.

Now the sharpest lesson for our own country: a bakery that runs lazy days doesn't just lose today's loaves. The unearned wages don't get spent at the spaza; the unsold bread pays no tax toward the clinic; and bakers who stand idle too long lose their speed — tomorrow's full day gets smaller because today was lazy. That's South Africa's unemployment, drawn as a distance: millions of hands inside the line, and the first, cheapest growth is simply walking back to the boundary — employing what we already have. THEN we grow the oven.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: line walks, line returns, and line moves told apart — and the cost of the lazy day counted in full. Close this, and term two's whole story — markets, intervention, and the boundary of the possible — is yours.
