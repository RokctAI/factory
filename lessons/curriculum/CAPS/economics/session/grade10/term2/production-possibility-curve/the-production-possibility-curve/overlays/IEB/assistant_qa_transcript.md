**Thandi:** Right, class questions! The one I heard most: real economies make thousands of goods, not two. Doesn't the two-goods assumption make the whole model useless?

**Tutor:** It makes the model USABLE — that is the difference between a model and a photograph. Try drawing a diagram with ten thousand axes and you will produce nothing anyone can read; collapse the economy into two goods and every deep truth survives the compression: fixed capacity, unavoidable trade-offs, rising opportunity cost, the gap between what we could produce and what we do. In practice the two axes usually stand for two CATEGORIES — consumer goods against capital goods is the classic pairing, and it carries a real national decision: enjoy more today, or build the machines that expand tomorrow's boundary. So treat the assumption the way a map treats scale. A map that showed every pebble would be useless; a map that simplifies honestly gets you home. The PPC is a map of scarcity, not a census of products.

***

**Thandi:** Next: if points on the curve are called efficient, is the diagram telling us which point the country SHOULD pick? Everyone in class chose a different favourite.

**Tutor:** And every one of you was equally right, because the diagram refuses to vote. Efficiency, in this model, only means no waste: on the boundary, producing more wheat must cost some wool, full stop. Which efficient point is BEST is a question about what society values — more food now or more clothing, more consumer comfort or more capital for the future — and that answer comes from people, through markets and through politics, not from geometry. This is the line between positive economics, which describes what is possible, and choices about what is desirable. The curve hands society an honest menu and says: everything here is attainable without waste; now argue about the mix. The arguing is real economics too — but the diagram's job ends at drawing the menu truthfully.

***

**Thandi:** This one caused a proper debate: a factory hires two hundred unemployed workers and output jumps. My friend swears that's economic growth. I say it isn't. Who wins?

**Tutor:** You do — and the distinction you have both just fought over is one worth mastering. Hiring idle workers moves the economy from an inside point TOWARD the boundary: recovery, more output squeezed from capacity that already existed. Growth is the boundary ITSELF advancing — new machines, better skills, new technology expanding what full employment could deliver. The reason the distinction matters is practical: the two problems have different medicines. An economy deep inside its curve needs demand, hiring and matching — putting existing hands to work. An economy already hugging its curve can only grow through investment and education. Prescribe the growth medicine to a recovery problem, or the reverse, and you waste years. Same jump in output, two different stories on the diagram — and only one of them moved the line.

***

**Thandi:** Last question: the lesson says idle resources today shrink tomorrow's curve. That sounds dramatic — how does standing still actually pull the boundary backwards?

**Tutor:** Because productive capacity is alive, and living things decay without use. Walk the chain slowly. A machinist who sits jobless for three years loses speed, confidence and the habits of the trade — the labour resource has degraded even though the person is still there. Machines left idle rust, and firms stop replacing them — the capital stock quietly shrinks. Young people who watch a jobless town stop training for work they never see — the future skills pipeline thins. And the discouraged eventually stop searching altogether, which shrinks the effective labour force itself. Each of those is a resource becoming smaller or worse, and resources are exactly what position the boundary. That is the cruellest arithmetic of long unemployment: the inside point does not wait patiently for rescue — it eats the curve. And it is why the lesson insists on the order of tasks: walk back to the boundary quickly, before the boundary walks back to you.
