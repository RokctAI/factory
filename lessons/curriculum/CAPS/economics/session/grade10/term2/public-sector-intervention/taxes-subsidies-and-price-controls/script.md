# Part 1 — Expert

The market forms prices, allocates, informs, rations and connects — and sometimes society overrules it. PUBLIC SECTOR INTERVENTION is the state stepping into markets deliberately: taxing some goods dearer, subsidising others cheaper, capping some prices, propping others up, and setting the price of labour itself through minimum wages. Today: each method defined, its market diagram consequences traced honestly — including the side effects — and the welfare instruments that bypass the market altogether. Every tool here is tested with the same discipline: what was intended, what the diagram predicts, what else follows.

## Subtopic: Why the State Steps In — and With What

The market's rationing answer — those willing and able to pay — is sometimes intolerable to society: essentials priced beyond the poor; and the market misfires in known ways: externalities uncharged (the smoke nobody pays for), harmful goods overconsumed, merit goods underconsumed, and incomes so unequal that FOR WHOM is answered brutally. Intervention is the public sector's response, and Grade 10 studies its main instruments in three families.

INDIRECT TAXES: taxes levied on goods and services rather than directly on income — VAT on most purchases; excise ("sin") taxes on alcohol and tobacco; the fuel levy. They raise revenue AND deliberately change behaviour: taxing a good makes it dearer, and dearer means less consumed — which is the point with cigarettes. SUBSIDIES: the tax in reverse — the state pays part of the cost of producing or providing a good, making it cheaper than the market alone would: subsidised public transport, agricultural support, free basic water's funded allocation. WELFARE: direct provision and transfers that bypass prices entirely — social grants placing buying power in poor households, public healthcare and education provided free or below cost at the point of use.

And the fourth family, today's centrepiece: PRICE CONTROLS — the state legislating the price itself: maximum prices to protect buyers, minimum prices to protect sellers, and the minimum wage to protect workers. Each is a direct overrule of the crossing point — which is why each has consequences the diagram exposes.

## Subtopic: Taxes and Subsidies on the Diagram

An indirect tax works through the SUPPLY curve. The tax is a cost sellers must hand to the state per unit — so at every quantity, sellers now need a higher price to offer what they offered before. The supply curve shifts LEFTWARD (equivalently, upward by the tax). Consequences at the new crossing: equilibrium price HIGHER, equilibrium quantity LOWER. Note the sharing: price typically rises by LESS than the full tax — sellers absorb part, buyers pay part; the burden splits between them. For a sin tax, the fall in quantity IS the objective: fewer cigarettes smoked. For VAT on essentials, the same mechanism is why zero-rating exists — bread and maize meal are spared to protect poor households.

A subsidy mirrors it exactly. The state pays sellers per unit, lowering their effective cost — supply shifts RIGHTWARD: equilibrium price LOWER, quantity HIGHER. Commuters ride subsidised buses cheaply and more of them ride; supported farmers plant more and food is cheaper than it would be. The costs, stated honestly: subsidies must be funded from taxation elsewhere — opportunity cost returns; and both instruments can misfire — a tax set too high breeds smuggling and illicit trade; a subsidy captured by producers may fatten margins instead of cutting prices.

Diagram discipline for the exam: taxes and subsidies SHIFT the supply curve; they do not merely move the price. Draw the shift, mark both equilibria, and state the sharing.

Stop for this section's questions now — tax and subsidy on the diagram: direction of shift, both new equilibrium values, the burden split, and one honest cost of each.

## Subtopic: Maximum Prices — the Ceiling

A MAXIMUM PRICE — a price ceiling — is a legislated price ABOVE which the good may not sell, set BELOW the equilibrium to make an essential affordable: bread in a crisis, rent in a housing squeeze, fuel in an emergency.

The diagram tells the sequence. At the ceiling price, quantity demanded is high — the low price invites buyers — but quantity supplied is LOW: the price no longer rewards production. Demanded exceeds supplied: a SHORTAGE, permanent for as long as the ceiling binds, because the price is legally forbidden from rising to clear it. The market's rationing function is broken by design — so something else must ration: queues form; first-come-first-served replaces willingness to pay; sellers ration informally — favoured customers, limits per buyer; and the shortage's pressure leaks into the BLACK MARKET: illegal sales above the ceiling, where the desperate pay more than the free market price would ever have been. Quality can quietly fall too — sellers unable to raise prices cut corners instead.

The honest verdict examiners reward: the ceiling delivers cheap bread to those who GET bread — and creates a shortage deciding who gets it by luck, queueing or connection. Intervention succeeds at its price goal and fails at its quantity goal — which is why states often prefer subsidies or grants (which move the curves or the incomes) to ceilings (which fight the crossing point directly).

The questions on this section are in front of you now — the ceiling's placement below equilibrium, the shortage mechanics, and the black market's origin.

## Subtopic: Minimum Prices and the Minimum Wage — the Floor

The mirror instrument: a MINIMUM PRICE — a floor — a legislated price BELOW which the good may not sell, set ABOVE equilibrium to protect SELLERS' incomes: historically common for farm produce, guaranteeing farmers a living price.

The diagram mirrors too. At the floor, quantity supplied is generous — the high price rewards producers — but quantity demanded shrinks. Supplied exceeds demanded: a SURPLUS. Unsold stock piles up, and the price may not fall to clear it — so the state typically must BUY the surplus (grain silos filling), store it, export it, or watch it waste; the policy's cost lands in the budget.

Now the floor that matters most in South Africa: the MINIMUM WAGE — a price floor in the LABOUR market, where households are the sellers and firms the buyers. Set above the market wage, the diagram predicts: more people offer labour (the better wage attracts), firms demand less labour (the higher cost deters) — a surplus of labour: unemployment pressure. That is the mechanical prediction, and it must be stated. But the full argument — and the syllabus wants both sides — does not end there. The case FOR: the unregulated market wage for unskilled work can fall below what life costs — the floor protects dignity, reduces working poverty, and higher wages feed spending back into the circular flow; employers may also gain more productive, stable workers. The case AGAINST: priced-out workers — the least skilled, the young — may lose jobs or never be hired, and enforcement pushes some work informal. The mature answer: a trade-off between protecting the employed and risking the excluded, its actual size an empirical question — measured, country by country, not settled by the diagram alone.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the floor's mechanics, the state's surplus problem, and the minimum wage argued honestly both ways.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same instruments from the street — because every one of them is already in your daily life: the cigarette prices, the taxi subsidy question, the bread queue stories your grandparents tell, and the wage debates on the news.

## Subtopic: The Referee Enters the Game

Think of the market as a football match: buyers and sellers playing, prices as the score, nobody in charge — and mostly, the game works. But sometimes the crowd demands a referee. When the game hurts people — smoke drifting into everyone's lungs, bread priced past the poorest — the STATE steps onto the field. Not to play; to change the rules.

The referee has four whistles. Whistle one: make it DEARER — slap a tax on the harmful thing. Every cigarette carries a state charge; the price climbs; some smokers cut down. The tax collects money AND discourages — two jobs, one whistle. Whistle two: make it CHEAPER — pay part of the cost of the good thing. The bus fare you pay isn't the full cost; the state quietly pays the rest, so workers can reach work. Whistle three: fix the SCORE itself — declare a price no one may exceed (a ceiling) or a price no one may go below (a floor). And whistle four: skip the game entirely — hand buying power straight to households: grants, free clinics, no-fee schools. The market isn't asked; the need is met directly.

Why blow any whistle at all? Because the game's natural rule — whoever can pay, plays — is sometimes a rule society won't accept for bread, medicine, or a working person's wage.

## Subtopic: Ceilings Make Queues, Floors Make Piles

The two score-fixing whistles have famous side effects, and one picture makes them unforgettable.

The CEILING: the state pushes the price DOWN below where the market would rest — bread capped cheap in hard times. Buyers cheer and crowd in; but watch the bakers: at that price, baking barely pays — they bake LESS. Many wanting, few baking: the shelves empty. And here's the cruel twist: the price can't rise to fix it — that's illegal now. So who gets the cheap bread? Whoever queued from 5 a.m. Whoever knows the baker. And in the alley behind the shop, someone sells hidden loaves at DOUBLE — the black market, born directly from the gap between what's legal and what's scarce. Cheap bread for some; no bread for others; expensive bread in the shadows. A ceiling makes QUEUES.

The FLOOR: the state holds the price UP above the resting point — say, to guarantee farmers a decent maize price. Farmers cheer and plant everything; but buyers at that dear price buy less. Much offered, little taken: the silos overflow. And the price can't fall to clear them — illegal again. So the state itself must buy the mountain, store it, ship it abroad, or watch it spoil — and the bill lands on the budget. A floor makes PILES.

One memory trick holds it all: the ceiling is pushed down low (below the resting point) — and low ceilings leave people queueing outside; the floor is raised up high (above the resting point) — and high floors leave stock piled on top. Queues under ceilings; piles on floors.

Your questions for this part are up now: ceiling or floor, queue or pile — and the shadow market's address.

## Subtopic: The Wage Floor at the Factory Gate

Now the floor everyone argues about at supper: the minimum wage — a floor under the price of WORK itself.

Picture the factory gate. Without any rule, the wage for unskilled work settles where the crowd of work-seekers meets the firms' hiring — and in a country with millions seeking, that resting wage can fall below what feeding a family costs. So the state sets a floor: no one may be paid less than this.

Now both truths, honestly — because both are real. Truth one: for everyone employed at the floor, life changes — rent paid, children fed, dignity restored; and those wages get spent at the spaza and the rank, feeding the whole neighbourhood's circle. Truth two: at the dearer wage, some firms hire fewer hands — the gate crowd grows; and the ones squeezed out first are the youngest and least skilled, the very people the floor meant to lift. Some work also slips into the shadows — jobs paying under the floor, off the books, with no protection at all.

So who's right — the aunt who says the wage saved her family, or the cousin who says it costs jobs? Possibly BOTH, about different people — and that's the honest answer. The real question is the balance: how many protected against how many priced out — and that's measured in the real world, year by year, not settled by shouting. What the diagram gives you is the shape of the trade-off; what the evidence gives you is its size.

That's this whole topic in one habit: for every intervention, ask three questions — what was it FOR, what does the diagram predict, and what ELSE follows? Tax, subsidy, ceiling, floor, wage — same three questions, every time.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: the wage floor argued both ways, and the three-question habit run on a fresh intervention. Referee wisely — that's the whole art of the public sector.
