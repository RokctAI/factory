**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! First: if ceilings cause shortages and black markets, why do governments ever use them? Are they just bad at economics?

**Tutor:** Sometimes — but the fairer answer is that ceilings buy something specific at a known cost, and in emergencies the purchase can be worth it. In a war, a disaster, a sudden crisis, letting bread or fuel spike to whatever the desperate will pay means the poor simply starve while the rich stockpile — and a temporary cap, paired with official rationing, spreads the pain more evenly until supply recovers. The failures we studied arrive with TIME: the longer a ceiling binds, the more production withers and the fatter the black market grows. So the honest policy rule is: ceilings are painkillers, not cures — short-term, paired with rationing, removed when the crisis passes. Governments go wrong when they leave the painkiller running for years and then express surprise at the empty shelves. Your exam answer should carry exactly that nuance: name the intention, the mechanism, the decay over time, and the better long-run instruments — subsidies and grants — that move curves and incomes instead of fighting the crossing point.

**Thandi:** Next: with the cigarette tax — if the point is fewer smokers, and it works, doesn't the government lose its own revenue? The two goals eat each other!

**Tutor:** They do tension against each other, and noticing it puts you ahead of most adults. The resolution lies in HOW MUCH smokers respond. Tobacco is addictive, so when the price rises, consumption falls — but slowly and partially: a large price rise produces a modest quantity fall. That means revenue per remaining smoker rises faster than smokers quit, and total revenue grows even as consumption shrinks — both goals advance, though neither fully. The genuine limit is the one from the lesson: push the tax too high and the response you get is not quitting but SMUGGLING — consumption continues, revenue collapses, and criminal networks profit. So excise policy is a balancing act on a narrow ledge: high enough to discourage, low enough not to hand the market to smugglers. And for your exams, the general principle underneath: the effect of any tax depends on how strongly buyers respond to price — an idea Grade 11 will formalise as elasticity, and you have just derived its intuition unaided.

**Thandi:** This one's from lived experience: my uncle says the farm floor prices of the old days kept his family's farm alive. My textbook says floors waste money on surpluses. Who's lying?

**Tutor:** Neither — they are describing the two ends of the same policy, and holding both is exactly the both-columns habit from the scarcity lesson. Your uncle's memory is accurate: a guaranteed price shields farm incomes from the brutal swings of weather and world markets, keeps rural towns and their schools alive, and secures domestic food production — real benefits, concentrated and visible in farming communities. The textbook's cost is also accurate: consumers pay more for food than they otherwise would — a burden heaviest on poor households; the state buys and stores mountains that may spoil or be dumped abroad; and production is pulled toward what the floor rewards rather than what buyers want — resources misallocated, diffuse and invisible costs spread across everyone. That pattern — concentrated visible benefits, diffuse invisible costs — explains why such policies are politically durable long after economists sour on them. The examinable skill is refusing the either-or: state who gains, who pays, through what mechanism, and let the weighing be explicit. Your uncle and your textbook meet in a complete answer.

**Thandi:** Last one: for the minimum wage, you said the answer is 'measured, not shouted.' But how do economists actually MEASURE something like that? You can't run the country twice.

**Tutor:** You can't — so economists hunt for situations that resemble running it twice, and this hunt is some of the finest detective work in the discipline. The classic method: find NEIGHBOURING labour markets where one side raised its minimum wage and the other didn't — two similar provinces, two adjacent regions, the same industry across a border — and compare employment before and after in both. The unchanged side serves as the counterfactual: what the changed side would likely have done anyway. Researchers also track the SAME firms and workers through a wage increase, watching hiring, hours, prices and closures; and sectoral studies compare industries with different exposure to the new floor. The findings, honestly summarised: modest minimum wage increases have generally produced smaller job losses than the simple diagram predicts — sometimes near zero — because firms adjust on many margins: prices, turnover, vacancies, hours; while very large or rigid increases in weak economies show clearer employment damage. Which is why the mature position is neither 'floors never cost jobs' nor 'floors always cost jobs' but: the trade-off is real, its size depends on the level, the economy and enforcement — and evidence, not slogan, sets the dial. That sentence, in an exam or an argument, is what economic literacy sounds like.
