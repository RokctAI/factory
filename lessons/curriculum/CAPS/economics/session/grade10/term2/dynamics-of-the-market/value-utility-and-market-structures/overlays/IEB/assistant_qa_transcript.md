**Thandi:** Question time before we close! First one: if utility is subjective and lives in each person, how can anyone measure it — and how does the market cope with millions of different utilities?

**Tutor:** Beautifully aimed question, because it exposes the market's cleverest trick. Nobody measures utility directly — there is no utility thermometer. What the market reads instead is what utility makes people DO: the festival-goer's thirst becomes an offer of thirty rand; the mild interest of someone at home becomes a refusal to pay ten. Every purchase is a small confession of how much satisfaction someone expects, and the demand curve is millions of those confessions stacked into one line. So the market never needs to know your feelings — it only needs your willingness to pay, which is your feelings translated into rands. That translation is also the system's blind spot, worth remembering: intense need without money translates into nothing, which is why buying power, not need alone, is what markets serve.

***

**Thandi:** Next one, and it bothered a few of us: the tomato market sounds perfect, but even there some grower could have the freshest tomatoes and charge more. Doesn't that break the model?

**Tutor:** It bends it exactly the way real life always bends models — and noticing that earns you credit, not trouble. The perfect market is a BENCHMARK, not a photograph: a standard we hold real markets against. The moment one grower's tomatoes are noticeably fresher, the homogeneity condition slips, that grower gains a sliver of pricing power, and the stall drifts one step toward monopolistic competition. Real markets live on a sliding scale between the poles, and your classification answer should say where on the scale and WHY, condition by condition: how many sellers, how similar the product, how free the entry, how visible the prices. The model's job is not to describe the world perfectly; it is to give you the measuring stick that makes the differences precise.

***

**Thandi:** Third question: why should a Grade 10 in Johannesburg care that the gold price is set somewhere else in the world? It feels far away from us.

**Tutor:** It is closer to you than the weather. When world buyers push the gold price down, mines on the Rand earn fewer dollars, shifts get cut, and a mineworker's family in Carletonville tightens its budget — that is the world market arriving at one household's kitchen table. The rand itself is priced on a world market every second, and that number decides what fuel costs at your local garage and what imported phones cost at the mall. South Africa earns its living selling commodities whose prices we do not control — so world markets are not foreign news; they are the invisible employer, price-setter and landlord of much of this economy. An economist in this country reads world prices the way a farmer reads clouds: because tomorrow's local reality is written there.

***

**Thandi:** Last one: the five functions sound like the market does everything itself. If prices allocate, inform, ration and connect — what's left for the government to do?

**Tutor:** Plenty — and the honest footnote in the lesson is exactly where the answer lives. Markets perform those five functions only for demand backed by money, and only for costs that appear in prices. Where a family's need for basic water has no income behind it, the rationing function excludes them without blinking. Where a factory's smoke costs its neighbours their health but appears on nobody's invoice, the information function sends a false message — the price says the product is cheaper than it truly is for society. And where one seller owns the only gate, price formation itself is distorted by market power. Each failure marks a doorway for the public sector: grants and free basic services where income is missing, taxes and rules where costs spill over, competition authorities where power concentrates. The market is a brilliant machine with known blind spots — and the next lesson is precisely about the state stepping into them.
