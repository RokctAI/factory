**Thandi:** Class questions before we wrap up! The big one first: I always mix up when to SHIFT a curve and when to just move along it. Is there a foolproof test?

**Tutor:** There is, and it never fails: ask whether the good's OWN price changed, or something else. Remember what a curve actually is — the standing relationship between this good's price and its quantity. When the egg price itself moves, you are reading a different point on the same relationship: a slide along the curve, a change in quantity demanded or supplied. When anything ELSE moves — incomes, feed costs, disease, substitutes, tastes — the relationship itself is redrawn, because at every price behaviour is now different: that is a shift of the whole curve. And here is the trap worth naming: after the outbreak shifts supply left and the price rises, households DO buy fewer eggs — but that is the slide along the unchanged demand curve caused by the new price, not a demand shift. Own price or other cause — settle that first, and the diagram draws itself.

***

**Thandi:** Next question: why do the two curves slope in opposite directions? It seems strange that one price makes buyers and sellers do opposite things.

**Tutor:** Because the same number wears two different faces. To a buyer, price is a COST: the higher it climbs, the more of the family budget each tray of eggs swallows, so fewer get bought — the curve runs downhill. To a seller, price is a REWARD: the higher it climbs, the more each tray pays for the feed, the labour and the early mornings, so more gets offered — the curve runs uphill. One number, a burden on one side of the counter and a prize on the other. And that opposition is precisely why the market works: a downhill line and an uphill line must cross exactly once, and that single crossing is where every buyer's plan and every seller's plan finally agree.

***

**Thandi:** Here's a sneaky one: in a shortage, who actually pushes the price up? Surely the shopkeeper would charge the moon every day if he could?

**Tutor:** He would love to — but the market only lets him when the BUYERS make it stick. A seller can write any number on the shelf; whether it survives is decided by the queue. In a shortage, more people want eggs than there are trays, so buyers start competing with each other — arriving earlier, taking extra, offering more — and a shopkeeper who watches his shelf empty by nine learns his price was below what the queue would pay. He raises it, and it holds, because buyer competition holds it there. Try that same increase in a quiet week and the unsold trays teach the opposite lesson fast. So in a competitive market, sellers do not command the price; they discover it. Surplus and shortage are the market's messengers, telling every seller where the resting place is and punishing whoever ignores the message.

***

**Thandi:** Last one from the class: if the outbreak makes egg prices jump, don't the egg farmers secretly win? Scarcity sounds profitable.

**Tutor:** Follow one farmer's books and the idea collapses. The higher price only applies to eggs a farmer still HAS — and the outbreak is exactly what destroyed most of them. A farm that lost its flock sells almost nothing at the wonderful new price, while the costs of the culled hens, the empty houses and the biosecurity are already spent. The genuine winners are the lucky few farms the virus missed — full production at shortage prices — and that lottery is why farmers pay for insurance and vaccinate ahead of outbreaks. Then widen the lens: households pay more for a staple protein, bakeries and food producers pay more for ingredients, and the country imports eggs at world prices. A disease outbreak creates no new wealth — it destroys some and redistributes what remains through the price. Reading a price spike that coldly, instead of hunting for villains, is exactly the discipline this topic is training in you.
