**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! The one everyone got wrong at least once: how do I KNOW whether to shift a curve or slide along it? I keep shifting demand when the price changes.

**Tutor:** One question decides it every time: did the good's OWN price change, or did something else? The curve IS the relationship between this good's price and its quantity — so when the maize price itself moves, you are simply reading a different point on the same relationship: a slide, a change in quantity demanded or supplied. When anything ELSE moves — income, tastes, fertiliser costs, weather, substitutes — the whole relationship is redrawn: at every price, buyers or sellers now behave differently, and that is a shift of the entire curve. The trap you fell into has a mechanism worth seeing: after a supply shift raises the price, buyers DO buy less — but that is the slide ALONG the unchanged demand curve caused by the new price, not a shift of demand. Ask own-price-or-other first, and the diagram draws itself.

***

**Thandi:** Next: why does the demand curve slope down but supply slope UP? Same market, same good — why opposite directions?

**Tutor:** Because price plays opposite roles on the two sides of the counter. To the buyer, price is a COST — the higher it climbs, the more of your pocket each unit eats, so fewer people find it worth it and each buys less: downhill from left to right. To the seller, price is a REWARD — the higher it climbs, the more every unit pays for the effort, the planting, the carrying, so more sellers stretch to bring more: uphill. One number, two opposite meanings — a burden to one side, a prize to the other — and the two slopes simply draw those two meanings. That opposition is also why the curves must CROSS: a leaning-down line and a leaning-up line meet exactly once, and the market's whole drama is the journey to that crossing.

***

**Thandi:** This one is sneaky: in a shortage, WHO exactly raises the price? The vendor wants high prices all the time — why can he only get them sometimes?

**Tutor:** The buyers raise it — through competition with each other — and that is the subtlety most learners miss. A vendor can announce any price he likes; the market decides whether it sticks. In a shortage, the queue is longer than the stock: buyers who truly need the good start offering more, arriving earlier, buying bigger — and a vendor who notices cans vanishing in twenty minutes learns that his asking price was below what the queue would pay. He raises it, and it sticks, because the competition among BUYERS holds it there. Try the same rise on a quiet day and the melting pile teaches the opposite lesson within hours. So sellers do not have price power in a competitive market — they have price DISCOVERY: the surplus and the shortage tell them where the resting place is, and punish them for ignoring it. That is why we say market forces set the price, even though human hands write the labels.

***

**Thandi:** Last one: the drought answer ends with a higher price and LESS maize sold. Wouldn't farmers love droughts then — scarcity making them rich?

**Tutor:** Work it through farm by farm and the romance dies quickly. The higher price applies to maize a farmer still HAS — and the drought is precisely the event that took most of it away. The farmer whose harvest halved sells half the bags at a price that rose, but rarely by enough to cover the loss; whether revenue rises or falls depends on how strongly buyers cling to maize — and meanwhile his costs of planting were already spent on hectares that yielded nothing. The farmers who genuinely gain are the lucky minority the drought missed — full harvest, drought prices — and that unevenness is exactly why farmers insure and why governments hold reserves. There is also the wider ledger: consumers — especially poor households for whom maize is the staple — pay more for less, and the nation imports at world prices. A drought creates no wealth; it destroys some and REDISTRIBUTES the rest through the price. Scarcity pricing is the market rationing what remains, not a bonus scheme — and reading it that way, coldly, is precisely the skill this topic trains.
