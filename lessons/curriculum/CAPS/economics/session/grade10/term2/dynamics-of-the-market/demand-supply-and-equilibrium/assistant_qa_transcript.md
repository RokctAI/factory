**Student:** I still don't get when to SHIFT a curve versus move ALONG it. Both look like "demand changed" to me.

**Thandi:** This is *the* classic confusion, so let's nail it. Ask one question: did the good's own price cause the change? If the price of maize meal itself changed, buyers slide up or down the *same* curve — that's a movement along, called a change in *quantity demanded*. If anything else changed — income, taste, the price of bread — buyers now behave differently at *every* price, so the whole curve picks itself up and moves — a shift, a change in *demand*. Quick test: "maize price rose, people bought less" = along. "Incomes rose, people buy more at every price" = shift. Feel the difference?

***

**Student:** Why does the price rise after the drought? The farmers didn't decide to charge more, and the shops didn't have a meeting about it.

**Thandi:** Exactly — nobody decided, and that's the magic! Picture the moment after the harvest halves: at the old price, people still want the usual amount of maize meal, but there's only half as much on the shelves. That's a shortage. Some buyers really need their pap, so they're willing to pay a bit more rather than go without — and sellers notice they can charge more and still sell out. Price creeps up. As it rises, some buyers cut back and farmers scrape together what they can, until the amount wanted equals the amount available. The price settled itself — market forces, no meeting required.

***

**Student:** In the drought question, why doesn't the demand curve ALSO move? Surely people buy less maize meal when it gets expensive?

**Thandi:** Sharp observation — and here's the subtle bit. People *do* buy less when the price rises, but look at *why* they buy less: because of the maize price itself. A change caused by the good's own price is a movement *along* the demand curve, never a shift. The curve only shifts if something else — income, tastes, substitutes — changes, and the question told us income is unchanged. So on your diagram: supply shifts left, and then buyers slide up along the *same* demand curve to the new equilibrium. One shift, one slide.

**Student:** How should I lay out my answer in a test so I don't lose marks on the diagram?

**Thandi:** Follow the lesson's six steps like a checklist, because markers award them almost line by line. Labelled axes — price vertical, quantity horizontal. Both original curves labelled D and S. Original equilibrium marked with dotted lines to both axes. The new curve S1 with an arrow showing the shift direction. New equilibrium P1 and Q1, dotted lines again. And then — this is the step people skip — two or three sentences explaining the shortage and how the price was bid up. The diagram shows *what* happened; your words show *why*. Both earn marks.
