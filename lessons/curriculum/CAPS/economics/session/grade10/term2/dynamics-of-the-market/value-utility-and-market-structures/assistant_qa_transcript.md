**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! First: if utility is subjective — living in each person — how can markets put ONE price on anything? Shouldn't water cost me R7 and my thirsty friend R25 everywhere, forever?

**Tutor:** The market price is not anyone's personal utility — it is where all those different personal valuations MEET. Every buyer carries a private ceiling: the most the good is worth to them today. The thirsty friend's ceiling is high; yours is low. The market gathers thousands of such ceilings on the demand side, thousands of seller floors on the supply side, and the crossing point becomes the one public number. Notice what that number does: everyone whose private worth exceeds it buys — and quietly pockets the difference between what they would have paid and what they did; everyone below it walks past. So subjective utility is not erased by the single price; it is SORTED by it. And where sellers can separate the groups — inside locked stadium gates — they charge the thirsty group its own higher price. One price per market, many worths per person: both true at once.

***

**Thandi:** Next: the perfect market needs 'perfect knowledge' and 'homogeneous products' — which sounds impossible. Why do we study a market that doesn't exist?

**Tutor:** For the same reason physics studies frictionless surfaces: the pure case isolates the forces. The perfect market shows what competition does when NOTHING obstructs it — price driven to the crossing point, no seller able to overcharge, resources steered purely by demand and supply. That gives economics its measuring stick: hold any real market against the benchmark and the DIFFERENCES become diagnosable — fewer sellers here, so watch for higher prices; branded products there, so watch for pricing power; hidden information here, so watch for exploitation. Competition authorities do exactly this when they judge a merger: how far from the benchmark will this market drift? So the perfect market is not a description that fails; it is an instrument that works. And note the delicious modern twist from today's lesson: the phone has dragged one impossible condition — perfect knowledge — surprisingly close to reality. Benchmarks can be approached, even when never reached.

***

**Thandi:** This one's sharp: the salon on our street charges more than the one two streets over, and BOTH stay busy. Doesn't that break the law of one price from the potato market?

**Tutor:** It would break it — if the two salons sold the same thing. They don't, and that is the whole insight of monopolistic competition. The potato market's one price rests on homogeneity: sack indistinguishable from sack, so nothing but price can matter. Your salon sells a bundle no other salon exactly duplicates: those hands, that braider's reputation, the walk from your gate, the vibe, the trust built over years. Differentiation gives each salon a SMALL monopoly — over its own loyal bundle — and small monopolies support small price differences. But test the limits and you find competition still policing the street: let the dearer salon push too far and loyalty frays — customers rediscover that two streets is not so far. So the structure predicts exactly what you observe: prices NEAR each other, not equal; each seller holding a little slack, none holding much. The law of one price is not broken; it simply only governs markets where the products are truly one.

**Thandi:** Last one: the functions say prices 'ration' scarce goods — those willing and able to pay, get. But that means the rich always eat first. Why do economists describe that so calmly?

**Tutor:** The calm is method, not approval — and the syllabus itself refuses to leave it there. Positive economics first: price rationing is a MECHANISM, and describing it accurately means naming its real virtues — it is automatic, queue-free, incorruptible by officials, and it signals producers to bring more of what is short, which no other rationing method does. Alternatives have their own costs: ration books breed black markets; queues ration by time — which burdens workers; official allocation invites favouritism. But describing the mechanism does not endorse its outcomes, and here the normative half enters with full force: rationing by purse answers FOR WHOM with 'those who have', and for essentials — food, healthcare, water — a society may rightly find that answer intolerable. That is precisely why every real economy overrides price rationing somewhere: free basic water, public clinics, school feeding schemes, grants that put buying power where income failed. Your next topic — public sector intervention — is exactly this override, studied properly. So the economist's calm is the discipline of separating how the machine works from whether we accept where it delivers — you need the first to do the second well. Anger without mechanism changes nothing; mechanism without conscience defends anything. The subject trains you to hold both.
