# Part 1 — Expert

Before demand and supply can be fully understood, three foundations must stand: what VALUE is and where it comes from, what kinds of MARKETS exist — perfect and imperfect — and what markets actually DO for an economy, including the world-spanning electronic markets of the present day. Today's session builds all three, and each carries its own family of examination questions: definitions, classifications, and functions.

## Subtopic: Value, Price and Utility

Start with UTILITY: the satisfaction or usefulness a consumer derives from a good or service. Utility is SUBJECTIVE — it lives in the user, not in the object: the same umbrella carries enormous utility in a storm and none on a clear day; a textbook has high utility for the learner writing next week and little for the graduate. Because utility is subjective, it varies between people, places and moments — and that variation is the raw material of all trade: exchange happens precisely because the same thing is worth different amounts to different people.

From utility, two kinds of VALUE. VALUE IN USE: how useful a thing is to its owner — water's value in use is immense; life depends on it. VALUE IN EXCHANGE: what a thing can fetch when traded — and here sits the famous paradox the syllabus expects you to resolve: water, essential to life, exchanges for almost nothing, while diamonds, largely ornamental, exchange for fortunes. The resolution: exchange value is governed not by total usefulness but by SCARCITY relative to wants — water is abundantly available in most places, diamonds are rare — and by the utility of the NEXT unit: another glass of water adds little to a person with plenty; another diamond still gleams. Plenty cheapens; scarcity dearens — whatever the total usefulness.

And PRICE: value in exchange, expressed in money. Money prices let all exchange values speak one language — which is what makes markets, accounting and the whole quantitative side of economics possible.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: utility's subjectivity, the two values distinguished, and the water-diamond paradox resolved through scarcity.

## Subtopic: Perfect and Imperfect Markets

A market is any arrangement bringing buyers and sellers into contact to trade — a physical place, a phone call, an app. Economics classifies markets by their COMPETITIVE STRUCTURE, and the benchmark is the PERFECT market, defined by strict conditions: MANY buyers and many sellers, none large enough to influence the price alone; a HOMOGENEOUS product — one seller's maize indistinguishable from another's; PERFECT KNOWLEDGE — all participants informed of prices and quality; FREE ENTRY AND EXIT — no barriers stopping new sellers joining or old ones leaving. Under these conditions every participant is a PRICE TAKER: the market's crossing point sets the price, and each buyer and seller simply accepts it. Real markets only approximate this — fresh produce markets and the share market come closest: identical products, many participants, posted prices.

Most real markets are IMPERFECT — one or more conditions fail, and someone gains influence over price. The MONOPOLY: a single seller — the sole water utility, a patented medicine — facing no competition, choosing its price subject only to what buyers will bear. The OLIGOPOLY: a few large sellers — banks, cellphone networks, fuel companies — each watching the others, competing sometimes on price, often on everything except price. MONOPOLISTIC COMPETITION: many sellers of SIMILAR but differentiated products — restaurants, hair salons, clothing labels — each with a small pricing power carved out by brand, location or style. The classification test runs on three questions: how many sellers; how similar the product; how free the entry. And the consequence scale runs with it: the fewer the sellers and higher the barriers, the more the market's price drifts from the perfect benchmark — the seller becomes a PRICE MAKER.

Stop for this section's questions now — the perfect conditions listed in full, the three imperfect structures classified, and price taker distinguished from price maker.

## Subtopic: World Markets and the Effects of Electronics

Markets no longer end at borders. WORLD MARKETS trade commodities, currencies and goods across the planet — the platinum price is set by global buying and selling, not in Rustenburg; the rand's value is a world market's verdict, updated by the second. For South Africa, deeply tied to commodity exports, world market prices are domestic realities: a lesson every mining town knows.

The syllabus highlights the ELECTRONIC transformation of markets, and its effects deserve careful listing. Electronics WIDENED markets: a buyer in Polokwane bids against one in London; a craft seller reaches customers on another continent through a platform. They ACCELERATED them: share and currency markets trade continuously, prices adjusting in seconds to news. They brightened KNOWLEDGE: prices are visible to anyone with a phone — a farmer checks the fresh-produce market price before accepting the middleman's offer; a shopper compares three stores in a minute. Perfect knowledge, one of the perfect market's conditions, has moved from textbook fiction toward daily fact. They lowered ENTRY barriers: a township seamstress with a social-media page enters the clothing market without a shopfront. And they created NEW market forms: online marketplaces, auction platforms, app-based services matching buyers and sellers algorithmically.

Balance the account, as the examination expects: electronics also concentrate power in platform owners, expose small sellers to world competition, and demand data access that not all South Africans yet have — the digital divide as a new barrier to entry. Markets became wider, faster and better informed; they did not automatically become fairer.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — world markets' local force, the electronic effects listed both ways, and the knowledge condition reconsidered.

## Subtopic: The Functions of Markets

Close with the syllabus's final requirement: what markets DO — their functions in an economy, five of them, each visible in everything you have already learned.

One: PRICE FORMATION — markets discover prices through the interaction of demand and supply; the crossing point of the last lessons is this function performed. Two: ALLOCATION of resources — prices formed in markets steer the factors of production: a rising price signals producers to make more, drawing labour and capital toward what society wants most; a falling price releases resources from what it wants less. This is how the WHAT-to-produce question is answered without a planner. Three: INFORMATION — prices are compressed messages: one number carries news about scarcity, costs, tastes and expectations to everyone at once; no committee could brief millions of participants as efficiently. Four: RATIONING — when a good is scarce, its rising price decides who receives it: those willing and able to pay; brutal at times, but automatic and queue-free. Five: LINKING buyers and sellers — markets connect people who need never meet: the coffee farmer and the café customer, joined through a chain of markets spanning the globe.

Hold the functions together with one sentence: markets form prices, and prices then allocate, inform, ration and connect. And hold the critical footnote from the term's first topic: markets perform these functions for those with buying power — where need lacks income, or costs land on third parties, the state's corrections enter, which is exactly next topic's subject: public sector intervention.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the five functions named and illustrated, and the bridge to intervention stated.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same three foundations from the street — a stadium water bottle, a potato market next to a phone shop, and the market that lives in your pocket.

## Subtopic: Why Water Costs R25 at the Stadium

Same bottle of water: R7 at the supermarket, R25 inside the stadium on a hot match day. Nothing about the water changed — so what changed? YOU did. Thirsty, hot, locked in with no other options — the satisfaction that bottle delivers is suddenly enormous. That satisfaction is what economists call utility, and the first lesson about it: it lives in YOU, not in the bottle. Same object, different moments, different worth.

Now the old riddle this idea unlocks: water keeps you alive and costs almost nothing; a diamond does nothing vital and costs a fortune. How? Separate the two kinds of worth. Usefulness-worth — value in use — water wins by a mile. But trading-worth — value in exchange — runs on a different fuel: SCARCITY. Water is nearly everywhere; another glass, when you have plenty, barely matters to you — so nobody pays much. Diamonds are rare; even one more is a big deal — so people pay dearly. The rule underneath: plenty makes cheap, scarce makes dear — regardless of how noble the thing is. And the stadium proves the rule in reverse: lock the gates, and suddenly water inside IS scarce — and its price triples.

Price, then, is just trading-worth spoken in rands — one language for all worths, so everything can be compared, counted and put in the books.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the stadium bottle are coming to you right now: utility's home, the two worths, and the riddle resolved.

## Subtopic: The Potato Market and the Phone Shops

Walk two markets in one morning and you'll know the whole classification.

First, the fresh-produce market at dawn: fifty farmers selling potatoes, a hundred buyers walking the rows. Every sack basically identical. Every buyer can see every price in ten minutes. Any farmer can join tomorrow; any can quit. Result: ONE going price for potatoes, and no single person can budge it — try charging double and buyers step to the next stall; try paying half and sellers ignore you. Everyone TAKES the price the whole market made. That's the perfect market — or as close as real life gets.

Now the phone shops. Three big networks sell airtime and data in the whole country — a handful of sellers, each watching the others like taxi rivals on one route: that's an oligopoly. The pharmaceutical company with the only patented pill — one seller, no substitutes: monopoly, a price MAKER, choosing its price subject only to what buyers can bear. And the street of hair salons — many sellers, but each a little different: this one's braids, that one's location, that one's vibe — each holding a small pricing power over its loyal customers: monopolistic competition.

The sorting questions, streetwise: how many sellers? Is what they sell identical or dressed up different? How easily can a newcomer set up shop? Answer those three and you can classify any market you walk past — and predict who takes the price and who makes it.

## Subtopic: The Market in Your Pocket

Last piece: the phone in your pocket is itself a marketplace — and it has quietly rewritten the rules.

Twenty years ago, a farmer knew only the price the middleman offered at the gate. Today she checks the fresh-produce market price on her phone BEFORE he arrives — and negotiates from knowledge. A shopper compares three stores in a minute. A seamstress with a social-media page sells to customers she's never met, no shopfront, no rent. And the big markets — shares, currencies, platinum — trade around the clock across the planet, the prices on your screen updating by the second. The textbook's "perfect knowledge" used to be a fantasy; the phone moved it halfway to fact — and knowledge is power in every negotiation.

But balance it like an economist: the platforms that host these markets are themselves owned — a few big companies standing at the gates of the new marketplace; the seamstress now competes with factories on other continents; and data costs money — those without it stand outside the electronic market entirely, a new kind of locked gate. Wider, faster, better informed — not automatically fairer.

And beneath all of it, old or electronic, markets still do the same five jobs: they FORM prices where buyers meet sellers; those prices STEER the country's effort toward what people want; each price is a one-number MESSAGE about scarcity everyone reads at once; rising prices RATION what's short; and markets CONNECT strangers — the coffee farmer and you, linked through a chain neither ever sees. One sentence holds it: markets make prices, and prices do the rest.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: the pocket market's gains and gates, and the five jobs spotted in your own street. Next lesson: what happens when the state steps into the market — and why it sometimes must.
