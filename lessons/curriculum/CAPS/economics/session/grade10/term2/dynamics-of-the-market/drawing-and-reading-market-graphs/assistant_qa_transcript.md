**Student:** Why does price go on the vertical axis? In maths we always put the thing that causes the change on the horizontal axis, and price causes the change here!

**Thandi:** You've spotted a genuine oddity — well done! You're right that by maths logic, price (the cause) should sit on the horizontal axis. Economics just... does it the other way, for historical reasons going back over a century, and the whole world followed. So it's pure convention now. The practical advice: don't fight it, just drill it — price vertical, quantity horizontal, every single time. An economics graph with swapped axes loses marks even if your numbers are perfect, because none of your readings will match the memo.

***

**Student:** When I read off the excess supply at R8, I got 80 instead of 40. What did I do wrong?

**Thandi:** Ah, I can see exactly what happened — you read the supply quantity (80) and stopped, instead of taking the *gap* between the two curves! Excess supply is a subtraction, always: quantity supplied *minus* quantity demanded, both at the same price. At R8: supply gives 80, demand gives 40, so the surplus is 80 minus 40 = 40 units. Picture a horizontal ruler lying on the graph at the height of R8 — the surplus is the stretch of ruler *between* the two curves, not the distance from the axis.

***

**Student:** My curves crossed at about R7, but the table says equilibrium should be at R6. Is my graph wrong or is close enough okay?

**Thandi:** In this case the graph is telling on itself — check it! With our table, the curves must cross exactly at R6 and 60 units, because that's the row where the columns match. Crossing at R7 usually means one plotted point is off, or your axis steps aren't even (that's the sneaky one — if the gap between 60 and 80 is smaller than the gap between 40 and 60 on your axis, everything bends). Re-check each dot against its row, and re-check that every step on each axis is the same size. When the plotting is right, the intersection lands on the table's equal row automatically.

***

**Student:** Will they always give me nice round numbers like these, or do I need to worry about ugly tables?

**Thandi:** Grade 10 questions are usually kind — steps of 20, prices in R2 jumps, like ours. But the *method* is what you're really learning, because later tables (and Grade 11's cost and revenue schedules) get messier. The recipe never changes: scan the table first, pick even scales that fit the ranges, plot row by row, and confirm the crossing point against the table. If you can do it with pretty numbers now, ugly numbers later are just the same dance with heavier shoes.
