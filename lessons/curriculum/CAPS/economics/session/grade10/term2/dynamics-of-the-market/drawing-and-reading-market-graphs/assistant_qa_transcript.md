**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! First: why does economics insist price goes on the VERTICAL axis? In maths, the thing that causes — the price — would go on the horizontal. It feels upside down!

**Tutor:** Your maths instinct is correct, and the honest answer is: tradition — a very old one. The economists who first drew these diagrams in the nineteenth century set price upright, and a century and a half of textbooks, examiners and markers have followed, so the convention is now the language itself: every memo, every Grade 11 cost curve, every Grade 12 diagram assumes it. There is also a small practical grace: we often read the market by asking \"at THIS price, what happens?\" — and with price upright, that question becomes the level flight across the page, which is a natural reading motion. But the deeper lesson is about conventions in any discipline: they are not right or wrong; they are AGREED, and fluency means honouring them without spending thought. Fight the convention and you translate in your head all year; adopt it and the diagrams start speaking to you directly.

***

**Thandi:** Next: the lesson says join the points with a smooth line. But we only know five actual points from the table — how can we draw the line BETWEEN them as if we know it?

**Tutor:** A genuinely sharp question — you have discovered interpolation, and every scientist makes the same move. The table samples the relationship at five prices; the smooth line is our reasoned claim that the behaviour between samples follows the same pattern — that at R5, quantity demanded sits around 70, between its neighbours at R4 and R6. Why is that claim safe here? Because buying behaviour changes gradually: there is no reason a market that wants 80 at R4 and 60 at R6 would leap to 300 at R5. The line, in other words, carries an assumption of smoothness — reasonable for markets with many buyers, and the same assumption your science graphs make between measured readings. Two cautions travel with it: your line is only as trustworthy as its anchoring points, which is why each dot must match its row exactly; and readings taken between points — like our R5 — are estimates, excellent ones, but estimates. The exam rewards the smooth line precisely because it shows you understand the relationship continues between the rows.

**Thandi:** This one: my two lines crossed at the right place, but my excess supply came out as 120 instead of 40. What did I do?

**Tutor:** You added when the reading asked you to subtract — 80 plus 40 instead of 80 minus 40 — and it is worth understanding why the wrong operation even tempts. At R8 the two readings answer different people: sellers would bring 80; buyers would take 40. The question \"how big is the imbalance?\" asks for the GAP between those two intentions — what remains unsold: 80 minus 40, the stretch of air between the curves at that height. Adding them creates a number with no meaning — no one brings 120, no one takes 120; it is two different groups' plans stacked into nonsense. The guard habit: when you take the two touchdowns, say their sentences aloud — \"sellers bring 80, buyers take 40\" — and the word BETWEEN does the arithmetic for you: the difference. And check against the picture: the gap you measured must be the visible horizontal distance between the curves — if your number is bigger than the whole axis, the picture itself is objecting.

**Thandi:** Last one: this feels like a lot of ceremony for one little diagram. Will the exam really reward all the dotted lines and labels, or can a rushed sketch get the same marks?

**Tutor:** The memoranda are explicit, and they are on ceremony's side — because each piece of ceremony is a separate mark with a separate purpose. Axis labels prove you know what the picture measures; the title proves you know what market it describes; D and S on the curves prove you know which line is which — a marker faced with two unlabelled lines cannot award the curve marks even if the shapes are perfect. The dotted lines at equilibrium are the evidence that you READ the graph rather than guessed near it — many memos allocate them their own mark. And even scales protect every reading that follows: one squashed step and your R8 flight lands on wrong numbers, bleeding marks downstream. Count it up and the \"ceremony\" is often half the question's total — the analysis marks stack on top. So the rushed sketch does not merely look untidy; it forfeits a specific, listed set of marks that the careful drawer banks in the same five minutes. Neatness here is not politeness. It is the answer.
