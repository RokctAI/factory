# Part 1 — Expert

Today's session is a pure skills session — the kind that quietly earns marks in every Economics paper you will ever write: turning a table of numbers into a proper graph, and reading answers straight off it. Our working example is a demand and supply schedule for a product at prices from R2 to R10, and by the end you will plot both curves, find the equilibrium, and read off the excess supply at R8.

## Subtopic: Reading the Table Before You Draw

Here is our table — an economist calls it a schedule. At each price, it records two numbers: the quantity buyers would demand and the quantity sellers would supply.

At R2, quantity demanded is 100 units and quantity supplied is 20 units. At R4, demand is 80 and supply is 40. At R6, demand is 60 and supply is 60. At R8, demand is 40 and supply is 80. At R10, demand is 20 and supply is 100.

Before drawing a single line, interrogate the table — this discipline prevents most graphing errors. Run your eye down the demand column: as price climbs from R2 to R10, quantity demanded falls from 100 to 20. Falling as price rises — that is the law of demand, so this column will become a downward-sloping curve. Now the supply column: as price rises, quantity supplied climbs from 20 to 100. Rising with price — the law of supply, an upward-sloping curve.

Notice something else while we are here: at R6, the two columns say the same number — 60 and 60. Remember that row; it is going to matter enormously.

One more habit: check the ranges. Prices run from R2 to R10, quantities from 20 to 100. Those ranges decide how we will scale our axes in a moment. A table read carefully is a graph half drawn.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: read the schedule's columns, name each law from its direction, and find the row that matters most.

## Subtopic: Axes, Scales and Plotting the Points

Now we set up the graph — and this is where presentation marks are won or lost.

Rule one: in economics, price always goes on the vertical axis and quantity on the horizontal axis. This is a convention you must follow even though it may feel backwards.

Rule two: choose even scales that use the space. Our prices run R2 to R10, so mark the vertical axis in equal steps of R2: two, four, six, eight, ten. Quantities run 20 to 100, so mark the horizontal axis in equal steps of 20: twenty, forty, sixty, eighty, one hundred. Equal steps — never squash three values into one block and stretch the next. An uneven scale bends curves and destroys every reading you take afterwards.

Rule three: label everything. "Price (R)" on the vertical axis, "Quantity" on the horizontal axis, and a heading for the graph. Markers award these labels; leave them out and you donate marks away.

Now plot the demand schedule, point by point. Each row of the table becomes one point: price R2 with quantity 100 — go up to 2 on the price axis, across to 100, mark the point. Price R4 with 80. R6 with 60. R8 with 40. R10 with 20. Join the points with a smooth line and label it D. It slopes downward, exactly as the table promised.

Now the supply schedule on the same axes: R2 with 20, R4 with 40, R6 with 60, R8 with 80, R10 with 100. Join and label S. Upward sloping. Two curves, one set of axes — a complete market picture.

Stop for this section's questions now — the three rules of setup and the plotting routine: axes, scales, labels, points.

## Subtopic: Finding Equilibrium on the Graph

Look at your drawing. The two curves cross at exactly one point. Read its coordinates: price R6, quantity 60. That intersection is the market equilibrium — the price at which quantity demanded equals quantity supplied.

Confirm it against the table, because your graph and your table must always tell the same story: at R6, demand is 60 and supply is 60. They match. If your curves cross anywhere other than a row where the two columns are equal, one of your points is plotted wrongly — go back and check each point against its row.

Mark the equilibrium properly: draw a dotted line from the intersection left to the price axis, meeting it at R6, and a dotted line down to the quantity axis, meeting it at 60. Label the point E. Those dotted lines are not decoration — they are how you show the examiner you can read a graph, and in many memos they carry their own marks.

Let us be clear about what equilibrium means in market language: at R6 the market clears. Every unit offered finds a buyer; every buyer willing to pay finds a unit. No surplus, no shortage, no pressure on the price to move. At any other price, as we are about to see, the market is out of balance — and the graph will show us by exactly how much.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — the intersection, its dotted lines, and the graph-against-table confirmation.

## Subtopic: Reading Off Excess Supply at R8

Now the exam question: find the excess supply at R8. This is where the graph becomes a measuring instrument.

Go to R8 on the price axis. Travel horizontally across the graph. You strike the demand curve first — drop down: quantity demanded is 40. Continue across to the supply curve — drop down: quantity supplied is 80.

At R8, sellers offer 80 units but buyers take only 40. The market does not balance: quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded by 80 minus 40 — 40 units. That gap is the excess supply, also called a surplus. On the graph it is the horizontal distance between the two curves at the height of R8. Shade or bracket that gap and label it "excess supply = 40 units".

Why does this happen at R8? Because R8 is above the equilibrium price of R6. A price above equilibrium always rewards sellers into offering plenty while pricing some buyers out — surplus. And the market's response, as you know from the law of markets: unsold stock pushes the price down toward R6.

The mirror-image skill: if the question asked for excess demand at R4, same technique — across at R4, demand is 80, supply is 40, shortage of 40 units. Below equilibrium, demand outruns supply.

Before we finish, your verification routine — run it every time. One: does each plotted point match its table row exactly? Two: does demand slope down and supply slope up? Three: does the intersection agree with the row where the columns are equal? Four: for any reading, did you take *both* values at the same price and subtract? The most common error in this question is reading the two quantities at different prices — the horizontal line at R8 must touch both curves.

A graph drawn with even scales, labelled axes, and dotted reading lines is not just neat — it is an answer machine. Feed it any price and it hands you the surplus or shortage. Master this machine now; Grade 11 will feed it cost and revenue curves, and the machine works exactly the same way — and this part's questions are with you now: readings at given prices, gaps named and sized, the verification routine run in full.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same skill with all the fear taken out — because a market graph is really just a picture of a table, and you have been reading tables since primary school.

## Subtopic: The Table Is the Boss

Here is the secret that calms everyone down: the graph invents NOTHING. Every point on it comes straight out of the table, and when graph and table disagree, the table is the boss — the graph is just its portrait.

So before drawing, have a conversation with the table. Ask the demand column: what do you do as price climbs? It shrinks — 100, 80, 60, 40, 20. So your picture will show a line stepping downhill. Ask the supply column the same: it grows — 20, 40, 60, 80, 100. A line climbing uphill. You now know what the finished picture MUST look like before your pencil moves — which means you can catch your own mistakes: if your demand line comes out uphill, you don't need a marker to tell you; the table already told you.

And spot the special row while you chat: at R6, both columns say 60. One price where buyers and sellers happen to agree completely. Circle that row in your head — your two lines are going to cross exactly there, and if they don't, a point is plotted wrong.

Table first, picture second, table forever the boss. That single habit removes most graphing errors before they happen.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on interrogating the table are coming to you right now: directions, ranges, and the agreeing row.

## Subtopic: Building the Picture Frame

Now the frame — because a good graph is mostly good setup, and setup is pure routine.

Three habits, that's all. Habit one: price stands UP the side, quantity lies ALONG the bottom — always, in every economics graph you will ever draw. It's the subject's handshake; do it without thinking. Habit two: even steps. Prices go 2, 4, 6, 8, 10 — each step the same size on the paper. Quantities go 20, 40, 60, 80, 100 — same-size steps again. Why so strict? Because uneven steps BEND lines that should be straight, and a bent line lies to you when you try to read it. Think of it like a ruler: a ruler with squashed centimetres measures nothing. Habit three: name everything — "Price (R)" up the side, "Quantity" along the bottom, a title on top, D and S on the lines themselves. Naming feels fussy; it's actually free marks sitting on the table.

Then the plotting is just postal work: each table row is an address. R2 and 100: go up to 2, across to 100, drop a dot. Five dots for demand, join them smoothly, write D. Five dots for supply, join, write S. If your dots came from the right addresses, the downhill line and the uphill line appear by themselves — the laws draw the picture for you.

Your questions for this part are up now: the three habits, and dots to their addresses.

## Subtopic: The Answer Machine

Last piece — the payoff. Once the picture stands in its frame, it becomes a machine that answers questions.

Where do the lines cross? Up at R6, across at 60 — drop dotted lines to both axes like landing gear. That's the market's resting place: at R6, every unit offered finds a buyer, every willing buyer finds a unit. The special row from the table, now visible as a crossing.

Now feed the machine the exam's question: what happens at R8? Put your finger on 8 up the side and travel straight across — a level flight. First you hit the demand line: look down — 40. Keep flying to the supply line: look down — 80. Same height, two readings: at R8, sellers bring 80, buyers take 40. The gap between your two landing points — 40 units — is the excess supply, sitting right there as a visible stretch of air between the lines. Above the resting place, sellers always outrun buyers.

And the mirror trick for below: at R4, the same level flight hits supply at 40 and demand at 80 — buyers outrun sellers by 40: excess demand. One flying rule keeps it honest: BOTH readings on ONE height. The classic error is reading one line at R8 and the other somewhere else — your finger flew crooked. Level flight, two touchdowns, subtract.

That's the whole skill: table interrogated, frame built with three habits, dots posted to their addresses, and the machine fed questions at level flight. Nothing here is talent — it is routine, and routine is learnable by anyone willing to draw five graphs.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: crossings found, gaps flown and measured, and the crooked-finger error caught before it costs. Draw five and this skill is yours for the rest of school.
