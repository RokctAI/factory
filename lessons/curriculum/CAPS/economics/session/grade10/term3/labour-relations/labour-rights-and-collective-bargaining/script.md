# Part 1 — Expert

The mill floor of the industrial revolution put owners and workers face to face; LABOUR RELATIONS is the system South Africa built to turn that confrontation into regulated negotiation. Today: why labour law exists at all; the three foundational statutes — the Labour Relations Act, the Basic Conditions of Employment Act, and the Compensation for Occupational Injuries and Diseases Act; the machinery of collective bargaining — unions, bargaining councils, workplace forums; and the dispute resolution ladder — the CCMA's conciliation, mediation and arbitration, and the labour courts above them. The topic examines as institutions matched to functions: know which body does what, and why.

## Subtopic: Why Labour Law Exists

Begin with the imbalance the law answers. An individual work-seeker facing an employer negotiates from weakness: the firm can usually wait longer than a hungry applicant; one worker among hundreds of applicants has no bargaining power over terms — take the offer or leave it. Industrial history showed where unregulated imbalance leads: the hours, wages and conditions that made the factory age notorious. Labour law exists to civilise that bargain — not to abolish the employment relationship but to set floors beneath it, rules around it, and collective weight behind the weaker side.

In South Africa the foundation is constitutional: the Bill of Rights guarantees fair labour practices, the right to form and join trade unions, the right of unions to organise and bargain collectively, and the right to strike. Employers hold their mirror rights — to form employers' organisations and, subject to law, to lock out. Beneath the Constitution stand the statutes, and international labour conventions — standards of the International Labour Organisation that South Africa has ratified — inform them.

The Labour Relations Act carries three principles the syllabus names. SELF-GOVERNMENT: the parties — organised workers and employers — regulate their own relationship through agreements they negotiate themselves; the state sets the framework, not the wage. MAJORITARIANISM: the union representing the MAJORITY of workers in a workplace or sector speaks for that workforce and gains organisational rights — one authoritative voice rather than a babble of fragments. CONSULTATION: employers must consult and disclose before decisions that affect workers — retrenchments above all; workers are parties to be engaged, not variables to be adjusted.

## Subtopic: The Three Acts

Three statutes divide the field; know each Act by its JOB.

The LABOUR RELATIONS ACT (LRA): the rules of ENGAGEMENT. It gives unions their organisational rights, regulates collective bargaining and its institutions — bargaining councils, workplace forums — protects the right to strike (with procedures: disputes referred, notice given) and the employer's lock-out; protects workers against unfair dismissal and defines fair procedure; and creates the dispute-resolution machinery — the CCMA and the Labour Courts. When the question is HOW parties negotiate, strike, or resolve disputes — the LRA is the answer's home.

The BASIC CONDITIONS OF EMPLOYMENT ACT (BCEA): the FLOOR beneath every individual job. It sets maximum ordinary working hours and overtime rules — the multipliers your Accounting classmates calculate; minimum leave — annual, sick, family responsibility, maternity; meal intervals and rest periods; notice periods for termination; and the ban on child labour and forced labour. These are minimums no contract may undercut: parties may agree better, never worse. When the question is WHAT any single employee is at least entitled to — the BCEA answers.

The COMPENSATION FOR OCCUPATIONAL INJURIES AND DISEASES ACT (COIDA): the SAFETY NET after harm. Workers injured on duty or contracting occupational diseases claim compensation from a state-administered fund financed by employer contributions — medical costs, disability payments, dependants' benefits after fatal accidents. The design is a trade: workers get compensation without having to sue and prove fault; employers fund the system and gain protection from individual lawsuits. When the question is what happens after the accident — COIDA.

Stop for this section's questions now — the three Acts matched to their jobs, with two provisions each; the floor-not-ceiling principle stated.

## Subtopic: Collective Bargaining and Its Institutions

COLLECTIVE BARGAINING is the engine of self-government: workers negotiating as one body, through representatives, over wages, hours and conditions — turning many weak individual bargains into one strong collective one.

The institutions, each with its function. TRADE UNIONS: membership organisations of workers — recruiting, representing individuals in grievances and hearings, negotiating collectively, and, when procedures are exhausted, organising lawful strikes. Their power is the sum the syllabus's earlier lessons prepared: one worker asking is a request; every worker asking together is a negotiation. EMPLOYERS' ORGANISATIONS mirror them on the other side. BARGAINING COUNCILS: standing negotiation bodies for a whole SECTOR — unions and employers' organisations jointly running sector-wide bargaining; agreements reached there can be extended to the entire sector, setting one floor for all competitors; councils also resolve disputes within their sectors. WORKPLACE FORUMS: consultation structures INSIDE a single large workplace — elected by all workers, union members or not — designed for ongoing consultation on workplace matters: restructuring, work methods — dialogue between negotiation rounds; consultation, not wage bargaining, is their lane.

Keep the levels distinct for the examiner: the union organises and negotiates; the bargaining council hosts sector-level negotiation; the workplace forum consults inside one workplace. Three rooms, three conversations.

## Subtopic: When Talks Fail — the CCMA and the Courts

Negotiation sometimes deadlocks; dismissals are sometimes disputed. The system's answer is a LADDER of dispute resolution, designed so that most disputes settle low and cheaply.

The COMMISSION FOR CONCILIATION, MEDIATION AND ARBITRATION — the CCMA: an independent statutory body, free to use, where most labour disputes must first go. Its three processes, distinguished precisely because the examination does. CONCILIATION: a commissioner brings the parties together and facilitates their OWN settlement — the commissioner chairs, probes, calms; the parties decide; nothing is imposed. MEDIATION: the intermediary goes a step further — actively PROPOSING solutions, which the parties remain free to accept or refuse. ARBITRATION: the commissioner hears evidence from both sides and DECIDES — an award, binding on the parties. The ladder's logic: talk first (conciliation), guided proposals if needed (mediation), an imposed decision only when the parties cannot decide (arbitration).

Above the CCMA stand the LABOUR COURT — a specialist court of law for labour matters: reviewing CCMA awards, interpreting the statutes, ruling on the lawfulness of strikes and retrenchments — and the LABOUR APPEAL COURT above it. And outside the machinery, the pressure instruments remain lawful last resorts: the protected STRIKE — workers withdrawing labour after procedures are followed — and the employer's LOCK-OUT. Protection matters: a procedural (protected) strike shields strikers from dismissal for striking; an unprotected one does not.

The system's design, in one sentence for the essay questions: rights in the Constitution, floors in the BCEA, rules of engagement in the LRA, compensation in COIDA, negotiation in councils and workplaces, disputes up a ladder from talking to deciding — confrontation converted, at every stage, into procedure.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same system from the factory gate — because every piece of this machinery answers a question workers have asked for two centuries: what do we do when we're not strong enough alone?

## Subtopic: One Voice Against the Wind

Picture one worker at the manager's door: "The wage is too low; the shifts are too long." The manager looks past him at the queue outside the gate — a hundred people wanting his job. What weight does his voice carry? Almost none. Alone, a worker negotiates against the wind.

Now picture ALL the workers at the door, together, with one spokesperson: "The wage is too low — for all of us." Suddenly the manager is not weighing one replaceable person against a queue; he is weighing the whole factory's output against the demand. THAT is the entire logic of the union: many weak voices, braided into one strong one. It's the stokvel principle applied to work — together, we can do what none of us can alone.

And the law doesn't just allow this braiding — the Constitution GUARANTEES it: the right to join a union, to bargain together, and, when all else fails, to strike. The employers hold mirror rights — their own organisations, and the lock-out. The state's role is referee, not player: it writes the rules of the contest and lets the sides settle the score themselves — that's the "self-government" idea. Two more house rules keep the contest orderly: the union that MOST workers join speaks for the workplace — one voice, not a shouting match of splinters; and the boss must CONSULT before big decisions that hit workers — retrenchment may never arrive as a surprise memo.

## Subtopic: The Three Rulebooks

South Africa keeps three rulebooks on the shelf above the factory gate, and each answers a different bad day.

Rulebook one — the LRA — for the day the ARGUING happens: it sets how unions organise, how bargaining works, how a legal strike must be called (procedures first, notice given), and where deadlocks go. It's the rulebook of the contest itself.

Rulebook two — the BCEA — for every ORDINARY day: the floor no job may sink below. Your hours have a legal maximum; overtime has its multipliers; leave — annual, sick, family, maternity — is yours by right, not by favour; notice must be given; and children may not be put to work. Read the floor idea correctly: a contract may promise MORE than the floor, never less — the rulebook is a minimum, not a menu. If a Saturday job ever offers you conditions below it, the offer itself is unlawful.

Rulebook three — COIDA — for the WORST day: the injury on duty, the disease from the work. The worker (or the family, after the worst) claims from a compensation fund that employers pay into — medical costs, disability support, dependants' benefits. And notice the deal inside it: the worker doesn't have to sue and prove whose fault it was — the fund pays; in exchange, employers fund the system rather than facing lawsuits. Compensation as a system, not a court battle.

Three rulebooks, three days: the arguing day, the ordinary day, the worst day. Name the day, and you've named the Act.

## Subtopic: The Ladder in the Referee's Room

Last piece: what happens when the two sides deadlock — the wage talks stall, or a worker says "that dismissal was unfair." The system's answer is a ladder, climbed one rung at a time, in a free referee's room called the CCMA.

Rung one — CONCILIATION: the referee sits the two sides at one table and helps them talk — cooling tempers, clarifying what each really needs. The referee proposes nothing and decides nothing; if agreement comes, it is the PARTIES' agreement. Most disputes end here — because most disputes are half misunderstanding.

Rung two — MEDIATION: the referee leans in — "have you considered this middle path?" — actively suggesting solutions. Still, the sides remain free: suggestions, not orders.

Rung three — ARBITRATION: talking is done. The referee hears both sides' evidence like a judge, then DECIDES — and the decision binds. The ladder's wisdom is its order: own-agreement first, guided-agreement second, imposed-decision last — because a settlement the parties built themselves is kept willingly, while an imposed one is merely obeyed.

Above the room stand the real courts — the Labour Court and its appeal court — for the hardest questions of law. And outside, the last-resort pressure tools stay lawful IF the ladder was climbed first: the protected strike — labour withdrawn after procedures, strikers shielded from dismissal for striking — and the employer's lock-out. Skip the procedures, and the strike loses its shield.

Hold the whole machine in one image: a contest with guaranteed rights on both sides, floors under every job, a rulebook for the contest, a fund for the injured, and a ladder that turns every deadlock into procedure instead of chaos. That's labour relations — the mill floor's old war, converted into rules.
