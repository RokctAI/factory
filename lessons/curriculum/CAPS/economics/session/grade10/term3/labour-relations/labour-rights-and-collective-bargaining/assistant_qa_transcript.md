**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! First: if unions make wages higher, don't they just worsen the unemployment we learned about last lesson? It feels like the two topics contradict each other.

**Tutor:** They don't contradict — they meet, and the meeting point is where honest analysis lives. Last lesson's diagram is real: push the wage above where the market would rest and firms, at the margin, hire fewer — collective bargaining faces the same trade-off as the legislated minimum wage. But three things complicate the simple picture. First, bargaining is not only about the wage LEVEL: unions negotiate safety, hours, training and procedure — gains that cost jobs far less than equivalent cash. Second, the bargained wage corrects a genuine imbalance: against an employer with waiting power, the unorganised wage settles BELOW what a fair contest would produce — the union may be moving the wage toward, not past, a balanced outcome. Third, the costs and gains land on different people: the employed insider gains; the outsider at the gate may wait longer — the insider-outsider tension, and it is real in South Africa. So the mature sentence: collective bargaining trades some employment risk for protection and voice, the balance depending on how far wages move and how the economy grows — the same evidence-not-slogan discipline as the minimum wage lesson. The topics are one topic, seen from two sides of the gate.

***

**Thandi:** Next: why does the law make the MAJORITY union the voice of everyone — even workers who never joined it? That seems unfair to the minority.

**Tutor:** It is a deliberate trade of some individual choice for collective effectiveness, and the reasoning deserves both halves. The problem majoritarianism solves: a workplace with five splinter unions, each speaking for a fraction, gives the employer the oldest tactic in the book — divide, delay, play voices against each other — and gives the workforce a babble instead of a bargain. One authoritative voice concentrates the collective weight that is the entire point of organising; agreements bind the workplace, so gains reach members and non-members alike — which also answers part of your fairness worry: the non-member is REPRESENTED in outcome even without joining. The remaining cost is real: minority unions have reduced standing, and a worker who dissents from the majority's line is carried along by it. The law softens this at the edges — thresholds for organisational rights, the freedom to build a rival majority — but the design choice stands: labour law prefers one strong voice with imperfect consent over many pure voices with no power. Name both the function and the cost, and you have answered as the examiner hopes.

***

**Thandi:** This one's practical: my cousin was dismissed and says she's 'taking them to the CCMA.' Walk us through what actually happens to her, step by step.

**Tutor:** A walk worth knowing before your first payslip. She refers the dispute to the CCMA — free of charge, on a simple form, within the time limit (for dismissals, quickly: thirty days is the mark to remember). First stop: CONCILIATION — a commissioner sits her and the employer at one table, probing whether the thing can settle: reinstatement, compensation, a reference, sometimes just a misunderstanding unwound. No lawyers needed; nothing imposed; most cases genuinely end here. If it fails, the commissioner certifies the deadlock, and she requests ARBITRATION: a hearing where both sides bring evidence and witnesses — was there a fair REASON for dismissal, and was a fair PROCEDURE followed? Those are the two questions the law asks of every dismissal. The commissioner then issues an award — binding: perhaps reinstatement, perhaps compensation, perhaps a finding that the dismissal stood. If either side believes the arbitration itself went legally wrong, review lies to the Labour Court — a review of the process, not a fresh trial. Two practical lessons for her and for you: keep every document — contract, payslips, warnings, the dismissal letter — because arbitration runs on evidence; and respect the time limits, because a late referral needs special permission. The machine is genuinely built for ordinary people to use without a lawyer — that is its design triumph — but it rewards those who arrive prepared.

**Thandi:** Last one: strikes make the news as chaos — burning tyres, lost weeks, anger. Is the right to strike really worth what it costs?

**Tutor:** Answer it the way the Constitution's drafters did, with eyes open on both ledgers. The cost ledger is real and must be stated: strikes forfeit wages workers can ill afford, halt production the economy needs, can turn violent at their worst, and long strikes scar firms, towns and trust. Nobody sensible romanticises them. The other ledger: without the FINAL possibility of collective refusal, bargaining is theatre — an employer who knows workers can never walk away can simply wait out every demand; the strike's credible existence is what makes the peaceful settlements happen, which is why the overwhelming majority of wage rounds end WITHOUT one. History adds its testimony: where strikes are banned, the imbalance the whole topic began with returns in full — and in South Africa specifically, the strike was among the few levers the disenfranchised ever held; its constitutional protection carries that memory deliberately. And note how much of the chaos the DESIGN already answers: protection only after procedures — referral, conciliation attempted, notice given — so the protected strike is the system working, the last rung reached in order, not a breakdown. So: worth it, not because strikes are good, but because the RIGHT to strike is what stands behind every quiet settlement — the stick that makes the carrot conversations real. An answer that holds both ledgers, then explains why the right survives its costs, is exactly the maturity this syllabus is building.
