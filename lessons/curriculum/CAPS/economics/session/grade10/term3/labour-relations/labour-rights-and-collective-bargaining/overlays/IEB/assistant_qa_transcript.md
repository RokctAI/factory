**Thandi:** Time for the class questions! First one, straight from the debate at the back: last lesson said pushing wages above equilibrium leaves workers unbought — and now this lesson celebrates unions pushing wages up. Which lesson are we supposed to believe?

**Tutor:** Believe both — the tension between them is not a contradiction, it is the actual subject. The diagram from last lesson does not switch off: raise the wage above where the market would settle it and firms, at the margin, hire fewer hands — collective bargaining and the legislated minimum wage sit on the same trade-off. But add three refinements before you conclude anything. One: unions bargain over far more than the wage number — safety, hours, training, fair procedure — and those gains cost employment much less than the same value taken as cash. Two: remember WHERE the unorganised wage starts. Against an employer who can outwait any individual, the lone-bargained wage settles below what an evenly matched negotiation would produce — so a union may be pulling the wage toward the balanced point, not past it. Three: track who carries which consequence. The employed member gains security and pay; the job-seeker at the fence may wait longer for a gate to open — insiders and outsiders, and in South Africa that tension is not theoretical. So the sentence that earns full credit runs: collective bargaining exchanges some employment risk for protection and voice, and the balance depends on how far wages move and how fast the economy grows. Two lessons, one gate — viewed from opposite sides.

***

**Thandi:** Next question: majoritarianism bothers some of us. If I never joined the big union, why does it get to speak for me? Where did my choice go?

**Tutor:** Your choice was traded — deliberately, in the open — for collective effectiveness, and a full answer weighs what was bought against what was paid. Start with the disease majoritarianism cures: picture a workplace where five small unions each claim a slice of the workforce. The employer's playbook writes itself — meet them separately, delay each one, quote one against another — and the workers arrive as a babble rather than a bargain. Concentrating recognition in the majority union restores the one thing organising exists to create: a single voice heavy enough to matter. Now your side of the ledger: what happens to the worker who never joined? The collective agreement covers the workplace, so the non-member receives the bargained wage, hours and protections all the same — unrepresented in membership, yet represented in outcome. What remains genuinely lost: minority unions carry less standing, and a dissenting worker is pulled along by the majority's chosen line. The law files down the sharpest edges — thresholds that give smaller unions certain organisational rights, and the permanent freedom to recruit until yesterday's minority becomes tomorrow's majority — but the core choice is honest and stated: one imperfect strong voice over many perfect weak ones. Give the cure, the benefit that reaches you anyway, and the cost — that three-part answer is the complete one.

**Thandi:** Third question, a practical one: say I get my first job at a restaurant and one Friday the manager just says don't come back. What are my actual steps?

**Tutor:** Walk it as you would walk it in real life, because one day you may. Step zero happens before any dispute: keep your papers — the contract, payslips, any warnings, the message that ended it. Labour disputes run on evidence, and the prepared person wins arguments the unprepared person loses. Step one: refer the dismissal dispute to the CCMA — no fee, a simple form, and quickly: for dismissals the clock is short, thirty days is the number to hold. Step two: CONCILIATION. A commissioner sits you and the restaurant at one table and tests whether the matter can settle — back pay, reinstatement, a proper reference, sometimes an apology and a misunderstanding untangled. No lawyers required, nothing imposed, and a large share of cases end right there. Step three, if the deadlock is certified: ARBITRATION. Now it resembles a courtroom — both sides bring evidence and witnesses, and the law asks its two standing questions about every dismissal: was there a fair REASON, and was a fair PROCEDURE followed? A Friday-night 'don't come back' with no hearing already stumbles on the second question. The commissioner's award binds: reinstatement, compensation, or a finding that the dismissal stood. Step four, rarely: if the arbitration itself misfired legally, the Labour Court reviews the process — a check on how the decision was made, not a second bite at the apple. The whole staircase was built so an ordinary worker can climb it without a lawyer — but climb it promptly, papers in hand.

**Thandi:** Last one: every strike on the news looks like losses all round — lost wages, lost production, sometimes worse. Why does the Constitution protect something that expensive?

**Tutor:** Because the drafters priced it with both ledgers open, and so should your answer. Concede the costs first, fully: strikers forfeit wages that thin households feel immediately; production stops that the economy can ill afford; the worst strikes turn destructive; and a long one leaves scars on firms, towns and trust that outlast the settlement. None of that is romantic. Now the other ledger, which is subtler: remove the final possibility of collective refusal, and every negotiation becomes theatre — an employer who knows the workers can never walk out simply waits, and the imbalance this whole topic began with returns intact. The strike's credible existence is precisely what makes strikes rare: the overwhelming majority of wage rounds settle peacefully BECAUSE the last resort stands behind the table. South Africa's history sharpens the point — for generations of workers here, denied the vote, withdrawal of labour was among the only levers within reach, and the Constitution remembers that on purpose. And observe how much of the televised chaos the design already addresses: protection attaches only AFTER the ladder — referral, attempted conciliation, notice given — so a protected strike is not the system failing but its final rung reached in proper order, while the unprotected wildcat carries no shield at all. So the mature verdict: the right earns its keep not through the strikes that happen but through the thousands of quiet settlements that happen because it could. Hold both ledgers, land on that verdict, and the essay writes itself.
