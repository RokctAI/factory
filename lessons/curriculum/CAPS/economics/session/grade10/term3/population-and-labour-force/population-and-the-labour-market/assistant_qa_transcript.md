**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! First — the one that upset people: why does the strict definition throw Jabu out? He WANTS work. Excluding him feels like the statistics lying to make things look better.

**Tutor:** The exclusion has a methodological reason, and your suspicion has a democratic one — both deserve stating. The strict definition demands active searching because it makes the count VERIFIABLE: a survey can ask 'what did you do in the last month to find work?' and record concrete answers — applications, enquiries, registrations. 'Wanting' work, without action, is harder to measure consistently: answers drift with mood and phrasing, and international comparison collapses if every country measures wishes differently. So the strict rate is the comparable, auditable core. BUT — and South Africa is the world's great case for this — where searching itself costs money that the poorest lack, and where jobs are so scarce that searching is rationally abandoned, the strict count systematically hides the deepest distress. That is why Statistics South Africa publishes BOTH rates, side by side, every quarter. The honest practice — and the full-marks exam answer — is to cite both and say what the gap between them measures: discouragement, concentrated exactly where hope is thinnest. Statistics don't lie here; readers who quote only the friendlier number do.

**Thandi:** Next: if the demand for labour is derived from the demand for goods, does that mean workers can never improve their position — we just wait for customers to buy more?

**Tutor:** Derived does not mean helpless; it means the levers are specific — and knowing them is the point. Lever one sits on the demand side but within reach: anything that raises the demand for what you produce raises the demand for you — which is why workers' fortunes rise with exports, investment and the upswing, and why growth policy IS employment policy. Lever two is yours alone: SKILL. Derived demand is derived per gate — move from the crowded gate to the scarce one and the same economy demands you at a different wage; training rewrites which demand you are derived from. Lever three is productivity: a worker whose output rises is worth more at any level of goods demand — tools, experience and education raise the ceiling a firm will pay. And lever four, next lesson's subject, is collective: organised workers negotiating the wage within the room the other levers create. So the derivation sets the weather, not the destiny: you cannot personally summon orders, but you can choose which queue you stand in, what you carry to it, and whether you negotiate alone.

**Thandi:** This one's from the map: why are the heaviest unemployment rates in the former homelands? Jobs could be created anywhere — why does geography keep winning?

**Tutor:** Because geography is congealed history, and firms choose locations by cold economics that history loaded. The homelands were deliberately placed far from mines, ports and cities — reservoirs of labour, not centres of production — with minimal infrastructure investment for decades. Firms today still locate where markets, suppliers, transport and skills cluster: the metros. That creates the cruel arithmetic your question senses: the people are where the jobs were never built, and moving toward work costs exactly what the jobless lack — transport money, urban rent, a foothold. Meanwhile a spaza economy in a low-income area finds thin local demand — the circular flow turning small — so even local entrepreneurship fights uphill. The mechanisms for your answers: distance as a cost wedge between worker and wage; infrastructure gaps deterring firms; thin local demand suppressing local hiring — and the policy directions that follow: transport subsidies, infrastructure investment, and bringing training TO where people are. When an exam table shows provincial unemployment, this mechanism — apartheid's spatial design meeting the firm's location logic — is the analysis the marks are waiting for.

**Thandi:** Last one: our population is still young and growing — is that good news or bad news for the economy? I've heard it argued both ways.

**Tutor:** Both arguments are real, and the honest answer is conditional — economists call it the demographic DIVIDEND, and the operative word is 'potential'. The promise: a young population means, for a few decades, a bulge of working-age people supporting relatively few elderly — more potential producers per dependant than an ageing country enjoys. Nations that caught that wave — several East Asian economies famously — rode it to transformation: the bulge worked, saved and built. The condition: the dividend pays ONLY if the young bulge is educated, healthy and EMPLOYED. A young cohort absorbed into work is a growth engine; the same cohort standing at closed gates is foregone output, strained grants, and social pressure — the dividend inverting into a burden. So the question 'good or bad news?' is really the question 'will the gates open?' — which hangs on everything this term teaches: the skills pipeline against the mismatch, investment moving the production boundary outward, the labour market absorbing rather than excluding. Your generation IS the dividend, still unbanked. Whether it pays out is not written in the population pyramid — it is decided by policy and economics, which is exactly why you are being taught them.
