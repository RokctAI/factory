# Part 1 — Expert

An economy is, before anything else, its people — producing, earning, consuming. Today we count them properly: how populations grow and pass through the demographic cycle; how migration reshapes them; who, inside the population, actually constitutes the LABOUR FORCE; how South Africa's labour force is distributed by age and geography; and how the labour market itself works — demand and supply meeting to set wages. The topic anchors the term's remaining lessons: labour relations next, unemployment after.

## Subtopic: Population Growth and the Demographic Cycle

Population SIZE changes through two doors: NATURAL GROWTH — births minus deaths — and MIGRATION — arrivals minus departures. The natural growth RATE is expressed per thousand of population: a country with 25 births and 10 deaths per thousand grows naturally at 15 per thousand — one comma five percent a year, doubling in roughly two generations if sustained.

The DEMOGRAPHIC CYCLE describes the path populations follow as societies develop, in four broad stages. Stage one — HIGH STATIONARY: high birth rates AND high death rates — many born, many dying young of disease and famine; population barely grows; all pre-industrial societies lived here. Stage two — EARLY EXPANDING: death rates FALL — clean water, vaccines, medicine, food security — while birth rates stay high: the gap between them is rapid population growth. Stage three — LATE EXPANDING: birth rates begin falling too — urbanisation, education (especially of women), family planning, children shifting from farm labour to school costs; growth slows. Stage four — LOW STATIONARY: low births, low deaths; population stabilises — and in several wealthy countries now declines without immigration.

South Africa sits in the later expanding phase: death rates long fallen, birth rates declining, growth continuing but slowing — a YOUNG population still, with large cohorts entering working age each year: the fact that drives everything in this term. MIGRATION adds its layer: internal migration — rural to urban, toward Gauteng and the Western Cape — reshaping provinces; and international migration in both directions, with the region's workers drawn to South Africa's economy as South Africans also emigrate.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: the two doors, the natural growth calculation, and the four stages with their driving causes.

## Subtopic: Who Is the Labour Force?

Precision of definition carries this section's marks — the terms nest inside each other like bowls.

The POPULATION: everyone. Inside it, the WORKING-AGE population: conventionally ages 15 to 64 — those who COULD work. Inside that, the LABOUR FORCE (the economically active population): those working-age people who are either EMPLOYED — working for pay or profit — or UNEMPLOYED BY THE STRICT DEFINITION: without work, AVAILABLE to work, and ACTIVELY SEEKING it. Outside the labour force stand working-age people neither working nor seeking: full-time learners and students, homemakers, the retired-early, the ill — and one group the examiner watches: DISCOURAGED WORK-SEEKERS, those who want work but have stopped searching because they believe none exists. The strict definition excludes them from unemployment; the EXPANDED definition counts them back in — and in South Africa the gap between the two definitions is several percentage points of human reality.

The UNEMPLOYMENT RATE: unemployed as a percentage of the LABOUR FORCE — not of the population; dividing by the wrong bowl is the classic error. The LABOUR FORCE PARTICIPATION RATE: the labour force as a percentage of the working-age population — how much of the potential team has stepped forward.

South Africa's labour force profile, in shape rather than perishable numbers: a YOUNG labour force, with youth unemployment far above the national rate — the young queue longest at the gate; a GEOGRAPHIC concentration — jobs clustered in metropolitan cores, Gauteng above all, while former homeland areas carry few jobs and heavy unemployment, a spatial pattern inherited from apartheid's geography; and participation below what a fully absorbing economy would draw, with discouragement doing the suppressing.

Stop for this section's questions now — the nested bowls defined strictly, both definitions of unemployment, and the two rates with their correct denominators.

## Subtopic: The Demand and Supply for Labour

The labour market is a market — with households and firms swapped across the counter: HOUSEHOLDS supply labour; FIRMS demand it; the WAGE is its price. Both curves have their own logic.

The DEMAND for labour is a DERIVED demand — the term is examinable: firms want workers not for their own sake but for the OUTPUT workers produce; demand for builders derives from demand for buildings. It follows that labour demand rises when the economy grows, when exports boom, when investment builds new capacity — and falls in the downswings of term one's business cycle. The demand curve slopes downward in the wage: at higher wages, firms hire fewer workers, substitute machines, or produce less.

The SUPPLY of labour comes from the population side: its size set by demographics and participation, its shape by skills. The supply curve slopes upward in the wage: better pay draws more people into seeking work and longer hours. Where the curves cross, the market wage forms — and everything term two taught about markets applies: a wage above equilibrium leaves labour unsold (unemployment among those seeking at that wage); a wage floor — the minimum wage — sits exactly where the intervention lesson put it.

The South African complication the syllabus expects: the labour market is not ONE market but many, divided by SKILL. For scarce skills — engineers, artisans, specialists — demand outruns supply: wages high and rising, posts unfilled. For unskilled labour, supply enormously outruns demand: wages pressed down, queues at every gate. The SKILLS MISMATCH — an education pipeline producing too few of what firms seek while millions offer what firms need less of — is the structural heart of South African unemployment, and the bridge to the next lessons.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — derived demand explained, both curves' logic, and the skills-divided market applied to South Africa.

## Subtopic: Reading the Labour Statistics

Close with the reading skills the examination rewards.

Skill one: DEFINITIONS before numbers. Every labour statistic depends on its definition — strict against expanded unemployment can differ by millions of people; quote which definition a figure uses before interpreting it.

Skill two: RATES with their denominators. The unemployment rate divides by the labour force; participation divides by working-age population; youth unemployment divides young unemployed by the young labour force. An answer that names numerator and denominator earns the method marks even before the arithmetic.

Skill three: STRUCTURE over headline. One national rate hides the structure: unemployment concentrated among the young, the unskilled, and particular provinces; participation suppressed by discouragement; employment split between formal and informal work. The examiner's scenario questions live in the structure — a table by age or province, and comment.

Skill four: MECHANISM in comments. Why is youth unemployment highest? First-time seekers lack experience and networks; entry-level demand is weakest exactly where supply floods. Why do former homeland areas carry the heaviest rates? Jobs concentrated elsewhere by history; distance and transport costs stand between people and posts. A comment that connects the number to its mechanism — through demand, supply, skills or geography — is the difference between describing a table and analysing it.

The final questions of this part are with you now: definitions quoted, denominators named, structures read, mechanisms connected.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same topic from the household gate — because every family already contains a population census, a labour force survey, and a wage negotiation or two.

## Subtopic: The Street as a Census

Run a census of one street, and the whole demographic topic appears. Count the births this year, count the funerals — the difference is the street's NATURAL growth. Add who moved in and who moved away — migration — and you have the street's population change, complete. A country is the same sum with more zeros.

Now the four seasons every society's numbers pass through — told as the street's history. Long ago: many babies born, many lives short — the street's numbers barely moved, cradles and funerals balancing. Then the clinic arrived — clean water, vaccines — and the funerals grew rare while the cradles stayed full: the street BOOMED; every house overflowing with children. A generation later: daughters finishing school, families planning smaller — cradles fewer; the boom easing. And some day, like the old countries overseas: few cradles, few funerals — numbers standing still, the street ageing.

South Africa's street is in the third season: still young, still growing, but slower — and here is the sentence that explains this whole term: EVERY YEAR, A BIG NEW CLASS OF YOUNG PEOPLE FINISHES SCHOOL AND ARRIVES AT THE GATE LOOKING FOR WORK. The size of that arriving class, against the number of gates that open — that is the drama of the next three lessons.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the street census are coming to you right now: the two doors of change, and the four seasons with what drove each.

## Subtopic: Who's On the Team?

Now sort one household, person by person, the way the statisticians do — because the words matter and the sorting is the exam.

Gogo, seventy-two: past working age — counted in the population, not in the working-age group. The eight-year-old twins: same — too young. Mama, working at the clinic: WORKING-AGE and EMPLOYED — on the team, playing. Uncle S'bu, who applied at three sites this month and phones about vacancies weekly: not working, but AVAILABLE and SEARCHING — on the team, benched but warming up: officially UNEMPLOYED. Your matric sister: working-age, but full-time at school — NOT on the team; learners don't count in the labour force. And cousin Jabu — two years of applications, nothing, and this year he's simply stopped asking: wants work, seeks none. The strict count says he's NOT unemployed — he left the team sheet. The expanded count says: look at him — of course he's unemployed. That gap between the two counts is thousands of real Jabus, and knowing BOTH definitions is knowing the truth has two measurements.

The two team numbers, street version: the unemployment rate asks — of everyone ON the team (playing plus benched), what share is benched? Never divide by the whole household — gogo and the twins aren't on the team sheet. And participation asks: of everyone OLD ENOUGH for the team, how many stepped forward at all? Jabu's quiet exit lowers that one — discouragement hides inside participation, not inside the strict unemployment rate.

Your questions for this part are up now: sort a fresh household onto and off the team sheet, and divide by the right group.

## Subtopic: The Wage Is a Price at a Gate

Last piece: the labour market, at the factory gate at dawn.

See it as a market with the counter turned around: this time the FAMILIES are the sellers — offering hours, hands and skills — and the FIRMS are the buyers. The wage is just the price where the two sides meet. And both sides behave exactly like term two's cooler-box market: offer higher wages and more people come to the gate (supply rises); but at higher wages the firm hires fewer and machines look cheaper (demand falls). Somewhere the two leanings cross — the going wage.

One idea gives the firm's side its depth: the firm doesn't want workers — it wants what workers MAKE. Nobody hires a builder for company; they hire because someone wants a wall. So when the economy booms and orders pour in, gates open wider; when the downswing comes, the same gates close — the demand for labour is BORROWED from the demand for goods. Economists say derived; the street says: no orders, no shifts.

And the gate is not one gate. At the engineers' gate, the queue is short and firms bid against each other — scarce skills, strong wages. At the general-labour gate, the queue stretches down the road — many offering, few needed — and the wage presses toward the floor the law sets. Same morning, same town, two opposite markets — split by SKILL. That's why the ladder out of the long queue is training: a certificate is a ticket from the crowded gate to the short one. And it's why the country's deepest labour problem has a name you now own: the skills mismatch — too many standing at the gate the economy needs least, too few trained for the gate it needs most.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: the turned-around market drawn, derived demand in street words, and the two gates — with the ticket between them. Next lesson: what happens when the people at the gate organise.
