# Part 1 — Expert

Terms one and two studied the economy as it IS; term three opens by asking how it CAME TO BE. Today we trace economic development from self-sufficient households to industrial economies: the birth of agriculture and surplus, barter and its failures, the emergence of money, the evolution of markets through specialisation, transport, law and business forms, the state's entry as regulator and builder — and finally industrialisation, when machines and mobilised energy remade production. The story examines as sequence and causation: each stage SOLVES a problem the previous stage created.

## Subtopic: From Self-Sufficiency to Surplus and Barter

The earliest economic unit was the SELF-SUFFICIENT household or community: hunting, gathering, later herding — each group producing everything it consumed. No trade, no markets, no money: the three basic processes collapsed into one household's labour. Self-sufficiency's constraint is brutal: you consume only what you yourself can make, and every skill must live in every household.

The AGRICULTURAL revolution broke the constraint. Settled farming — planting, harvesting, storing — produced, for the first time, SURPLUS: more than the producers themselves needed. Surplus is the hinge of all economic history, because it releases people from food production: some can now specialise as toolmakers, weavers, builders — and specialists producing what they do NOT intend to consume must EXCHANGE. Trade is surplus's child.

The first exchange was BARTER: goods directly for goods — a goat for grain, pots for cloth. And barter's failures are a set-piece the examination expects in full. The DOUBLE COINCIDENCE OF WANTS: trade requires finding someone who has what you want AND wants what you have — search costs that strangle exchange. INDIVISIBILITY: half a goat buys nothing; large goods cannot make small purchases. NO COMMON MEASURE of value: is a goat worth three pots or five? Every pair of goods needs its own ratio. NO STORE of value: perishable surplus rots — wealth cannot wait. Each failure is an invitation for the invention that follows.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: self-sufficiency's constraint, surplus as the hinge, and barter's four failures stated precisely.

## Subtopic: Money and the Evolution of Markets

MONEY answers barter's failures point by point — which is why economists define it by its FUNCTIONS. A MEDIUM OF EXCHANGE: accepted by everyone, it dissolves the double coincidence — sell to anyone, buy from anyone else. A UNIT OF ACCOUNT: all values expressed in one measure — prices replace endless ratios. A STORE OF VALUE: sell the perishable surplus, hold the money, buy later — wealth can wait. And divisibility: coins and notes make change; any size of purchase works. Early moneys were commodities themselves — cattle, salt, shells, iron — chosen for durability, divisibility, portability and scarcity; precious metals won on all four, and coined money, then paper, then today's electronic entries followed. In Southern Africa, cattle long served as money and store of wealth — a local anchor for the concept.

With money in hand, MARKETS evolved along several tracks at once, and the syllabus names them. SPECIALISATION deepened — the division of labour: workers concentrating on single trades, then single tasks, multiplying productivity; specialisation and markets feed each other, since specialists need markets to sell in and buy from. TRADE AND TRANSPORT expanded together: roads, ships, later rail — every transport advance widened the circle a market could serve, turning local surplus into regional and international trade. MERCANTILE AND COMMERCIAL LAW emerged because strangers trading need enforceable rules: contracts, weights and measures, courts — trust made institutional. And BUSINESS FORMS evolved: the lone trader, then partnerships pooling capital, then companies — ownership divided into shares, risk spread, capital gathered at scales no individual could reach. TECHNOLOGICAL PROGRESS threaded through all of it, each improvement in tools and methods raising what markets could carry.

Stop for this section's questions now — money's four functions matched to barter's four failures, and the market's evolutionary tracks named with their logic.

## Subtopic: Governments Enter the Market

As markets grew, the STATE grew into them — and the syllabus asks for its three historic entries.

First, INVOLVEMENT IN TRADE: rulers regulated marketplaces, chartered trading companies, controlled ports and routes, and protected merchants — because trade could be taxed and taxed trade funded power. The mercantile era's chartered companies — armed, monopolistic, state-licensed — brought European trade to these shores, with consequences South Africa's history carries still.

Second, TAXATION: the state's claim on economic activity — customs on trade, levies on land and produce, later taxes on income and spending. Taxation transformed the state's relationship to the economy: rulers now had a permanent interest in economic growth, since larger economies yield larger revenues — and taxpayers acquired a permanent interest in how rulers spend.

Third, FINANCING INFRASTRUCTURE: markets need what no single trader will build — roads, harbours, bridges, later railways, electricity and communications. Infrastructure is expensive, benefits everyone, and repays its builder only indirectly: the classic case for public investment. States that built infrastructure widened their markets and grew; those that did not, stagnated. The pattern persists into the present: every budget's infrastructure allocations continue this oldest of state economic functions.

Note the analytical thread: each state entry answers a market LIMITATION — unenforced contracts, unfunded common goods — exactly the intervention logic of term two, now seen as history.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — the three entries, each with its motive and its modern descendant.

## Subtopic: Industrial Development

The final stage transformed everything: INDUSTRIALISATION — production moving from hand and home to machine and factory, beginning in eighteenth-century Britain and spreading worldwide.

Its components, as the syllabus lists them. MANUFACTURING: goods made at scale in factories — the workshop's dozen becoming the factory's thousands, prices falling as volumes rose. TECHNOLOGICAL PROGRESS: machines embodying knowledge — the spinning frame, the power loom, precision tools making tools. The MOBILISATION OF ENERGY: the decisive break — human and animal muscle replaced by water, then steam from coal, then electricity and oil: production unshackled from the limits of living bodies. And MECHANISATION: machines taking over tasks across farming, transport and industry — the tractor, the railway, the assembly line.

The consequences ran everywhere. Productivity multiplied: one worker with machines out-producing dozens without. URBANISATION: factories gathered workers into cities — the labour force topic waiting in the next lessons was born here. New CLASSES and conflicts: industrial employers and industrial workers — the labour relations of weeks ahead. Transport and communication revolutions knitted world markets — globalisation's foundations. And costs arrived with the gains: harsh factory conditions, child labour, pollution — the very violations that summoned labour law and regulation.

For South Africa, industrialisation arrived through MINING: diamonds and gold industrialised the economy rapidly, drew railways inland, concentrated labour — under the migrant labour system whose social costs the country still carries. Development's story here, as everywhere, is gains and costs entangled: the analytical habit this whole term will demand.

The final questions of this part are with you now: industrialisation's four components, its consequences both ways, and the South African route through mining.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same ten-thousand-year story told small — one village, growing up. Every stage of world history happened, in miniature, on this one hillside.

## Subtopic: The Village That Fed Itself — Then Grew Extra

Start with one hillside village, long ago. Every family does everything: grows its food, sews its clothes, builds its hut, carves its tools. Nobody trades, because nobody has extra — and nobody CAN have extra, because doing everything means doing nothing particularly well. Life is full-time survival.

Then someone masters farming properly — planting seasons, storing grain — and one year, for the first time, there's MORE than the family can eat. Extra. Hold that word, because everything that follows in ten thousand years of economics follows from it. Extra means one family can eat without every member farming. The clever-handed son stops farming and makes pots all day — and his pots, made all day every day, are better than anyone's. The neighbour weaves. Someone smiths. Each becomes excellent at ONE thing — and now each NEEDS the others. The village has invented specialists, and specialists must trade.

First they swap: pots for grain, cloth for goats. And swapping is misery in four ways. You must find the exact person who wants pots AND has grain — the great hunt. You can't pay half a goat for a small blade. Nobody agrees what anything is worth — every deal is a fresh argument. And you can't save up goats' worth of wealth — wealth with legs walks off, wealth that's food rots. Four miseries — remember them, because the next invention kills all four at once.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the village are coming to you right now: why no-extra meant no-trade, and swapping's four miseries.

## Subtopic: The Invention That Fixed Everything

One market day, the village agrees: these iron pieces — rare, lasting, easy to carry, splittable — count as PAYMENT for anything. Watch four miseries die in one afternoon.

The great hunt is over: sell your pots to ANYONE with iron; buy grain from ANYONE who has it. The two halves of a swap have been cut apart — that's what money IS: half a trade, holdable. The half-goat problem dies: iron splits into big pieces and small — any size of purchase works. The endless arguments die: everything gets a price in iron — one measuring stick for all value, and suddenly comparing a pot to a goat to a day's labour is arithmetic, not war. And wealth can WAIT: sell the harvest before it rots, hold the iron, buy in winter. Sturdy, splittable, scarce, carryable — every good money in history passed those tests, from cattle and salt and shells to gold, to notes, to the numbers on a phone. The form keeps changing; the four jobs never do.

And with money working, the village market explodes outward. Better paths, then roads, then a harbour — each mile of transport pulls in more villages, more specialists, more goods. Strangers now trade with strangers — so the village writes RULES: honest scales, kept promises, an elder to judge disputes — trust, made official. And traders team up: first partners sharing a boat, later many families each owning a SHARE of a big venture, so no one person carries the whole risk. Markets, money, roads, rules, and shared ownership — the whole modern economy, assembled piece by piece on one hillside.

## Subtopic: The Chief, and Then the Machines

Two more arrivals complete the story. First: the CHIEF — the state — walks into the market. He does three things, and notice each one still happens every year in the national budget. He REGULATES the trade routes and licenses the big traders — order, and control. He TAXES: a share of every harvest and every deal — and from then on the chief WANTS the market to grow, because his income grows with it. And he BUILDS what no trader ever would alone: the bridge over the river, the harbour wall, the great road — everyone benefits, no single trader could afford it, so it takes everyone's tax. Regulate, tax, build: three-thousand-year-old jobs, still the state's jobs today.

Then, the machines. For all its markets and money, the village still makes everything by HAND — and hands set the limit. Until the day of the water wheel, then the coal engine: suddenly cloth is woven by machine, a hundred times faster; work moves from huts into the mill; families move from the hillside to the mill town. One worker with a machine out-produces a whole lane of hand-weavers — goods get cheap, cities get big, and two new kinds of people face each other across the mill floor: those who OWN the machines and those who WORK them. Their arguments — wages, hours, safety — are the very labour relations you'll study in two weeks; and the mill's smoke and its children at the looms are why the rulebooks came.

South Africa's version of the machine-day came out of the ground: diamonds, then gold — mines industrialising the country at speed, railways racing inland, men drawn from every village to the compounds. Gains and costs, tangled — which is this whole term's honest lens.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: the chief's three jobs traced to today's budget, the machine-day's gains and costs both counted, and the village's whole journey — extra, specialists, money, market, state, machines — told in order. Ten thousand years, one hillside: now it's yours.
