**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! First: the story makes it sound like barter died when money arrived. But people still swap — my cousin traded his old phone for a bicycle last month. Did barter ever really end?

**Tutor:** Barter never died; it retreated to the corners where its miseries don't bite. Your cousin's trade worked because the double coincidence happened to hold — he wanted a bicycle, the other party wanted that phone — and when the coincidence gifts itself, barter is perfectly efficient: no money needed, no change required. What money ended was RELIANCE on such luck for an economy's daily functioning. Notice too that your cousin almost certainly negotiated using money as the measuring stick — 'the phone is worth about R1 500, the bike about the same' — so even his barter borrowed money's unit-of-account function to find fairness. Barter also resurfaces at scale whenever money BREAKS: in hyperinflations, when notes lose value by the hour, people flee back to swapping goods — and rediscover all four miseries within weeks. So the accurate statement is: money didn't abolish barter; it demoted barter from the economy's engine to its occasional convenience — and barter's returns in crises are the proof of what money quietly does every day.

***

**Thandi:** Next: you said surplus released people into specialisation. But couldn't a village have specialists WITHOUT surplus — just very talented people doing what they're best at?

**Tutor:** Talent without surplus starves — that is the hard arithmetic underneath. A full-time potter makes no food; he eats only if farmers grow MORE than the farmers themselves need and part with the extra for pots. Before surplus, every household had to secure its own food first, which caps any talent at a part-time hobby squeezed around survival — the gifted carver whittles at night and farms all day, and his skill never compounds into mastery. Surplus is precisely the food that feeds the non-farmer: its size sets how many specialists a society can carry. You can read whole economies with that one ratio — where farming barely feeds the farmers, nearly everyone farms; where one farmer feeds dozens, as in industrial economies, the dozens become teachers, nurses, engineers, artists. Every specialist you will ever meet — including your future self — is fed by someone else's surplus. That is why the lesson called surplus the hinge: talent supplies the possibility, but surplus pays for it.

**Thandi:** This one's uncomfortable, so I'll ask it straight: the chartered trading companies in this history — armed, monopolistic, state-licensed — are how colonisation reached us. Is the syllabus asking us to admire this story?

**Tutor:** No — it is asking you to UNDERSTAND it, and understanding includes its violence. The analytical content stands: chartered companies solved real commercial problems — pooling capital for voyages no individual could fund, spreading risk through shares, carrying state authority into contracts across oceans. That machinery is genuinely the ancestor of the modern company. AND the same machinery, armed and licensed, seized land, monopolised trade by force, and helped build the colonial order whose economic patterns — who owned what, who laboured for whom, who was moved where — South Africa is still unwinding; the migrant labour system of our own mining industrialisation sits in that lineage. These are not two stories, one economic and one moral; they are one story, and the syllabus's own habit — gains and costs, both columns, honestly counted — is exactly the right instrument for it. An examiner asking about mercantile companies rewards the candidate who names both the institutional innovation and its human cost. History's tools built the present; naming precisely what they built, and on whom the costs fell, is not a digression from economics — it is economics, done with its eyes open.

**Thandi:** Last one: each stage 'solves the previous stage's problem' — money fixed barter, the state fixed the market's gaps, machines fixed the limits of hands. So what problem did the MACHINES create — and what's solving it?

**Tutor:** The finest question of the day, because it turns the lesson's method onto the present. The machines created several problems, and you can watch history answering each. Harsh factories and child labour summoned labour LAW and the unions — your topic in two weeks: the collective bargaining that turned the mill-floor confrontation into negotiation. The machines' smoke and waste created environmental damage on a new scale — summoning the regulation, environmental law and sustainability thinking your first term introduced, an answer still being written. Mechanisation's displacement of hands created recurring technological unemployment — answered, imperfectly, by education systems retraining people toward the work machines create rather than destroy; that race between machine and skill is ongoing, and today's version — automation and artificial intelligence — is simply its newest lap. And industry's concentration of economic power summoned competition law and the regulated mixed economy you live in. Notice the pattern that makes this subject coherent: every solution is itself imperfect and eventually generates the next problem — which is why economics never finishes. The village is still growing up; your generation inherits its newest problems, and — if the pattern holds — will build its newest answers. That is not a burden. That is what being in history feels like.
