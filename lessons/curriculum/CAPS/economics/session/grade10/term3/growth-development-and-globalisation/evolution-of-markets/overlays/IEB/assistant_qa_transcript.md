**Thandi:** Let's take the class questions before we finish! First up: money supposedly ended barter — but my aunt swapped a sack of oranges for her neighbour's spare paraffin last week, and half the neighbourhood trades favours. So did barter actually die?

**Tutor:** Barter is alive — it simply lost its job as the economy's engine and kept a small retirement post in the corners where its defects don't bite. Your aunt's swap succeeded because the double coincidence happened to be standing right there over the fence: she had oranges and wanted paraffin; the neighbour had paraffin and wanted oranges. When luck delivers the coincidence free of charge, barter is quick and clean — no change to count, no notes to carry. What money abolished was an economy's DEPENDENCE on that luck for its daily bread. And look closer at your aunt's deal: I would wager the fairness check ran through money anyway — the oranges are worth about so many rand, the paraffin about the same — meaning even her barter leaned on money's unit-of-account function as the invisible yardstick. One more piece of evidence seals the argument: whenever money collapses — in hyperinflations where notes lose value between breakfast and supper — whole societies stampede back to barter, and within weeks they are drowning in all four miseries again: hunting for partners, arguing values, unable to save. Barter's brief comebacks during monetary disasters are the clearest demonstration of the work money performs silently every ordinary day.

***

**Thandi:** Next question: the lesson says surplus created specialists. But surely talent creates specialists? If someone in the valley was a brilliant ironworker, wouldn't he specialise anyway, surplus or not?

**Tutor:** Let the arithmetic answer, because it is merciless: a man who works iron all day grows no millet, so he eats only if somebody ELSE grows more millet than their own family needs and will part with the extra for hoes. No surplus, no spare food; no spare food, no full-time ironworker — however brilliant. Before surplus, talent could only live as a sideline: the gifted smith farming from dawn to dusk and hammering by firelight, his skill never getting the ten thousand daylight hours that turn ability into mastery. Surplus is not a nice-to-have in this story; it is the wage fund of every non-farmer. And the ratio scales all the way up to the modern world: where one farmer's work feeds barely one family, nearly everyone must farm; where one farmer feeds fifty — as mechanised agriculture manages — forty-nine people are released to be teachers, engineers, nurses, coders, artists. Run the test on yourself: every specialist you have ever met, including the specialist you are studying to become, eats food grown by somebody else's surplus. Talent nominates the specialist; surplus pays the salary. That is exactly why the lesson called surplus the pivot of the whole story.

***

**Thandi:** Now the hard one, and the class wants it answered properly: those chartered companies — armed, holding monopolies, licensed by kings — are the ships that colonisation arrived on. Why is that sitting in our economics lesson like something to be admired?

**Tutor:** It sits there to be understood — and understanding, done honestly, is the opposite of admiration. Hold both truths at full strength. Truth one, the institutional truth: chartered companies solved genuine commercial problems no one had solved before — voyages too expensive for any single fortune were financed by pooling capital through shares; ruinous risk was spread across many holders; and state authority travelled with the ship, making contracts enforceable across oceans. The modern company you will study in business contexts descends directly from that machinery. Truth two, the human truth: that same machinery, armed and licensed, seized land, crushed competitors by force rather than price, and constructed the colonial economy — who owned, who laboured, who was moved and to where — whose patterns South Africa is still dismantling; the migrant labour system that fed our own mining industrialisation belongs to that same lineage. Refuse the temptation to pick one truth and discard the other: they are one story, and this term's own method — gains and costs, two columns, counted without flinching — was built for precisely this material. Economics that names the innovation while ignoring the violence is propaganda; economics that names the violence while ignoring the machinery cannot explain how the present was built. Name both. That is the whole discipline, practised with open eyes.

***

**Thandi:** Last question, and it's a clever one from the front row: if every stage solves the previous stage's problem — money fixed barter, the state fixed the market's gaps, machines fixed the limits of hands — what problem did the machines create, and what is solving THAT?

**Tutor:** That question turns the lesson's engine onto the present, which is exactly what the engine is for. The machines created at least four problems, and history's answers to each are still under construction. Problem one: the mill's brutal hours, its dangers and its children at the looms — answered by labour law and by workers organising, the entire system of rights and bargaining you studied earlier this term; the mill floor summoned it into being. Problem two: smoke, waste and poisoned rivers at industrial scale — answered, much later and still incompletely, by environmental regulation and the sustainability thinking threaded through your first term; that answer is being written in your lifetime. Problem three: machines displacing hands, trade by trade — answered, imperfectly, by education and retraining that move workers toward the work machines create rather than the work they destroy; today's automation debates are the newest lap of a race that began with the power loom. Problem four: industrial wealth concentrating into few hands — answered by competition law, by taxation, and by the regulated mixed economy you actually live in. Now step back and see the pattern the whole lesson has been teaching: every solution is imperfect and eventually breeds the next problem, which breeds the next solution — that is why economics never finishes, and why the subject cannot bore you unless you stop looking. The valley is still growing up. Your generation stands in the newest chapter — and gets a hand in writing the answers.
