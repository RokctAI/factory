## Subtopic: Perfect Markets: Equilibrium, Profit and the Shutdown Point

Perfect competition is a benchmark, not a photograph: many buyers and sellers, a homogeneous product, free entry and exit, perfect information, no control over price. The closest South African example is fresh produce sold on a large market floor. Because no single farmer can move the price, the individual business is a PRICE TAKER, and its demand curve, described verbally, is a horizontal line at the market price — demand equals price equals average revenue equals marginal revenue.

Equilibrium is where marginal cost equals marginal revenue while marginal cost is rising. Everything the examiner asks flows from comparing price with average cost at that output. If price sits above average cost, the business earns ECONOMIC PROFIT — the rectangle between price and average cost, across the equilibrium quantity. If price sits below average cost, the business runs an ECONOMIC LOSS. If price just touches the lowest point of the average cost curve, the business earns NORMAL PROFIT — no more than its resources could earn elsewhere, and in the long run free entry and exit drags every perfect competitor to exactly this point: newcomers chase profits and compete them away, leavers thin out losses until they close.

The SHUTDOWN decision is its own favourite question. In the short run a business keeps producing at a loss as long as price covers average VARIABLE cost, because every unit sold still contributes something toward fixed costs that must be paid anyway. The moment price falls below average variable cost, producing adds to the bleeding, and shutting down loses only the fixed costs. Verbally: the shutdown point is where the price line touches the minimum of the average variable cost curve.

Close the section with COMPETITION POLICY: the Competition Act of 1998, enforced by the Competition Commission which investigates, the Competition Tribunal which adjudicates, and the Competition Appeal Court. Its scoreboard includes the bread cartel case and construction collusion around the 2010 stadiums — real names the evaluation questions expect.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: characteristics, the price-taker's flat demand curve, the three equilibrium positions, shutdown, and the competition authorities.

## Subtopic: Imperfect Markets: Monopoly, Oligopoly and Monopolistic Competition

A MONOPOLY is a single seller of a product without close substitutes, behind high barriers to entry — natural monopolies like electricity transmission, or artificial ones built on licences and patents. Because the monopolist IS the industry, its demand curve slopes downward, and marginal revenue lies BELOW the demand curve: to sell one more unit, price must fall on all units. Equilibrium is still where marginal cost equals marginal revenue, but price is read UP on the demand curve above that output — so price exceeds marginal revenue, output is lower and price higher than perfect competition would deliver. A monopoly can earn economic profit even in the long run, because barriers keep rescuers out — but it is not guaranteed profit; a monopolist facing weak demand can run losses.

OLIGOPOLY: a few large interdependent sellers — South African banking, fuel retail, cellular networks. Interdependence is the defining word: each firm's best move depends on rivals' reactions. That is the logic of the KINKED DEMAND CURVE, told verbally: above the current price, demand is elastic because a lone price-riser loses customers to rivals who hold still; below it, demand is inelastic because a price-cutter is matched immediately and wins little. The kink at the current price makes prices sticky, so oligopolists compete through NON-PRICE competition — advertising, branding, loyalty programmes — or drift into COLLUSION: overt collusion in cartels, tacit collusion through price leadership. Both are illegal under the Competition Act.

MONOPOLISTIC COMPETITION blends the two worlds: many sellers, easy entry, but DIFFERENTIATED products — hair salons, restaurants, spaza shops with their own loyal corners. Differentiation gives each seller a gently sloping demand curve and short-run economic profit, but easy entry copies success until only normal profit remains in the long run.

For the compare-and-contrast essay, hold the four structures against the standard checklist: number of firms, nature of product, entry, price control, information, examples, demand curve shape, and long-run profit.

Stop for the questions on this section now — monopoly's two curves, the kinked demand story, collusion types, and the four structures compared.

## Subtopic: Market Failures, State Intervention and Cost-Benefit Analysis

Markets fail when prices misallocate resources, and the syllabus lists six causes to discuss in detail: externalities, missing markets, imperfect competition, lack of information, immobility of factors, and unequal distribution of income and wealth.

EXTERNALITIES are the centrepiece, and their graphs must be told in words. Negative externality — a factory polluting a river: the market weighs only private cost, so the supply curve based on private cost lies to the right of the true social-cost curve, and the market produces too much at too low a price; the efficient outcome sits at the crossing of social cost and demand, with less output and a higher price. Positive externality — education, vaccination: private demand understates social benefit, so the social-benefit curve lies to the right of private demand and the market produces too little. PUBLIC GOODS are the missing market: non-rivalrous and non-excludable, like street lighting — no one can be charged at the point of use, so private supply collapses under free riding and the state must provide.

STATE INTERVENTION follows, each tool with its verbal graph. A MAXIMUM price, set BELOW equilibrium to keep essentials affordable, creates a shortage — quantity demanded exceeds supply — and breeds queues and black markets. A MINIMUM price, set ABOVE equilibrium, creates a surplus; the minimum wage is the labour-market version, defended for dignity and attacked for pricing some workers out. Taxes on harm — the sugar levy, fuel levies — shift supply left to shrink overproduction; subsidies on good things shift supply right. Direct provision covers public goods, and redistribution runs through progressive tax and grants.

COST-BENEFIT ANALYSIS is the state's decision tool for big projects: count ALL costs and benefits, private and social, over the project's life; discount them; proceed when social benefits exceed social costs. It forces the unpriced consequences — pollution, commuting time, health — into the sum, which is exactly why the syllabus attaches it to market failure.

The questions for this section arrive now: causes of failure sorted, both externality graphs in words, price controls with their shortage and surplus, and the logic of cost-benefit analysis.

## Subtopic: Contemporary Issues: Inflation, Tourism and Environmental Sustainability

Three topics share the second half of Paper 2, and each carries a full essay possibility.

INFLATION: a sustained and significant increase in the general price level. Measured by the CPI's weighted basket and the PPI at the factory gate. The inflation rate is the percentage change in the index — new minus old, over the OLD index, times 100. Demand-pull inflation comes from spending outrunning capacity: credit booms, government expenditure, export surges. Cost-push comes from the supply side: wage settlements above productivity, a weaker rand raising import costs, fuel and electricity increases, droughts. Consequences run through creditors losing to debtors, fixed incomes eroding, savers punished, bracket creep, and expectations feeding the spiral. The response is inflation targeting: the Reserve Bank holds CPI inflation inside 3 to 6 percent using the repo rate — powerful against demand-pull, blunt against cost-push, because interest rates cannot cheapen oil.

TOURISM: the movement of people away from their usual homes for leisure, business or other purposes, measured through arrivals and spending. Its effects reach GDP directly, employment intensively — tourism is labour-intensive and skills-light at entry, so it absorbs job seekers fast — plus infrastructure investment and externalities both good and bad. Benefits flow to households through jobs and markets for crafts, to businesses through demand, to the state through taxes, and to infrastructure that residents also use. South Africa's profile leans on wildlife, heritage sites and indigenous knowledge systems — and policy suggestions cover marketing, spreading tourists beyond the famous sites, sensible taxing and infrastructure development.

ENVIRONMENTAL SUSTAINABILITY: development that meets present needs without robbing future generations. The state of the environment covers pollution, erosion, deforestation, climate change, conservation and preservation. Markets fail here because social costs go unpriced — so government grants property rights, charges for environmental use, levies green taxes like the plastic bag levy, pays subsidies, issues marketable permits, and commands directly. Internationally: the Rio summit, the Kyoto Protocol, the Paris Agreement on climate change, and the Sustainable Development Goals — with evaluation resting on the gap between signatures and delivery.

Pause for the final Expert questions now — the inflation formula and its two engines, tourism's effects and beneficiaries, and the market and state tools for sustainability.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same paper through one township high street — because every market structure, failure and fix in Paper 2 is already trading there.

## Subtopic: The Spaza and the Giant: Market Structures Made Plain

Walk the street. The vegetable stalls at the taxi rank are near-perfect competition: many sellers, the same tomatoes, and no stall can charge more than the stall beside it — the price is simply given. Each seller's demand, told as a picture, is a flat line: sell all you want at the going price, sell nothing above it. When tomato profits are good, more stalls appear by Friday, and the profits melt back to normal. That melting is the long run doing its work.

Now the giant: the electricity supplier is a monopoly — one seller, no substitute, and you cannot open a rival grid behind your house. The monopolist chooses its output like everyone else, where the cost of one more unit equals the revenue from one more unit, but then charges what the demand curve will bear above that point — less produced, more charged, and nobody can enter to fix it.

Between them sit the few and the many. The banks and cell networks are the FEW — an oligopoly, where nobody moves a price without watching the others, the way taxi associations on one route watch each other's fares. So they fight with adverts, brands and loyalty points instead of prices. The hair salons are the MANY with a difference — monopolistic competition, where every salon is slightly its own thing, keeps its regulars, earns nicely for a season, and then watches a new salon open two doors down.

Quick check — questions on the street's four markets are coming to you right now: who takes the price, who makes it, who watches rivals, and who differentiates.

## Subtopic: When the Market Drops the Ball

The market is a brilliant delivery system with a few famous blind spots, and you can see them all from the same street corner.

Blind spot one: costs dumped on others. The scrapyard burns waste and the smoke drifts over the school. The scrapyard's prices count its own costs — never the coughing next door. So burning is cheaper than it should be, and there is more of it than the town would ever choose. That is a negative externality: the true cost curve sits higher than the one the market uses.

Blind spot two: benefits that leak to everyone. Each learner the school educates makes the whole street richer — safer, more skilled, better customers — but families weigh only their own benefit, so left alone the town buys too little education. Positive externality: the market undershoots on the best things.

Blind spot three: things nobody can sell. The streetlight shines on payers and non-payers alike, so no shop will ever build one for profit. Public goods must come from the municipality or not at all.

Then the fixes, each with its catch. Cap the price of bread below what the market wants and the shelves empty by ten — a maximum price makes a shortage. Set a minimum wage above the going rate and workers who keep jobs live better while some never get hired — a floor makes a surplus, and in the labour market a surplus means unemployment. Tax the smoke, subsidise the school, build the light. And before the municipality builds anything big, it should count EVERYBODY'S costs and benefits, not just the ones with price tags — that is cost-benefit analysis in one sentence.

Your questions for this part are up now: the three blind spots, the shortage and the surplus, and which fix answers which failure.

## Subtopic: Prices, Tourists and the Planet in One Sweep

Three last stories, one street.

Inflation is the street's rising tide. When everyone is flush and spending — grant day, bonus month, cheap credit — the stalls mark up because they can: that is demand-pull, prices pulled from the till side. When the wholesaler's diesel, the rand and the electricity bill all jump, the stalls mark up to survive: cost-push, prices shoved from the back door. The cure must match the cause. The Reserve Bank's repo rate cools the flush spenders beautifully, but it cannot make diesel cheaper — remember that one sentence and the evaluation marks are yours. The target band, 3 to 6 percent, exists so the street can plan: rents, stock, school fees, a year ahead.

Tourism is the street's export that never leaves home. Every foreign visitor eating at the shisa nyama, buying beadwork, sleeping at the guest house, is South Africa selling to the world without shipping a single box. The money spreads wide — the cook, the crafter, the cleaner, the taxi — because tourism hires many hands quickly. That is why the state markets the country, spreads visitors beyond the famous parks, and builds the roads and water that tourists need and residents keep.

And sustainability is the street's own yard. Free dumping means a dirty river, because free things get overused — so the town makes the polluter pay: a levy on the plastic bag, a charge for the dump, a permit that can be traded, a fine for the worst. The world plays the same game at country scale — Kyoto, Paris, the Sustainable Development Goals — and the honest evaluation is the same too: the promises are easier than the delivery. And here come the final questions of the lesson, right now: matching the cure to the inflation cause, tracing the tourist's rand through the street, and pricing the planet so the future is not the one who pays. Take the street with you into Paper 2 — every graph in the paper is hanging on it somewhere.
