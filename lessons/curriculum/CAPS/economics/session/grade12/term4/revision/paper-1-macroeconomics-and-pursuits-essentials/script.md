# Part 1 — Expert

Paper 1 carries 150 marks across two main topics — Macroeconomics and Economic Pursuits — and the examiners rebuild it every year from the same skeleton: circular flow, business cycles, the public sector, foreign exchange, trade policy, growth strategies and the indicators that measure them. This revision session walks that skeleton end to end, flagging the definitions, calculations, curve stories and evaluation lines that keep returning. Everything here is drawn from the official examination guideline, so treat each stop as a promise about what the paper can ask.

## Subtopic: The Circular Flow, the Multiplier and Business Cycles

Start where the paper starts: the OPEN four-sector circular-flow model. Four participants — households, businesses, the state and the foreign sector — linked by two kinds of flows. REAL flows carry factors of production and goods; MONEY flows carry income and spending in the opposite direction. Households supply labour to the factor market and receive wages; businesses supply products to the product market and receive revenue; the state collects taxes and returns services and grants; the foreign sector connects through exports and imports. The financial sector sits in the middle, turning savings into investment, and the foreign exchange market converts rands whenever trade crosses the border.

The examinable equalities live here. Leakages equal injections in equilibrium: L equals S plus T plus M, and J equals I plus G plus X. The economy is in equilibrium when Y equals C plus I plus G plus X minus M. Learn the three methods of measuring GDP — production, income and expenditure — and the national account conversions: basic prices to market prices by adding taxes and subtracting subsidies on products, and GDP to GNP by adjusting for income earned by foreigners and by South Africans abroad.

Then the MULTIPLIER, the calculation Paper 1 loves. The multiplier equals one divided by one minus the marginal propensity to consume, which is the same as one divided by the marginal propensity to save. If the mpc is 0,6, the multiplier is one divided by 0,4 — that is 2,5 — so an injection of R10 billion of new investment eventually raises income by R25 billion. Described verbally, the graph shows aggregate spending rising with income, the forty-five degree line marking equilibrium, and a small upward shift of the spending line producing a much larger rightward move in equilibrium income.

Business cycles complete the section. A cycle runs through recovery, prosperity or boom, recession and depression, around a rising trend line. Monetarists blame EXOGENOUS shocks from outside the market system and trust markets to self-correct; Keynesians see ENDOGENOUS causes inside the economy — swings in confidence and spending — and call for government to intervene. Forecasting rests on leading, coincident and lagging indicators, the length and amplitude of the cycle, the trend line, extrapolation and moving averages.

Pause here — the first questions are with you now: flows and equalities, the multiplier calculation, GDP methods, and the two explanations of the cycle.

## Subtopic: The Public Sector, the Budget and Fiscal Policy

The public sector exists to supply public goods, conserve resources and manage the economy, and its five macro objectives are a list you must produce on demand: economic growth, full employment, price stability, exchange rate stability with balance of payments equilibrium, and economic equity. Set against those objectives stand the PROBLEMS of public provisioning — weak accountability, inefficiency, difficulty assessing needs, awkward pricing of free services, struggling parastatals and the privatisation debate.

The BUDGET is the annual money plan. Know the difference between the Medium Term Expenditure Framework, the rolling three-year spending plan, and the Medium Term Budget Policy Statement, the October mini-budget that updates Parliament. Track the exam's favourite figures: where revenue comes from — personal income tax, VAT and company tax carry most of it — where spending goes, and public debt expressed as a percentage of GDP. Personal income tax questions can hand you a tax table and ask for a calculation, so practise reading brackets, rebates and thresholds.

FISCAL POLICY works through those two levers, taxation and government spending. It is goal-bound, demand-based and cyclical. Its effects reach income distribution through progressive taxation, consumption through VAT and transfers, and incentives through marginal rates — which is where the LAFFER CURVE enters. Described verbally: tax revenue on the vertical axis, the tax rate on the horizontal. Revenue is zero at a rate of nought percent and zero again at a hundred percent, because nobody works when everything is taxed away. Revenue rises, peaks at some optimal rate, then falls — so beyond the peak, CUTTING the rate can raise revenue. The curve is the examiners' favourite way to ask whether higher rates always mean more money.

Close with PUBLIC SECTOR FAILURE: management failure, apathy, lack of motivation, bureaucracy, political interference, structural weaknesses and special interest groups — each one a reason the state's good intentions misfire, with effects on resource allocation, income distribution and social stability.

Stop for this section's questions now — objectives against problems, MTEF against MTBPS, the Laffer story told in words, and the reasons for public failure.

## Subtopic: Foreign Exchange Markets and the Balance of Payments

International trade happens for demand reasons — population size, income levels, tastes — and supply reasons — natural resources, climate, labour, technology and specialisation. South Africa sells minerals, fruit, wine and vehicles to the world, and buys oil, machinery and electronics back.

The BALANCE OF PAYMENTS records it all in three accounts. The CURRENT account: goods exports plus net gold exports plus services and income receipts, minus merchandise imports, payments for services and income payments. The CAPITAL TRANSFER account, small and quiet. The FINANCIAL account: direct investment, portfolio investment and reserve assets. A mining boom lands in the current account; a foreign company buying a South African bank lands in the financial account as direct investment; a foreigner buying JSE shares is portfolio investment. Corrections of a deficit are a standard eight-marker: raise interest rates to attract capital, control imports, borrow, promote exports, substitute imports, or let the exchange rate adjust.

Now the market itself. The DEMAND for dollars comes from importers, South Africans travelling abroad and capital flowing out; the SUPPLY of dollars comes from exporters, inbound tourists and foreign investment flowing in. Described verbally, the graph puts the rand price of one dollar on the vertical axis and the quantity of dollars on the horizontal; demand slopes down, supply slopes up, and the crossing point sets the exchange rate. If South Africans import more, the demand curve shifts right and the dollar costs more rands — the rand has DEPRECIATED. If exports surge, supply shifts right and the rand APPRECIATES. Reserve depreciation and appreciation for floating rates; devaluation and revaluation belong to fixed systems. South Africa runs a FREE FLOATING system with occasional Reserve Bank smoothing.

Finish with TERMS OF TRADE: the index of export prices divided by the index of import prices, times 100. Rising terms of trade mean each ton of exports buys more imports — an improvement even if volumes never move.

The questions for this section arrive now: the three accounts sorted, deficit corrections, the verbal graph of the rand, and the terms of trade formula.

## Subtopic: Economic Pursuits: Trade Policy, Growth and Indicators

Economic Pursuits supplies half of Paper 1, and it opens with the trade-policy debate. EXPORT PROMOTION pushes local producers into world markets with incentives, subsidies and marketing support; its danger is subsidising firms that never learn to compete. IMPORT SUBSTITUTION protects home industries behind tariffs and quotas so they can replace imports; its danger is sheltered, inefficient producers and higher prices at home. The arguments FOR protection: infant industries, employment, self-sufficiency, prevention of dumping. The arguments FOR free trade: specialisation, economies of scale, wider choice, innovation and better international relations. The syllabus answer is a DESIRABLE MIX, tilted toward freer trade through the World Trade Organisation, with South Africa plugged into SACU, SADC, AGOA and BRICS.

Growth and development next — and define both, because the distinction earns marks. GROWTH is the increase in real GDP; DEVELOPMENT is the improvement in living standards, capabilities and choices. The demand-side approach stimulates spending through monetary and fiscal policy; the supply-side approach cuts the cost of doing business, improves market efficiency and builds skills. Attach the policy alphabet in order: the RDP rebuilt basic services after 1994, GEAR chased stability and growth, AsgiSA and JIPSA targeted bottlenecks and skills, the New Growth Path chased jobs, and the National Development Plan aims at 2030. Regional policy adds Spatial Development Initiatives, corridors like the Maputo corridor, Industrial Development Zones at Coega and East London, and their successors the Special Economic Zones. The North-South divide frames it globally: rich industrialised North, developing South, unequal living standards and unequal power in trade.

Last, the INDICATORS that measure performance. Economic indicators: inflation by CPI and PPI, the exchange rate and terms of trade for foreign trade, employment and unemployment rates against the economically active population, labour productivity, the repo rate, and money supply from M1 to M3. Social indicators: life expectancy, child mortality, access to clean water and sanitation, education enrolment, electricity and housing. Comparisons across countries run through the IMF, the World Bank and the UN's standardisation.

Pause for the final Expert questions now — trade policy arguments both ways, growth against development, the policy timeline, and sorting economic from social indicators.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the whole of Paper 1 again, but told the way you would explain it at the taxi rank — because every model in this paper is really a story about money moving through a town.

## Subtopic: Round and Round: The Economy as One Big Taxi Route

Picture the economy as a taxi route through one town. Households climb in with labour to sell; businesses pay them wages; the wages ride back to the shops as spending; the shops pay wages again. Money circles like the taxi — that is the circular flow. Some passengers get off the route: money saved, money taxed, money spent on imported goods. Those are the LEAKAGES. Some new passengers climb on: investment, government spending, export earnings. Those are the INJECTIONS. The route runs steady when the people getting off equal the people climbing on — leakages equal injections, and the town is in equilibrium.

The MULTIPLIER is just the story of one new passenger. A firm invests R10 million in a new plant. The builders earn it and spend most of it at the supermarket; the supermarket staff earn that and spend most of it again. If people spend sixty cents of every rand, the arithmetic says the town ends up richer by two and a half times the original R10 million. One divided by the part people do NOT spend — one over 0,4 — gives 2,5. That is the whole formula.

And the BUSINESS CYCLE is the route across a year: busy season, peak, quiet season, trough — around a slowly rising trend, because the town itself keeps growing. One camp says the potholes come from outside shocks and the road fixes itself; the other says the swings start with the passengers' own moods and the town council must step in. Those are your monetarists and Keynesians in taxi language.

Quick check — questions on this part are coming to you right now: leakages against injections, the multiplier as repeated spending, and the phases of the cycle.

## Subtopic: The Nation's Purse and the Price of the Rand

The government is the biggest household in the country, and the budget is its purse. Money in: mostly personal income tax, VAT on nearly everything you buy, and company tax. Money out: education, social grants, health, policing — and interest on debt, the item that grows whenever the purse borrows. The three-year spending plan is the family's long view; the October mini-budget is the mid-year honesty check. And the Laffer curve is a lemonade-stand truth: price the lemonade at nothing and you earn nothing; price it so high nobody buys and you also earn nothing; somewhere between sits the best price. Tax rates work the same way — squeeze too hard and the takings shrink.

Now the rand. The exchange rate is nothing more than the PRICE of one currency in another, set by buyers and sellers like tomatoes at a market stall. Who needs dollars? Importers, travellers, investors leaving. Who brings dollars? Exporters, tourists, investors arriving. Lots of dollar-buyers and few sellers — the dollar gets expensive, and the rand has weakened. A weak rand is not simply bad news: exports become bargains for the world and tourism booms, but petrol and imported medicine cost more. That two-sided answer is exactly what evaluation questions want.

The balance of payments is the country's bank statement with the world: the current account is everyday buying and selling, the financial account is the savings and investment page. When the statement runs short, the fixes are household fixes — earn more by exporting, spend less on imports, borrow carefully, or let prices adjust through the exchange rate.

Your questions for this part are up now: the purse, the lemonade price, who buys and sells the rand, and reading the country's bank statement.

## Subtopic: Growing the Pie: Trade, Plans and Report Cards

Should the town buy its bread from the bakery next door or from the cheaper bakery two towns over? Protect the local bakery and you keep jobs close by but pay more for worse bread. Open the road and bread gets cheaper and better, but the local baker must compete or close. That is protectionism against free trade in one loaf — and the syllabus answer is balance: shelter the genuinely young businesses for a while, open the road for the rest, and sign sensible agreements with the neighbours, which is what SACU, SADC and BRICS are.

Growth against development is the difference between a bigger pie and more people actually eating. GDP can climb while the same families stay hungry — that is growth without development. The government's plans, from the RDP's houses and taps through GEAR's discipline to the National Development Plan's 2030 targets, are all attempts to bake the pie bigger AND slice it wider. The zones and corridors — Coega, the Maputo corridor, the Special Economic Zones — are attempts to move the ovens to where the unemployed already live.

And the indicators are simply the country's report card. Inflation is the price column, unemployment the jobs column, life expectancy and clean water the wellbeing columns. One warning the examiners reward: never read one column alone. A country can score well on growth and fail on sanitation; the report card only makes sense whole. Read the numbers the way you would read a learner's report — looking for the pattern, not the single mark. And here come the final questions of the lesson, right now: the bakery debate, growth against development with the plans in order, and the report card read as a whole. Walk into Paper 1 knowing the skeleton never changes — only the numbers do.
