**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! The first one is the graph panic question: Paper 2 is full of cost and revenue curves, and some of us freeze the moment a question says draw. Is there a way to build every market graph from the same starting point?

**Tutor:** There is — every equilibrium graph in this paper is assembled from the same four pieces, in the same order. Piece one: axes — price, cost and revenue on the vertical, quantity on the horizontal. Piece two: the demand side, and here the structures differ in ONE way only. Perfect competition gives the individual business a horizontal line, because it takes the price; every imperfect structure gives a downward slope, with the monopolist adding a marginal revenue curve below it. Piece three: the cost side, identical everywhere — the U-shaped average cost curve, and marginal cost cutting it at its lowest point from below. Piece four: equilibrium — find where marginal cost equals marginal revenue, drop to the quantity, rise to the price. Then every question reduces to one comparison: price against average cost at that quantity. Above it, economic profit; below it, economic loss; touching, normal profit. Practise assembling those four pieces in that order five times and the panic goes, because you are no longer memorising eight different pictures — you are running one procedure with one switch: is the demand line flat or sloping?

**Mandy:** Next, the one the group argued about at lunch: if monopolies are so bad, why does the state RUN some of them, like electricity transmission? It feels like a contradiction we could be asked about.

**Tutor:** It is exactly the contradiction the examiners love, and the resolving concept is the NATURAL monopoly. For most goods, competition disciplines price and quality, so we outlaw monopolising conduct through the Competition Act. But some industries have enormous fixed costs and falling average costs over the whole market — building a second national grid or a parallel water network would double the infrastructure to serve the same houses. There, one producer is genuinely the cheapest arrangement, so competition would waste resources. The policy question then changes from how do we break it up to how do we stop it exploiting its position — and the answers are regulation of tariffs, public ownership, or both. That is why South Africa keeps transmission in state hands while prosecuting bread and construction cartels: the first is a natural monopoly managed in the public interest, at least in intention; the second is artificial collusion that steals from consumers. In an evaluation answer, complete the thought with the honest caveat: public ownership solves the exploitation problem only if governance holds, and South Africa's load-shedding years show a state monopoly can fail consumers in its own way — through inefficiency rather than price gouging.

**Mandy:** Third one: in the practice test, a data response gave a monopoly graph and asked for the loss. Several of us found the right rectangle but still lost marks. What is the marker looking for?

**Tutor:** The marker follows a checklist, and each tick is a mark, so show every step even when the answer looks obvious. Step one: state the equilibrium rule — the business produces where marginal cost equals marginal revenue. Step two: read the equilibrium QUANTITY off that intersection. Step three: at that quantity, read the PRICE from the demand curve, and the average COST from the average cost curve — and say which is which. Step four: the loss per unit is cost minus price; the total loss is that difference times the quantity, and the words show all calculations mean exactly what they say — write the subtraction and the multiplication as number sentences, with rand signs and units. Step five: name the rectangle in words: the loss is the area between the average cost and the price, across the equilibrium quantity. Learners who lose marks usually skip steps one and four — they point at the right area but never anchor it in the MC-equals-MR rule and never write the arithmetic line. Remember the scaffolding: the first small questions in a data response are read-off marks, the middle ones are two-mark explanations, and the final calculation carries four — structured exactly for this checklist.

**Mandy:** Last one: with inflation, tourism AND environment all examinable, how do we prepare essays without writing out the whole textbook?

**Tutor:** Prepare skeletons, not essays — five headings and a judgement each, and trust the session's stories to flesh them out. For inflation: definition and measurement; demand-pull causes; cost-push causes; consequences by group — creditors, savers, fixed incomes; measures, ending with the one-liner that the repo rate fights demand-pull well and cost-push badly. For tourism: definition and measurement; effects on GDP and employment; benefits to households, businesses and the state; South Africa's profile with indigenous knowledge and heritage sites; policy suggestions across marketing, spatial spread, taxing and infrastructure. For environment: the state of the environment; why markets fail here; government tools from charges to permits; international agreements from Rio through Kyoto to Paris and the Sustainable Development Goals; then the evaluation that signatures have outrun delivery. Each skeleton fits on an index card. Rehearse expanding one heading into a paragraph — two marks per developed fact is the marking rhythm — and remember the essay's own arithmetic: an introduction worth up to two, a main body of twenty-six, an additional insight section of ten, and a conclusion of two. Three cards, one rhythm, forty marks.
