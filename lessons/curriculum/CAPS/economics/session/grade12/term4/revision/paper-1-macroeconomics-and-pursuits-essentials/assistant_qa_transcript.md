**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First one, and half the room asked it: there is so much in Paper 1 — circular flow, budgets, exchange rates, all the growth plans. How do we know what to revise hardest?

**Tutor:** Revise to the paper's architecture, because the architecture never changes. Question 1 is thirty compulsory objective marks split evenly across Macroeconomics and Economic Pursuits — that is where definitions and one-line concepts pay, so your glossary work covers it. Section B gives three forty-mark questions and you answer two: one macro, one pursuits, one combined — every one built from short items, two data-response questions and two eight-mark paragraphs. Data response means graphs and tables, so the multiplier calculation, the exchange rate diagram, budget figures and the balance of payments accounts are the highest-probability material. Section C is the essay, and the guideline tells you the essay pool in advance: the topics flagged for detailed discussion — smoothing of cycles through the new economic paradigm, public sector objectives and failure, reasons for international trade, protectionism and export promotion, the growth approaches and policies. So the strategy writes itself: definitions for Section A, calculations and diagrams for the data responses, and four or five prepared essay skeletons for Section C. Nothing in the paper falls outside that map.

**Mandy:** Next: the multiplier keeps tripping people. Someone got a multiplier of 0,4 in the practice test and just carried on. How should we sanity-check ourselves?

**Tutor:** With one rule: the multiplier is always greater than one in these problems, because the marginal propensity to consume sits between nought and one. The formula is one divided by one minus the mpc. If the mpc is 0,6, then one minus 0,6 is 0,4, and one divided by 0,4 is 2,5. The learner who got 0,4 stopped one step early — they calculated the marginal propensity to save and never did the final division. So run three checks every time. First, does my answer exceed one? If not, I have the mps, not the multiplier. Second, does mpc plus mps equal exactly one? If not, my subtraction slipped. Third, multiply back: 2,5 times the R10 billion injection gives R25 billion of total income — if the question asks for the change in income, that final multiplication is where the last marks live. And remember the interpretation sentence that turns arithmetic into full marks: the multiplier is larger when households spend a bigger share of each extra rand, because each round of the circular flow passes on more spending before it leaks away into saving, tax and imports.

**Mandy:** This one comes from the group at the back: when a question says the rand depreciated, some of us write that this is bad for South Africa and lose marks. Why?

**Tutor:** Because depreciation is not a verdict, it is a price change — and price changes always cut two ways. When the rand weakens, South African goods become cheaper in dollars, so exporters of fruit, wine, vehicles and minerals sell more, and a foreign tourist's money stretches further here, so tourism gains. At the same time everything imported — oil, machinery, medicine, electronics — costs more in rands, which feeds into petrol prices and then into transport costs across the whole economy, pushing cost-push inflation. So a one-sided answer is a half answer. The examiners' command words tell you this: evaluate, discuss, how and why questions on the exchange rate expect both directions plus a conclusion that depends on context — who you are matters, because an exporter smiles at the same movement that hurts a commuter. Structure it in three sentences: winners with a reason, losers with a reason, then a judgement such as the net effect depends on how import-dependent production is and whether exporters use the advantage. That third sentence is the higher-order mark.

**Mandy:** Last one: the alphabet soup — RDP, GEAR, AsgiSA, JIPSA, NGP, NDP. Is there a trick to keeping the growth policies straight?

**Tutor:** Turn the alphabet into a timeline with one job per plan, because the examiner asks what each policy focused on, not its full text. Start in 1994: the RDP, rebuilding basics — houses, water, electricity, clinics. Then GEAR in 1996, the discipline plan — stabilise debt and chase growth through a stricter budget. Then the skills pair: AsgiSA accelerating shared growth by attacking bottlenecks, with JIPSA as its skills arm targeting priority skills like engineering and artisans. Then the New Growth Path in 2010, whose one word is jobs, through infrastructure and green economy drivers. Finally the National Development Plan, the long-horizon map to 2030 — eliminate poverty, reduce inequality. Say each plan's decade and its single job and you can answer matching items, short questions and even build the essay's body. One refinement earns the evaluation marks: note that the plans shifted from delivery, to stability, to skills, to jobs, to a whole-society vision — and that implementation, not design, is where South Africa's growth policies have struggled. That last sentence, offered as your own judgement, is exactly what the additional part of an essay rewards.
