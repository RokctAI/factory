# Part 1 — Expert

This session is a full practice run of Economics Paper 1 — Macroeconomics and Economic Pursuits, 150 marks. The questions are authored to the official examination guideline, in the paper's own shapes: the compulsory objective question, the forty-mark data questions, and the forty-mark essay. Work each question before the answer arrives; the answers come with the marking logic attached, because knowing how the marks land is half the training.

## Subtopic: Section A: Objective Items, Answered and Explained

Question 1 is compulsory and worth 30 marks: eight multiple-choice items at two marks, eight matching items at one, six give-the-term items at one.

Try three multiple-choice items now. Item one: the withdrawal of money from the circular flow through savings, taxes and imports is known as... options: injections, leakages, real flows, subsidies. The answer is leakages — two marks for circling one word, which is why Section A rewards glossary discipline. Item two: the phase of the business cycle where economic activity is at its highest point before turning down... options: trough, recovery, peak, extrapolation. The answer is peak. Item three: the South African exchange rate system is best described as... managed fixed, free floating, pegged to gold, dual. Free floating — with the Reserve Bank smoothing extremes but not defending a level.

Matching items pair a description with a concept. The rolling three-year expenditure plan of government — the Medium Term Expenditure Framework. The account of the balance of payments recording direct and portfolio investment — the financial account. The economists who see business cycles as caused by forces inside the economy — the Keynesians.

Give-the-term is the sharpest test, because no options appear and abbreviations earn nothing. The compensation paid for the use of capital as a factor of production — interest. A deliberate lowering of a currency's value under a fixed exchange rate system — devaluation. The additional income generated by an initial injection into the economy — the multiplier effect. Write the full term, spelled out; the marking guideline refuses acronyms and examples here.

Pause here — the first practice questions are with you now, all in Section A's three shapes.

## Subtopic: Short Items: Banking the First Marks of Every Question

Every Section B question opens with short items worth four marks: two name-type marks and one two-mark application item. Openers, not obstacles — train yourself to bank them in seconds.

Practise the naming pairs. Name TWO leakages from the circular flow — savings, taxes; imports would also count. Name TWO instruments of monetary policy — the repo rate, open market transactions; cash reserves and moral suasion also score. Name TWO South African trade protocols — SACU and SADC, with BRICS, AGOA and the African Union all acceptable. One mark each, and the marking guideline accepts any correct pair from the approved list — never leave these blank.

The two-mark application item asks what, why or how, and wants one solid line of reasoning. Example: what effect will a depreciation of the rand have on South African exports? Exports become cheaper in foreign currency, so export volumes increase — two marks for the direction plus the reason. Another: why is the informal sector difficult to include in GDP figures? Because its transactions are not recorded through registered channels, so production goes unmeasured. One sentence, cause and consequence, done.

The discipline across all short items is proportion: a two-mark answer is one or two lines, never a paragraph. The mark allocation is the instruction — spend words where marks are.

Stop for the practice questions on this section now: naming pairs and two-mark applications, at pace.

## Subtopic: Data Response: Reading a Business Cycle Diagram

Data response questions carry ten marks each, scaffolded from easy to difficult, and Paper 1's favourite stimulus is the business cycle. Picture the diagram this question supplies, described in words: a wave-shaped line rising and falling around a straight upward-sloping trend line, with point A at the bottom of a dip, point B climbing, point C at the crest, and the vertical distance between crest and trend labelled X.

First questions, one mark each. Identify the phase at point A — the trough. Identify the phase at point B — recovery, sometimes called the expansion. These are read-off marks; the answers sit in the diagram.

Middle questions, two marks. Briefly describe the term trend line — the general direction in which the economy moves over the long term, drawn through the cycles as the average path of growth. Why is point C a dangerous moment for inflation? Because at the peak, spending presses against full capacity, so excess demand pulls prices upward — the Phillips-curve moment where inflation is highest and unemployment lowest.

Difficult questions, four marks. Explain what distance X measures and why forecasters care. X is the amplitude — the intensity of the cycle, how far activity swings from the trend. A large amplitude signals violent booms and busts, so policymakers lean harder on stabilisation; a small amplitude signals a gentler cycle. Two developed facts, two marks each — that is the four-mark rhythm across every data response in the paper.

The practice questions for this section arrive now, built on the same diagram described in words.

## Subtopic: Data Response: A Balance of Payments Table

The second data response supplies a table from the current account, in millions of rands: goods exports 1 200; net gold exports 100; services receipts 200; income receipts 150; merchandise imports 1 400; payments for services 250; income payments 200; current transfers minus 50.

One-mark starters. Which item shows South Africa's earnings from tourists visiting the country? Services receipts. Which single item is largest on the debit side? Merchandise imports.

Two-mark description. Briefly describe the term trade balance — the value of goods exports plus net gold exports minus merchandise imports, the goods-only snapshot inside the current account. Here: 1 200 plus 100 minus 1 400 gives minus 100 million — a trade deficit.

Now the four-mark calculation, and write it as number sentences. The balance on the current account equals all receipts minus all payments plus net transfers: 1 200 plus 100 plus 200 plus 150, giving 1 650; minus 1 400, minus 250, minus 200, giving minus 200; plus transfers of minus 50. The balance is minus 250 million — a current account deficit. Marks land on the method line, the additions, the subtraction and the labelled answer with units.

The follow-up is the higher-order tail: how could the authorities correct this deficit? Choose two measures and develop them: raise interest rates to attract foreign capital into the financial account, promote exports through incentives and trade missions, allow the exchange rate to depreciate so imports cost more and exports earn more, or apply import measures within trade-agreement limits. Two developed measures, four marks.

Pause for this section's practice questions now — the table stays in front of you.

## Subtopic: The Eight-Mark Paragraphs: Middle and Higher Order

Each Section B question closes with two eight-mark paragraph questions: one middle-order, one higher-order. Eight marks means four developed facts at two marks each — or a graph described plus its explanation.

Middle-order practice: with the aid of a verbal graph description, explain how the Laffer curve illustrates the effect of tax rates on tax revenue. Model answer, spoken as you would write it: the Laffer curve plots tax revenue against the tax rate. At a rate of nought percent revenue is zero, because nothing is collected. At one hundred percent revenue is again zero, because working and investing lose all reward. Between the extremes revenue rises to a maximum at the optimal rate, then falls as higher rates destroy incentives. Therefore a government taxing beyond the optimal point can raise MORE revenue by CUTTING rates. Four linked statements, each worth two — and the graph description itself can substitute for one of them.

Higher-order practice: why does expansionary fiscal policy not always create the growth it promises? Build an argument, not a list. Government spending financed by borrowing can crowd out private investment as public borrowing pushes interest rates up. If the economy is already near capacity, extra demand spills into inflation and imports rather than output. Leakages shrink the multiplier — a high import propensity sends stimulus abroad. And where public sector failure rules — poor management, corruption, bureaucracy — the rand spent yields little real service. Any four developed points earn the eight; the marker rewards reasoning in context, not textbook headings recited.

The practice questions on both paragraph shapes are with you now.

## Subtopic: The Essay, Built Live: Smoothing of Business Cycles

Section C offers two forty-mark essays; you choose one. The mark allocation is fixed and must drive your plan: introduction, maximum 2; the main body, maximum 26; the additional part, maximum 10; conclusion, maximum 2. Build this one live: discuss in detail how monetary and fiscal policy can be used to smooth out business cycles, and evaluate the effectiveness of these demand-side policies.

Introduction, two marks — one clean definition, nothing borrowed from the question: business cycles are the successive periods of expansion and contraction in economic activity around a long-term trend. Do not announce what you will discuss; define and move.

Main body, twenty-six — organise by policy, and link every tool to smoothing, because facts without the link earn one mark instead of two. Monetary policy in the downswing: the Reserve Bank lowers the repo rate, cheapening credit, lifting consumption and investment; open market purchases inject liquidity; moral suasion encourages lending. In the upswing, reverse every lever to cool excess demand. Fiscal policy in the downswing: government raises its spending and cuts taxes, injecting demand into the circular flow through the multiplier; in the upswing it trims spending and lets progressive taxation bite. Add the stabiliser point: progressive taxes and grants adjust automatically with income, smoothing without new decisions. Described verbally for the graph marks: aggregate demand shifts right in stimulus, left in restraint, moving equilibrium output along aggregate supply.

Additional part, ten marks — the evaluation the question ordered: policies act with lags, so stimulus can arrive at the wrong phase; the Phillips curve warns that demand management trades inflation against unemployment; repo changes cannot fix supply shocks like droughts or power cuts; and fiscal space is not free — debt service crowds tomorrow's budgets. Judged whole: demand-side policy smooths moderately well, and works best paired with supply-side reform.

Conclusion, two marks: a fresh closing judgement — smoothing succeeds when the policy mix matches the phase and the cause of the cycle — not a repeat of the body.

Practice questions on essay architecture are with you now.

## Subtopic: The Second Essay Option and Final Marking Wisdom

The second Section C option comes from Economic Pursuits. Practise planning it in skeleton form: discuss in detail South Africa's initiatives in regional development, and evaluate their success. Introduction: regional development aims to spread economic activity beyond established cores to where people live. Body headings, each expanded with two-mark facts: Spatial Development Initiatives linking infrastructure to private investment; corridors, with the Maputo corridor the flagship; Industrial Development Zones at Coega, East London and Richards Bay, tied to ports; and Special Economic Zones extending the model with tax incentives and one-stop support. Additional part: the honest scorecard — jobs and exports created, but concentrated gains, slow uptake in some zones, and infrastructure gaps blunting the incentives. Conclusion: zones work where governance, infrastructure and location align.

Now the marking wisdom that turns near-misses into full marks across the whole paper. One: obey command verbs — name means a word, describe means a sentence or two, discuss in detail means developed paragraphs, evaluate demands your judgement. Two: the mark allocation is the recipe — two marks per developed fact almost everywhere, so count facts before writing. Three: in calculations, the formula line and the units are marks; write them even when the arithmetic feels easy. Four: in essays, never repeat the introduction in the body, and never spend the conclusion summarising — both are separately marked responses. Five: choose questions by your strongest DATA topics, because forty of the eighty Section B marks sit in data response and paragraphs, not in the short items.
