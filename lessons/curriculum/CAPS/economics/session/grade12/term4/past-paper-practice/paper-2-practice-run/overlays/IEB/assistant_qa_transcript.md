**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First: the graphs. Some of us can draw the monopoly diagram from memory but still lose marks when a question asks us to EXPLAIN it. What are we missing?

**Tutor:** You are missing the words, because the marks live in the words. A drawn graph with no story earns the drawing marks only; the explanation marks pay for the procedure told in sentences. Practise narrating every graph in this fixed order: axes first — price and cost against quantity; curves second — demand sloping down, marginal revenue below it, average cost in its U, marginal cost cutting the U at its lowest point; equilibrium third — output where marginal cost crosses marginal revenue; and the reading last — price straight up on the demand curve, average cost at the same quantity, and the gap between them named as profit or loss. Four sentences, in order, every time. The session called graphs procedures rather than artwork, and this is what that means: a marker can credit a procedure because each step is a checkable fact, but cannot mark a beautiful picture that arrives unexplained. If you can say your graph with the lights off, you can also repair it under pressure — because the words rebuild the picture, never the other way round.

**Mandy:** Next: the inflation calculation. Half of us divided by the new index and got six comma five instead of seven percent. Why is it always the old one?

**Tutor:** Because a rate of change measures growth from where you started, not from where you ended. Think of it away from Economics: if your marks improve from sixty to seventy-five, you judge the improvement against the sixty you started with — fifteen over sixty, a quarter better. Divide by the seventy-five you finished with and you shrink your own achievement. Prices work identically: the index climbed nine comma one points from a starting level of one hundred and thirty, so the journey is nine comma one over one thirty — seven percent. Write the formula line before substituting a single number: new minus old, over old, times one hundred. That line is worth a mark on its own, and it is also your protection — with the formula visible, a slip in the arithmetic still leaves method marks standing. And check the answer against sense: prices clearly rose by roughly seven parts in a hundred and thirty, so an answer near seven percent feels right, while thirteen or two should trigger alarm. Calculations reward the careful, not the quick.

**Mandy:** Third: in the levy question, someone wrote five solid facts against the sugar tax and still lost marks. How does a full paragraph fall short?

**Tutor:** Because a judgement question pays for balance and a verdict, not for volume on one side. The instruction was evaluate — and evaluation is a scale, not a hammer. Five facts against the levy is a strong case for the defence with the prosecution missing: where is the health harm priced into the drink, the revenue for clinics, the reformulation the levy actually produced? A marker reading one-sided work can award the developed facts, but the marks reserved for weighing — for holding both sides and landing a reasoned verdict — stay uncollected. Build every evaluation on three moves: the case for, the case against, and the weighing sentence that answers the actual question, such as: the levy works where the harm is clear and alternatives exist, and its fairness improves when the revenue visibly funds health. That last sentence is the highest-value line in the paragraph, because it is the only one that could not be copied from a textbook. It is your thinking — and the judgement marks exist precisely to buy it.

**Mandy:** Last one: with so many market structures, learners mix up which curve belongs to whom. Any memory system that survives exam nerves?

**Tutor:** Anchor each structure to one local example and let the example carry the curves. Fresh produce on a market floor: nobody can charge above the going price, so the individual seller's demand line is FLAT — perfect competition, price taker, normal profit in the long run because new stalls keep arriving. The electricity grid: one seller, no substitute, so demand slopes DOWN and marginal revenue hangs below it — monopoly, price maker, profit that can persist behind barriers. The big banks: a few giants watching each other, so the demand curve KINKS at the current price and prices go sticky — oligopoly, where the fighting happens in adverts and loyalty points. Hair salons: many sellers, each slightly its own thing, so demand slopes gently down but entry is easy — monopolistic competition, where good seasons attract copies. Four examples, four curve shapes, and under nerves you do not recall a diagram — you recall the market floor, the grid, the banks and the salons, and the diagrams walk in behind them. Test yourself backwards tonight: name the structure from the curve, then the curve from the structure. When both directions run smoothly, the confusion is gone for good.
