**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First, the one everyone whispers: the exam says draw a correctly labelled graph. How much of the mark is the drawing and how much is the labels?

**Tutor:** The labels ARE the marks — the drawing only carries them. When a graph question is marked, the guideline awards ticks for named components: the axes labelled price, cost and revenue against quantity; each curve named — demand or average revenue, marginal revenue, average cost, marginal cost; the equilibrium point identified where marginal cost equals marginal revenue; and the price and quantity read off with dotted guide lines to the axes. A beautiful freehand curve with no labels earns almost nothing; a shaky curve with every component named earns almost everything. That is why this session described every graph in words — if you can SAY the picture, component by component, you can label it, and the marks follow. Train with a checklist of five: axes, curves, equilibrium, price and quantity lines, and the shaded area if profit or loss is asked. And when the question says use the given graph instead of draw, the same checklist becomes your reading order — you extract the labelled parts in sequence instead of supplying them. Either direction, the discipline is identical: graphs in Economics are labelled sentences, not artwork.

**Mandy:** Next: in the monopoly calculation, the difference between economic profit and normal profit cost people marks again. Can you settle it once and for all?

**Tutor:** With one sentence and one comparison. The sentence: NORMAL profit is the minimum earning needed to keep the entrepreneur in the business, and it is already counted INSIDE average cost as the reward for entrepreneurship. The comparison: economic profit exists only when price stands ABOVE average cost. So when the graph shows price R150 and average cost R125, the R25 gap times output is ECONOMIC profit — profit beyond what keeps the owner interested. When price equals average cost exactly, the business earns normal profit — not zero money, but zero EXTRA: the owner is paid what their effort and capital could earn elsewhere, no more. When price falls below average cost, the business earns less than its resources deserve — economic loss — even if some cash still flows. The exam tests this most sharply in the long-run stories: perfect competitors end at normal profit because free entry shaves every economic profit away; monopolists can hold economic profit because barriers stop the shaving. If you remember that normal profit lives inside the cost curves, every one of those statements follows without memorising.

**Mandy:** Third: the paragraph questions say four times two or two times four. What does that marking code actually mean for how we write?

**Tutor:** It is the recipe for the paragraph's shape. Four times two means the marker expects FOUR separate facts, each developed enough to earn TWO marks — so you write four short, complete statements, each with its reason or link. Two times four means TWO bigger ideas, each built to FOUR marks — a claim, its mechanism, and its consequence or example, twice over. The failure pattern is mismatching: giving eight bare list-items to a four-times-two question earns four marks at best, because bare facts cap at one mark each; giving one long paragraph to a two-times-four question risks the marker finding only one idea. So before writing, read the bracket and set your skeleton: four bullets or two blocks. Then apply the development rule from the marking guideline: a fact in context, fully explained, earns two; a fact merely named earns one; an example alone earns one, but an example explained earns two. In higher-order paragraphs, at most a quarter of the marks can come from bare listing — the rest must be reasoning. The bracket is not decoration; it is the examiner telling you the paragraph's architecture in advance. Read it, build to it, and the eight marks assemble themselves.

**Mandy:** Last one: with two essays to choose from — markets or inflation — how do we pick under pressure, when both look doable?

**Tutor:** Pick with the graph test and the heading count, in that order. First, the graph test: Section C essays in Microeconomics almost always carry a described-graph demand — long-run equilibrium, the kinked curve, externality diagrams. If the markets essay asks for a graph you can narrate component by component without hesitation, that is strong evidence for it; if the graph is the part you fear, the contemporary essay just gained ground, because inflation essays lean on structured discussion more than curve work. Second, the heading count: silently name the body headings for each option. The markets comparison needs the checklist — firms, product, entry, price control, information, demand curve, long-run profit, efficiency, examples. The inflation essay needs the policy families — monetary, fiscal, other — plus the evaluation of targeting. Whichever list you can recite further, faster, is your essay; a body you can heading-map will fill its twenty-six marks, while a body you must improvise will stall at fifteen. And remember the additional part when choosing: ask which topic you hold a genuine OPINION about, with reasons — because ten of the forty marks want exactly that, and borrowed opinions read as padding. Graph you can narrate, headings you can recite, judgement you actually hold: three tests, and the choice makes itself.
