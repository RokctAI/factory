# Part 1 — Expert

This session is a full practice run of Economics Paper 2 — Microeconomics and Contemporary Economic Issues, 150 marks. The question shapes are identical to Paper 1: a compulsory thirty-mark objective question, three forty-mark questions of which you answer two, and two essays of which you write one. What changes is the terrain: cost and revenue curves, market structures, market failure, inflation, tourism and the environment. Every graph in this run is described in words, exactly as you must be able to describe it yourself, and every answer arrives with its marking logic showing.

## Subtopic: Section A: Objective Items Across Micro and Contemporary Issues

Warm up with multiple-choice items in the paper's own voice. Item one: oligopolies that collude in a formal, written manner are called... options: price leaders, cartels, monopolists, price takers. Cartels — the classic guideline example, two marks. Item two: the market structure with many sellers offering differentiated products and easy entry is... perfect competition, monopoly, monopolistic competition, oligopoly. Monopolistic competition. Item three: a sustained increase in the general price level is... deflation, stagflation, inflation, disinflation. Inflation — and note how the objective items recycle exact definition wording, which is why precise glossaries outscore vague understanding here.

Matching items next. A market with one seller of a product without close substitutes — monopoly. Prices that rise while output falls and unemployment climbs — stagflation. The system of processing waste material into new products — recycling; the guideline itself uses that one.

Give-the-term, where no options rescue you. The point where price equals the minimum of average variable cost, below which a business stops producing — the shutdown point. The index measuring price changes at the factory gate — the producer price index, written in full. The number of tourists as a percentage of the local population is not asked; what IS asked stays inside the guideline lists, which is exactly why revising from the guideline beats revising from memory.

Pause here — the first practice questions are with you now, in all three objective shapes.

## Subtopic: Short Items: Quick Marks in the Micro Sections

Section B openers again: two naming marks, one two-mark application.

Name TWO characteristics of perfect competition — many buyers and sellers; homogeneous products; free entry and exit; perfect information. Any two, one mark each. Name TWO forms of non-price competition — advertising and branding; packaging, service and loyalty programmes also score. Name TWO causes of market failure — externalities and missing markets, with imperfect competition, lack of information, immobility of factors and unequal income distribution all on the approved list.

The two-mark application item: why can a monopolist not charge any price it likes? Because the demand curve still rules — at higher prices consumers buy less, so the monopolist chooses a price-quantity pair ON the demand curve, not above it. Another: how does a subsidy on public transport affect low-income households? It lowers the effective fare, freeing income for other needs and improving access to work — direction plus reason, two marks.

One habit to carry: when a short item says name or list, resist explaining. The mark is for the item; the explanation spends writing effort the paper has not paid for. Save the sentences for where the marks are.

Stop for the practice questions on this section now — naming lists and two-mark applications at pace.

## Subtopic: Data Response: The Monopolist's Graph, Described and Solved

The stimulus, spoken as a picture: a downward-sloping demand curve labelled AR, with the marginal revenue curve falling twice as steeply below it; a U-shaped average cost curve; and a marginal cost curve cutting through its lowest point. The equilibrium quantity of 100 units sits where marginal cost crosses marginal revenue. Directly above, the price on the demand curve reads R150. At the same quantity, the average cost curve reads R125.

One-mark starters. What is the selling price? R150 — read straight from the demand curve above equilibrium. What quantity does the business produce? One hundred units.

Two-mark description: briefly describe the term monopoly — a market structure with a single seller of a product without close substitutes, protected by barriers to entry.

Four-mark reasoning: why does the monopolist's marginal revenue lie below its demand curve? Because to sell an additional unit, the price must be lowered on ALL units sold, so the extra revenue from the last unit is less than its price. Two developed statements, four marks.

Four-mark calculation, shown as number sentences: profit per unit equals price minus average cost — R150 minus R125 gives R25. Total economic profit equals R25 times 100 units — R2 500. Method line, subtraction, multiplication, labelled answer. If the numbers had run the other way, with average cost at R150 and price at R125, the same chain would deliver a R2 500 economic loss — the procedure never changes, only the direction of the gap.

The practice questions for this section arrive now, anchored on the same described graph.

## Subtopic: Data Response: Inflation Figures and the Weighted Basket

The second data response supplies a table: the consumer price index standing at 120,0 last year and 127,2 this year, alongside a mini-basket with three categories — food weighted 40 percent with prices up 8 percent, transport weighted 35 percent with prices up 6 percent, and education weighted 25 percent with prices up 4 percent.

One-mark starters: which organisation publishes the CPI? Statistics South Africa. Which category carries the greatest weight? Food.

Two-mark description: briefly describe headline inflation — the rate of change of the all-items CPI for all urban areas, the number announced monthly.

Four-mark calculation one: the inflation rate. New index minus old, over old, times 100: 127,2 minus 120,0 gives 7,2; divided by 120,0 and multiplied by 100 gives 6,0 percent. The classic trap is dividing by the new index — always the OLD index, because a rate measures growth from where prices started.

Four-mark calculation two: the weighted average increase from the mini-basket. Multiply each weight by its increase and add: 0,4 times 8 gives 3,2; 0,35 times 6 gives 2,1; 0,25 times 4 gives 1,0. The weighted increase is 6,3 percent. Notice the teaching inside the arithmetic: food's heavy weight drags the average toward food's experience, which is why low-income households, whose real baskets are food-heavy, live a harsher inflation than the headline number tells.

The higher-order tail: is this inflation demand-pull or cost-push, if the table also showed fuel and electricity leading the increases? Cost-push — costs entering from the supply side — and the policy point follows: the repo rate will cool spending but cannot cheapen fuel.

Pause for this section's practice questions now — table and basket in front of you.

## Subtopic: The Eight-Mark Paragraphs: Graphs in Words and Judgements in Context

Middle-order practice: with the aid of a described graph, explain the kinked demand curve of the oligopolist. Model answer: the oligopolist sits at a current price where its demand curve bends. Above that price, demand is elastic — a lone price increase loses many customers to rivals who hold still. Below it, demand is inelastic — a price cut is matched at once, winning little extra volume. The kink makes total revenue fall in BOTH directions, so the rational oligopolist leaves price alone, and competition migrates to advertising, branding and service. Four linked statements at two marks each, and the verbal graph counts among them.

Higher-order practice: evaluate whether the minimum wage helps or harms low-paid workers. Argue both sides and land a judgement. In favour: it lifts the working poor toward a living income, narrows exploitation where employers hold power, and boosts spending in poor communities, feeding local demand. Against: set above the market rate it prices some unskilled workers out of jobs entirely — a labour surplus is unemployment — and can push small employers into the informal sector. Judgement: the effect depends on the level at which the floor is set and on enforcement; a modest, well-enforced floor helps more than it harms, while an aggressive one exports its costs to the unemployed. That final weighing sentence is the higher-order mark the paragraph exists to test.

A second higher-order shape to rehearse: propose measures a municipality could take against river pollution by nearby workshops, and justify them. Charge for waste disposal so dumping carries a price; levy penalties that exceed the cost of compliance; issue tradeable permits to cap total discharge; and educate on cheaper clean alternatives. Each proposal justified by its incentive logic — the polluter pays, so the externality enters the polluter's own arithmetic.

The practice questions on both paragraph shapes are with you now.

## Subtopic: The Essay, Built Live: Compare and Contrast Market Structures

Take the Section C instruction: compare and contrast perfect competition and monopoly in detail, and use a described graph to explain the monopoly's long-run equilibrium position. The mark allocation drives the plan: introduction maximum 2, main body maximum 26, additional part maximum 10, conclusion maximum 2 — total 40.

Introduction, two marks: a market structure describes how a market is organised — the number of sellers, the nature of the product and the ease of entry. Clean, definitional, no borrowed question wording.

Main body, twenty-six: run the comparison as paired contrasts, two marks per developed pair. Number of firms — very many against one. Nature of product — homogeneous against unique without close substitutes. Entry — free against blocked by natural cost advantages, licences or patents. Price control — the price-taking competitor against the price-making monopolist, though still bound by demand. Information — perfect against restricted. Demand curve — horizontal for the individual competitor against downward-sloping for the monopolist, with marginal revenue below it. Long-run profit — normal profit only, as entry competes gains away, against persistent economic profit behind barriers. Efficiency — the competitor pressed to lowest-cost production and allocative efficiency, against the monopolist producing less at a higher price. Examples — fresh produce markets against a sole utility. Nine developed contrasts and the body is full.

The graph paragraph, told in words: in the monopoly's long-run equilibrium, output sits where marginal cost equals marginal revenue; the price stands above it on the demand curve; and because barriers hold, price can remain above average cost, leaving the shaded rectangle of economic profit between price and average cost across the equilibrium quantity.

Additional part, ten marks — your own weighing: monopoly is not automatically evil — economies of scale can make one large producer cheaper than many small ones, and regulated natural monopolies exploit that; but unregulated monopoly transfers welfare from consumers and dulls innovation, which is why the Competition Act polices abuse of dominance. A balanced verdict with South African anchors — utilities regulated, cartels prosecuted — earns the ten.

Conclusion, two marks: one closing judgement — structure shapes conduct, so policy watches structure.

Practice questions on the comparison essay are with you now.

## Subtopic: The Second Essay Option: Combating Inflation, Planned to the Mark

The alternative essay comes from Contemporary Issues: discuss in detail the measures used to combat inflation, and evaluate the success of inflation targeting in South Africa. Plan it to the same forty-mark grid.

Introduction, two: inflation is a sustained and significant increase in the general price level, measured by the consumer price index.

Main body, twenty-six, organised by policy family with every measure tied to the inflation it fights. Monetary policy first: the Reserve Bank raises the repo rate, making credit dearer across every lending rate, cooling consumption and investment — the direct answer to demand-pull pressure; open market sales withdraw liquidity; cash reserve requirements and moral suasion tighten lending further. Fiscal policy second: restraint in government spending trims demand injections; taxation withdraws spending power; discipline over administered prices — electricity, fuel levies — attacks a cost-push engine directly. Other measures third: promoting competition so price-setters face rivals, raising productivity so wages can grow without pushing unit costs, and moderating the wage-price spiral through negotiated settlements.

Additional part, ten — evaluate targeting honestly. Successes: since 2000 the 3-to-6 percent band has anchored expectations, kept South Africa far from the hyperinflation cases, and made the Bank's reactions predictable, which itself steadies prices. Limits: the repo rate is a demand weapon, so imported oil shocks, drought food prices and administered tariffs — cost-push forces — resist it; the squeeze lands on borrowers and job-seekers first, raising the social cost of each disinflation; and the band's width invites debate about whether the midpoint should anchor lower. Verdict: targeting has delivered credibility more reliably than comfort, and its success depends on fiscal policy rowing in the same direction.

Conclusion, two: the cure must match the cause — a final line the body has earned but not spoken.
