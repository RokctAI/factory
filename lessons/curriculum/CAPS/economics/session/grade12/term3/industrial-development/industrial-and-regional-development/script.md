# Part 1 — Expert

South Africa digs up iron ore, ships it abroad for a few hundred dollars a ton, and buys it back as machinery at many times the price. That gap — between selling rocks and selling products — is what industrial policy exists to close. This session covers the two halves the syllabus pairs together: INDUSTRIAL development, government's deliberate effort to grow manufacturing, and REGIONAL development, its effort to spread that growth across the map instead of leaving it piled up in Gauteng, eThekwini and Cape Town. The examinable core is South Africa's actual strategies — and the higher-order skill of judging whether they are appropriate.

## Subtopic: Why Countries Industrialise

INDUSTRIAL DEVELOPMENT means expanding the secondary sector — manufacturing, construction, processing — relative to simply extracting and exporting raw materials. Why do governments pursue it deliberately rather than leaving it to the market? Four arguments carry the essay.

First, VALUE ADDED. Each stage of processing multiplies the value of the original material: iron ore becomes steel, steel becomes an axle, an axle becomes a bakkie. The country that performs those stages keeps the wages, profits and taxes they generate. Turning raw materials into products before export is called BENEFICIATION, and it is the stated ambition of South African policy — export the bakkie, not the ore.

Second, EMPLOYMENT. Manufacturing absorbs semi-skilled labour on a scale that mining, with its mechanisation, and finance, with its skill demands, cannot. For an economy with mass unemployment, factories are the classic ladder between the farm and the office.

Third, STABILITY. An economy that exports mainly primary commodities rides the roller-coaster of world commodity prices — flush when platinum booms, gutted when it crashes. A diversified industrial base spreads the risk across many products and markets.

Fourth, LINKAGES. A factory pulls other activity into existence: BACKWARD linkages to its suppliers — the component makers, the transporters, the toolmakers — and FORWARD linkages to the businesses that use or sell its output. One vehicle plant supports a web of hundreds of firms. This is why manufacturing is called a growth engine rather than just a sector.

Hold the counterweight too, because evaluation cuts both ways: industrialisation is not free. Protecting infant industries taxes consumers through higher prices; subsidies claim scarce public money; and history shows support that never ends breeding industries that never grow up. Every strategy in this lesson must eventually face the question — is the industry becoming competitive, or merely comfortable?

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: beneficiation and value added, the employment and stability arguments, the two directions of linkage, and the infant-industry caution.

## Subtopic: South Africa's Industrial Strategies

The syllabus expects named, dated strategies, so anchor these.

The umbrella is the NATIONAL INDUSTRIAL POLICY FRAMEWORK of 2007, implemented through rolling INDUSTRIAL POLICY ACTION PLANS — the IPAPs — updated by the Department of Trade, Industry and Competition. The IPAPs pick priority sectors — automotives, clothing and textiles, agro-processing, metals, chemicals, green industries — and back them with targeted support rather than blanket protection.

Know the flagship instruments. The AUTOMOTIVE PRODUCTION AND DEVELOPMENT PROGRAMME, the APDP, rewards vehicle makers for producing and investing locally through import-duty credits and investment allowances; it is the reason seven global manufacturers assemble vehicles in South Africa and vehicles rank among the country's top manufactured exports. The CLOTHING AND TEXTILES COMPETITIVENESS PROGRAMME subsidises upgrading in a labour-intensive industry battered by cheap imports. Section 12I tax allowances reward large new industrial investments. The BLACK INDUSTRIALISTS SCHEME finances black-owned manufacturers, tying industrial policy to transformation. And state development finance flows through the INDUSTRIAL DEVELOPMENT CORPORATION, the IDC, which lends to industrial projects the commercial banks find too slow or too risky.

Add the demand-side lever: LOCALISATION. Through designated procurement, state entities must buy specified products — buses, rail stock, cables, clothing for uniforms — from local producers, using the government's own trolley to guarantee factories a market.

The umbrella above all of it is the NATIONAL DEVELOPMENT PLAN's goal of raising manufacturing's share of output and employment. Small business support through the Department of Small Business Development and incubation programmes completes the picture, since today's component supplier is yesterday's workshop.

For balance, know the headwinds these strategies fight: unreliable and expensive electricity, port and rail bottlenecks, skills shortages, and competition from established Asian manufacturing. A strategy on paper is not a strategy in a load-shedding schedule — that sentence, properly placed, earns evaluation marks.

## Subtopic: Regional Development: Spreading the Map

Industry clusters. Left alone, firms settle where suppliers, customers, ports and skills already are — which is why Gauteng, with two percent of the land, produces around a third of national output. REGIONAL DEVELOPMENT is the deliberate counter-pull: policies that steer economic activity toward neglected areas, both to unlock their resources and to bring work to where people actually live, instead of forcing every job-seeker into overcrowded metros.

South Africa's history makes this loaded. Apartheid's decentralisation policy bribed industries to relocate to homeland border areas — and mostly failed, because incentives could not outweigh the absence of infrastructure, markets and skills. Factories collapsed the moment subsidies stopped. That failure is the standard cautionary tale: geography incentives without real economic foundations buy temporary factories, not development.

Democratic South Africa's instruments are different in design. SPATIAL DEVELOPMENT INITIATIVES — SDIs — concentrate public investment along corridors with genuine economic potential. The flagship is the MAPUTO DEVELOPMENT CORRIDOR: the N4 toll road, rail and border upgrades linking Gauteng's industry to Maputo's port, funded through public-private partnership, moving Mpumalanga's steel, petrochemicals and crops to a harbour hundreds of kilometres closer than Durban.

Second, INDUSTRIAL DEVELOPMENT ZONES, upgraded by the SEZ Act of 2014 into SPECIAL ECONOMIC ZONES. An SEZ is a fenced, purpose-built industrial area attached to a port or airport, offering world-class infrastructure plus incentives: a reduced corporate tax rate of 15 percent, building allowances, employment tax incentives, and duty-free import of inputs for export production. Know the names: COEGA outside Gqeberha, with its deep-water port of Ngqura, the largest; East London, anchored on vehicle assembly; Richards Bay, minerals and metals; DUBE TRADEPORT beside King Shaka airport, perishables and electronics; Saldanha Bay for oil-and-gas services; Tshwane's automotive SEZ feeding the Rosslyn plants.

Third, industrial parks and township and rural enterprise programmes — smaller-scale attempts to revive the old decentralisation-era estates and bring light industry to where unemployment is deepest.

## Subtopic: Judging the Strategies

Grade 12 asks not just WHAT the strategies are but whether they are APPROPRIATE — a judged answer against criteria, and the criteria are the mark scheme.

Benchmark one: alignment with international best practice. Successful industrialisers — South Korea, Malaysia, more recently Vietnam — share a recognisable recipe: support tied to EXPORT PERFORMANCE so that firms must prove themselves in world markets; support that is TEMPORARY and conditional, withdrawn from failures; heavy parallel investment in skills and infrastructure; and macroeconomic stability underneath. Measure South Africa against that recipe. The APDP passes the export test — vehicles must compete globally to earn the credits — and it is the strategy evaluators score highest. Blanket protection of uncompetitive sectors scores lowest.

Benchmark two: results. Ask the examiner's questions of each strategy. Has manufacturing's share of GDP risen? It has drifted down over two decades, from over 19 percent toward 12 to 13 percent — deindustrialisation, the single most damning aggregate fact. Have the SEZs attracted private investment beyond the state's own spending? Coega and Dube TradePort show real tenants and exports; several other zones remain largely empty sheds. Has employment grown? Vehicle assembly thrives, but clothing employment collapsed under import competition despite support. A balanced verdict cites successes AND failures, by name.

Benchmark three: the binding constraints test. The best-designed incentive cannot compensate for electricity that fails, ports that queue ships for weeks, rail that loses freight to trucks, and skills that emigrate. Appropriateness therefore depends on sequencing: fixing network industries may matter more than any subsidy. That is the strongest single evaluation point available to you.

Benchmark four: cost and governance. Incentives spend public money; the question is rands per sustainable job. Zones that require permanent subsidy repeat the homeland mistake in modern clothing. Good governance — clean procurement, policy certainty, coordination between departments and provinces — is itself a location factor: investors price political risk exactly as they price transport costs.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the best-practice recipe, the deindustrialisation fact, named successes and failures, the binding-constraints argument, and cost per job as a criterion.

## Subtopic: The Bakkie and the Bag of Ore

Put two exports side by side on the dock. A bag of iron ore: South Africa earns a few hundred rand, and the wages of the digging. A bakkie: South Africa earns hundreds of thousands of rand — and inside that price sit the wages of the steelworker, the component maker, the assembler, the painter, the seat-stitcher, the transporter, plus the profits and taxes of every firm along the way. Same iron. Different countries got rich off it, depending on who did the making.

That is the whole argument for industrial development in one picture. Every step of making — ore to steel, steel to parts, parts to bakkie — is called adding value, and doing those steps at home before exporting is called beneficiation. The country that stops at digging sells the cheapest link in the chain and buys back the most expensive one.

And a factory never stands alone. Behind the vehicle plant stand the firms that feed it — tyres, glass, wiring, seats, steel, delivery trucks. In front of it stand the dealerships, the mechanics, the parts shops. Hundreds of businesses hang off one assembly line, which is why losing a plant devastates a whole town and winning one transforms it.

So government leans on the scale: tax breaks for factories, rewards for carmakers that build here, cheap loans from the state's industrial bank, and a rule that the state itself must buy local buses, cables and uniforms. The catch — and say this too — is that help must have a finish line. An industry that only survives on protection is a permanent tax on everyone else, paid in higher prices. Support the child to grow up, not to stay a child.

## Subtopic: Why the Factory Went to Coega

Now the map problem. Ask why the factory is never in YOUR town, and the honest answer is: factories go where factories already are. The suppliers are in Gauteng, the port is in Durban, the engineers are where the other engineers are. Left alone, money piles up in three city regions and the rest of the country exports its young people to them.

Government's counter-move is to build the missing foundations in chosen places and then sweeten the deal. Take Coega, outside Gqeberha. The state built a deep-water port and a fenced industrial zone with roads, power lines and water already in place. Then it says to investors: locate here and pay company tax of 15 percent instead of the normal rate, get help with buildings, hire young workers with a tax break, import your inputs duty-free if you export what you make. That package — a special economic zone — now hosts real factories, and East London's zone keeps a vehicle plant fed, and Dube TradePort flies fresh produce and electronics out beside the King Shaka runway.

Sometimes the sweetener is a road instead of a fence. The Maputo Corridor upgraded the N4 from Gauteng to Mozambique's harbour, and suddenly Mpumalanga's steel and fruit had a shorter road to a ship. Farms, factories and border posts along the route woke up — a corridor pulls a whole line of towns, not one zone.

But history keeps the warning lit. Apartheid tried to bribe factories into the homelands, far from customers, suppliers and skills. The subsidies flowed, the factories stood, the subsidies stopped, the factories died. The lesson: you can pay a factory to ARRIVE anywhere; it only STAYS where business actually works. Zones and corridors succeed when they fix the real foundations — transport, power, ports, skills — and fail when they are only a fence and a tax break in an empty field.

## Subtopic: Marking the Government's Homework

The exam's favourite instruction here is EVALUATE — is South Africa's strategy appropriate? Here is how to answer like a marker, in four moves.

Move one: state the test. Around the world, industrial policy works when help is tied to exporting, is temporary, and stands on working infrastructure and skills. That is the checklist strong answers open with.

Move two: give the wins, by name. The car programme works — global brands assemble here because rewards depend on producing and exporting, and vehicles rank among our top manufactured exports. Coega and Dube TradePort have real tenants, real jobs, real cargo. The Maputo Corridor moves real freight. Never say everything failed; the marker knows better.

Move three: give the failures, by name, with the same calm. Manufacturing's slice of the economy has shrunk for twenty years — from about a fifth toward an eighth — which means the overall scoreboard reads deindustrialisation. Clothing jobs collapsed despite support. Some zones are mostly empty sheds — the state's money went in, private money did not follow. Never say everything worked; the marker knows better than that too.

Move four: name the real bottleneck, because this is where top marks live. The best incentive in the world cannot outweigh power cuts, broken rail, queuing ports and scarce skills. A factory can be paid to arrive; it stays only where trucks move, lights stay on and workers can be found. So the appropriate strategy may be less about new sweeteners and more about fixing electricity, transport and training first — the foundations every factory needs, in every province at once.

Then close with a verdict in one sentence: the direction is right, the flagship programmes earn their keep, but the strategy underperforms while the foundations crumble — fix the constraints and the incentives can finally bite.
