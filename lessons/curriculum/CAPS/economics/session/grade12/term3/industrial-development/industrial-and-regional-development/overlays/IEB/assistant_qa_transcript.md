**Mandy:** Time for class questions! Opening one: if beneficiation is so obviously profitable — sell the bakkie, not the ore — why hasn't it just happened by itself? Why does anyone still export raw minerals?

**Tutor:** Because the profit is obvious only when you ignore the costs of getting there, and firms never ignore costs. Beneficiation is energy-hungry, capital-hungry and skills-hungry. Smelting chrome or refining metal consumes enormous electricity — and a country where power is unreliable and tariffs climb every year has turned its natural advantage into a disadvantage; several smelters have actually closed or moved for exactly this reason. Then capital: a processing plant costs billions and repays itself over decades, so investors demand policy certainty and working logistics before committing — a queuing port or a broken railway quietly deletes the business case. Then skills: metallurgists, artisans and process engineers are scarce and internationally mobile. Meanwhile, the raw export is easy: world markets stand ready to buy ore as it is, today, no plant required. So the ore leaves because the path of least resistance is open while the path of most value is blocked — and that reframes the whole policy question. The point of industrial strategy is not to command beneficiation into existence but to unblock the path: reliable power, working rail, trained artisans, credible rules. In an essay, that link — beneficiation fails where the foundations fail — joins this topic to the binding-constraints argument and earns the evaluation mark.

***

**Mandy:** Next question: the SEZ tax rate of fifteen percent — isn't that unfair to every factory OUTSIDE the fence, paying the full rate a few kilometres away?

**Tutor:** It is unequal by design, and whether it is unfair depends on what the discount actually buys — so let us weigh it like assessors. The case for the fence: the zone is trying to overcome a genuine market failure. Factories follow factories, so a region with no industry cannot attract its first plant without an artificial push; the discount compensates the pioneer for arriving where suppliers and skills are still thin. If the zone works, the pioneers attract the suppliers, the suppliers attract more plants, and eventually the fence becomes unnecessary — the subsidy was scaffolding, not a permanent wall. The case against: the factory just outside the fence competes for the same customers with a heavier tax load, which distorts competition without creating anything new — especially when a firm that would have invested ANYWAY simply relocates into the zone to harvest the discount. Economists call that deadweight: public money buying behaviour that was already coming. The honest test is additionality — did the zone create investment that would not otherwise exist, or merely move and relabel it? Coega's harbour-anchored tenants lean toward the first; an empty zone offering discounts with no takers proves neither. So answer with the balance: geographically targeted incentives are defensible as temporary scaffolding for genuinely new activity, and indefensible as a permanent discount that punishes the unlucky neighbour.

**Mandy:** Third one, from the debate at lunch: if Gauteng already works so well, why not let everyone just move there? Building factories in far-off provinces sounds like fighting gravity.

**Tutor:** That is the sharpest question in regional economics, and serious people sit on both sides of it. The pro-Gauteng case is real: clustering is productive — firms share suppliers, workers change jobs without changing cities, ideas jump between factories — so concentrating people where the jobs are can raise national income faster than scattering jobs where people are. Migration to cities is, historically, how most countries developed. But now the counter-case, and it is not sentimental. First, congestion: Gauteng's water, power, housing and transport are already straining — beyond a point, each arriving household costs more infrastructure than it adds output, while the sending regions hollow out. Second, people are not perfectly mobile: land, family networks, language and the cost of city housing anchor millions where they are; policy that only serves movers abandons the anchored. Third, some regional assets genuinely cannot move — a deep-water harbour at Ngqura, coal and minerals in the north, farmland along the N4 — and unlocking a fixed asset needs investment where the asset stands. That is why the modern design is corridors and port-anchored zones rather than factories in the veld: it does not fight gravity, it creates NEW gravity where a real asset justifies it, and the homeland failure is the proof of what happens when you subsidise against geography instead of with it. The balanced answer: back the movers with functioning cities AND back the fixed assets with corridors and zones — and refuse to build anything whose only foundation is a subsidy.

**Mandy:** Last one: for the evaluation essay, can I just conclude that the strategies failed because manufacturing's share fell? It sounds like the strongest single fact.

**Tutor:** It is the strongest single fact and the weakest possible conclusion — keep the fact, upgrade the reasoning. Three repairs. First, mind the counterfactual: the share of manufacturing has drifted down in most economies as services expand, and South Africa's slide has worldwide company; the honest question is not why is the share falling but whether it fell faster than peers and whether policy slowed or accelerated the slide. Without the APDP, would there be seven global carmakers assembling here, or none? Judging a medicine requires imagining the patient without it. Second, disaggregate: a single aggregate hides opposite stories — vehicles thriving under export-linked support, clothing collapsing under import pressure despite support, zones splitting into working ports and empty fields. A verdict that names both columns is worth more than a verdict that averages them. Third, assign the causes correctly: much of the damage traces to electricity, rail and ports — constraints outside industrial policy's control — so the fair charge against the strategies is not that they caused deindustrialisation, but that they could not outweigh the broken foundations beneath them, and that money kept flowing into sweeteners while the foundations went unfixed. Structure it as the lesson taught: state the test, credit the wins, name the failures, point at the bottleneck — then your closing sentence, whatever verdict it reaches, is earned rather than announced.
