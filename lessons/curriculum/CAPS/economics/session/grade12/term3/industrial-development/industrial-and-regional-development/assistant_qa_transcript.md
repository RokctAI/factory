**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First: if beneficiation is so obviously profitable — the bakkie beats the bag of ore every time — why does South Africa still export so much raw material? It sounds like leaving money on the dock.

**Tutor:** Because each step up the chain has an entry price, and the entry price is where the argument gets honest. Smelting and manufacturing are ferociously energy-intensive, and South African electricity has gone from being the world's cheapest to expensive and unreliable — a smelter that loses power for hours does not just pause, it can be destroyed. Processing also needs capital, specific skills, and, above all, competitiveness: it is not enough to MAKE the steel product, you must make it at a price and quality that beats established producers in Asia who have decades of scale behind them. Where those conditions exist, South Africa does beneficiate — that is exactly what the vehicle industry is: imported ideas, local steel, aluminium and labour, exported as finished cars. Where they do not, forcing beneficiation can subtract value rather than add it: if turning R100 of ore into product costs R130 here but R90 elsewhere, the world simply buys elsewhere and the ore stays unsold too. So the mature essay answer is conditional: beneficiation is the right ambition, and the binding constraints — electricity, logistics, skills — decide how much of it is genuinely profitable at any moment. Fix the constraints and the chain climbs almost by itself; ignore them and beneficiation slogans stay slogans.

**Mandy:** Next: what is actually the difference between an IDZ and an SEZ? The textbook uses both and half of us are just writing the letters and hoping.

**Tutor:** Hope less, and use the history, because the distinction is a genuinely nice mark-earner. The Industrial Development Zones came first, from around 2000 — Coega, East London, Richards Bay — and they were defined narrowly: fenced industrial estates linked to a port or airport, aimed at export industries, offering good infrastructure and customs advantages but surprisingly thin tax incentives. The result was underwhelming tenancy, and the policy was rethought. The Special Economic Zones Act of 2014 is the upgrade: it kept the existing zones, absorbed them into a wider family — the concept now covers more than just port-linked export estates — and, crucially, attached a serious incentive package: the 15 percent corporate tax rate, building allowances, the employment tax incentive for younger workers, and duty-free or VAT-relieved inputs inside customs-controlled areas. So the clean sentence for the exam is: every IDZ became an SEZ, but not every SEZ is an old IDZ, and the SEZ regime differs mainly in the depth of its incentives and the breadth of its purposes. If the question asks for evaluation, add the punchline: changing the letters and sweetening the tax did not automatically fill the sheds — zones with genuine anchors, like Coega's port and East London's assembly plant, attracted tenants, while some newer zones still wait for private investment to follow the public money.

**Mandy:** This one caused a debate: my cousin's town has a half-empty industrial park from years ago. Would it not be better to just give the incentives to Gauteng, where industry actually works, instead of chasing factories into the countryside?

**Tutor:** You have just restated, in family terms, the central tension of regional policy — efficiency versus equity — and both sides deserve their paragraph. The efficiency case says exactly what you said: agglomeration is real, factories are more productive where suppliers, customers and skills already cluster, so a rand of incentive in Gauteng buys more output than a rand in a remote park. Push subsidies against geography and you get the homeland story — factories that lived on subsidy and died with it, and your cousin's half-empty park is plausibly a descendant of that era. But the equity case is not sentimental; it is also economic. People are not costless to move: when all opportunity concentrates in three metros, the countryside exports its youth, the cities import overcrowding — informal settlements, stressed services, long commutes — and entire provinces' resources lie unused. There is also a political-stability argument: development that visibly excludes whole regions is not durable. The modern compromise, and the syllabus's own answer, is to be selective: do not scatter incentives everywhere, but back places with genuine economic logic — a deep-water port at Coega, an existing assembly cluster in East London, a trade route to Maputo — and connect the rest of the country with infrastructure rather than bribes. So the defensible verdict is neither pole: not every town can have a factory, but a country that develops only Gauteng develops a queue at Gauteng's edge.

**Mandy:** Last one: for the essay, how do we criticise government strategy without just writing a list of complaints? Loadshedding, Transnet, corruption — where does economics end and moaning begin?

**Tutor:** The difference between analysis and moaning is structure: a complaint names a problem, an evaluation connects the problem to the strategy's own objectives with a criterion in between. Build every critical paragraph in three sentences. Sentence one, the strategy's goal, stated fairly: the SEZ programme aims to attract private export-oriented investment to purpose-built zones. Sentence two, the evidence, specific and balanced: Coega and Dube TradePort host real tenants and exports, while several newer zones remain largely publicly funded sheds, and manufacturing's overall share of GDP has kept falling. Sentence three, the criterion that explains the gap: incentives cannot compensate for unreliable electricity, rail and ports, so the binding constraint lies outside the zones' fences — which implies the remedy, sequencing infrastructure repair before or alongside incentives. Notice what that structure does: loadshedding stops being a moan and becomes a mechanism — the reason a correctly designed incentive underperforms. Do the same with corruption: it is not just wickedness, it is a location factor, because investors price policy uncertainty exactly as they price transport costs, so clean procurement is industrial policy. And always end an evaluation with a verdict sentence that takes a position — direction right, execution constrained, fix the foundations first. Examiners cannot award judgement marks to an answer that never judges.
