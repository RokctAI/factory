**Mandy:** Class questions before we finish! First up, from three different people, so it's clearly bugging everyone: if the carbon tax is so clever, why not just ban pollution completely? A ban sounds stronger than a tax.

**Tutor:** A ban IS stronger — and that is exactly why it is usually the wrong tool. Follow the logic. A total ban on emissions is a ban on electricity, cement, steel, fuel and fertiliser: you would not be banning pollution, you would be banning the economy that feeds and employs people, which fails the economic and social legs of sustainability while serving the environmental one. So bans are reserved for cases where the harm is acute and there is no acceptable quantity — leaded petrol, dumping toxins in a river, hunting an endangered species. For everything else, the real question is not whether to pollute but HOW MUCH and WHO SHOULD CUT FIRST, and that is where the tax outperforms the rule. A tax lets each firm compare its own clean-up cost with the tax rate: firms that can cut cheaply cut a lot, firms that can only cut expensively pay while they retool, and the total reduction happens at the lowest cost to society. A uniform rule cannot see those differences — it demands the same from the nimble and the desperate alike. And the tax never sleeps: even a firm already below the standard keeps saving money by improving further, whereas a rule rewards reaching the minimum and then stopping. So the exam-grade formulation is the mixing rule from the lesson: ban what kills, tax what harms, and never confuse the strength of an instrument with its suitability.

**Mandy:** Next question: the lesson said the ozone treaty worked but climate treaties keep struggling. What made ozone so much easier to fix?

**Tutor:** Beautiful comparison — and the differences between the two problems are practically a checklist for why collective action succeeds or fails. First, the source: CFCs came from a handful of industrial chemicals made by a small number of companies in a small number of countries, while carbon comes from the energy base of every economy on earth — coal, oil and gas are not one product but the bloodstream of production. Second, the substitute: chemists had affordable replacement gases ready within years, so quitting CFCs cost consumers almost nothing; replacing fossil energy means rebuilding grids, transport and industry over decades. Third, the visibility: the ozone hole was a single, photographable emergency with an immediate health story — skin cancer — while climate damage arrives diffusely, unevenly and mostly in the future, which invites every negotiator to hope the costs land on someone else's coastline and someone else's term of office. Fourth, the free-rider mathematics: with so few producers, monitoring compliance was cheap; with two hundred countries burning carbon, each is tempted to let the others cut first. Montreal, in other words, was the easiest possible version of the game, and climate is the hardest. That is not a counsel of despair — it explains WHY the climate architecture evolved from Kyoto's binding-for-some to Paris's everyone-pledges-and-ratchets design: when you cannot compel, you build transparency and peer pressure instead. Use Montreal as proof cooperation can work, and use these four differences to explain why climate needs a different machine.

**Mandy:** Third one, and it got heated at the back: why should South Africa cut emissions at all when we're a small share of the world total and we didn't cause most of the historical problem?

**Tutor:** That argument deserves a serious answer, because half of it is correct and the incorrect half is expensive. The correct half: historically, the wealth of the industrialised world was built on carbon, and South Africa's contribution to the accumulated stock is small — which is exactly why the Rio principle of common but differentiated responsibilities exists, and why the just-transition money flows from rich countries to us rather than the other way. Nobody serious asks South Africa to decarbonise at Denmark's pace on South Africa's budget. Now the incorrect half: concluding that we should therefore do nothing. Three reasons. One: we are not actually small per person — a coal-dominated grid makes this one of the most carbon-intensive economies anywhere, so our exports carry a heavy carbon backpack precisely when trading partners are starting to charge for carbon at their borders; doing nothing taxes our own exporters. Two: the damage arrives here regardless of who caused it — the region warms at roughly double the global average, and our farms and water systems sit in the front row. Three: the transition is arriving with financing attached — the 8,5-billion-dollar partnership exists because the world needs a demonstration that a coal country can change justly, and being the demonstration brings investment that bystanders do not get. So the balanced verdict: differentiated responsibility, yes; exemption from responsibility, no — and self-interest, not charity, is doing most of the arguing.

**Mandy:** Last one: my uncle says the plastic-bag levy did nothing — everyone just pays the few cents and takes the bag anyway. Doesn't that prove environmental taxes fail?

**Tutor:** Your uncle has spotted something real, and what he has spotted is not that environmental taxes fail — it is that a tax set too low is a tax in name only. The mechanism of a corrective tax is the price signal: it works when the charge is large enough to change the comparison a person or firm makes at the moment of decision. A few cents against the convenience of a bag barely registers, so behaviour barely changes — the levy raises a little revenue and buys a little guilt, and that is all. Notice the contrast with cases where the signal has teeth: when the charge per unit is meaningful, as with a carbon price that rises year by year, firms redesign processes because the saving justifies the engineering. So the levy's weakness is calibration, not concept. There is a second honest critique hiding in your uncle's point: for a corrective tax to be respected, people must see the revenue fighting the harm — funding recycling, clean-ups, alternatives — otherwise it reads as just another collection. And there is a third lesson for evaluation answers: price signals work best alongside available alternatives; a levy on bags bites harder where sturdy reusable bags are cheap and normal. So write it this way: the instrument is sound, the dose was homeopathic — the failure of a weak tax is an argument for setting the price at the social cost, not for abandoning the price.
