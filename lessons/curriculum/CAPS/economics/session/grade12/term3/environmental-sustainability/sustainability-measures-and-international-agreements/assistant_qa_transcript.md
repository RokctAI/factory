**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! The first one is the one everyone argues about at home: South Africa has mass unemployment and load-shedding. Is it not a luxury for a country like ours to worry about the environment before it has fed and employed its people?

**Tutor:** That question deserves a careful answer rather than a slogan, because the syllabus expects you to hold both truths. Start by granting what is true in it: development IS the priority the Constitution and the electorate set, energy poverty is real, and a policy that shut the coal fleet tomorrow would create a humanitarian crisis. But the jobs-versus-environment framing hides three errors. First, it assumes environmental damage is free — it is not; it is a bill deferred. The Highveld's polluted air is paid for in asthma medication, lost work days and early deaths, and the 2022 Durban floods destroyed billions of rands of infrastructure and hundreds of lives; that money and labour were subtracted from development, not saved for it. Second, it assumes the poor are protected by ignoring the environment, when they are its first victims — the household beside the dump, the farmworker in the drought, the fisher on the emptied stock. Environmental protection is not a middle-class hobby; the commons ARE the assets of the poor. Third, it treats the choice as either-or when the newest opportunities are both-and: Working for Water pays unemployed workers to restore rivers, renewables construction is employment, and the Just Energy Transition Partnership exists precisely to fund development THROUGH the green transition rather than instead of it. So the mature formulation is: a poor country cannot afford environmental perfectionism, and it can afford environmental neglect even less, because neglect taxes exactly the future growth it hopes to have. Sequencing and justice, not either-or, is the answer that earns the evaluation marks.

**Mandy:** Next: what is actually the difference between a carbon tax and tradable permits? Both make polluters pay, and half the class writes them up as the same tool.

**Tutor:** They share a philosophy — price the externality — but they fix opposite variables, and that is the distinction to write. A tax fixes the PRICE of pollution and lets the QUANTITY find itself: government says every ton of carbon costs this many rand, each firm compares the tax with its own cost of cleaning up, the cheap cleaners clean and the expensive ones pay, and total emissions settle wherever those choices land. You know the price for certain; you discover the quantity afterwards. Permits run the mirror image: government fixes the QUANTITY — a hard cap of so many tons for the whole industry — issues that many allowances, and lets firms trade them, so the PRICE finds itself in the market. You know the environmental outcome for certain; you discover the cost afterwards. That difference decides which tool fits which problem. Where the danger is crossing a physical threshold — a total emissions budget the atmosphere can take — the cap's certainty is the point, which is why the European Union built an emissions trading scheme. Where a government wants a steady, predictable incentive plus revenue it can recycle into rebates and transition funding, a tax fits — which is South Africa's choice, and revenue is not a small point: our carbon tax raises money in the same motion that it discourages carbon. Both beat pure command and control on cost, because both let the cheapest cleaners do the most cleaning. Write the fixed-price-versus-fixed-quantity sentence, attach the EU example to permits and the South African example to the tax, and that question is fully banked.

**Mandy:** This one comes straight from the news: if the Paris Agreement is voluntary — every country writes its own homework — why does anyone expect it to work at all?

**Tutor:** Because its designers concluded, after Kyoto, that in a world with no global government, an agreement everyone joins loosely beats an agreement few join tightly — and then they built machinery to make the looseness tighten over time. Understand what Kyoto proved: binding targets sound strong, but a sovereign country that dislikes its target simply stays out, as the United States did, or is outside the target list altogether, as the fast-growing emitters were — so the strictness applied to a shrinking share of world emissions. Paris inverted the trade: universality first. Nearly every nation, rich and poor, files a nationally determined contribution. Then three ratchets work on those promises. Transparency: contributions and progress are public, measured and internationally reviewed, so laggards are visible to their own citizens, investors and trade partners — embarrassment is a real currency between states. The ratchet mechanism proper: contributions must be renewed every five years and are expected to strengthen each round, never weaken. And the global stocktake — the first was Dubai in 2023 — adds up everyone's promises against the temperature goal and announces the gap, forcing the next round to respond. Is it enough? Current pledges still overshoot the goal, and honesty requires saying so. But the evaluation answer has two sides: the voluntary design got every major emitter inside one framework for the first time, redirected trillions in investment signals, and created the JETP-style finance now flowing to South Africa — while its weakness remains that no court compels a country that shrugs. Name both the mechanism and the gap, and end with the sharp sentence: Paris is not weak because it is voluntary; it is voluntary because anything binding would have been empty.

**Mandy:** Last one: essays keep demanding recent examples, and the class panic is real — how recent is recent, and what if we cite something and it has changed by exam time?

**Tutor:** Examiners are testing whether your knowledge is alive, not whether you memorised this morning's headlines — so build your recency from durable landmarks, not moving news. For this topic, four citations will not age against you. The carbon tax: in force since 2019, Africa's first, with rates legislated to rise — that trajectory is the fact, and it holds regardless of the current rand figure. The loss and damage fund: created at Sharm el-Sheikh in 2022 and operationalised at the following COP — a genuine structural change in climate diplomacy, safe to cite for years. The first global stocktake at Dubai in 2023, with its call to transition away from fossil fuels — the first time that phrase survived into a COP decision, which is why it matters. And the Just Energy Transition Partnership: announced at Glasgow in 2021, 8,5 billion dollars pledged, South Africa as the world's test case — even as details evolve, the existence and design of the partnership are settled history. Notice the pattern: cite the DECISION and its date, not the implementation status, because decisions do not un-happen while implementation wobbles weekly. Then armour your sentence with framing: writing that the fund was established in 2022 and disbursement has been contested is both accurate and evaluative — you have shown recency AND judgement in one line. And if an exam question references something newer than your preparation, anchor it to the framework you do know: any new climate decision is either a rule, a price, a fund or a promise, and this lesson taught you how to analyse all four. The toolkit never goes out of date; only the examples rotate through it.
