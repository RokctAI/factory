# Part 1 — Expert

Economics began this course as the study of scarce resources — and this final term topic asks the question the whole subject has been postponing: what happens when the scarce resource is the planet itself? Air, rivers, soil and climate do not pass through any market, carry no price tags, and belong to everyone and no one, so the market that allocates bread efficiently fails spectacularly at allocating clean air. This session covers the syllabus circuit: why environmental damage is an ECONOMIC problem, the state of the environment, the toolkit of measures that make sustainability pay, and the international agreements from Rio to Paris and beyond.

## Subtopic: Why Markets Fail the Environment

Start with the definition every essay must anchor on. SUSTAINABLE DEVELOPMENT, from the Brundtland Commission of 1987, is development that meets the needs of the present WITHOUT compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs. It stands on three legs — economic, social and environmental — and the syllabus treats the environment as capital: natural resources are assets, and a country that grows by consuming its natural capital is not earning income, it is spending inheritance.

Why does a free market damage the environment? Three failures, and they are the analytical heart of the topic.

First, EXTERNALITIES. A factory dumping effluent into a river imposes real costs — dead fish, sick downstream users, treatment plants — on people who are not part of the factory's transactions. Those costs appear on nobody's invoice, so the factory's private cost sits below the true SOCIAL cost, its product is priced too cheaply, and too much of it is produced. Pollution is not an accident of the market; it is what the price system produces when part of the cost is invisible.

Second, PUBLIC GOODS. Clean air and a stable climate are non-excludable — nobody can be fenced out — and non-rival — my breathing does not use up yours. No one can charge for them, so no private firm will supply them, and everyone hopes to FREE RIDE on everyone else's restraint.

Third, MISSING PROPERTY RIGHTS — the TRAGEDY OF THE COMMONS. A fish stock owned by nobody is raced to exhaustion by fishers who each reason correctly that the fish they leave will simply be caught by someone else. What everybody owns, nobody protects. Grazing commons, aquifers, forests and the atmosphere itself all repeat the pattern.

Hold the three failures together and the policy logic of the whole lesson follows: make the invisible costs visible, price the unpriced, and give the ownerless an owner or a rule.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: the Brundtland definition word-perfect, externalities and social cost, public goods and free riding, and the tragedy of the commons.

## Subtopic: The State of the Environment

The syllabus expects the damage catalogued soberly, globally and locally.

CLIMATE CHANGE leads. Carbon dioxide and other greenhouse gases from fossil fuels trap heat; the planet has already warmed by more than one degree Celsius since industrialisation, bringing intensified droughts, floods and storms. South Africa is doubly exposed: our economy runs on coal — Eskom's fleet makes us one of the world's most carbon-intensive economies — and our region is warming at nearly twice the global average, with water-stressed agriculture in the crosshairs. The Western Cape's near-miss with Day Zero and the Durban floods are the local face of a global curve.

WATER: South Africa is one of the driest countries on earth, with rainfall around half the world average. Rivers carry mining acid drainage, untreated sewage from failing municipal works, and agricultural runoff; wetlands — nature's own filters — have been drained and built over.

AIR: coal-belt towns in Mpumalanga's Highveld breathe some of the world's most polluted air, with measurable costs in asthma, lost work days and early deaths — externalities with names and addresses.

LAND AND BIODIVERSITY: soil erosion and overgrazing eat farmland; deforestation continues globally at millions of hectares a year; species loss runs at rates biologists compare to prehistoric extinctions. Biodiversity is economic capital — the genetic library for medicine, crops and the tourism industry the previous lesson priced.

WASTE: landfills leak, plastic accumulates in rivers and oceans, and a throwaway production model treats the earth as both endless mine and bottomless dustbin.

The economic reading of this catalogue matters more than its misery: every item is future GDP quietly draining away — harvests lost, tourists deterred, workers sickened, cities rebuilt after floods. Environmental damage is not a rival concern to economic growth; unmanaged, it is a tax on all future growth.

Stop for this section's questions now — the climate mechanism and South Africa's double exposure, water and air specifics, biodiversity as capital, and damage read as future GDP lost.

## Subtopic: Making Sustainability Pay: the Policy Toolkit

Government's toolkit divides into command and market, and the exam wants both with examples.

COMMAND AND CONTROL: direct regulation. Emission standards for factories and vehicles; zoning that keeps industry off wetlands; environmental impact assessments before mines and developments; protected areas and quotas — fishing quotas, hunting permits, water-use licences. Strengths: certainty and enforceability where danger is acute. Weaknesses: inspection is costly, standards give no reward for doing better than the minimum, and a uniform rule ignores that cutting pollution is cheap for some firms and ruinous for others.

MARKET-BASED MEASURES price the externality so the polluter pays. ENVIRONMENTAL TAXES: South Africa's CARBON TAX, in force since 2019 as Africa's first, prices each ton of carbon dioxide; the plastic-bag levy and the tyre levy work the same logic at the till. A tax slid into the price makes the social cost private, and every producer now saves money by polluting less — the incentive works continuously, not just at the inspector's visit. SUBSIDIES flip the sign: rebates for solar water heaters, support for renewable energy through the renewable procurement programme that has built wind and solar farms across the Karoo and beyond. TRADABLE PERMITS cap total pollution and let firms trade the allowances: the cap guarantees the environmental outcome, trading finds the cheapest cutters, and the European emissions trading scheme is the working example. Finally, assigning PROPERTY RIGHTS cures the commons where feasible — community ownership of conservancies turns wildlife from a poaching target into a village asset, the logic behind community game reserves bordering the Kruger.

Add the supporting rank: education and information — recycling behaviour, eco-labelling that lets consumers price ethics; and government's own footprint — public procurement, green building standards, and rehabilitating what is broken through programmes like Working for Water, which pays unemployed workers to clear alien vegetation that drinks the country's scarce rivers, buying water and jobs with one budget.

Evaluation sentence to carry: commands set the floor, prices move the average, and the best regimes mix both — a standard where poison is acute, a tax where efficiency matters, permits where a hard cap is the point.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — command tools with examples, the carbon tax and levies, permits and their cap-plus-trade logic, property rights as commons cure, and the mixing rule.

## Subtopic: The World's Promises: International Agreements

Climate and biodiversity are global commons, so no single country's virtue suffices — hence the treaty ladder the syllabus requires, rung by rung.

MONTREAL, 1987: the proof of concept. The world agreed to phase out CFCs, the chemicals shredding the ozone layer; substitutes were found, the phase-out held, and the ozone hole is healing — the most successful environmental treaty ever, cited whenever cynics say cooperation is impossible.

RIO, 1992 — the Earth Summit: sustainability's constitutional convention. It produced Agenda 21, the global action plan for sustainable development, the Convention on Biological Diversity, and the UN Framework Convention on Climate Change — the UNFCCC — the parent treaty under which all later climate negotiation happens, with its principle of COMMON BUT DIFFERENTIATED RESPONSIBILITIES: all nations must act, but the rich, who built their wealth on carbon, must carry more.

KYOTO, 1997: the first binding attempt — quantified emission cuts, but only for developed countries, and with the United States never ratifying and major emerging emitters outside the targets, its coverage shrank as its lesson grew: binding-for-some does not move a global curve.

JOHANNESBURG, 2002: the World Summit on Sustainable Development, on South African soil — implementation-focused, linking environment to poverty, water and energy access.

PARIS, 2015: the architecture that governs now. Nearly every nation pledges NATIONALLY DETERMINED CONTRIBUTIONS — self-set, progressively tightened emission targets — aiming to hold warming well below two degrees and pursue one and a half. Universal where Kyoto was partial; voluntary where Kyoto was binding — its strength and its weakness in a single design.

Since Paris, the annual COPs have added rungs: Glasgow 2021 agreed to phase DOWN coal and finalised carbon-market rules; Sharm el-Sheikh 2022 created the LOSS AND DAMAGE fund, compensating vulnerable countries for climate harm already unavoidable; Dubai 2023's first global stocktake called for transitioning away from fossil fuels. And South Africa stands in the middle of the story's newest chapter: the JUST ENERGY TRANSITION PARTNERSHIP, announced at Glasgow, in which wealthy nations pledged 8,5 billion dollars to help this coal country decarbonise without abandoning coal workers and coal towns — the phrase JUST TRANSITION carrying the social leg of sustainability into energy policy.

The final questions of this part are with you now: Montreal's proof, Rio's three offspring, Kyoto against Paris as designs, the recent COP additions, and South Africa's JETP.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same topic from the bank of one river — because every concept in it lives in a village with an upstream and a downstream.

## Subtopic: The River Nobody Owns

A factory sits upstream of a village. Its pipe pays nothing to empty into the river. Downstream, the fish die, the water needs boiling, the clinic queue grows. Add up the factory's books and business is excellent — because the biggest cost of its production is being paid by people who never appear in its books. That cost has a name: an externality. It is real, it is in rands — medicine, lost catches, boiled water — but it lands outside the factory's fence.

Why does the river get treated this way when nobody would dump sludge in their own yard? Because the river is everybody's and nobody's. Your yard has an owner who defends it. The river has no owner to send the bill. And whatever everybody owns, nobody protects — the fisherman who spares a fish knows the next boat will simply take it, so both boats race to catch the last one. Economists call it the tragedy of the commons, and it explains empty seas, vanished forests and dirty air in one sentence: the price tag is missing.

Clean air is even trickier. Suppose the village hires a truck to stop illegal dumping, and the air clears. EVERYONE breathes easier — including the neighbour who paid nothing. You cannot fence the fresh air to paying customers only, so everyone waits for someone else to pay, and the truck is never hired. That waiting game is called free riding, and it is why clean environments do not appear by themselves: no seller can charge for them.

So remember the diagnosis in one line: the market damages the environment not because people are wicked, but because the damage is priced at zero. Everything in the rest of this lesson is one idea — put the missing price back.

## Subtopic: Sending the River a Bill

If the problem is a missing price, the fixes write price tags — four of them, each with a village version.

Tag one: the RULE. The village chief bans dumping outright, fines offenders, and inspects the pipe. That is command and control — emission standards, licences, protected areas. It works where the poison is deadly and certainty matters. Its weakness: the inspector must keep coming, and the factory that COULD clean up cheaply has no reason to do more than the minimum the rule demands.

Tag two: the TAX. Charge the factory per litre of muck. Suddenly cleaning equipment stops being a cost and becomes a saving — every litre not dumped is money kept. Nobody needs to catch the factory; its own accountant now polices the pipe, all year round. That is the carbon tax on every ton of carbon, the plastic-bag levy at the till: pollute less, pay less.

Tag three: the REWARD. Pay for the good behaviour instead: rebates for solar heaters, support that has planted wind turbines and solar farms across the Karoo. Where the tax pushes, the subsidy pulls.

Tag four: the OWNER. Give the commons a keeper. Hand the village stretch of river — or the wildlife on community land — to the community itself, with the right to charge users and the reason to protect the asset. A springbok owned by nobody is poached; a springbok that pays the village's school fees is guarded. That is why community conservancies border the Kruger, and why Working for Water pays local teams to clear the alien trees drinking the river: the village becomes the river's paid bodyguard.

The smart answer mixes tags: a hard rule where lives are at stake, a tax to keep every factory improving, rewards to speed the new, and owners wherever the commons can be given one.

## Subtopic: Promises Between Countries

Last stretch: the atmosphere is the biggest river of all — every country's pipe empties into it, so the village meeting has to be a meeting of nations. Getting nearly two hundred countries to keep one promise is the hardest free-rider problem on earth: each is tempted to let the others cut first.

Yet it has worked once, completely. In 1987 in Montreal the world agreed to stop making the gases tearing the ozone layer, and the layer is healing today. Keep that treaty in your pocket for any question sneering that cooperation is impossible.

Climate took longer. Rio in 1992 wrote the family constitution — everyone must act, but the countries that grew rich burning carbon must carry more of the load. Kyoto in 1997 tried strict homework for the rich only; the biggest polluter never signed and the fast-growing ones had no homework, so the curve barely moved. Paris in 2015 flipped the design: EVERY country writes its own promise, shows it publicly, and must tighten it over time. Everyone is in; nobody is forced — which is exactly its strength and its weakness, and saying both sides is the evaluation answer.

Since then the yearly climate meetings — the COPs — have added three things worth naming: a promise to phase down coal, a fund to compensate countries already suffering loss and damage they did not cause, and a first global stocktake telling the world to move away from fossil fuels.

And South Africa? We are the test case the world watches. A coal country, promised 8,5 billion dollars by richer nations to change its energy system — on one condition baked into the name: the transition must be JUST. Coal workers retrained, coal towns given new industries, the lights kept on while the grid greens. Sustainability's full definition come home: meet today's needs — jobs, power, growth — without spending the inheritance of the generation writing exams after you.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: Montreal's lesson, Kyoto against Paris in one sentence each, the newest COP additions, and the just transition explained to a coal town. Hold the river and its bill — this topic closes the syllabus by pricing what the whole course called scarce.
