# Part 1 — Expert

Most exports leave the country in a container. Tourism reverses the arrow: the customer travels TO the product, spends foreign money inside our borders, and leaves the product standing for the next customer. That reversal is why economists treat tourism as an export industry — an invisible export earning visible rands — and why a country with mass unemployment studies it as seriously as mining or manufacturing. This session covers the syllabus's full circuit: what tourism is, why it has grown worldwide, what it does to GDP, jobs, households and the state, South Africa's own profile including indigenous knowledge systems, and the policies that could grow it further.

## Subtopic: What Counts as Tourism

Definitions first, because the paper tests them precisely. A TOURIST, on the UN World Tourism Organisation's definition, is a person who travels outside his or her usual environment for less than a year, for leisure, business or other purposes not connected with earning money at the destination. Each clause works: OUTSIDE the usual environment excludes commuting and everyday shopping; LESS THAN A YEAR excludes migration; NOT REMUNERATED at the destination excludes work — a consultant flying in to be paid in Johannesburg is a business traveller counted separately from someone paid FROM home attending a conference. A visitor who stays at least one night is a tourist proper; one who arrives and leaves the same day is a DAY VISITOR or excursionist — counted, but spending far less.

Then the three directions of flow. DOMESTIC tourism: residents travelling inside their own country — the Johannesburg family driving to Margate in December. INBOUND tourism: foreigners arriving here — the German couple at the Kruger gate, and every rand they spend is an EXPORT earning foreign exchange. OUTBOUND tourism: South Africans travelling abroad — an import, leaking spending out. A country's tourism balance nets the two, and South Africa runs a healthy surplus: more is earned from arrivals than residents spend abroad.

Categories of purpose complete the definitions: leisure and holiday, business tourism including conferences and exhibitions — the MICE market of meetings, incentives, conferences and events — visiting friends and relatives, the VFR category that dominates domestic travel, plus medical, sports, religious, educational and cultural tourism. Each category has different spending patterns, seasons and infrastructure needs, which is why policy targets them separately.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: every clause of the tourist definition, day visitors against tourists, the three flows with their export-import logic, and the purpose categories.

## Subtopic: Why Tourism Has Grown

World tourism grew from a few million international trips a year in 1950 to well over a billion before the pandemic — one of the great expansions in economic history — and the syllabus wants the reasons sorted.

On the DEMAND side: rising real incomes worldwide, and especially the growth of middle classes in Asia, turned travel from luxury to expectation. Paid leave and pensions created the time. Longer, healthier lives created retired travellers with decades to fill. Smaller families and urban lifestyles raised the appetite for escape.

On the SUPPLY side: transport got cheap and fast — the jet aircraft, then low-cost carriers, collapsed the price of distance; a flight that once cost a year's salary now costs a week's. Communication collapsed the cost of knowing and booking: television and film sold destinations, then the internet and the smartphone let a traveller research, compare, book and review without an agent. Package holidays industrialised the product.

Then the enablers: peace and openness. Tourism follows safety — borders that open, visas that ease, currencies that convert. The end of the Cold War opened half a continent; and South Africa's own inflection point was 1994, when the end of apartheid and of sanctions turned an isolated pariah into one of the world's fastest-growing destinations within a decade. Nothing in the topic illustrates the politics-tourism link better, and examiners reach for it.

Note the vulnerability that is the mirror of the growth story: tourism is a luxury spend, first cut in hard times, and it collapses under shocks — the 2020 pandemic shut world tourism almost to zero in a single quarter, erasing millions of livelihoods and, in South Africa, silencing an industry that had carried around one job in every twenty. Fast growth, fragile floor: hold both.

## Subtopic: What Tourism Does to an Economy

Now the effects, organised exactly as the syllabus marks them: GDP, employment, externalities, and the benefit flowing to households, businesses, infrastructure and the state.

GDP: tourist spending on accommodation, transport, food, guiding and curios is final demand, feeding directly into production. Before the pandemic, tourism contributed around 3 percent of South African GDP directly and closer to 8 or 9 percent once indirect effects are counted. Why the difference? The MULTIPLIER: the hotel buys vegetables, laundry and security; the lodge worker spends wages at the spaza; each round of re-spending adds income — the same chain built in the circular-flow lesson, and tourism's chain is long because its inputs are heavily local.

EMPLOYMENT: tourism is LABOUR-INTENSIVE — beds must be made, food cooked, guests guided by people, not machines — so each million rand of tourism demand creates more jobs than a million rand of mining or banking. Better still for South Africa's unemployment profile, it absorbs semi-skilled workers, employs many women and young people, and reaches rural corners where no factory will ever locate: a game lodge is an export industry in a village.

HOUSEHOLDS earn wages and small-business income; BUSINESSES from airlines to bed-and-breakfasts earn revenue; INFRASTRUCTURE built for tourists — airports, roads, communications — serves residents after the tourists leave; and the STATE collects VAT on every bed-night, taxes on every business, and tourism levies, funding services far from any game reserve.

Now the honest ledger of costs, because evaluation questions live here. Externalities: crowding and congestion in season; pressure on water and land; litter and damage in fragile environments; the danger of CULTURAL commodification — traditions performed as products until they hollow out. LEAKAGES: where hotels are foreign-owned, food imported and packages sold abroad, much of each tourist rand exits before multiplying — the standard criticism of enclave resorts. And seasonality makes tourism jobs insecure: the coast hires in December and retrenches in February. Strong answers weigh both columns.

## Subtopic: South Africa's Profile, IKS and Policy

What does South Africa actually sell? SCENERY AND WILDLIFE: the Kruger Park and the private reserves, the Garden Route, the Drakensberg, Table Mountain — nature is the anchor product, and the Big Five its global brand. HERITAGE AND CULTURE: ten UNESCO World Heritage Sites, including Robben Island, the Cradle of Humankind, iSimangaliso Wetland Park and uKhahlamba-Drakensberg; township tourism; battlefield routes; the winelands. EVENTS AND BUSINESS: Cape Town and Durban conference centres, sports tours, festivals. CLIMATE: sunshine sold to northern winters.

The syllabus flags INDIGENOUS KNOWLEDGE SYSTEMS — IKS — as a tourism asset in their own right: the accumulated knowledge, practices and expressions of indigenous communities. Traditional medicine and healing, San tracking skills and rock art interpretation, storytelling, craft-making from beadwork to pottery, indigenous cuisine and traditional dance turn culture into livelihood — Zulu cultural villages, Khoi and San heritage routes, craft markets feeding rural incomes. Two policy duties attach: AUTHENTICITY, keeping the culture owned and told by its own communities rather than staged around them, and BENEFIT-SHARING, ensuring the community, not only the tour operator, earns from its knowledge.

Policy architecture: the 1996 White Paper on the Development and Promotion of Tourism set the framework — government leads, the private sector delivers, communities must benefit. The Tourism Act of 2014 governs the sector; SOUTH AFRICAN TOURISM markets the country abroad; the TOMSA levy of one percent on tourism services funds that marketing; grading councils keep quality; and the National Development Plan names tourism a priority growth sector.

Policy SUGGESTIONS — the improvement question the paper loves: ease visas, especially e-visas for growth markets like China and India; improve safety and the visible policing of tourist areas, since crime perception is the industry's biggest brake; invest in transport links — more direct flights, better rural roads; train guides, chefs and hospitality workers; support township, rural and IKS-based products so benefits spread; market beyond the Cape-Kruger corridor; and keep load-shedding and water failures from undoing the brand. Each suggestion earns marks when tied to a named obstacle.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the four product pillars, IKS with its two policy duties, the White Paper's division of roles, and matched obstacle-suggestion pairs.

## Subtopic: The Export That Walks In

Think about how South Africa usually earns foreign money: dig platinum, load it on a ship, and watch it leave for good. Now watch the other way. A German couple lands at O.R. Tambo with euros in their pocket. They sleep in Soweto, ride to Kruger, buy beadwork in Mpumalanga, tip a guide, drink wine in Stellenbosch — and fly home. The platinum left forever. The Kruger is still here, ready to be sold again tomorrow.

That is why tourism is called an invisible export: the buyer travels to the product, the foreign money stays, and the product never leaves. Every foreign visitor is a walking container of foreign exchange.

Keep the directions straight with one supper-table picture. Your aunt from Soshanguve holidaying in Durban: domestic tourism — rands moving inside the family of the nation, supporting jobs but bringing no new money in. The German couple: inbound — new money in, an export. Your cousin flying to Dubai: outbound — our money out, an import. A country wants the inbound column tall and proud.

And check what the couple's money touched: a hotel, a taxi, a park, a craft stall, a restaurant, a guide. Six industries fed by one holiday. No container export does that on its way to the harbour.

## Subtopic: One Busload, Many Pay Packets

Now park one safari bus outside a village lodge and count what happens, because this is the jobs argument in miniature.

The lodge itself employs the receptionist, the cleaners, the cooks, the guides, the trackers, the maintenance man. That is the DIRECT ring. But the kitchen buys vegetables from local farmers, the laundry is washed in the village, a security company watches the gate, a builder fixes the boma — the INDIRECT ring, jobs in firms that sell to the lodge. Then every worker in both rings spends wages at the spaza, the taxi, the tavern, the salon — the INDUCED ring, the multiplier from the circular-flow lesson wearing khaki. One busload of guests, three rings of pay packets.

Notice three things about those jobs. They are made of hands and hospitality — beds, meals, stories — so they hire PEOPLE, in numbers, not machines; a rand of tourism buys more employment than a rand of mining. They hire where nothing else does: no factory is coming to a village beside a game reserve, but the reserve itself is there, unmovable, an export industry with a village attached. And many are first jobs for women and young people — the very groups our unemployment hits hardest.

Now the honest whisper: watch where the rand leaks. If the lodge is foreign-owned, the food imported, the package paid in London — the bus is full but the village stays poor; the rand multiplied somewhere else. That is why policy shouts LOCAL: local ownership, local supply, community shares in lodges — the difference between tourism that visits a village and tourism that feeds one.

## Subtopic: Selling What Only We Have

Last piece: what does the visitor actually come for — and what should policy fix first?

The world has beaches and hotels everywhere. The visitor flies eleven hours because of what exists only here: the Big Five in the wild, Table Mountain, the Drakensberg, Robben Island's cell, the Cradle of Humankind — and the living cultures. That last one is the syllabus's special guest: indigenous knowledge. A San tracker reading the sand, a healer explaining the plants, a gogo telling the story her gogo told her, beadwork whose patterns mean something — that knowledge is a product no other country can copy, and it earns in villages the Kruger's gate fees never reach. Two rules keep it right: the community must own the telling — culture performed WITH people, not ABOUT them — and the community must bank the earnings, not just wave at the bus.

So what stops more of the world from coming? Ask the obstacles like a businessperson and match each with its fix. Visas take too long — so introduce e-visas for the giant new markets, China and India. Visitors fear crime — so police the tourist routes visibly and fix the airport-to-hotel journey where first impressions are made. Flights are few and expensive — so land more direct routes. The lights go out — and a load-shedding video travels the world faster than any advert; keeping the power on IS tourism marketing. Skills are short — so train the chefs, guides and managers the growth would need. And spread the map: the Cape and the Kruger are full stories; the Wild Coast, the Karoo, the battlefields wait for their turn.

Close with the sentence that frames every answer on this topic: tourism is the export that walks in, multiplies through three rings of pay packets, sells what only we have — and grows exactly as fast as we make the visit easy, safe and shared.
