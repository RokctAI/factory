**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First one: if tourism is such a jobs machine, why doesn't government just pour everything into tourism and stop worrying about factories? It sounds easier than building an SEZ.

**Tutor:** Because a wise portfolio never bets the house on its most fragile asset, and tourism — for all its virtues — is the economy's most weather-sensitive crop. Run the comparison properly. Tourism's strengths are real: labour-intensity, so each rand of demand hires more hands than mining or banking; rural reach, because the Drakensberg cannot be relocated to Sandton; low import content, so the multiplier chain runs long; and foreign exchange without depleting anything — the Kruger is still there tomorrow. But now the weaknesses. Demand is a luxury spend, first thing cut in a global downturn — and 2020 showed the extreme case, an industry switched off in a quarter, which no factory sector experienced. It is seasonal, so incomes are insecure. It is perception-fragile: one crime headline or load-shedding video moves bookings in ways no manufacturing order book suffers. And its jobs, while plentiful, are on average lower-paid and lower-skilled than industrial jobs, so it complements rather than replaces industrialisation as a route to higher productivity. The mature answer, and the one the syllabus itself takes by treating both topics in the same term: tourism is a priority sector inside a DIVERSIFIED strategy — push it hard, precisely because it hires where factories cannot, but never instead of the factories, because a country of waiters with no workshops has traded one vulnerability for another.

**Mandy:** Next: the export idea still feels upside down. The tourist takes the holiday home in their memory, nothing leaves the country — so what exactly did we export?

**Tutor:** You export the SERVICE, and the test for an export has nothing to do with objects crossing borders — it is about who pays whom across borders. Define an export as production at home paid for by foreign money. When Toyota ships bakkies to Belgium, South African production is paid in foreign currency: export. When the German couple sleeps at a lodge, eats at a restaurant and hires a guide, South African production — accommodation, meals, guiding, all produced here, by South African workers — is paid in euros the couple brought in: the same transaction economically, with the geography reversed. The customer moved instead of the goods. That is why statisticians record tourism receipts in the balance of payments as a services export, sitting in the current account beside transport and insurance. And the reversal carries a genuine advantage worth stating in essays: an ordinary export leaves the country and is consumed once; the tourism product is not consumed away — the mountain, the park, the museum remain, saleable again the next morning, which makes tourism closer to renting out a national asset than selling one. The caveat rides along: what CAN be depleted is the asset's quality — an overcrowded, littered, over-built destination is a rented asset being run down — which is why sustainability and tourism policy always travel together, and why next week's environmental topic is this topic's other half.

**Mandy:** This one caused real debate: cultural villages where tourists watch dancing and buy beadwork — one group said it preserves culture and pays for it, the other said it turns people into a zoo. Who is right for exam purposes?

**Tutor:** For exam purposes, both — and the marks live in the criteria you use to tell good practice from bad, not in picking a side blindly. The case for is solid economics and solid culture: performance and craft create income from knowledge that costs nothing to have and everything to lose; paying audiences give the young a reason to learn traditions that unpaid ones abandon; and craft markets and heritage routes carry money into rural households that no other industry visits. The case against is the commodification danger: when the tour operator scripts the culture, schedules it for the bus, edits it for foreign comfort and keeps the margin, the community becomes scenery in its own story — performed ABOUT, not WITH, and paid in crumbs. So the examiner's distinction is governance, and you can write it as three tests. Ownership: does the community, or an outside operator, control the enterprise and its content? Authenticity: is what is shown decided by the culture's own bearers — including the right to keep parts private — or staged to stereotype? Benefit-sharing: do the earnings reach the households whose knowledge is the product — through wages, shares, levies — or leak to a head office? Apply the tests and the same activity scores differently at different sites, which is exactly the nuanced answer a discussion question wants. Close with the syllabus link: indigenous knowledge systems are named in policy precisely because they pass the only-here test — nobody else can sell San tracking or that grandmother's story — and product that cannot be copied is the strongest product a destination owns.

**Mandy:** Last one: for the policy-suggestion question, how many suggestions do we need, and how do we stop them sounding like a wish list — more flights, less crime, please and thank you?

**Tutor:** The difference between a wish list and a policy answer is that a policy answer earns each suggestion with a mechanism — obstacle, action, and the channel through which the action grows tourism. Build each one as a three-link sentence. Take visas: the obstacle is that long paper processes deter exactly the growth markets with the newest middle classes; the action is e-visas and visa waivers for China and India; the channel is that arrivals from those markets respond strongly to friction, as our own waiver experiments showed. Safety: the obstacle is perception as much as incident — surveys of potential visitors rank crime fear first; the action is visible policing on defined tourist corridors, airport-to-hotel; the channel is that perception risk prices itself into bookings, so visible reassurance converts directly. Access: few direct flights raise the total price of the trip; land new routes and the whole destination gets cheaper without a rand of subsidy to hotels. Reliability: a load-shedding or water-failure story damages the brand internationally; keeping infrastructure working is destination marketing by other means. Spread: the Cape-Kruger corridor is maturing, so the growth frontier is township, rural and IKS-based product — which also fixes the benefit-sharing problem in the same move. Four or five of those, each with its three links, plus one honest trade-off acknowledged — safety spending has opportunity costs; visa openness needs border capacity — and you have written a policy paragraph, not a letter to Santa. Structure earns the marks; wishing never does.
