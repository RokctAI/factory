**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First: the two unemployment rates confuse everyone. If the expanded rate is the more honest number — it counts people who gave up — why does the news, and the exam, usually lead with the strict rate?

**Tutor:** Both numbers are honest; they answer different questions, and the choice between them is about comparability, not concealment. The strict rate follows the international definition — no work, available for work, and actively searching in the reference period — precisely so that South Africa's number can be compared with Brazil's or Nigeria's, and with its own past, on identical terms. Change the definition and you lose the comparison. The expanded rate adds discouraged work-seekers: people who want work and are available but have stopped searching, usually because there are no jobs where they live or transport to look costs money they do not have. That gap between the two rates — often eight to ten percentage points here — is enormous by world standards, and that is the analytical gold in it: in most countries the two rates sit close together, because someone who wants work keeps looking; a huge gap is the statistical signature of hopelessness, of rural areas and townships where searching itself has stopped paying. So use them as a pair. Quote the strict rate when comparing internationally or tracking the cycle; quote the expanded rate when describing the true scale of the social problem; and quote the GAP when you want to impress an examiner, because the distance between the definitions is itself an indicator.

**Mandy:** Next: how can the HDI and GDP per capita disagree? Surely the richer country just wins on everything.

**Tutor:** Money buys development, but it does not buy it automatically, and the disagreements are where the index earns its keep. The HDI averages three things — income, life expectancy and education — so a country converts money into a high score only if the money actually reaches clinics and classrooms. The classic contrasts make the point. Some oil-rich states carry enormous incomes per head but middling HDI ranks, because wealth concentrated at the top coexists with mediocre schooling and health for the rest. Meanwhile a famously instructive case like Cuba, or the Indian state of Kerala, achieves rich-world life expectancy and literacy on a poor-world income, by spending what little there is on primary health and universal schooling. South Africa is the textbook disappointment in the other direction: an upper-middle-income economy whose HDI rank sits well below its income rank, dragged down historically by the AIDS collapse in life expectancy and persistently by unequal education quality. And that is the deeper lesson for your essays: GDP per capita is an average, and averages hide distribution. A country with our Gini coefficient can have a comfortable mean and a miserable median. The HDI narrows that blindness without curing it — which is why the full dashboard, not any single gauge, is the syllabus's real answer.

**Mandy:** This one caused an argument: if PPP shows that poor countries are richer than the exchange rate suggests, does that mean world poverty is exaggerated and the statistics have been scaring us unnecessarily?

**Tutor:** Careful — PPP corrects a measurement distortion; it does not feed anyone. Walk the logic slowly. At market exchange rates, a Malawian farmer's annual income converts to a few hundred dollars, which sounds like instant starvation. PPP points out that her food, housing and services are priced locally, not at New York prices, so her real consumption is higher than the raw conversion implies — perhaps two or three times higher. That is a genuine correction, and every serious poverty line, including the World Bank's international line of a few dollars a day, is already expressed in PPP terms precisely for this reason. So the scary statistics you have seen are the corrected ones: hundreds of millions of people live below poverty lines that already account for cheap local prices. There are two further honest wrinkles. First, PPP baskets are calculated from periodic international price surveys, and they are imperfect — poor households consume different baskets from the average, and some economists argue the corrections still flatter or understate particular regions. Second, PPP applies to what money buys locally, but some essentials — imported medicines, fuel, machinery, smartphones — cost world prices everywhere, and for those a poor country's low income buys exactly as little as the exchange rate says. So the exam-safe formulation is: PPP comparisons are more accurate for living standards, market-rate comparisons remain relevant for international purchasing power, and world poverty measured properly, in PPP, remains vast. The correction changes the ruler, not the reality.

**Mandy:** Last one: the IMF question. We borrowed billions from the IMF during the pandemic — was that a rescue or a trap? People online say very different things.

**Tutor:** Both views have a pedigree, so let the facts pick the lane. The 2020 loan was about 4,3 billion dollars from the IMF's Rapid Financing Instrument — an emergency pandemic facility, and here is the detail that decides the argument: it carried almost none of the classic conditionality. No structural adjustment programme, no imposed austerity package; the government set out its own intentions in a letter, and the interest rate was far below what markets were charging South Africa at the time. On those facts, calling it a trap is hard — it was cheap money in an emergency, and it has been serviced without drama. The genuine cautionary tales live elsewhere: countries that borrowed repeatedly under full conditionality — think of several Latin American and African programmes in the 1980s and 1990s, or Greece after 2010 — where the price of rescue was deep spending cuts, privatisations and social pain, and where economists still argue whether the medicine cured or prolonged the disease. That is the balanced essay: the IMF as lender of last resort genuinely prevents defaults and currency collapses, which hurt the poor first and worst; conditionality can force overdue repairs but has historically also imposed one-size-fits-all austerity at the bottom of a slump, when cutting deepens the hole. Then the punchline your marker will remember: the surest way to keep the paramedic's conditions off your country is not to need the ambulance — sound public finances are sovereignty. A once-off, low-condition emergency loan is not a trap; a habit of them becomes one.
