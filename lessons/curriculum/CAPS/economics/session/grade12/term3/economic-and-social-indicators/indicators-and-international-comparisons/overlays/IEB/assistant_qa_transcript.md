**Mandy:** Question time before we pack up! Here's the first: if leading indicators can see the future, why does anyone bother with the lagging ones? Isn't unemployment data basically useless by the time it arrives?

**Tutor:** Useless for prediction, indispensable for confirmation — and economies need both. Leading indicators are early but noisy: building plans get cancelled, job adverts get pulled, share prices jump at rumours. If the Reserve Bank changed policy every time a leading series twitched, monetary policy would zigzag like a startled springhare. Lagging indicators are slow but solid: when unemployment finally falls, you know — not suspect, know — that the recovery was real, because employers only rehire once they are convinced. Think of a doctor's practice: symptoms are leading, the blood test is lagging, and the diagnosis needs both. There is also a deeply human use for the lagging series. Unemployment is not just a cycle-confirmation device; it measures households in distress, and policy aimed at those households needs the accurate late number, not the hopeful early one. So in a data question, match the tool to the task: forecasting leans on the headlights, policy verification leans on the mirror, and current management reads the speedometer. An answer that assigns each type its correct JOB, rather than ranking them best to worst, is the answer that collects full marks.

**Mandy:** Next one, and half the class argued about it at break: South Africa's GDP grows some years, but people say life isn't getting better. Which indicator settles who's right?

**Tutor:** Two subtractions and one division settle it. Start with GDP per capita: if output grows nought comma nine percent while population grows one comma five, output per person is shrinking — the economy expanded and the average citizen got poorer, both true at once. That single arithmetic step reconciles the newspaper headline with the household experience more often than any other in this course. Then convert nominal to real: growth measured in rands includes inflation's hot air, and only the deflated number tells you whether production actually rose. Finally, check the sharing: a rising average can coexist with most people standing still if the gains pool at the top, and South Africa's Gini around nought comma six three warns you that our averages hide more than most countries' averages do. Then look sideways at the social panel — life expectancy, infant mortality, service delivery — because those tell you whether growth is reaching bodies and homes rather than just spreadsheets. The polished exam sentence: GDP measures the size of the economy, not the distribution or the experience of it, so judgement requires per-capita, real, and social indicators read together. Anyone who answers a wellbeing question with GDP alone has brought one instrument to a job that needs the panel.

**Mandy:** Third question: purchasing power parity sounds like a trick to make poor countries look richer. Who decides what money 'really' buys, and can we trust it?

**Tutor:** Healthy suspicion — aim it at the method and the method survives. PPP is built from a giant price-collection exercise: the International Comparison Program, coordinated through the World Bank, prices an enormous common basket — food, rent, transport, services — in every participating country. From those prices it computes how many rands buy the same basket as, say, one dollar buys in the United States. That ratio, not the market exchange rate, then converts incomes. Why is this MORE honest rather than less? Because the market exchange rate is set by trade and capital flows — by demand for a currency to buy exports and bonds — not by the price of a haircut in Polokwane. Most of what people consume is local and never crosses a border, so the exchange rate simply carries no information about it. The caveats are real: baskets differ across cultures, quality is hard to match, and PPP figures are revised when new price rounds arrive. So treat PPP as the better lens with known scratches. In an answer: market-rate conversion for questions about international purchasing power — repaying foreign debt, importing machinery — and PPP conversion for questions about how people live. Choosing the right conversion for the question asked is exactly the skill being tested.

**Mandy:** Last one: my cousin says the IMF loan in 2020 means South Africa was bankrupt. True or false?

**Tutor:** False — but instructively false, so let us take it apart. Bankruptcy for a country would mean defaulting on its debts; South Africa did not default. What happened in 2020 is that the pandemic blew a sudden hole in public finances — tax revenue collapsed while health and relief spending surged — and the IMF had opened a special low-conditionality emergency facility for exactly that shock. South Africa drew about four comma three billion dollars from it, at an interest rate far below what markets were charging at the time. That was a financing choice, not a rescue from collapse: the same money could have been borrowed on bond markets at greater cost. Why did it make headlines anyway? Because of what IMF borrowing usually signals. The Fund's classic loans come with conditionality — enforced austerity and reform — and countries that reach that point have typically lost market access altogether. South Africa's emergency loan carried no such programme, which is precisely why calling it bankruptcy overstates it. The exam-ready formulation: distinguish the IMF's classic role — crisis lender with conditions, for countries in balance-of-payments distress — from emergency facilities used by members still able to borrow commercially. And keep your cousin's instinct: a country that borrows from the paramedic, even cheaply, is telling you its public finances are under strain worth watching.
