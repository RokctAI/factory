# Part 1 — Expert

A doctor never judges a patient by temperature alone — pulse, blood pressure and bloodwork each tell a different part of the truth. An economy is judged the same way, through INDICATORS: regularly published statistics that reveal how production, prices, money, labour and living standards are moving. This session builds the full dashboard the syllabus requires — economic indicators, social indicators, and the discipline of comparing countries without fooling yourself — plus the two global institutions, the IMF and the World Bank, that read these dashboards for the whole world.

## Subtopic: Reading the Dashboard: Indicator Types and Timing

An ECONOMIC INDICATOR is a statistic that signals the performance of the economy — compiled by Statistics South Africa, the Reserve Bank and the treasury on a fixed calendar, so that policy, business and unions all argue from the same numbers.

The first classification is TIMING, because indicators do not all speak about the same moment. LEADING indicators change BEFORE the economy changes — new building plans passed, job advertisements, new vehicle sales, share prices, orders placed with factories. They are the headlights, and the Reserve Bank publishes a composite leading indicator watched precisely because it turns months before the business cycle does. COINCIDENT indicators move WITH the cycle — industrial production, retail sales, real GDP itself. They are the speedometer, reporting where the economy is right now. LAGGING indicators confirm a turn only AFTER it has happened — unemployment, which falls late in a recovery because firms rehire cautiously; inventories; the number of insolvencies. They are the rear-view mirror, useless for prediction but essential for confirming that a turning point was real.

Second classification: LEVEL against RATE. The level of GDP tells you the economy's size; the growth RATE tells you its direction. An exam data-response gives you both, and the skill is refusing to confuse them — an economy can be large and shrinking, or small and booming.

Third: NOMINAL against REAL. Any indicator measured in rands is inflated by rising prices, so serious comparison across time deflates to real terms. Real GDP growth is nominal growth stripped of inflation — and the single most repeated correction in this topic: if nominal GDP grew 8 percent while inflation was 6, the economy really grew only about 2.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: leading, coincident and lagging with correct examples, level against rate, and nominal against real.

## Subtopic: The Economic Indicators

Now the dashboard itself, gauge by gauge, as the syllabus lists it.

PRODUCTION: real GDP growth is the headline gauge — the annual percentage change in the volume of final goods and services produced. Pair it with GDP per capita for living standards: if GDP grows 1 percent while population grows 1,5, output per person is falling even as the economy grows. That single subtraction explains a decade of South African frustration.

PRICES: the CPI for consumers, the PPI at the factory gate — covered in depth in their own lesson, but on this dashboard remember their role: inflation is the gauge the Reserve Bank is legally tasked to watch, with its 3-to-6-percent target band.

MONEY AND CREDIT: the MONEY SUPPLY aggregates M1, M2 and M3. M1 is the most liquid money — coins, notes and demand deposits. M2 adds short and medium-term deposits; M3, the broadest measure, adds long-term deposits and is the aggregate the Bank tracks. Beside it sits credit extension to the private sector — fast money-supply and credit growth warns of future inflation, which is why these are monitored even though South Africa no longer sets formal money-supply targets.

INTEREST RATES: the repo rate is the policy lever; prime is what banks charge their best customers. Watch the REAL interest rate — nominal minus inflation — because that is what savers earn and borrowers pay in truth.

THE EXTERNAL GAUGES: the exchange rate of the rand, the balance on the current account, and the TERMS OF TRADE — export prices divided by import prices, as an index. Terms of trade improve when the prices of what we sell, platinum, gold, coal, rise against the prices of what we buy, oil and machinery; an improvement is a national raise, earned without producing one extra ton.

LABOUR: the unemployment rate — the unemployed as a percentage of the economically active population. Know the two definitions: the STRICT rate counts only those actively searching; the EXPANDED rate adds discouraged work-seekers who have given up. The gap between them, several percentage points wide, is itself an indicator — of hopelessness. Employment numbers from the Quarterly Labour Force Survey complete the gauge.

PRODUCTIVITY: output per worker per hour. Rising productivity is the only source of rising wages that does not feed inflation — remuneration outrunning productivity is a cost-push engine.

## Subtopic: The Social Indicators

Economic gauges measure the machine; SOCIAL indicators measure the passengers — whether growth is reaching human lives. The syllabus groups them.

DEMOGRAPHICS: population growth and LIFE EXPECTANCY at birth. Life expectancy is the great summary statistic of development — it compresses nutrition, healthcare, sanitation and safety into one number. South Africa's dipped catastrophically during the untreated AIDS years, then recovered by more than a decade of years as antiretroviral treatment rolled out — proof that policy shows up in this gauge within a generation.

NUTRITION AND HEALTH: the INFANT MORTALITY RATE — deaths before age one per thousand live births — and under-five mortality, the most sensitive indicators of poverty because infants die first when water, food and clinics fail. Add child stunting, HIV prevalence, and access measures such as doctors and nurses per thousand people.

EDUCATION: literacy rates, school enrolment, completion rates and — the modern refinement — learning outcomes, because enrolment without learning flatters the gauge. Education is both a social indicator and the economy's future supply of skills; it appears on both sides of the dashboard.

SERVICES AND HOUSING: the percentage of households with piped water, sanitation, electricity and formal housing. These are the indicators where post-1994 South Africa shows its clearest gains — electrification climbed from around two-thirds of households to about ninety percent — and where every service-delivery protest is a data point about the remainder.

URBANISATION: the share of the population living in cities — a development indicator because opportunity concentrates in towns, and a warning indicator when cities grow faster than their services.

Two composite measures pull the threads together. The HUMAN DEVELOPMENT INDEX, from the UN Development Programme, blends life expectancy, education and income per person into a single score between 0 and 1 — designed to dethrone GDP as the sole measure of progress. And the GINI COEFFICIENT measures inequality, from 0, everyone equal, to 1, one person owns everything. South Africa's Gini, around 0,63 to 0,65, is among the highest ever recorded — the number behind the sentence, repeated in every essay, that South Africa is one of the most unequal societies on earth.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — life expectancy and infant mortality as summary gauges, the services indicators, the HDI's three ingredients, and the Gini read correctly.

## Subtopic: Comparing Countries: PPP, Pitfalls and the Global Institutions

International comparison is where indicator work gets technical, and the syllabus flags three corrections.

Correction one: PER CAPITA. China's GDP dwarfs Denmark's; Danes are far richer. Always divide by population before comparing living standards.

Correction two: PURCHASING POWER PARITY. Converting incomes at market exchange rates understates poor countries, because a rand buys far more haircuts, taxi rides and vegetables in Polokwane than its dollar equivalent buys in New York. PPP conversion revalues each country's income by what money actually buys locally — and it can double or triple a developing country's measured income. Any honest cross-country table says whether it is market-rate or PPP.

Correction three: COVERAGE. Indicators miss the informal sector, unpaid household work and subsistence farming — all proportionally larger in poorer countries — so measured gaps overstate true gaps. Definitions also differ: comparing unemployment across countries means checking whose definition is stricter.

Now the two institutions the syllabus attaches to globalisation, both born at Bretton Woods in 1944. The INTERNATIONAL MONETARY FUND is the world's financial paramedic: it monitors members' economies through surveillance, and lends to countries in balance-of-payments crises — when a country cannot pay for its imports or service its foreign debt. Its loans carry CONDITIONALITY: policy changes, often austerity, demanded in exchange, which is why IMF rescues are both lifelines and lightning rods. The WORLD BANK is the development financier: long-term loans and expertise for infrastructure, education, health and reform in developing countries, and the publisher of much of the comparative data this lesson runs on. The one-line separation examiners want: the IMF handles short-term crisis finance and stability; the World Bank funds long-term development projects. South Africa belongs to both, and in 2020 borrowed over four billion dollars from the IMF's emergency pandemic facility — a rare, and debated, use of that lifeline.

The final questions of this part are with you now: per capita and PPP corrections, the informal-sector blind spot, and the IMF cleanly separated from the World Bank.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the whole dashboard again, from the driver's seat of one taxi on one road — because every indicator is just a way of asking how the trip is going.

## Subtopic: Headlights, Speedometer, Mirror

Picture the economy as a taxi on the N1 at night. The driver has three kinds of information, and so does the country.

The HEADLIGHTS show what is coming. For the economy: plans approved for new buildings, job adverts in the paper, orders placed at factories, new taxis bought. Nobody passes a building plan for fun — someone intends to build next year. So when these lights brighten, good times are probably ahead; when they dim, brake early. That is a LEADING indicator.

The SPEEDOMETER shows right now. Retail tills ringing, factories producing, GDP itself — the speed the economy is actually doing this quarter. COINCIDENT indicators.

The REAR-VIEW MIRROR shows where you have been. Unemployment is the classic one, and it surprises people that jobs are a mirror, not a headlight — but think like a boss. When business turns bad, you delay retrenching, hoping it passes; when business turns good, you delay hiring, until you are sure. So jobs move LAST. By the time unemployment falls, the recovery is old news. LAGGING indicators confirm; they never predict.

One more driving habit and this section is done. Never confuse the size of the taxi with its speed — a big economy can be slowing, a small one flying. And never trust a speed reading taken in rands without asking what inflation did: if takings grew 8 percent but prices rose 6, the real trip only got 2 percent longer. Rands stretch; kilometres do not.

## Subtopic: The Passengers' Report

Now step to the back of the taxi and ask the passengers how the trip is treating THEM. That is what social indicators do — because a rising GDP with sick, hungry passengers is a fast taxi going nowhere good.

Ask the oldest passenger: how long do people around here live? LIFE EXPECTANCY wraps everything — food, clinics, water, safety — into one number. South Africa watched it collapse when AIDS went untreated and climb back by more than ten years when treatment arrived. One number, an entire policy story.

Ask the young mother: do babies here see their first birthday? INFANT MORTALITY is the sharpest poverty gauge on earth, because when water is dirty and clinics are far, the smallest passengers pay first.

Ask the schoolchild: are you in school, and are you LEARNING? Enrolment without learning is a full classroom and an empty gauge.

Ask the household: does water come from a tap, does the toilet flush, do the lights work? Taps, toilets and electricity are development you can touch — and the gains since 1994, like electricity reaching about nine homes in ten, are as real as the protests over the homes still waiting.

Then two clever summary numbers. The HDI takes health, schooling and income, and squeezes them into one score out of 1 — so countries can be ranked by human lives, not just money. And the GINI asks the sharpest question of all: however much money the taxi carries, how is it SHARED among the passengers? Zero means shared equally; one means a single passenger holds everything. South Africa scores around 0,63 — nearly the top of the world's unfairness table. Two passengers in this taxi live in different countries that happen to share a border of skin and street.

## Subtopic: Comparing Taxis Honestly

Last skill: when two countries compare dashboards, three honest rules keep the comparison from lying.

Rule one: divide by the passengers. A taxi earning R10 000 with twenty aboard is poorer per person than one earning R6 000 with four. China's economy is a giant; a Dane is richer than a Chinese citizen many times over. PER CAPITA first, always.

Rule two: ask what money BUYS where it lives. A hundred rand converted to dollars looks tiny in New York, but in Polokwane it fills a plate, rides a taxi and cuts your hair. Comparing at raw exchange rates makes poor countries look poorer than they live. The fix is called purchasing power parity — repricing incomes by what they actually buy at home — and it can double a country's measured standing. Check which version any table uses before quoting it.

Rule three: remember what the meter never sees. The spaza with no till slip, the gogo minding four grandchildren, the vegetables grown behind the house — real production, invisible to official statistics, and a bigger share of life in poorer countries. Official gaps between rich and poor countries are real, but the meter exaggerates them.

And who runs the comparisons for the whole world? Two Bretton Woods institutions, and keep them apart cleanly. The IMF is the paramedic: when a country cannot pay its foreign bills, the Fund lends — with strict conditions attached, which saves the patient and starts the argument. The World Bank is the builder: long, patient loans for dams, schools, clinics and reforms, plus the statistics this whole lesson stands on. Paramedic for crises, builder for development — South Africa is a member of both, and called the paramedic once, in the pandemic, for over four billion dollars.
