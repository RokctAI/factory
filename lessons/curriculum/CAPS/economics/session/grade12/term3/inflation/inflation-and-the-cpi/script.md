# Part 1 — Expert

Every month, on a Wednesday morning, Statistics South Africa releases one number — and that number moves the repo rate, wage negotiations, social grants, rent contracts and the price of bread. The number is the inflation rate, and it is manufactured, carefully and publicly, from an index called the CPI. This session takes the machine apart: what inflation is, how the CPI is built and calculated, why prices rise, who bleeds when they do, and what the Reserve Bank and Treasury can do about it. Nothing in the Economics paper is more certain to appear than this.

## Subtopic: Inflation Defined and the CPI Basket

INFLATION is a sustained and significant increase in the GENERAL price level over time — and every word in that definition is doing work, because examiners award the definition mark only when all three tests are present. SUSTAINED: a once-off jump, such as a VAT increase raising prices in a single month, is not inflation unless prices keep climbing. GENERAL: bread rising alone is a relative price change; inflation requires the average of prices across the economy to rise. OVER TIME: inflation is always measured against an earlier period, usually the same month a year before. The mirror concept is DEFLATION, a sustained fall in the general price level — rarer, and dangerous in its own way because households postpone buying and firms postpone producing. DISINFLATION means prices still rising but more slowly: inflation falling from seven percent to five percent is disinflation, not deflation.

Now the measuring instrument. The CONSUMER PRICE INDEX is a weighted index of the prices of a representative BASKET of goods and services bought by households. Statistics South Africa builds it in four moves. First, household surveys establish what South Africans actually buy — the basket, several hundred items from mielie meal to cellphone data to funeral policies. Second, each item receives a WEIGHT reflecting its share of household spending: housing and utilities carry roughly a quarter of the basket, food and non-alcoholic beverages under a fifth, transport about a seventh. Third, price collectors record tens of thousands of prices every month, in shops and online, across the country. Fourth, the weighted average of those prices is expressed as an index with a BASE YEAR set equal to 100.

Two siblings of the headline number matter. HEADLINE CPI is the all-items index for all urban areas — the number in the news. CORE inflation strips out food, fuel and energy, the most volatile items, to reveal the underlying trend that policy can actually influence. And alongside the CPI stands the PRODUCER PRICE INDEX, the PPI, which tracks prices at the factory gate rather than the till. The PPI measures the cost of production; the CPI measures the cost of living — and because factory-gate increases feed through to shelves months later, the PPI is watched as an early warning of where the CPI is heading.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: the three tests in the definition, deflation against disinflation, how the basket and weights are built, and CPI set against PPI.

## Subtopic: Calculating with the CPI

The examination asks for numbers, so drill the two core calculations until they are automatic.

Calculation one: constructing the index. In the base year a household's representative trolley costs R4 000, and the index is set at 100. Two years later the same trolley costs R4 480. The index now stands at 4 480 divided by 4 000, times 100 — that is 112,0. The index number is not a price and not a percentage; it is a ratio to the base year. An index of 112 says: what cost R100 in the base year costs R112 now.

Calculation two: the inflation rate, which is the PERCENTAGE CHANGE in the CPI between two dates. The formula: new index minus old index, divided by the old index, times 100. Suppose the CPI moves from 112,0 to 118,7 over a year. The difference is 6,7 points; divide by 112,0 and multiply by 100: 5,98 percent — call it 6,0 percent. State the formula, substitute, calculate, and give the answer with a percentage sign; each step carries a mark. Note the trap the markers love: the inflation rate divides by the OLD index, not the new one, and the change in INDEX POINTS is not the inflation rate — 6,7 points is not 6,7 percent.

Now the weighted version, because the CPI is a weighted average and a question can make you build one. A simplified basket has three items. Food carries a weight of 50 percent and food prices rose 10 percent. Transport carries 30 percent and rose 5 percent. School costs carry 20 percent and rose 20 percent. The weighted inflation rate is 0,5 times 10, plus 0,3 times 5, plus 0,2 times 20 — that is 5 plus 1,5 plus 4, giving 10,5 percent. Notice what weighting does: the item with the biggest price rise did not dominate, because its share of spending is small. This is also why different households live different inflation rates — a poor household spending half its income on food experiences food inflation far more heavily than the average basket admits.

Finally, run the formula backwards, the harder variant. If last year's CPI was 110,0 and inflation over the year was 6,0 percent, the new CPI is 110 times 1,06 — that is 116,6. And use the CPI to deflate money values: a wage that rose 4 percent in a year of 6 percent inflation FELL in real terms by roughly 2 percent. The rand figures grew; the trolley they can fill shrank.

Stop for this section's questions now — index construction, the inflation-rate formula forwards and backwards, the weighted basket, and nominal against real.

## Subtopic: Types and Causes of Inflation

Inflation comes in kinds, and the kind determines the cure — that link is the discussion question hiding in every inflation essay.

DEMAND-PULL inflation arises when total spending grows faster than the economy's capacity to produce: too much money chasing too few goods. Its engines are the components of aggregate demand. Consumers borrow cheaply and spend; government runs large deficits; investment booms; export earnings surge. Each pushes the aggregate demand curve rightward, and with supply unable to stretch, prices are bid up. The give-away symptoms: shortages, waiting lists, an economy running hot.

COST-PUSH inflation comes from the supply side: production costs rise and producers pass the increase on. The classic pushes — wage settlements above productivity growth, imported inputs made dearer by a weaker rand, oil and fuel price shocks, administered prices such as electricity tariffs climbing year after year, droughts raising food prices, and firms with market power widening profit margins. Cost-push is nastier because it arrives together with falling output: the economy gets the worst of both, rising prices and rising unemployment, the combination called STAGFLATION.

The distinction is examinable in graph form, in words. For demand-pull, describe the aggregate demand curve shifting right along a fixed aggregate supply curve: prices up, output up. For cost-push, the aggregate supply curve shifts left: prices up, output DOWN. Same symptom at the till, opposite movements underneath.

Two more terms complete the set. HYPERINFLATION is inflation out of control — prices rising so fast that money stops working, as Zimbabwe demonstrated in 2008 when prices doubled in days and citizens abandoned the currency. And low, stable inflation — creeping inflation of a few percent — is not the enemy; most central banks treat a small positive rate as consistent with a growing economy, which is precisely why South Africa's target is a band above zero rather than zero itself.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — demand-pull and cost-push with their causes sorted correctly, the two curve stories told in words, stagflation, and hyperinflation.

## Subtopic: Consequences and the Fight Against Inflation

Why fight inflation at all? Because it redistributes wealth arbitrarily, punishes the wrong people, and corrodes growth.

Start with the redistribution. Inflation transfers wealth from CREDITORS to DEBTORS: a loan repaid in next year's rands is repaid in shrunken rands, so lenders lose and borrowers quietly gain. It savages people on FIXED INCOMES — pensioners and grant recipients whose rands buy less every month unless payments are adjusted. Savers lose whenever the interest rate they earn is below the inflation rate, because the REAL interest rate — nominal rate minus inflation — is then negative. Wage earners with bargaining power defend themselves through increases; the unorganised poor cannot, which is why inflation is often called the cruellest tax.

Then the efficiency damage. Inflation creates uncertainty, and uncertainty postpones investment. It erodes export competitiveness when South African prices rise faster than trading partners' prices, worsening the trade balance. It pushes taxpayers into higher brackets without any rise in real income — bracket creep, a silent tax increase. And it feeds on itself through EXPECTATIONS: once workers and firms expect seven percent, they contract for seven percent, and expectation becomes reality. Breaking that spiral is the whole art of policy.

South Africa's answer since 2000 is INFLATION TARGETING. The Reserve Bank must keep CPI inflation inside a target band of 3 to 6 percent, and in recent years it has aimed explicitly at the middle of that band. The weapon is MONETARY POLICY: the repo rate, the rate at which the Bank lends to commercial banks. Raise the repo and every lending rate follows; borrowing slows, spending cools, demand-pull pressure eases. The Bank's independence matters here — a central bank that cannot be ordered to print money for the treasury is the taxpayer's protection against the hyperinflations of history. But note the tool's limit, a favourite evaluation point: the repo rate works on DEMAND. Against cost-push inflation — an oil shock, a drought, an electricity tariff — higher interest rates cannot lower the cost of production; they can only suppress demand around it, at the price of output and jobs. The Phillips curve captures the trade-off: in the short run, less inflation tends to cost more unemployment.

Fiscal and supply-side measures complete the toolkit. Government can restrain its own spending and borrowing, resist above-inflation increases in administered prices, and promote competition and productivity so that supply grows into demand. Against wage-price spirals, some countries have tried direct wage and price controls — and the syllabus expects you to know they usually fail, because controls suppress the symptom while shortages and black markets grow underneath.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the redistribution from creditors to savers to fixed incomes, bracket creep and expectations, the target band and the repo mechanism, and why monetary policy struggles against cost-push.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same machinery from the till slip in your hand — because the CPI is really just the country's grocery trolley, weighed and priced every month.

## Subtopic: The Trolley That Tells the Truth

Imagine one trolley that stands for every household in South Africa. Into it goes what people actually buy — mielie meal, taxi fare, electricity, airtime, rent, school shoes, a funeral policy. Statistics South Africa built that trolley by asking thousands of households what they spend money on, and the answer decides how much space each item takes. Housing takes the biggest corner of the trolley. Food takes a big corner too. Cinema tickets take a sliver.

Every month, price collectors walk the shops and price the SAME trolley again. In the starting year the trolley cost R4 000, and we call that 100 — not rands, just a marker, like calling the start of a race zero. Two years later the same trolley costs R4 480. Divide 4 480 by 4 000, times 100: the marker now reads 112. That marker is the CPI. It does not tell you what anything costs; it tells you how far prices have travelled since the start.

The inflation rate is simply how fast the marker moved this year. Marker last year: 112. Marker now: 118,7. It moved 6,7 points; against last year's 112 that is a 6 percent journey. That 6 percent is the number on the news — and now you know it is not guessed, not felt, not voted on. It is one trolley, priced twice, compared.

One more honest wrinkle. Your trolley is not the country's trolley. A family spending half its money on food feels food prices double-strength; a family with a car feels every petrol hike. So when someone says the official rate must be a lie because THEIR shopping got so expensive — both can be true. The official trolley is an average, and nobody is average.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the trolley are coming to you right now: how the basket is chosen, what the index marker means, and how the moving marker becomes the inflation rate.

## Subtopic: Two Fires, Two Hoses

Prices can be pushed up from two different sides, and telling the two apart is the skill the essay wants.

Fire number one starts at the till. Everyone has money and everyone is spending — bonuses, cheap loans, government projects, a good export year. The shops cannot restock fast enough, and when many hands reach for few goods, sellers raise prices because they can. That is DEMAND-PULL: buyers pulling prices up. You can spot it by the queues — things sell out, order books are full, the economy feels busy.

Fire number two starts at the back door of the factory. The rand weakens, so imported parts cost more. Fuel goes up, so every delivery costs more. Electricity tariffs jump. A drought makes maize scarce. The producer's costs have risen, so the price on the shelf rises to survive — even though customers are not spending more. That is COST-PUSH: costs pushing prices up from behind. You can spot it by the misery — prices rising while shops are QUIET, the double pain called stagflation.

Why does the difference matter? Because the country's main firehose — the Reserve Bank raising the repo rate — sprays only the FIRST fire. Higher interest rates make loans expensive, so people spend less, so the pulling stops. But point that hose at a cost fire and nothing good happens: petrol does not get cheaper because your bond repayment went up. The hose just soaks the shoppers while the kitchen keeps burning — spending falls, jobs go, and the cost pressure remains. That is the single most valuable evaluation sentence in this topic: the repo rate fights demand-pull well and cost-push badly.

Your questions for this part are up now: sorting causes into the right fire, the tell-tale signs of each, and why the interest-rate hose only reaches one of them.

## Subtopic: Who Wins and Who Loses When Prices Run

Inflation is not just a number; it quietly moves money from some pockets to others, and knowing whose is the last piece of the topic.

The losers first. Gogo with her pension: her income is fixed, prices are not, so every month her envelope buys a little less — unless the grant is raised to match inflation, which is exactly why grant increases are announced against the CPI. The saver: money in an account earning 5 percent while inflation runs 6 is money quietly evaporating — the real return is minus 1. The worker without a union: no bargaining power, no increase, a pay cut nobody announced. And every shopper pays a sneakier tax: a raise that only matches inflation can still lift you into a higher tax bracket, so the taxman takes a bigger slice of buying power that never grew.

Now the strange winners. The borrower: take a loan today, repay it in shrunken rands tomorrow — inflation eats debts as happily as it eats savings. That includes the biggest borrower of all, the government. And anyone who owns things whose prices ride the wave — property, stock, shares — floats while cash sinks.

That is why the target band of 3 to 6 percent exists. Not zero — a little inflation keeps the economy moving. Not double digits — that way lies Zimbabwe in 2008, wheelbarrows of banknotes that could not buy bread by afternoon. Just low enough, and steady enough, that a family can plan a year ahead: sign a lease, save for school fees, price a spaza's stock, without the ground moving under the numbers.

So read the news line like an economist now. When inflation prints at 6,0 percent, you know where the number came from — one trolley, priced twice. You can ask which fire is burning — pull or push. You can predict the Bank's move — repo up if it is demand, agony if it is costs. And you can say who is bleeding — the pensioner, the saver, the unorganised — and who is quietly smiling — the borrower. And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: winners and losers sorted, the reason for the band, and the whole chain from trolley to repo told in your own words.
