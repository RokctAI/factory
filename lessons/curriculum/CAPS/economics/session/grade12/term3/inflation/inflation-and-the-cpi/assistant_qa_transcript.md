**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First one, and it comes up every single year: petrol went up, taxi fares went up, and my family says food is double what it was. So why does the news say inflation is only six percent? Somebody must be lying.

**Tutor:** Nobody is lying; two different things are being measured. The official rate prices ONE standard trolley — the average household's basket, weighted by what surveys show South Africans as a whole spend. Your family's trolley is not that trolley. A household that spends half its income on food lives food inflation at double strength, because food carries only about a fifth of the weight in the national basket. A commuting household feels every fuel and taxi increase the same way. Statistics South Africa itself publishes separate indices for different spending groups, and the index for low-income households regularly runs above headline CPI in months when food and transport lead the increases. There is also a trick of memory to watch: we notice the prices that jumped and forget the ones that crawled. Airtime and clothing have had years of near-zero increases, but nobody stands in a shop feeling grateful for the price that did not move. So the honest statement is this: the official six percent is a true average, your family's higher number is a true experience, and the examiner will reward you for explaining how a weighted average allows both to be true at once.

**Mandy:** Next: if the Reserve Bank can cool inflation by raising the repo rate, why does everyone complain when it does? It sounds like the Bank is doing exactly its job.

**Tutor:** Because the medicine and the disease hurt different people, and that tension is the heart of every evaluation question on this topic. When the repo rises, every bond repayment, car instalment and overdraft in the country gets more expensive within days. Households cut spending, firms shelve expansion, and some workers lose jobs — that is not a side effect, that is the mechanism: inflation cools BECAUSE demand is being squeezed. Now ask who carries that squeeze. A family with a bond carries it directly. A small business living on credit carries it. A worker retrenched when orders dry up carries it hardest of all. Meanwhile the people inflation was hurting — pensioners, savers — are helped only gradually as prices settle. So the complaint is real: the costs arrive fast and land on borrowers and job-seekers, while the benefits arrive slowly and spread thinly across everyone. The Bank's defence is equally real: unchecked inflation would eventually hurt the same people worse, and a central bank that flinches teaches everyone to expect high inflation, which makes the next fight more expensive. A top answer states both sides and then adds the sharpest point from the lesson: if the inflation is cost-push — tariffs, fuel, drought — the repo squeeze buys very little cooling for a great deal of pain, and that is when the complaints are most justified.

**Mandy:** This one caused an argument at the back: why does the target allow ANY inflation? Surely the perfect number is zero — prices that never move.

**Tutor:** Zero sounds perfect and behaves badly, for three reasons economists take seriously. First, measurement: the CPI slightly overstates true inflation, because it struggles to capture quality improvements — when a phone costs the same but does twice as much, the index sees no change. Aiming at measured zero would therefore mean true deflation. Second, the danger zone problem: an economy at zero has no cushion. Any shock tips it into falling prices, and deflation is a trap — households postpone buying because things will be cheaper next month, firms cut production and wages, debts grow heavier in real terms, and the postponement feeds itself. A small positive rate keeps the economy a safe distance from that cliff. Third, flexibility: wages almost never fall in rand terms — no workforce accepts a pay cut gracefully. With a little inflation, a struggling firm can hold wages steady and let real labour costs ease gently, keeping people employed. At zero, the only way to cut real wages is retrenchment. That is why the band sits at 3 to 6 percent: low enough that money keeps its meaning and contracts stay plannable, high enough to keep clear of the deflation cliff and to let the labour market breathe. When you evaluate the framework in an essay, credit it for anchoring expectations, and note the standing debate about whether the band should be narrower or lower — but zero is nobody's serious proposal.

**Mandy:** Last one: in the calculation, why must we divide by the OLD index and not the new one? Half of us divided by the new number and got a different answer.

**Tutor:** Because an inflation rate answers a question about a journey, and a journey is measured from where you started. When the CPI moves from 112 to 118,7, the question is: by what fraction did prices grow compared with what they WERE? The growth is 6,7 points; the starting point is 112; so the rate is 6,7 over 112, which is 5,98 percent. Divide by the new index instead and you are answering a different question — what fraction of today's level is the change — which nobody asked. The difference looks small here, but it is a real mark lost, and in high-inflation cases it becomes a large error: if an index doubles from 100 to 200, dividing by the old index gives the correct 100 percent, while dividing by the new gives 50 percent, which is nonsense. Anchor it with the everyday version: if your taxi fare goes from R10 to R12, everyone instinctively says fares rose 20 percent — two rand on the TEN you used to pay, not on the twelve you pay now. The same instinct, written as a formula, is new minus old, over OLD, times 100. Keep the index-point trap beside it in your notes: the 6,7-point move is not 6,7 percent, and writing points where the question wants a percentage costs the mark even when your subtraction was perfect.
