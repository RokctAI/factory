# Part 1 — Expert

The circular flow showed an economy settling at an equilibrium; the multiplier showed how a shock moves it. This session studies the movement itself. Real economies do not grow in a straight line — they swell and shrink in waves called BUSINESS CYCLES, and the syllabus asks four things of you: name the parts of the wave, explain what causes it, state what government does about it, and read the instruments forecasters use to see the next wave coming. South Africa has lived every phase of this chapter within recent memory, which makes it the easiest topic in Paper 1 to illustrate with real events.

## Subtopic: The Anatomy of the Cycle

Define first. A BUSINESS CYCLE is the successive period of expansion and contraction in aggregate economic activity — measured by real GDP — around a long-term growth trend. The definition carries three examinable words: SUCCESSIVE, because the phases follow one another in order; REAL, because we track goods produced, not prices; and TREND, because the wave rides on a slowly rising path.

Describe the diagram in words, since you must be able to draw and label it. The horizontal axis carries time; the vertical axis carries the level of real output. A straight line rises gently from left to right — the TREND LINE, the economy's long-term growth path. Around it snakes a wave. Starting from the bottom: the TROUGH, the lowest point, where activity stops falling. Then the UPSWING or expansion — production, income, employment and spending all rising, the wave climbing up through the trend line. Then the PEAK, the highest point, where activity stops rising — often with factories near capacity and prices accelerating. Then the DOWNSWING or contraction — output, income and employment falling — until the next trough. Peak and trough together are the TURNING POINTS of the cycle.

Two measurements on that drawing earn marks. The LENGTH of a cycle is the distance from one peak to the next peak, or trough to trough, measured in time. The AMPLITUDE is the vertical distance between the trend line and a peak or a trough — it measures the INTENSITY of the cycle: a large amplitude means a violent boom or a deep slump, and the larger the amplitude, the stronger the shock that produced it and the longer its effects last.

Attach the vocabulary of severity. A RECESSION is conventionally declared after two consecutive quarters of falling real GDP. A DEPRESSION is a severe, prolonged recession. South Africa supplies dated examples: the 2009 downswing that followed the global financial crisis, and the 2020 collapse under the Covid lockdowns — the deepest trough in the modern record — followed by a recovery upswing. Load-shedding years illustrate an economy dragged below its trend even outside formal recession.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: the definition word by word, the four phases in order, the two turning points, and length and amplitude read off the described diagram.

## Subtopic: Why Cycles Happen — Causes and Types

Examiners ask WHY the wave exists, and the syllabus organises the answers into two camps plus a catalogue of cycle types.

EXOGENOUS explanations locate the cause OUTSIDE the market system. Weather and natural disasters strike agriculture; wars and political shocks cut trade routes; oil price shocks raise every producer's costs at once; a pandemic closes the shops by decree. On this view — held by MONETARISTS — markets are inherently stable and self-correcting, and cycles are caused by outside disturbances, above all by GOVERNMENT ITSELF: money supply expanded too fast, then choked; policy that stimulates before elections and squeezes after. The cure, monetarists conclude, is not more intervention but stable, predictable policy.

ENDOGENOUS explanations locate the cause INSIDE the market system. This is the KEYNESIAN view: markets are not self-correcting, because prices and wages are sticky and expectations feed on themselves. Business confidence rises, firms invest, the multiplier magnifies the spending, rising demand induces yet more investment through the accelerator — an upswing that feeds itself. Then confidence cracks, investment is postponed, the multiplier runs in reverse, and the downswing feeds itself the same way. Because the instability is internal, Keynesians conclude government MUST lean against it. Hold both views with their policy conclusions attached — the contrast is a standard eight-mark question.

Then the catalogue of cycle types, named for their discoverers and ordered by length. KITCHIN cycles, roughly three to five years, driven by businesses building up and running down INVENTORIES. JUGLAR cycles, roughly seven to eleven years, driven by investment in MACHINERY and equipment. KUZNETS cycles, roughly fifteen to twenty years, driven by BUILDING and construction. KONDRATIEFF waves, forty-five to sixty years, driven by fundamental TECHNOLOGICAL revolutions — steam, rail, electricity, and in our own era digital technology. Shorter cycles ride on the backs of longer ones, which is why no two downswings look identical.

Stop for this section's questions now — exogenous against endogenous with the monetarist and Keynesian labels correctly attached, each school's policy conclusion, and the four cycle types matched to their lengths and drivers.

## Subtopic: Leaning Against the Wave — Monetary and Fiscal Policy

Government does not accept the cycle passively; it deploys two instruments to SMOOTH it, and you must be able to run each one in both directions.

MONETARY POLICY belongs to the South African Reserve Bank, whose mandate is to protect the value of the currency, operationally an inflation target of three to six percent. Its main lever is the REPO RATE — the rate at which the SARB lends to commercial banks. Raise the repo and every other interest rate follows: borrowing costs more, credit shrinks, spending cools. Its supporting instruments: OPEN-MARKET TRANSACTIONS, selling government securities to drain cash from the banks or buying them to release it; the CASH RESERVE REQUIREMENT, the fraction of deposits banks must hold back; and MORAL SUASION, persuasion of the banks. In a DOWNSWING the Bank runs EXPANSIONARY policy — the repo rate was cut to a record low of 3,5 percent in 2020 to carry households and firms through the lockdown trough. In an inflationary UPSWING it runs CONTRACTIONARY policy — the sharp repo increases of 2022 and 2023 answered inflation that had broken above the target band.

FISCAL POLICY belongs to the National Treasury and works through the budget. In a downswing, EXPANSIONARY fiscal policy: raise government spending, cut taxes, accept a larger deficit, and let the multiplier turn the injection into a larger rise in income. In an overheating upswing, CONTRACTIONARY fiscal policy: restrain spending, raise taxes, shrink the deficit. Alongside deliberate decisions stand the AUTOMATIC STABILISERS — progressive income tax, which takes less as incomes fall, and social grants and unemployment insurance, which pay out more as jobs are lost. They dampen the cycle without anyone lifting a pen, which is why they act faster than any announced package.

State the limits, because evaluation earns the top marks. Both policies act with LAGS — a repo change takes months to reach spending, and infrastructure money takes years to leave the ground — so badly timed policy can amplify the wave it meant to flatten. Fiscal expansion financed by heavy borrowing raises debt-service costs and can crowd out private investment. And no demand-side instrument fixes a supply-side constraint: cheaper credit builds no power stations.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — the SARB's four instruments, each policy run in expansionary and contractionary directions with a South African example, automatic stabilisers, and the limits stated.

## Subtopic: The New Economic Paradigm and Reading the Future

The syllabus closes the topic with two modern pieces: the new paradigm of smoothing, and the toolkit forecasters use.

The NEW ECONOMIC PARADIGM answers an old dilemma. Pure demand management bought employment at the price of inflation; the new paradigm aims at stability itself, arguing that cycles are best SMOOTHED by combining moderate, predictable DEMAND-SIDE policy with active SUPPLY-SIDE measures. Demand-side: inflation targeting and disciplined budgets, so that expectations stay anchored. Supply-side: measures that raise the economy's capacity and efficiency — infrastructure, skills, competition, lower costs of doing business — so that the trend line itself climbs faster and demand can grow without hitting the inflation ceiling. Smoothing, note, does not abolish the cycle; it narrows the amplitude.

Then FORECASTING, whose features the syllabus lists explicitly. Economic INDICATORS are series that move with the cycle in a known time order. LEADING indicators turn BEFORE the economy does — new manufacturing orders, building plans approved, share prices, business confidence surveys; the SARB publishes a composite leading indicator watched precisely because it peaks months ahead of the peak. COINCIDENT indicators turn WITH the economy — retail sales, industrial production. LAGGING indicators turn AFTER — unemployment, which keeps rising after the trough because firms rehire only once recovery is certain; a COMPOSITE indicator bundles several series into one index to cancel out the noise of any single one.

Three techniques complete the kit. The TREND LINE projects the long-run path. EXTRAPOLATION extends an observed pattern forward — useful, but built on the assumption that the future repeats the past, which turning points exist to break. MOVING AVERAGES smooth a jagged series by averaging each period with its neighbours, stripping out short-term noise so the underlying direction shows. Add LENGTH and AMPLITUDE from the anatomy of the cycle — a long, high upswing warns that a turning point is near — and you can answer any question that ends "…which features underpin forecasting?"

The final questions of this part are with you now: the new paradigm as demand-side stability plus supply-side capacity, the three indicator families with examples in the right time order, and extrapolation and moving averages with their uses and limits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same chapter from a town that lives it — because a business cycle is not a wave on a chart; it is a good year and a bad year arriving at every till in the country at nearly the same time.

## Subtopic: A Town That Breathes

Take a coastal town whose life is tourists, and watch four seasons that are really four phases.

First, the quiet bottom. Guesthouses half-empty, waiters on short shifts, the curio sellers packing up early. Nothing is getting worse anymore — it is simply flat. That is the TROUGH.

Then word spreads of a good December. Bookings come in; the guesthouse hires two cleaners; the restaurant orders more fish; the builder gets a contract for four new rooms. Those wages get spent at the spaza and the taxi rank, and THOSE takings get spent again — the town climbing month by month. That is the UPSWING, and the climbing-feeding-climbing is the multiplier working upward.

Then the town is FULL. Every room taken, every table booked, prices creeping up because more visitors are chasing the same beds. The builder cannot find an extra bricklayer at any wage. The town cannot do more than it is doing. That is the PEAK.

Then something breaks the mood — petrol prices jump, or a flood closes the road, or visitors simply feel poorer. Bookings thin. The guesthouse lets a cleaner go; her spending vanishes from the spaza; the fish order shrinks. Each loss causes the next. That is the DOWNSWING, and it runs until things stop getting worse and the town sits quiet again — a new trough, waiting.

One picture to keep: the town's activity is a wave, and underneath it runs a slow slope — a little more each decade as roads improve and skills grow. The wave is the cycle; the slope is the trend; how far the wave swings from the slope is the amplitude.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the town are coming to you right now: the four phases spotted in the story, the turning points, and the wave told apart from the slope it rides on.

## Subtopic: The Thermostat and the Budget

Somebody watches the wave and leans against it — two somebodies, with two different levers.

The Reserve Bank holds the price of borrowed money. Think of the repo rate as a THERMOSTAT for spending. Town too cold — trough, empty rooms, idle builders? Turn the rate DOWN: bond repayments shrink, car finance gets cheaper, the guesthouse can afford the loan for those four new rooms. That is what the record-low rates of 2020 were: heat for a frozen economy. Town running too hot — full rooms, prices climbing? Turn the rate UP: loans pinch, spending cools, the price-climb slows. That is what the rate hikes of 2022 and 2023 were. One dial, two directions.

The Treasury holds the budget, and it leans the same two ways. In the bad year, government can spend more and tax less — fix the road, hire the teachers, let the deficit widen — and every rand it spends goes round the town's tills. In the fat year, it does the opposite and puts something away. And part of the budget leans automatically, with nobody deciding: when the waiter loses his job he stops paying income tax and starts drawing from the UIF, so his spending falls by less than his wage did. The safety net is also a shock absorber.

But be honest about the levers, because that honesty is what a Grade 12 essay wants. Both work SLOWLY — the rate cut of March reaches the shops around Christmas, and the road budget becomes wages only when the graders arrive next year. Sometimes the medicine lands after the fever has passed. And neither lever builds a power station: if the town's problem is that the lights go out, cheap loans and big budgets just queue money behind the outage.

Your questions for this part are up now: the thermostat turned both ways with the real years attached, the budget leaning both ways, the automatic shock absorber, and the two honest limits.

## Subtopic: Reading the Signs Before the Wave Turns

Last piece — how anyone knows what is coming, and how to say it in exam language.

The town has its own early warnings. Before the good season, the signs come FIRST: bookings made months ahead, the builder's plans approved at the municipality, the wholesaler's orders rising. Economists call these LEADING indicators — new orders, building plans, share prices, confidence surveys — the things people do TODAY because of what they expect TOMORROW. Some signs move WITH the season — the tills themselves, retail sales, production: COINCIDENT. And some arrive LATE: the guesthouse only hires permanent staff months after the recovery started, just as unemployment nationally keeps rising after the trough has passed — LAGGING.

Two tricks make the signs readable. A single week's takings jump around — a wet weekend, a public holiday — so smooth them: average each month with its neighbours, and the jagged line becomes a curve you can trust. That is a MOVING AVERAGE. Then extend the curve forward along its current path to guess next quarter — that is EXTRAPOLATION, and it works right up until the turning point, which is exactly the moment it fails. Say that in an essay and you sound like an economist: extrapolation assumes the future repeats the past, and cycles exist because sometimes it does not.

And the modern wisdom, in one sentence: government no longer tries to abolish the wave, it tries to keep it SMALL — steady, predictable policy on the demand side, and on the supply side everything that lifts the slope the wave rides on: skills, infrastructure, reliable electricity, easier business. Narrow the amplitude, raise the trend.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: leading, coincident and lagging signs sorted from the town's story, the moving average and extrapolation each with its limit, and the smoothing sentence written in your own words. Keep the wave in your head — business cycles link straight into fiscal policy, the public sector and every growth essay in Paper 1.
