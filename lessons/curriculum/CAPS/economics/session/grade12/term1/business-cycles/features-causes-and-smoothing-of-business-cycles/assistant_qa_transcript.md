**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First one that comes up a lot: if everyone knows the phases of the cycle, why does anyone get caught by a recession? Surely businesses would just prepare at the peak.

**Tutor:** Because knowing that a downswing must eventually come is not the same as knowing WHEN, and the timing is what ruins people. Look at what the peak feels like from inside it: order books are full, prices are rising, credit is easy, and every recent month has been better than the one before. Extrapolation — the human habit, not just the statistical technique — says next year will look like this year. So the guesthouse borrows to build four more rooms at exactly the moment demand is about to turn, and when the downswing arrives it faces the new debt with the old income gone. That is not stupidity; it is the structure of the problem. Turning points are the one feature of the cycle that the past does not reveal, which is why forecasters lean so heavily on leading indicators — new orders, building plans, confidence surveys — the few series that tend to bend before the economy does. Even those give direction, not dates. And notice the deeper Keynesian point hiding in your question: if every firm DID cut back at the peak to prepare, their combined caution would itself trigger the downswing they feared. Expectations are not just predictions of the cycle; they are part of its machinery. That is precisely why the syllabus treats confidence as an endogenous cause and not merely a mood.

***

**Mandy:** Next: monetarists say government causes cycles, Keynesians say government must cure them. In an essay, which side is the right answer?

**Tutor:** Neither, and the marks are in showing you know why the question stays open. Present them as two diagnoses that lead to two prescriptions. The monetarist diagnosis: markets left alone tend back to equilibrium, and the historical record of booms and busts tracks policy mistakes — money supply expanded too fast, then squeezed; spending raised before elections. Prescription: rules rather than discretion, stable money growth, predictable budgets. The Keynesian diagnosis: prices and wages adjust slowly and confidence feeds on itself through the multiplier and accelerator, so an economy can settle far below full employment and stay there. Prescription: government must lean against the wind, spending into the trough and restraining at the peak. Then earn the evaluation marks by testing each against South African evidence. The 2020 collapse was clearly exogenous — no market caused the lockdowns — yet recovery relied on deeply Keynesian tools: grants, UIF payouts, record-low interest rates. Meanwhile our chronic weak growth looks more like the monetarist and supply-side warning: it persists through every stance of demand policy, because its causes are electricity, ports, skills and confidence in policy itself. So the honest conclusion is the syllabus's own new paradigm — steady, rule-like demand management plus supply-side repair. Write that and you have used both schools instead of picking a team.

**Mandy:** This one causes arguments every year: when interest rates go up, the news calls it fighting inflation, but my family just sees the bond repayment jump. How is making everyone poorer supposed to help anyone?

**Tutor:** Hold on to that discomfort, because it is the actual mechanism, not a side effect. Inflation in an overheating upswing means too much spending chasing too little output. The Reserve Bank cannot conjure more output — it holds no factories — so its only lever is the spending side. Raising the repo rate makes credit dearer everywhere at once: bonds, vehicle finance, store cards, business overdrafts. Households with debt feel squeezed and cut back; firms postpone borrowing-financed expansion; total demand cools; and the pressure lifting prices eases. The pain you describe IS the transmission channel. Now, why endure it? Because the alternative is worse and falls on the same people harder. Inflation above the target eats the value of every wage and every grant month after month — an invisible pay cut for the whole country, cruellest to those who cannot negotiate raises. And once people EXPECT high inflation, they build it into wage demands and price setting, and it becomes self-fulfilling and far more expensive to remove. The three-to-six percent target exists to anchor those expectations. So the fair way to state it in an essay: contractionary monetary policy trades visible short-term pain by borrowers for protection of the currency's value for everyone — and its burden is uneven, which is a legitimate evaluation point, not a reason it does not work.

**Mandy:** Last one: the leading indicator turned down three months ago, but the economy still grew this quarter. Does that mean the indicator is broken?

**Tutor:** No — it means it is doing exactly what a LEADING indicator does, and reading it correctly is a skill examiners love to test with data-response questions. Leading means it turns BEFORE the economy, typically six to twelve months before, so a downturn in the composite leading indicator is compatible with several more quarters of growth; the message is about where the economy is heading, not where it is standing. Three cautions make you a careful reader. First, one month means little — these series are noisy, which is why composites bundle many signals and why you smooth with a moving average before judging direction; look for three or more months of consistent movement. Second, leading indicators sometimes cry wolf: a dip driven by one component, say a share-price wobble that reverses, predicts nothing. That is why forecasters read the three families together — leading for the warning, coincident series like retail sales and production to confirm the turn is actually arriving, lagging series like unemployment to confirm afterwards how deep it ran. Third, the indicator gives direction, never size: it cannot tell you whether the coming downswing is a ripple or 2009. So in a data question, write the disciplined sentence: the sustained decline in the leading indicator signals a probable downswing within the coming year, subject to confirmation by coincident indicators. That is full marks, and it is also simply true.
