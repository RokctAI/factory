Hey, it's Mandy — business cycles can feel like a topic with too many lists: four phases, two schools, four cycle types, three indicator families. Here's the secret: it is really ONE picture with labels, and everything in the topic hangs off that picture.

Draw it in your head. Time along the bottom, real GDP up the side, a gentle rising line — the trend — and a wave snaking around it. Now attach the labels clockwise: trough at the bottom, upswing climbing, peak at the top, downswing falling. Peak-to-peak is length, trend-to-peak is amplitude. That's the whole anatomy, and the diagram question is free marks if you label calmly.

The two schools are a two-line contrast, so learn it as a pair. Monetarists: cycles come from OUTSIDE the market — mostly from government mistakes — so the cure is stable, predictable policy. Keynesians: cycles come from INSIDE — confidence, multiplier, accelerator — so government must lean against them. Cause outside, hands off; cause inside, hands on. Say both, then point at South Africa 2020 for exogenous and confidence swings for endogenous, and you have an evaluation paragraph.

Cycle types: sort them by size, smallest to biggest — Kitchin stock-take, Juglar machines, Kuznets buildings, Kondratieff technology. Three-to-five, seven-to-eleven, fifteen-to-twenty, forty-five-to-sixty. Inventory, investment, construction, revolution.

Policy is a thermostat question: downswing means repo down, spending up, taxes down; overheating means the reverse. Always name a real move — the 3,5 percent repo of 2020, the hikes of 2022 — because dated examples separate the top essays.

And the indicators: leading come first, like orders and building plans; coincident move with, like retail sales; lagging come last, like unemployment. First, with, last.

One picture, one pair of schools, one size-order, one thermostat, one first-with-last. You've got this!
