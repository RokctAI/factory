**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! Here's the first one: if the four phases always come in the same order, why can't a business just look at the diagram and know exactly when to expand and when to pull back?

**Tutor:** Because the diagram tells you the ORDER of the phases, never their TIMING, and timing is where fortunes are made and lost. Think about what the peak feels like from inside it. The mine's order book is full, overtime is standard, every recent month beat the one before. The human habit of extrapolation whispers that next year will look like this year — so the contractor signs for three new bakkies on credit at precisely the moment demand is about to turn, and when the downswing arrives he owes new-boom money on old-slump income. That is not foolishness; it is the structural cruelty of turning points, which are the one feature of the cycle the past cannot reveal. It is exactly why analysts lean on leading indicators — equipment orders, building plans, confidence surveys — the few series that tend to bend before the economy does, and even those give direction rather than dates. And notice the Keynesian sting hidden in your question: if every firm DID pull back at the peak to be safe, their collective caution would itself cause the downswing they were bracing for. Expectations are not merely forecasts of the cycle — they are one of its engines, which is why confidence is classified as an endogenous cause and not just a mood.

***

**Mandy:** Next one: the monetarists blame government for cycles and the Keynesians want government to fix them. If a question asks me to discuss both, am I supposed to pick a winner?

**Tutor:** No — you are supposed to show that each school is a diagnosis wired to a prescription, and then test both against evidence. The monetarist diagnosis: markets drift back to equilibrium on their own, and history's booms and busts track policy blunders — money growth accelerated then slammed shut, spending loosened before elections. Prescription: rules, not discretion; stable money; predictable budgets. The Keynesian diagnosis: prices and wages adjust slowly, and confidence compounds through the multiplier and accelerator, so an economy can sink below full employment and simply stay there. Prescription: lean against the wind — spend into the trough, restrain at the peak. Now the evaluation, which is where the marks concentrate. The two thousand and twenty collapse was as exogenous as shocks come — no market mechanism ordered a lockdown — yet the rescue was pure Keynes: emergency grants, UIF payouts, a record-low repo rate. Meanwhile South Africa's stubbornly weak trend growth behaves exactly as the monetarist and supply-side school warns: it survives every setting of the demand dial, because its roots are electricity, logistics, skills and policy credibility. So conclude with the synthesis the topic itself teaches — the new paradigm: rule-like demand management plus supply-side repair. That answer uses both schools instead of joining one.

**Mandy:** This one comes straight from home: when the repo rate goes up, the news says inflation is being fought, but all my family notices is the bond repayment jumping. How does squeezing households help them?

**Tutor:** Keep that discomfort in the answer, because the squeeze is not a side effect of the mechanism — it IS the mechanism. Inflation in an overheating economy means too much spending chasing too little output. The Reserve Bank owns no farms, mines or factories, so it cannot add output; its only handle is spending. Raising the repo makes credit dearer everywhere simultaneously — bonds, vehicle finance, store accounts, business overdrafts — so indebted households trim, firms shelve credit-financed expansion, total demand cools, and the upward pressure on prices eases. Why accept that pain? Because the alternative hurts the same people more and for longer. Inflation above the target quietly cuts every wage and every grant, month after month, and it is harshest on those with no power to negotiate increases. Worse, once people EXPECT high inflation they bake it into wage claims and price setting, and it becomes self-fulfilling and far costlier to unwind — the three to six percent target exists precisely to stop expectations escaping. So the balanced sentence for an essay is this: contractionary monetary policy trades visible, uneven short-term pain by borrowers for protection of the currency's value for everyone — and the unevenness of that burden is a fair evaluation point, not proof the policy fails.

**Mandy:** Last one: say the composite leading indicator has fallen for four months, but this quarter's GDP still grew. Is the indicator wrong?

**Tutor:** No — it is doing precisely what the word leading promises, and reading it calmly is a skill data questions reward. Leading means the series turns BEFORE the economy, usually six to twelve months before, so a falling leading indicator alongside a growing economy is not a contradiction; it is a forecast coexisting with the present. Three disciplines make you a careful reader. First, insist on persistence: these series are noisy, which is why composites bundle many components and why you smooth with a moving average — four consistent months is worth attention, one month is not. Second, remember that leading indicators sometimes cry wolf: a dip driven by a single component, say a share-price wobble that reverses next month, predicts nothing, so check whether the decline is broad across components. Third, confirm with the other families: leading gives the warning, coincident series like retail sales and production confirm the turn as it arrives, and lagging series like unemployment tell you afterwards how deep it ran. And the indicator never gives magnitude — it cannot say whether the coming downswing is a ripple or a two thousand and nine. So the disciplined sentence is: the sustained, broad-based decline in the leading indicator signals a probable downswing within the coming year, subject to confirmation by coincident indicators. That sentence is accurate, and it is exactly how professionals speak.
