**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! The one everybody argues about at home: when the rand weakens the news treats it as a catastrophe, but the lesson said depreciation helps close a trade deficit. So which is it — disaster or medicine?

**Tutor:** It is both at once, distributed to different people on different timetables — and laying out that distribution IS the model answer. The winners first. An exporter earns dollars and pays wages in rands, so a weaker rand converts the same dollar sales into more rand revenue: the citrus farmer, the wine cellar, the platinum mine all get a raise without shipping one extra carton. Tourism gets the same gift — the country becomes a bargain holiday, and every arriving visitor's spending counts as an export. Local factories competing with imports gain shelf space as the imported rival's price climbs. Now the losers. Every importer, immediately: fuel is invoiced in dollars, so transport costs rise within weeks, and transport hides inside the price of nearly everything. Imported machinery, medicine, wheat and electronics all cost more — imported inflation, which lands heaviest on low-income households because food and transport dominate their spending. Government pays more rand to service its dollar debts. And note the timing asymmetry that data questions love: the import bill rises the very day the rand falls, but export volumes need seasons and signed contracts to grow, so the trade balance frequently worsens before it improves. So reject the either-or: a gradual, stable depreciation restores competitiveness; a violent, jumpy one imports inflation and frightens off the investors financing our deficit. The line to remember: stability matters more than the level.

**Mandy:** Next: comparative advantage proves both countries gain from trade. Then why are there protests about cheap imports and lost factories? The theorem can't be wrong and the protesters right at the same time... can it?

**Tutor:** It can, because the theorem and the protests answer different questions — and seeing both at once is precisely the higher-order skill. The theorem is about COUNTRIES: total consumption in each trading nation can exceed its own production frontier. It is silent about the people inside each total. Open the border to inexpensive imported clothing and millions of consumers each save a little every month, while the workers of a few textile towns lose everything at once. Spread-thin gains, heaped-up losses: the national sum is positive, the local experience is ruin, and the people in the heap march, reasonably. Economics does not answer them by closing the border — that destroys the aggregate gain — but by insisting the winners compensate the losers: retraining, unemployment insurance, deliberate investment in the towns that absorbed the shock. Then add two honest qualifications. The theory assumes labour glides between industries, but a machinist does not become a citrus-export logistics manager by Friday; adjustment takes years and policy. And a country may fairly ask WHAT it specialises in: if your current comparative advantage is unprocessed minerals, you might use policy to build future advantages in goods with more growth in them — which is the beneficiation debate you will meet in the trade topic next term. Gains from trade: real, aggregate, unevenly shared. An essay carrying all three clauses is a strong essay.

**Mandy:** This one kept us arguing after class: South Africa runs a current-account deficit almost every year. Why haven't we simply run out of money?

**Tutor:** Because a deficit is not paid out of a fixed jar — it is financed by a continuing FLOW, and the flow lasts exactly as long as foreigners want South African assets. Watch the machinery turn. Our import bill plus the dividends and interest owed to foreign investors exceeds our export earnings: that is the current-account deficit. The books still balance because, on the financial account, foreigners are buying what we offer — government bonds, JSE shares, occasionally a whole factory. The country is, in effect, paying for part of its imports with claims on its own future: dividends to remit, interest to pay, assets now owned abroad. That arrangement can roll on for decades, and has. Its weak point is not arithmetic but APPETITE. A large slice of our financing is portfolio money — hot money — and it can exit between breakfast and lunch. When global risk appetite sours, or a downgrade shakes confidence in our policy, the inflow stalls and the adjustment arrives brutally compressed: the rand falls hard, imports are strangled by their new prices, and the deficit closes the painful way. That scenario is called a sudden stop, and it is the reason the Reserve Bank sits on a cushion of reserves and the reason economists file persistent deficits under vulnerability rather than sin. The kitchen version is exact: a family can outspend its wages for years while the relative keeps lending — the crisis is not the borrowing; it is the morning the relative says no.

**Mandy:** Last one: if a weaker rand is such good news for exporters, why doesn't the Reserve Bank just push the rand down and hold it there? Free competitiveness!

**Tutor:** Because a currency cannot be talked down for free — every method of forcing it down sends a bill that eventually eats the prize. Follow the mechanics. To weaken the rand deliberately, the Bank must sell rands and buy dollars, day after day, which expands the money supply. That is stimulus wearing a disguise, and its first product is inflation. Now recall what competitiveness actually is: the REAL exchange rate. If the rand drops ten percent but our prices then inflate ten percent faster than our trading partners', the exporter's advantage evaporates entirely — the country keeps the dearer bread and loses the edge. Suppress the inflation and you must raise interest rates, which attracts foreign capital and pushes the rand straight back up: the instrument cancels itself. Second bill: credibility. The Reserve Bank's entire regime rests on inflation targeting and a market-priced float. A central bank caught steering its currency downward invites speculators to test it and partners to retaliate — competitive devaluation is the beggar-thy-neighbour game of the nineteen-thirties, and modern trade rules exist partly to stop everyone losing it together. Third bill: the state's own dollar debt and fuel purchases cost more rand the moment the currency slides. Hence the settled position: let the float absorb shocks, keep inflation anchored so any depreciation stays REAL, and build export strength out of the things that truly create it — reliable power, functioning ports, skills. A cheap currency is a painkiller. Those are the cure.
