# Part 1 — Expert

The four-sector circular flow ended with a foreign sector buying our exports and selling us imports. This session opens that sector fully: why nations trade at all, the national bank statement on which every cross-border rand is recorded — the balance of payments — the market where rands are priced against dollars, and what a country does when its foreign accounts run out of balance. This is the topic behind every news line about the rand weakening, and the examiners' favourite place to set data-response questions with real figures.

## Subtopic: Why Nations Trade — Absolute and Comparative Advantage

Start with the reasons for international trade, which the syllabus lists on both sides of the counter. On the DEMAND side: no country produces everything its people want — South Africa grows no coffee and assembles no aircraft — and differences in income, population size and taste create demand that home production cannot meet. On the SUPPLY side: countries differ in NATURAL resources — our platinum, coal, sunshine and coastline; in LABOUR and skills; in CAPITAL and technology; and in climate. Specialisation follows: produce what your endowments favour, trade for the rest, and both sides consume more than they could alone.

The theory sharpens into two terms. A country has an ABSOLUTE advantage when it can produce a good with fewer resources than another country — more output per worker, full stop. COMPARATIVE advantage is subtler and is the true engine of trade: a country should specialise where its OPPORTUNITY COST is lowest — where it gives up least by producing that good rather than another — and this holds even if one country is absolutely better at everything.

Work the standard two-country example. Suppose with the same resources South Africa can produce either 100 tons of maize or 50 tons of steel, while Germany can produce 120 tons of maize or 240 of steel. Germany is absolutely better at both. But look at opportunity costs: a ton of steel costs South Africa two tons of maize, while it costs Germany only half a ton of maize; a ton of maize costs Germany two tons of steel but costs South Africa only half a ton of steel. So Germany's sacrifice is smallest in steel, ours in maize. Let each specialise where its sacrifice is least and trade at any rate between the two opportunity costs — say one maize for one steel — and BOTH countries end up consuming beyond their own production possibilities. That result, gains from trade without anyone being best at anything, is the deepest sentence in the topic.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: demand-side and supply-side reasons for trade, absolute against comparative advantage, and the opportunity-cost comparison worked from a two-country table.

## Subtopic: The Balance of Payments

Every transaction between South Africa and the rest of the world is recorded in the BALANCE OF PAYMENTS — the country's bank statement with the world, kept by the Reserve Bank. Learn its accounts in order, because data questions hand you the statement and ask where an item belongs.

The CURRENT ACCOUNT records trade and income flowing now. Merchandise exports and imports — minerals, vehicles and fruit out; oil, machinery and electronics in — whose difference is the TRADE BALANCE. Then services: transport, tourism — a foreign tourist's spending here is a service EXPORT. Then income: dividends and interest crossing the border, and current transfers such as remittances. South Africa's current account typically runs a small deficit — our import bill plus dividends paid to foreign owners of our firms usually outweigh what commodity exports bring in.

The CAPITAL TRANSFER account is tiny: debt forgiveness and migrants' transfers of assets. The FINANCIAL ACCOUNT is the mirror of the current account: how the country PAYS for its net imports. DIRECT investment — a foreign firm building a factory here, lasting ownership. PORTFOLIO investment — foreigners buying our shares and government bonds, easily reversed, which is why it is nicknamed hot money. OTHER investment — loans and deposits. A current-account deficit must be financed by a financial-account surplus: we import more than we export, and the difference is covered by foreigners acquiring our assets. Finally the official GOLD AND FOREIGN-EXCHANGE RESERVES held by the SARB, the shock absorber that rises or falls so the whole statement balances.

Guarding the system internationally stands the INTERNATIONAL MONETARY FUND, the IMF: 190 member countries, a pool of currencies, and the lender that steps in when a member cannot pay its way — with loans that arrive tied to CONDITIONS, typically spending cuts, tax reform and liberalisation, which is why IMF rescues are both lifelines and controversies.

Stop for this section's questions now — the accounts in order, sample items placed correctly, why a current-account deficit needs a financial-account surplus, hot money named and explained, and the IMF's role with its conditionality.

## Subtopic: The Foreign Exchange Market and How the Rand Is Priced

Rands buy nothing in Tokyo. Trade therefore requires a market where currencies themselves are bought and sold: the FOREIGN EXCHANGE MARKET — no single trading floor, but the world's banks dealing around the clock. The PRICE established there is the EXCHANGE RATE: the number of rands one unit of foreign currency costs, say eighteen rand to the dollar.

The rate is set by demand and supply, so build both curves in words. The DEMAND for dollars comes from everyone who must PAY in dollars: South African importers of oil and iPhones, South Africans travelling abroad, our firms paying dividends or repaying dollar loans, residents investing overseas. The SUPPLY of dollars comes from everyone converting dollars INTO rands: foreigners buying our coal and wine, tourists landing in Cape Town, foreign investors buying South African bonds or building factories here. Picture the diagram exactly as any market: quantity of dollars on the horizontal axis, the rand price of a dollar on the vertical; the demand curve slopes downward, the supply curve upward, and where they cross is the day's rate.

Now shift the curves. Oil price doubles: importers need more dollars at every rate, demand shifts right, and the rand price of the dollar rises — the rand DEPRECIATES, each dollar costing more rand. A commodity boom or a wave of foreign investment brings dollars in: supply shifts right and the dollar cheapens — the rand APPRECIATES. Use those two words with care: under a floating rate, market movements are depreciation and appreciation; DEVALUATION and REVALUATION are reserved for deliberate changes made by a government under a fixed rate.

Three exchange-rate SYSTEMS complete the theory. FREE-FLOATING: the market alone sets the rate — South Africa's system, with the SARB intervening only rarely. MANAGED floating: the central bank buys and sells to steer the rate inside an unannounced band. FIXED or pegged: the rate is set by decree and defended with reserves. Floating rates absorb shocks and free the central bank to target inflation, at the price of volatility; fixed rates give traders certainty, at the price of spending reserves to defend a rate speculators may attack.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — who demands and who supplies dollars, the diagram described with its axes, depreciation and appreciation caused by curve shifts, the vocabulary of fixed against floating, and the three systems compared.

## Subtopic: Correcting a Disequilibrium

A balance of payments is in DISEQUILIBRIUM when deficits — or surpluses — persist beyond what normal financing can carry. Persistent DEFICITS drain reserves, pile up foreign debt, and end in crisis if untreated; persistent SURPLUSES, though comfortable, mean the country consumes less than it earns and its trading partners accumulate the matching deficits. Correction operates through four channels — learn them as a toolkit and attach the cost of each.

Channel one: EXCHANGE-RATE adjustment. Under floating, correction is partly automatic — a deficit means excess demand for foreign currency, so the rand depreciates, making our exports cheaper abroad and imports dearer at home; export volumes rise, import volumes shrink, and the trade gap narrows. The cost: dearer imported fuel, machinery and food feed straight into inflation — imported inflation — and if exporters cannot expand supply, the price falls before the volumes respond.

Channel two: DEMAND management. Contractionary monetary and fiscal policy — higher interest rates, restrained budgets — cool total spending, and since imports are bought out of income, import spending falls with it. Higher rates also attract foreign portfolio inflows, financing the gap. The cost: slower growth and higher unemployment; the deficit is cured by making the patient poorer.

Channel three: DIRECT controls. Tariffs and quotas on imports, and EXCHANGE CONTROL — rationing residents' access to foreign currency, which South Africa has used within living memory and retains in relaxed form. The cost: retaliation by trading partners, evasion, and the inefficiencies of a rationed market. Trade rules under the World Trade Organisation constrain this channel.

Channel four: BORROWING and reserves — draw down reserves or approach the IMF to finance the gap while other corrections work. The cost: conditionality and debt.

For a persistent SURPLUS, run the toolkit in reverse: allow appreciation, expand domestic demand, liberalise imports. Essays earn their top marks by matching the tool to the cause: a deficit born of an overvalued currency wants channel one; one born of an overheated economy wants channel two.

The final questions of this part are with you now: disequilibrium defined on both sides, the four channels each with its mechanism and its cost, and the tool matched to the cause.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same topic at the scale of one family business — because a country trading with the world is a household writ large: it earns, it spends, it banks, and its money must be changed at the door.

## Subtopic: The Braai Exchange

Two neighbours, two skills. Sipho bakes; Anna farms chickens. Sipho could keep chickens too — badly, at the cost of half his baking day. Anna could bake — badly, losing chicken time she is brilliant at. So they do not even discuss it: he bakes for both households, she raises chickens for both, they trade over the fence, and both eat better than either could alone. Nobody legislated it; the saving of wasted effort did.

Now the harder case, the one the exam loves. Suppose Anna is better at BOTH — faster baker and better farmer. Should she drop Sipho? Count what each hour costs her. An hour of baking costs Anna two chickens she would have raised; an hour of baking costs Sipho almost nothing, because his chickens barely lay anyway. Anna's baking hour is expensive TO HER; Sipho's is cheap TO HIM. So Anna still gains by leaving the baking to Sipho and pouring her hours into the chicken run where she is unbeatable — and Sipho gains a market. Being better at everything does not mean it pays to DO everything: do what costs you least, trade for the rest. Countries are Sipho and Anna with flags — we sell the world platinum and fruit; we buy oil and aircraft, though with enough sacrifice we could scratch at both.

But there is a catch at the fence: Anna will not take Sipho's bread coupons as payment forever, and Japan will not take rands. Between every two traders stands the question — whose money settles the deal? Hold that; it is the next part.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the fence trade are coming to you right now: why trade needs no permission, the both-gain result when one side is better at everything, and each choice expressed as what it costs the chooser.

## Subtopic: The Rand Has a Price Tag Too

Everything you buy has a price in rands. Here is the strange loop: the RAND ITSELF has a price, quoted in other money — around eighteen rand per dollar — and that price moves for exactly the reasons bread's price moves: buyers and sellers.

Who is queueing to BUY dollars with rands? Everyone who must pay the world: the fuel importer, the shop importing phones, the family paying for a daughter studying overseas, the pension fund buying American shares. Who is queueing to SELL dollars for rands? Everyone the world is paying: the mine shipping coal to India, the wine estate paid in euros, the German tourists at the waterfront, the foreign fund buying our government bonds. Long queue at the buy window, short at the sell window: the dollar gets dearer — the rand WEAKENS, depreciates. Queues reversed: the rand FIRMS, appreciates.

Test it on headlines. Oil price spikes — importers need more dollars — rand weakens. Foreign funds fall in love with our bonds — dollars pour in — rand firms. Load-shedding scares investors off — the sell-window queue empties — rand weakens. You can now explain most currency headlines with two queues.

And who controls the price? In South Africa, nobody — the rand FLOATS, and the Reserve Bank mostly lets the queues decide. Some countries instead FIX the price of their money and spend their savings defending it — which works until a day comes when the whole world queues on one side, betting the defence must break. Floating is bumpy but honest; fixed is calm until it snaps.

Your questions for this part are up now: the two queues named with real members, depreciation and appreciation read off the queues, three headlines explained, and floating against fixed in one honest sentence each.

## Subtopic: When the Family Spends More Than It Earns

Run a family kitchen like a country and the balance of payments loses all its terror.

The family EARNS from the world: wages from the mine, rent from the back room, the odd chicken sold. It SPENDS to the world: groceries, transport, school fees, DSTV. Write both columns in a notebook — that notebook is a current account. If spending beats earning this month, the gap must be COVERED: someone dips into savings, borrows from an uncle, or sells the second bicycle. That covering column is the financial account. And the small tin of emergency cash on the top shelf is the reserves. Every country keeps exactly this notebook; South Africa's is kept by the Reserve Bank, and our family usually spends slightly more than it earns, covered by the world buying our shares and bonds — the uncle's loan, at national scale.

One month is fine. Every month, forever, is not. The savings thin, the uncle starts adding conditions to his loans — spend less on meat, fix the roof first, show me your budget. That uncle with conditions exists for countries too: the IMF, the lender countries approach when nobody else will cover the gap, and its conditions — cut spending, reform taxes — are as unwelcome at national scale as at the kitchen table.

So how does the family fix a chronic gap? The same four ways a country does. Its labour can get CHEAPER to the world — the depreciating rand does this for a country automatically, making our goods a bargain abroad and imports dear at home. It can SPEND LESS overall — belt-tightening, the higher-interest-rate cure, effective and painful. It can BLOCK some spending outright — no more DSTV; tariffs and import controls, until the neighbours retaliate by refusing the chickens. Or it can BORROW properly and buy time. The skill — in the exam and in the country — is matching the cure to the disease: a family that overspends needs the belt; a family whose labour is overpriced needs the price cut.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: the notebook's columns matched to the accounts, the tin on the shelf, the uncle with conditions, and the four family cures matched to the four national ones. Keep the queues and the notebook — they carry you through trade policy in term two and every rand headline between now and the final.
