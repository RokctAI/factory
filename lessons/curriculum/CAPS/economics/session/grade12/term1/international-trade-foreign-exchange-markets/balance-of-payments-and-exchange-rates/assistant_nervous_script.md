Hey, it's Mandy — I know this topic feels like three topics stapled together: trade theory, a bank statement, and a currency market. Here's the calm way through: it is one story in three scenes, and each scene has one trick.

Scene one, why trade. The only trap is absolute versus comparative advantage, and the key word is SACRIFICE. Absolute asks who produces more. Comparative asks who gives up less — opportunity cost. When the table gives you two countries and two goods, don't stare at the big numbers; divide to find what one ton of each good COSTS in the other good, then give each country the good that costs it least. Even a country that is worse at everything has a least-bad option — that is the whole theorem.

Scene two, the balance of payments. Placement questions are free marks with one sorting rule: is the item trade or income happening NOW? Current account. Is it buying or selling ASSETS — shares, bonds, factories, loans? Financial account. Tourist spending here: current, a service export. Toyota building a plant: financial, direct investment. Foreigners buying our bonds: financial, portfolio — call it hot money and say why: it can leave in an afternoon.

Scene three, the rand. Draw the market for DOLLARS: quantity of dollars along the bottom, rand-per-dollar up the side. Then one sorting rule again: whoever must PAY the world demands dollars; whoever the world is PAYING supplies them. Importers, travellers, dividend-payers demand; exporters, tourists arriving, foreign investors supply. Any headline is just one curve shifting — oil price up, demand right, rand weaker. And keep the word pairs clean: depreciation and appreciation for the floating market, devaluation and revaluation only when a government moves a fixed rate.

For corrections, remember four medicines: cheaper currency, tighter belts, blocked imports, borrowed time — each with a side effect you can name.

Divide for sacrifice, sort now-versus-assets, queue the dollar buyers and sellers. Three tricks, whole topic. You've got this!
