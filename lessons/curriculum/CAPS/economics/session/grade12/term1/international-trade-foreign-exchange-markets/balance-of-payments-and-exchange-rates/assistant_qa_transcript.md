**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First, the one I hear every time the rand makes the news: everyone talks about a weak rand like it's a national disaster. But our lesson said depreciation helps fix a trade deficit. So is a weak rand good or bad?

**Tutor:** It is both, for different people at different speeds — and sorting the winners from the losers IS the exam answer. Start with who wins. Exporters are paid in dollars and pay their workers in rands, so when the rand weakens, the same dollar price converts into more rands: the wine estate, the fruit farmer, the platinum mine all see revenue jump without selling one case more. Tourism wins the same way — South Africa becomes a cheap destination, and every foreign visitor's spending is an export. Local manufacturers competing with imports win too, because the imported rival suddenly costs more on the shelf. Now who loses. Every importer, immediately: fuel is priced in dollars, so transport costs rise within weeks, and fuel is in the price of everything that travels by road. Imported machinery, medicines, electronics, wheat — all dearer. That is imported inflation, and it hits poor households hardest because fuel and food dominate their baskets. Government loses on its dollar-denominated debt, which now costs more rand to service. And there is a timing trap worth a mark: the import bill rises the day the rand falls, but export volumes take months to respond — contracts must be signed, crops grown, capacity added — so the trade balance often gets WORSE before it gets better. So refuse the question's either-or: a moderate, stable depreciation helps competitiveness; a violent, volatile one imports inflation and scares off the very investors who finance our deficit. The mature sentence is that the LEVEL matters less than the stability.

**Mandy:** Next: comparative advantage says both countries always gain from trade. But if that's true, why is half the world angry about trade? Somebody must be losing.

**Tutor:** The theorem is true and the anger is justified, because they are about different things — and holding both is exactly the higher-order skill. Comparative advantage proves that both COUNTRIES gain: total consumption in each country can rise beyond its own production possibilities. What the theorem does not say is that every PERSON inside each country gains. When South Africa opens to cheap imported clothing, consumers across the country gain a little each — better prices, more choice — while clothing workers in Newcastle lose their jobs. The gains are spread thin over millions; the losses fall in a heap on a few towns. Nationally the sum is positive; locally it can be devastation, and the people in the heap vote and march. That is not a flaw in the arithmetic — it is a distribution problem, and economics answers it not by closing the border but by insisting the winners compensate the losers: retraining, social insurance, regional development for the towns that carried the cost. Add the subtler qualifications: the theory assumes workers move smoothly between industries, which they do not — a machinist does not become a fruit-export manager by Tuesday. And a country can worry about WHAT it specialises in: if your comparative advantage today is raw minerals, you may reasonably use policy to build advantages in things with more future — which is precisely the industrial-policy debate waiting in the term-two trade topic. So: gains from trade are real, aggregate, and unevenly shared; the essay that says all three things wins.

**Mandy:** This one puzzled the whole class: how can South Africa keep running a current-account deficit year after year? At some point don't we simply run out of money?

**Tutor:** We would, if a deficit were financed out of a fixed pot — but it is financed by a FLOW, and the flow can continue as long as foreigners keep wanting South African assets. Look at the machinery. Our imports plus the dividends and interest we pay foreign investors exceed what our exports earn: that is the current-account deficit. The rand payments still balance because, on the financial account, foreigners buy what we sell them — government bonds, shares in our companies, the occasional factory. In effect, the country pays for part of its imports not with exported goods but with claims on its future: interest to be paid, dividends to be remitted, assets now foreign-owned. That can run for decades. What it cannot survive is a loss of APPETITE. Much of our financing is portfolio investment — hot money — which can leave in an afternoon. When global risk mood sours or investors doubt our policy, the inflow stops, and then the adjustment arrives all at once: the rand drops sharply, imports are choked off by their new price, and the deficit closes the painful way. That is the sudden stop, and it is why the Reserve Bank holds reserves as a shock absorber and why persistent deficits are called a vulnerability, never a crime. The kitchen-table version is exact: a family can outspend its wages for years while the uncle keeps lending — the crisis is not the borrowing, it is the day the uncle changes his mind. And the day he changes his mind is the day you meet the other uncle, the one with conditions.

**Mandy:** Last one: if a weak rand boosts our exporters, why doesn't the Reserve Bank just push the rand down on purpose and keep it there?

**Tutor:** Because a currency's value cannot be wished down without paying for it somewhere else, and each way of paying eventually cancels the prize. Think about what deliberately weakening the rand requires: the SARB would have to sell rands and buy dollars continuously, expanding the money supply. That is stimulus by another name, and its first consequence is inflation — which quietly REVERSES the trick. Competitiveness depends on the REAL exchange rate: if the rand falls ten percent but our prices then rise ten percent faster than our trading partners', exporters end up exactly where they started, only with dearer bread. To stop the inflation, the Bank would have to raise interest rates — which attracts foreign money and pushes the rand back UP. The instrument fights itself. Second, the credibility cost: the SARB's whole regime is inflation targeting, and markets believe the rand floats. A central bank seen manipulating its currency invites speculators to test it and trading partners to cry foul — competitive devaluation is the beggar-thy-neighbour policy of the 1930s, and modern trade rules and G20 commitments frown on it precisely because everyone doing it leaves everyone worse off. Third, the fiscal side: a deliberately weak rand raises the rand cost of our dollar debt and of every state fuel purchase. So the settled South African answer: let the float absorb shocks, keep inflation anchored so that whatever nominal depreciation happens becomes REAL competitiveness, and pursue export strength through the things that actually create it — reliable electricity, working ports, skills. A cheap currency is a painkiller; those are the cure.
