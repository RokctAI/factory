**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First: this feels like magic money. If R1 000 of government spending really becomes R5 000 of income, why does the government not simply spend forever and make everyone rich?

**Tutor:** Because the multiplier multiplies SPENDING, and spending only becomes wealth when there are unused resources for it to activate. Trace the two cases. In an economy with idle factories and unemployed workers, the extra spending is met with extra production: the bricklayer really does lay bricks, the spaza really does sell more mielie meal, and the R5 000 of income corresponds to R5 000 of new goods and services that did not exist before. Everybody is genuinely better off. Now run it in an economy already at capacity. The spending still multiplies, the incomes still rise, the number still comes out at R5 000 — but there are no more bricks to lay and no more mielie meal to sell. More rands are chasing the same goods, so prices rise instead of output. The multiplier has multiplied inflation. So the tool tells you how far a rand travels; it does not promise that anything new gets made along the way. Add the second limit: the money must be found somewhere. Financed by borrowing, it must be repaid with interest, and heavy state borrowing can lift interest rates so that businesses invest less, cancelling part of the gain. That is why an honest answer always pairs the calculation with the conditions.

***

**Mandy:** Next: our formula gave a multiplier of five with an MPC of 0,8, but the realistic South African one gave only two. Which number should we use in an essay?

**Tutor:** Use the one the question's data supports, and say why. If the question gives you only an MPC or only an MPS, the simple model is what is being tested: k equals one over one minus the MPC. If it gives you a marginal propensity to tax and a marginal propensity to import as well, it is asking for the open-economy version, where the denominator carries all three leakages. Beyond the arithmetic, the economics of that gap is worth a paragraph on its own. South Africa's multiplier is genuinely on the smaller side, for reasons that are structural: our tax system captures a meaningful share of each additional rand, and we import a large share of what we consume and almost all of our machinery and fuel. Each round of spending therefore loses more of itself than it would in a more self-sufficient economy. That has a policy consequence you can state with confidence: the effectiveness of a stimulus in South Africa depends heavily on WHAT is bought. Wages for local construction, maintenance, schooling and health services keep rands circulating domestically; a programme dominated by imported equipment sends much of its value abroad in the first round. Same rand, very different chain length.

***

**Mandy:** This one caused an argument: if saving is a leakage that shrinks the multiplier, is saving bad for the economy? Our whole lives we are told to save.

**Tutor:** It is one of the oldest puzzles in the subject and the resolution turns on distinguishing the short run from the long run. In the short run, and particularly in a downswing, a sudden rush by everyone to save more does reduce total spending. Shops sell less, firms cut production, incomes fall — and because incomes have fallen, the country may end up saving no more in total than before, despite everyone trying harder. That is the paradox of thrift, and it is a genuine short-run effect that a good answer can name. In the long run the picture reverses completely. Saving is what supplies the financial market with funds; those funds finance investment in machinery, factories, roads and skills; and that investment is what expands the economy's productive capacity — its ability to produce more in future. A country that saves nothing must borrow abroad to invest, which is precisely South Africa's structural position and a real vulnerability. So the mature statement is this: saving reduces demand today and builds capacity for tomorrow, and which effect matters more depends on whether the economy's problem right now is too little spending or too little capacity. Naming both, and choosing between them with a reason, is what earns the higher-order marks.

***

**Mandy:** Last one: how can a multiplier be a decimal or less than one? Someone said a country could have a multiplier below one and I thought that was impossible.

**Tutor:** Take the formula seriously and you will see it cannot fall below one in the models the syllabus uses, but you will also see exactly why people say it. In the formula k equals one over the sum of the leakages, the leakages are fractions of a rand, so their sum lies between zero and one, which means one divided by that sum is always greater than one. Even with heavy leakage — say the marginal propensities to save, tax and import together take eighty cents in the rand — the multiplier is one over 0,8, which is 1,25. The first round alone puts a whole rand of income into somebody's hands, so the total can never be less than the injection itself. What people mean when they claim a multiplier below one is something the simple model leaves out: the OFFSETS. If government borrows to fund the spending and interest rates rise, some private investment is cancelled. If households expect future tax increases, they may save more today. If the extra demand pulls in imports faster than assumed, the leak is larger than measured. Net of those offsets, the observed effect on national income can indeed come out smaller than the amount spent. So distinguish the two carefully in an essay: the calculated multiplier from the formula, and the net effect after offsets, which is what the real economy delivers.
