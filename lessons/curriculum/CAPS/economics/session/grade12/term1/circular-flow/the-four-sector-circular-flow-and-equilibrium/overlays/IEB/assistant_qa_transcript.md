**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First one: saving is listed as a leakage, but we're also told banks lend savings out to businesses. If my saved rand comes back as a loan, was it ever really a leakage?

**Tutor:** It was — for a moment, and that moment is the whole point. Track the rand through time. The instant a household banks it, the rand steps out of the spending stream: no shop rings it up, no producer earns it, and if the story ended there, output would sag. What usually happens next is the detour: the bank, pension fund or bond market passes the rand to a business that uses it to buy a delivery van or instal a sewing line, and at that second moment the rand re-enters the flow as investment, earned by whoever built the van. So the rand is not counted twice; it is followed through a door and back. The deep point is that the return trip is neither automatic nor equal in size. Households save on their own timetable — caution, retirement, a house deposit — while firms invest on a completely different one: expected demand, interest rates, nerve. In an anxious year the savings pile up on one side of the door while investment hangs back on the other, leakages beat injections, and national income shrinks. That possible mismatch is exactly why we write the equilibrium condition as a condition to be tested, not a truth that always holds.

***

**Mandy:** Next: in our numbers, investment was three hundred but saving was only two hundred and fifty. If the financial market only had two hundred and fifty to lend, where did the extra fifty come from?

**Tutor:** From the rest of the world, and noticing that is thinking like an economist about an open economy. Domestic saving is not the only money on the shelf. Foreigners place funds in South African bonds, shares, bank deposits and businesses, and those funds are just as lendable. Now look at the trade side of our same figures: imports of three hundred against exports of three hundred in the balanced case — but the moment you shift the numbers so the country buys more from the world than it sells, the difference must be settled somehow, and it is settled by foreign capital flowing in. The two gaps are mirror images: a country investing more than it saves is, through the same arithmetic, drawing in foreign financing. That has been South Africa's long-standing position — domestic saving too thin to fund domestic investment, with foreign capital bridging the gap. The equilibrium condition already accommodates all of this, because X and M sit inside the totals. And it hands you a ready-made evaluation sentence: investment financed by foreign funds is real investment, but it leaves the economy exposed on the day those funds decide to go home.

***

**Mandy:** This one split the class down the middle: if government taxes exactly as much as it spends, the leakage equals the injection — so a balanced budget changes nothing, right?

**Tutor:** Wrong, and the reason is that a taxed rand and a spent rand do not carry the same amount of spending. Follow one rand each way. Tax a rand away from a household and you have not removed a full rand of spending, because a slice of that rand was never going to be spent — it was headed for a savings account, say twenty cents of it. So the tax removes only about eighty cents of actual demand. Now let government spend a rand on a teacher's salary or a pallet of bricks: the entire rand lands in the flow as expenditure, because the state does not skim a saving share off first. Net effect of the balanced pair: demand rises. Then add composition, which is even more powerful. Tax tends to fall hardest on higher-income households, who save a large fraction of every rand; grants and public wages flow to households who spend nearly every cent. A balanced budget that moves rands from high-saving hands to high-spending hands lifts total demand without moving the deficit at all. So the sentence worth writing is: a balanced budget is not a neutral budget — both its size and its composition do real work.

***

**Mandy:** Last one: the statisticians say real GDP grew this year, but everyone I know says times are harder. Can both be true?

**Tutor:** Both can be true, and separating the claims is a skill worth carrying into every data question. The word "grew" hides three different tests. Test one: did output rise, or only prices? That is what deflating settles — seven thousand billion nominal over a deflator of one hundred and twenty-five is five thousand six hundred billion real, and only the real figure counts goods. Suppose the real figure did rise; move to test two: did it rise faster than the number of people? Divide by roughly sixty-three million South Africans and you get output per person. If real output grows one percent while population grows one and a half percent, output per person falls, and the average household genuinely has less even as the total economy sets records. Test three: who received the growth? An economy can expand through capital-intensive sectors — mining machinery, automated plants — that add output while hiring few people, so the gains concentrate while most households feel nothing. The disciplined conclusion runs: total real output rose; output per person may still have fallen; and distribution decides whether growth was felt. Three sentences, three different facts — and an answer that sees all three stands far above one that stops at the headline.
