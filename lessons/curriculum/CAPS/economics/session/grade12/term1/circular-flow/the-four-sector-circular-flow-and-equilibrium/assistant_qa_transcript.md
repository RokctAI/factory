**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First one: if saving is a leakage and investment is an injection, why do textbooks also say saving CAUSES investment? It sounds like the same rand is being counted twice.

**Tutor:** It is one rand doing two different jobs at two different moments, and the financial market is the hinge between them. When a household saves, that rand leaves the spending stream — no shop receives it, no producer earns it — so at that instant it is a genuine withdrawal from the flow, and output would fall if nothing else happened. What happens next is that the rand sits in a bank, a pension fund or the bond market, and that institution lends it to a business that buys a machine or builds a warehouse. At THAT moment the same rand re-enters as investment spending, and a producer of capital goods earns it. So saving is not counted twice; it is tracked through a detour. The important economic point is that the detour is not automatic and not guaranteed to be equal in size. Households decide to save for their own reasons — caution, retirement, a deposit on a house — while businesses decide to invest for entirely different reasons: expected demand, interest rates, confidence. When savers are cautious and businesses are gloomy, savings pile up while investment lags, leakages exceed injections, and the flow shrinks. That gap is precisely why the equilibrium condition is written as a condition, not an identity.

***

**Mandy:** Next: our set of figures had saving of 300 but investment of 350, so more came out of the financial market than went into it. Where did the extra 50 come from — is the model cheating?

**Tutor:** Not cheating — that is the model being honest about an open economy. Investment need not be financed by domestic saving alone. It can be financed by foreign saving: money that people in other countries place in South African bonds, shares, bank deposits and businesses. In our figures the country was importing 400 and exporting 300, so it was buying more from the world than it sold to it, and that gap was covered by capital flowing in from abroad. Read the two facts together and they are the same fact: a country that spends more on foreign goods than it earns from foreign buyers must be receiving foreign funds to settle the difference, and those funds can finance investment at home. That is exactly South Africa's structural position — a country that has long invested more than it saves domestically, and has drawn in foreign capital to close the gap. It also explains why the model still balances at 1 200 against 1 200: the arithmetic of the equilibrium condition already contains the foreign financing, because imports and exports sit inside the totals. And it carries a warning worth stating in an essay: relying on foreign funds to finance domestic investment leaves an economy exposed if those funds turn around and leave.

**Mandy:** This one confused a lot of us: government spending is an injection and tax is a leakage — so if the government spends exactly what it taxes, does it have no effect on the economy at all?

**Tutor:** It has a considerable effect, and the reason is that a rand taxed and a rand spent do not have equal force. Think about who holds the rand at each step. When government taxes a rand away from a household, that household does not lose a full rand of SPENDING, because part of the rand would have been saved rather than spent. Perhaps twenty cents of it was destined for a bank account. So taxing a rand removes only about eighty cents of spending from the flow. But when government spends a rand on a nurse's salary or a load of bricks, the whole rand enters the flow as expenditure — the state does not save a slice of it on the way. So a balanced change — tax a rand more, spend a rand more — still adds net spending to the economy. Beyond that arithmetic, composition matters enormously: taxing higher-income households, who save a large share of income, and spending on grants and public employment for households who spend nearly everything they receive, moves rands from low-spending hands into high-spending hands and lifts total demand at unchanged budget totals. A balanced budget is not a neutral budget, and saying so precisely is the kind of remark that separates a good answer from an average one.

***

**Mandy:** Last one: real GDP grew, so people say the economy grew — but the news says South Africans got poorer. Both can't be right, can they?

**Tutor:** Both can be right, and holding the two together is one of the sharpest habits this topic can give you. Three different questions hide inside the word "grew". First, did output rise at all, or did only prices rise? Nominal GDP can climb purely on inflation, which is why we deflate: nominal six thousand billion against a deflator of one hundred and twenty gives five thousand billion in real terms, and only that real figure is about goods. Second, did output rise faster than the population? Divide real GDP by roughly sixty million people and you get output per person; if real output grows by one percent while the population grows by one and a half, real GDP per capita FALLS, and the average person genuinely has less even though the economy is technically larger. Third, who received the extra output? An economy can grow through capital-intensive sectors that employ few people, so measured growth arrives while wages and employment stagnate for most households. So the honest sentence is: the economy grew in real terms, output per person may have fallen, and the distribution of what was produced decides whether anyone feels better off. Write those three distinctions into an essay and you have answered a question most candidates only half-see.
