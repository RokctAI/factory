# Part 1 — Expert

Grade 10 drew the circular flow as a picture and named its leakages and injections. Grade 12 turns that picture into ARITHMETIC — the model that carries the whole of Paper 1's macroeconomics. Today: the closed and open economies and their four participants; the leakages and injections that must balance; the three ways the flow is measured and the aggregates the national accounts report; and how to read an economy that is out of equilibrium. Every essay on business cycles, fiscal policy or the balance of payments is written on top of this diagram.

## Subtopic: Closed, Open, and the Four Participants

Define before you draw — markers award the definition mark separately from the diagram mark.

A CLOSED economy has no foreign sector: only households, businesses and — in the three-sector version — the government. An OPEN economy adds the FOREIGN SECTOR, and South Africa, exporting minerals and vehicles and importing oil and machinery, is emphatically open. The full model is therefore the FOUR-SECTOR model: households, businesses, government, and the foreign sector.

Name what each participant does. HOUSEHOLDS own the factors of production and supply them to businesses; in return they earn wages, rent, interest and profit, which they spend, save in part, and pay tax on. BUSINESSES buy factor services, produce goods and services, pay out incomes, and INVEST — buying capital goods. The GOVERNMENT taxes, spends on goods, services and transfers, and produces public goods. The FOREIGN SECTOR buys our exports and sells us imports.

They meet in four MARKETS, and naming them earns marks. The FACTOR market, where households sell labour, land, capital and entrepreneurship. The PRODUCT market, where output is sold. The FINANCIAL market — banks, insurers, the stock exchange — which collects savings and channels them to borrowers. And the FOREIGN EXCHANGE market, where rands are traded for other currencies so that trade can settle.

Two flows run in opposite directions around the diagram, and confusing them is the standard error. The REAL flow is the movement of factor services one way and goods and services the other — things, not money. The MONEY flow is income and expenditure moving the opposite way. Every real flow has a money flow facing it; that is why the diagram closes.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: closed against open, the four participants with their functions, the four markets named, and the real flow kept strictly apart from the money flow.

## Subtopic: Leakages, Injections and the Equilibrium Condition

Not every rand of income returns to the flow, and not every rand entering the flow came from household income. Economists call the exits LEAKAGES (withdrawals) and the entrances INJECTIONS.

The three LEAKAGES: SAVING (S) — income not spent, passing into the financial market; TAXATION (T) — income claimed by government; and IMPORTS (M) — spending that leaves to pay foreign producers. The three INJECTIONS: INVESTMENT (I) — business spending on capital goods, financed out of the financial market; GOVERNMENT SPENDING (G); and EXPORTS (X) — foreign spending entering the flow.

Line them up in pairs, because the pairing is how the model is examined. Saving leaves through the financial market and investment returns through it; taxation leaves to government and government spending returns from it; imports leave to the foreign sector and exports return from it.

Now the condition. The economy is in EQUILIBRIUM when total leakages equal total injections:

S + T + M = I + G + X

Note the word TOTAL. The pairs need not match individually — saving may exceed investment while exports exceed imports — and the economy is still in equilibrium provided the three-against-three sum balances. Examiners set that trap deliberately.

Work it. Take a year, in rand billions: saving 300, taxation 500, imports 400 — leakages of 1 200. Investment 350, government spending 550, exports 300 — injections of 1 200. Equal, so national income is at rest: production, income and expenditure are consistent, and nothing in the flow is pushing output up or down.

The same equilibrium can be written from the expenditure side as Y = C + I + G + (X − M), where Y is national income and C is household consumption. The two statements are the same fact seen from two angles: what is spent equals what is earned equals what is produced.

Stop for this section's questions now — the three leakages and three injections paired to their markets, the total-against-total rule, and the equilibrium computed from a set of figures.

## Subtopic: Measuring the Flow — Three Methods, Three Aggregates

The flow can be measured wherever you cut it, and the national accounts cut it in three places. The PRODUCTION method sums the value ADDED by every producer — value added, never total sales, or intermediate goods are counted twice. The INCOME method sums the incomes paid to the factors of production: compensation of employees, net operating surplus, plus consumption of fixed capital. The EXPENDITURE method sums final spending: C + I + G + (X − M). Measured accurately, all three give the same total — the flow has one size, whichever bank of the river you count from.

Then the conversions, which are pure marks if the sequence is memorised. GDP at MARKET PRICES is output valued at what buyers actually pay — including taxes on products such as VAT and excise, and net of subsidies. Strip those out and you have GDP at FACTOR COST (basic prices) — what producers actually receive:

GDP at factor cost = GDP at market prices − taxes on products + subsidies on products

Illustrate with round figures. GDP at market prices R6 000 billion, taxes on products R700 billion, subsidies on products R100 billion: 6 000 − 700 + 100 = R5 400 billion at factor cost.

Next, GROSS DOMESTIC against GROSS NATIONAL. GDP counts production INSIDE our borders, whoever owns the factors. GNI counts income earned by SOUTH AFRICAN-owned factors, wherever they are: add primary income received from the rest of the world, subtract primary income paid to it. With R80 billion received and R200 billion paid: 6 000 + 80 − 200 = R5 880 billion. Because South Africa hosts more foreign-owned capital than it owns abroad, our GNI sits BELOW our GDP — a structural feature worth stating in an essay.

Finally, NOMINAL against REAL. Nominal figures are in current prices and rise when prices rise; REAL figures are deflated to a base year, and only real growth means more goods. With nominal GDP of R6 000 billion and a deflator of 120 against a base of 100: 6 000 × 100 ÷ 120 = R5 000 billion in real terms. Divide by a population of roughly 60 million and real GDP per capita is about R83 000 — and note the discipline that follows: if real GDP grows more slowly than population, output per person FALLS even while the economy "grows".

The questions on this section are in front of you now — the three methods, the two conversions worked in the correct order, and real per capita output distinguished from nominal totals.

## Subtopic: Reading a Disequilibrium

Equilibrium is a knife-edge, and the examinable skill is describing what happens when the economy leaves it.

Suppose leakages EXCEED injections. Return to the figures and let exports fall from 300 to 250: injections are now 1 150 against leakages of 1 200. More is draining out than is coming in, so businesses find goods unsold and stocks piling up. They cut production and lay off workers; incomes fall; households, poorer, save less and pay less tax and buy fewer imports — so leakages themselves shrink. The economy contracts until the smaller leakages once more equal the injections. Equilibrium is restored, but at a LOWER level of national income and higher unemployment. That is the downswing of the business cycle described in circular-flow language.

Now the reverse. Let injections exceed leakages — a surge of government infrastructure spending, or a mining boom lifting exports. Stocks run down, firms raise output and hire, incomes rise, and rising incomes generate extra saving, tax and imports until leakages catch up. Equilibrium returns at a HIGHER national income. If the economy still has idle capacity, this shows up mainly as more output and employment; if it is already near capacity, it shows up mainly as INFLATION — the same injection, two very different results.

Three points that lift an answer from adequate to strong. First, the flow is self-correcting in DIRECTION but not in OUTCOME: it always finds a new equilibrium, with no guarantee that the new one is a good one — an economy can settle comfortably at high unemployment. Second, this is exactly where policy enters: fiscal policy works on G and T, monetary policy works on S and I through the interest rate, and trade policy works on X and M — every macroeconomic instrument in the syllabus is an attempt to move a leakage or an injection. Third, the adjustment is not one-for-one: a rand of extra injection raises national income by MORE than a rand, because each recipient re-spends part of it. That amplification is the multiplier, and it has a lesson of its own.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the contraction and expansion sequences traced step by step, the capacity condition that decides output against prices, and each policy instrument matched to the leakage or injection it moves.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same flow from a small town on payday — because the circular flow is not a diagram invented by economists, it is literally where your family's money goes and where it comes back from.

## Subtopic: The Money That Goes Around the Township

Friday. A shift ends at the plant, and wages land in bank accounts. Those wages came from the factory, which sold what the workers made. That is the whole model already: households hand over their WORK, businesses hand back MONEY; households hand back the money as SPENDING, businesses hand over GOODS. Two rings turning in opposite directions — one carrying things, one carrying rands.

Follow a Friday wage. Some goes over the counter at the spaza — straight back to a business. Some goes on a taxi fare, some on school shoes, some on airtime. Every one of those rands returns to a business, becomes somebody else's wage next Friday, and comes round again. Money does not get "used up"; it circulates.

But two more players stand in that street. The GOVERNMENT is there before the wage even lands, taking tax off the payslip — and there again as the clinic, the school, the road-repair crew and the grant in a grandmother's account. And the FOREIGN SECTOR is there too: the fuel in the taxi arrived by tanker, and the coal from the mine down the road left on a ship. Money can leave the town, and money can arrive from far away.

So: four players — families, businesses, government, the world outside — meeting in four places: where jobs are traded, where goods are sold, where money is banked and borrowed, and where rands are swapped for dollars.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the Friday wage are coming to you right now: the two rings turning opposite ways, the four players, and the four places they meet.

## Subtopic: Taps and Drains

Picture a bath with the plug half out. Water pouring in from taps, water running out through drains, and the LEVEL sitting wherever those two rates balance. The level is national income — the size of the economy.

Three DRAINS pull money out of the flow. You SAVE some of your wage — it sits in the bank instead of at the till. You pay TAX — the payslip is smaller than the wage. And you buy IMPORTED things — that money boards a plane. Save, tax, import: three ways a rand leaves the town.

Three TAPS pour money in. A business BORROWS from the bank and builds a new warehouse — your savings coming back as somebody's investment. GOVERNMENT SPENDS — a new clinic, teachers' salaries, a road. And the world BUYS what we produce, so export money comes in. Investment, government spending, exports: three ways a rand arrives.

Now the only rule that matters: the bath sits still when the taps together match the drains together. Not tap-for-drain — TOGETHER. Saving can be bigger than investment and it is still fine, as long as government spending and exports make up the difference. Try it with numbers: drains of 300 saved, 500 taxed, 400 imported — 1 200 out. Taps of 350 invested, 550 government, 300 exported — 1 200 in. Level steady.

And you can measure the water three ways and get the same answer: count what was MADE, count what was EARNED, or count what was SPENT. Same bath, three tape measures — which is why the national accounts publish production, income and expenditure and expect them to agree.

Your questions for this part are up now: three drains, three taps, the together-not-pairs rule, and one balance checked with real figures.

## Subtopic: When the Bucket Won't Sit Still

Last piece, and it is the one that explains recessions in ordinary words.

Suppose a drain widens or a tap closes — the world stops buying our coal, say, so export money drops from 300 to 250. Now 1 200 is running out and only 1 150 coming in. The level falls. In real life that means: goods sit unsold, so the factory cuts a shift; a cut shift means smaller wages; smaller wages mean less at the spaza, so the spaza owner orders less; and so it goes around, smaller each turn.

Here is the part people miss. The bath DOES settle again — but lower. When incomes fall, people automatically save less, pay less tax and buy fewer imported things, so the drains narrow until they match the taps once more. Everything balances, and a lot of people are out of work. Balance is not the same as good.

Turn it the other way and the same machinery runs forward: government starts a big building programme, or a mining boom lifts exports. Orders rise, shifts are added, wages grow, spending grows, and the level climbs until the widened drains catch up. One warning attached: if the factories were already running flat out, the extra money cannot buy extra goods — it just bids up PRICES. Same tap, different result, depending on whether there was room to grow.

And that is exactly what every policy argument you will ever hear is about. Cutting interest rates? Trying to turn the investment tap. A budget with more spending or lower tax? Government working its own tap and its own drain. A weaker rand making our exports cheap abroad? The export tap again. Once you can see the taps and drains, you can place any headline in the diagram — and that is what an examiner is really testing.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: the falling level traced round by round, why a settled bath can still be a poor one, the capacity warning, and headlines matched to the tap or drain they move.
