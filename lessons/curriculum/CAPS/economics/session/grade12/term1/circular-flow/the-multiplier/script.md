# Part 1 — Expert

The circular flow established that an injection raises national income. This session establishes by HOW MUCH — and the answer is never rand for rand. A single rand of new spending is received as income, part of it is spent again, that spending is received as income again, and the original rand echoes through the economy several times before it dies out. That echo is the MULTIPLIER: the syllabus's one genuinely quantitative macroeconomic tool, and the reason a finance minister's spending announcement is worth more than its own number.

## Subtopic: Autonomous Spending, the MPC and the MPS

Three definitions must be exact, because the formula is built out of them and markers award the definition before the calculation.

AUTONOMOUS consumption is spending that does not depend on current income — the food, transport and rent a household buys even when income falls to nothing, financed by dissaving, borrowing, grants or family. INDUCED consumption is the part that DOES vary with income. Written as a consumption function: C = C₀ + cY, where C₀ is autonomous consumption and c is the fraction of each extra rand of income that is consumed.

That fraction is the MARGINAL PROPENSITY TO CONSUME — the MPC — defined as the change in consumption divided by the change in income: MPC = ΔC ÷ ΔY. Its partner is the MARGINAL PROPENSITY TO SAVE — the MPS — the change in saving divided by the change in income. In the simple model where income is either spent or saved, MPC + MPS = 1.

Work one. A household's income rises by R2 000 a month and its spending rises by R1 500. MPC = 1 500 ÷ 2 000 = 0,75. MPS = 500 ÷ 2 000 = 0,25. The two sum to 1, as they must.

Two cautions that separate careful candidates from careless ones. First, MARGINAL is not AVERAGE: the MPC concerns the extra rand, not the whole income; a household may spend ninety percent of its total income but only seventy-five percent of an increase. Second, the MPC is systematically HIGHER for poorer households — a grant recipient spends essentially every additional rand, while a high-income earner banks a large share of a bonus. That single fact does real work later: it is why the DESTINATION of an injection changes its power, and it is a point worth making in any essay on redistribution.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: autonomous against induced consumption, the two marginal propensities computed from a change in income, the rule that they sum to one, and marginal kept apart from average.

## Subtopic: The Multiplier — Formula and Derivation

Now build the tool. Suppose an injection of R1 000 — a business buys equipment, or government pays for road repairs. The builder receives R1 000 as income. With an MPC of 0,8 she spends R800, which becomes somebody else's income; that person spends R640; the next R512; and so on, each round smaller by the factor 0,8.

Total income created is the sum of the series: 1 000 + 800 + 640 + 512 + … Because each term is 0,8 of the one before, the series converges, and its sum is the first term divided by (1 − 0,8) — that is, 1 000 ÷ 0,2 = R5 000. One rand of injection produced five rands of income.

Hence the MULTIPLIER, symbol k:

k = 1 ÷ (1 − MPC), which is the same as k = 1 ÷ MPS

and the working relationship:

ΔY = k × ΔJ, where ΔJ is the change in the injection.

Check the identity on our numbers: MPC 0,8 gives MPS 0,2; 1 ÷ 0,2 = 5. The two forms agree because MPC and MPS sum to one.

Read the formula for what it says: the multiplier is large when people spend a large share of extra income and small when they save it. The leakage is what stops the echo — every rand that does not get re-spent leaves the chain and ends that branch of it.

Describe the diagram in words, because a graph question can ask for it. Draw total spending on the vertical axis and national income on the horizontal, with a 45-degree line where spending equals income. The aggregate spending line starts at autonomous spending on the vertical axis and rises with a slope equal to the MPC; equilibrium is where it cuts the 45-degree line. Now shift the spending line UP by the injection — a vertical shift equal to ΔJ. Because the spending line is flatter than the 45-degree line, the new crossing point lies further to the right than the vertical shift alone: the HORIZONTAL movement in income exceeds the VERTICAL shift in spending, and the ratio between them is the multiplier. A steeper spending line — a bigger MPC — pushes the crossing further right still.

Stop for this section's questions now — the series and its sum, both forms of the formula, ΔY = k × ΔJ, and the 45-degree diagram described in words.

## Subtopic: Working the Multiplier with Rand Figures

Examinations set the multiplier as calculation, so drill the sequence: state the formula, substitute, calculate, then INTERPRET. The interpretation sentence carries a mark of its own.

Case one. Government announces R6 billion of additional infrastructure spending. The MPC is 0,75. Formula: k = 1 ÷ (1 − 0,75) = 1 ÷ 0,25 = 4. Then ΔY = 4 × R6 billion = R24 billion. Interpretation: national income rises by R24 billion, four times the original injection, as the initial spending is re-spent round after round.

Case two, running the formula backwards, which is the harder examination variant. National income rose by R30 billion after an injection of R7,5 billion. The multiplier is 30 ÷ 7,5 = 4, so 1 ÷ (1 − MPC) = 4, giving 1 − MPC = 0,25 and MPC = 0,75. From figures to behaviour: households spent three-quarters of each additional rand.

Case three, the realistic South African version. In an open economy with a government, income leaks THREE ways, so the denominator carries all three marginal propensities:

k = 1 ÷ (MPS + MPT + MPM)

where MPT is the marginal propensity to tax and MPM the marginal propensity to import. Take MPS 0,10, MPT 0,25 and MPM 0,15. The leakages sum to 0,50, so k = 1 ÷ 0,50 = 2. The same R6 billion of infrastructure spending now raises national income by only R12 billion, not R24 billion.

That contrast is the lesson's most examinable point. An economy that taxes heavily and imports a large share of what it consumes has a SMALL multiplier — much of every new rand exits the country or returns to the treasury instead of circulating. It follows that the COMPOSITION of spending matters: money spent on locally produced services such as construction labour, schooling or maintenance circulates further than money spent on imported machinery, because its import leakage is smaller.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — the multiplier computed forwards and backwards, the three-leakage version applied, and the interpretation sentence written out in full.

## Subtopic: Leakages, Limits and the Reverse Gear

The multiplier is powerful and it is conditional. State the conditions, because uncritical use of the tool costs marks in discussion questions.

Condition one: SPARE CAPACITY. The multiplier expands real output only if factories, land and workers are standing idle so that extra demand can be met with extra production. In an economy already at capacity, the same injection meets a fixed quantity of goods and lifts PRICES instead — the multiplier then multiplies inflation, not employment. South Africa's large stock of unemployed labour is precisely why demand-side stimulus is argued for here; the argument weakens the closer the economy runs to its constraints, and its binding constraints may be electricity, ports and rail rather than labour.

Condition two: TIME. The rounds are not instantaneous. Income earned this quarter is spent the next, so the full effect takes many months — which is why stimulus often arrives after the downswing it was meant to fight.

Condition three: FINANCE. If government borrows heavily to fund the injection, interest rates may rise and private investment fall — crowding out, which offsets part of the multiplier's gain.

Now the reverse gear, which candidates routinely forget. The multiplier works DOWNWARDS with equal force. A fall in exports of R5 billion in an economy with a multiplier of 2 reduces national income by R10 billion: the mine cuts shifts, the retrenched spend less, the retailers order less, and the contraction compounds. Every recession in the syllabus is a multiplier running backwards, and every set of automatic stabilisers — progressive tax that falls with income, unemployment insurance that pays out when work stops — exists to shrink the downward multiplier by keeping leakages and spending steadier than they would otherwise be.

Finally, the multiplier's partner in the business-cycle chapter: the ACCELERATOR. Rising consumer demand induces firms to invest in new capacity, and that investment is itself an injection, which multiplies again. Multiplier and accelerator feeding each other is a standard explanation of why upswings and downswings gather their own momentum.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the three conditions stated, the downward multiplier computed on a fall in exports, automatic stabilisers explained as multiplier-dampers, and the accelerator linked to the cycle.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same idea from a pay packet on a Friday in a small town — because the multiplier is really just the story of what happens to one rand after it lands.

## Subtopic: One Rand, Many Pockets

Government fixes the road through a small Eastern Cape town and pays a local crew R1 000 for the week's work. Follow that thousand rand.

The bricklayer takes it home. She keeps R200 in the bank for her daughter's school fees and spends R800 — meat, mielie meal, paraffin, taxi fare. That R800 is now the spaza owner's income. He, too, keeps a fifth and spends R640 restocking from the wholesaler and paying his helper. The wholesaler keeps a fifth and spends R512. The helper spends part of hers at the tavern, and the tavern owner spends part of his at the barber.

Stop and count what one thousand rand has now done. It has been INCOME to a bricklayer, a spaza owner, a wholesaler, a helper, a tavern owner and a barber — six people paid, from one payment. Add the whole chain and the town's total income rises by five thousand rand: one thousand, then eight hundred, then six hundred and forty, then five hundred and twelve, on and on until the amounts are too small to notice.

That is the whole idea. A rand does not stop when it is spent; it becomes somebody's income and goes round again. Economists call the total effect the multiplier — but the town already knew it. Ask any shop owner near a big construction site what happens to their takings when the site opens.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the thousand rand are coming to you right now: the chain of six pockets, the shrinking rounds, and the total the town ended up with.

## Subtopic: Why the Chain Gets Shorter Each Time

Now the important half: WHY does each round get smaller, and what decides how small?

Because at every stop, a slice of the rand steps out of the chain. Three exits. Some gets SAVED — sitting in an account, not at a till. Some goes in TAX — VAT at the counter, tax on the wage. And some buys something IMPORTED — a Chinese kettle, imported fuel, foreign-made cooking oil — so that money leaves the country entirely and never becomes anybody's income here again.

The bigger those slices, the sooner the chain dies. Say a fifth is saved, a quarter goes to tax and fifteen cents in every rand buys imported things: half of every rand steps out at each stop. Then a thousand rand of road repairs adds only two thousand to the country's income, not five. Same road, same wage — but half the money leaves the chain each round instead of a fifth.

Two things follow, and they are the whole reason politicians argue about budgets. First, WHO gets the money matters. Give a rand to a household with nothing and almost all of it gets spent — the chain runs long. Give the same rand to a household that already has plenty and much of it goes straight into savings — the chain runs short. Second, WHAT the money buys matters. Wages for local builders, teachers and cleaners circulate through local shops. A single imported machine pays a factory in another country, and our chain ends after one link.

And the same logic runs in reverse, which is the part that hurts. When a mine cuts shifts, the lost wage is not just a lost wage: the spaza loses a customer, the wholesaler loses an order, the barber loses a client. One closure empties several tills.

Your questions for this part are up now: the three exits from the chain, how they shorten it, why the destination and the content of spending change the total, and the chain running backwards.

## Subtopic: What the Chain Means on Budget Day

Last piece — turning the story into the sentences that earn marks.

Whenever a budget promises new spending, ask three questions and you will sound like an economist. Is there SPARE CAPACITY? If workers are idle and factories half-empty, extra spending gets met with extra production and real jobs. If everything is already running flat out — or the trains and the power stations cannot carry more — the extra money just chases the same goods and PRICES rise. Same rand, opposite result.

How much LEAKS? Add up the saving, the tax and the import slice. A small leak means a long chain and a big total; a big leak means the money is gone after a round or two. This is why "buy local" is an economic argument and not only a patriotic one.

And how LONG does it take? Each round takes weeks or months, so a stimulus announced today lands in the shops over the following year — sometimes after the trouble it was meant to fix has passed.

Then the fair objection, which strong answers always mention: where did the money come from? If government borrowed it, it must be repaid with interest, and heavy borrowing can push interest rates up so that businesses build less — one hand giving while the other takes.

So the honest summary. One rand of spending is worth more than one rand of income, because it goes round. How much more depends on how much leaks out at each stop, whether there is room to produce more, and how the spending was funded. Say those three things about any budget announcement, and you have written a Grade 12 answer.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: spare capacity, leakage and timing applied to a spending announcement, and the funding objection stated in your own words. Take this one carefully — the multiplier turns up again in business cycles, in fiscal policy and in the growth essays.
