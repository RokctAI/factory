Hey, it's Mandy — the public sector chapter looks like a mountain of lists, and I know the temptation is to memorise all of them the night before and hope. Don't. The whole topic is four questions, asked in order, and each list answers exactly one of them.

Question one: why does the state exist in the economy? Answer with pairs — each market failure matched to a state role. Public goods, so the state provides; the streetlight is your example, non-rivalrous and non-excludable, killed by the free-rider. Externalities, so the state taxes harms and subsidises merit goods like education. Monopoly, so the state regulates. Unequal incomes, so the state redistributes through progressive tax and grants. Four pairs, four marks each time.

Question two: why is providing hard? Five problems: accountability, efficiency, assessing needs, pricing, and the parastatals. If you blank, think of one municipal office and one water bill — the missing daily vote, the guessing of needs, the stepped tariff — and the list rebuilds itself from the story.

Question three: how does the budget work? In, out, gap. In: income tax, VAT, company tax, excise — sort them direct on income, indirect on spending. Out: grants, education, health, and the interest line that grows fastest. Gap: the deficit, financed by bonds. And the Laffer curve is just a hump: zero revenue at zero percent, zero at a hundred, a crest somewhere between — and the honest extra sentence, nobody knows where the crest is.

Question four: what happens when the state itself fails? Causes — management, apathy, bureaucracy, politics, corruption — then effects, always ending with who suffers most: those who cannot buy private substitutes.

Close every essay with the balance: markets fail, so the state exists; states fail, so the state must be watched. Four questions, four answers, one closing sentence. You've got this!
