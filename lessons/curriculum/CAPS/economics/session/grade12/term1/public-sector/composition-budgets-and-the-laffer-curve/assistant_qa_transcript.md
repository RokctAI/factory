**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! The first one comes up every single year: if government can just tax us to pay for things, why does it borrow at all? Why not raise taxes instead of running a deficit?

**Tutor:** Because taxing and borrowing are not interchangeable — they take from different people at different times, and each has a limit the other does not. Start with timing. A deficit in a downswing is deliberate: raising taxes in a recession would drain spending exactly when the multiplier needs feeding, deepening the trough government is trying to fill. Borrowing lets the state spend into the downswing and, in principle, repay in the fat years — that is expansionary fiscal policy working as designed. Second, the nature of the spending matters. A port or a power station yields benefits for fifty years, so there is a fairness logic in letting fifty years of taxpayers service the loan rather than loading the whole cost onto this year's workers. The trouble begins when borrowing funds CONSUMPTION rather than assets — salaries, interest on old debt — year after year through every phase of the cycle. That is the structural deficit, and it converts borrowing from a bridge into a treadmill. Third, remember the Laffer discipline: taxes cannot simply be raised at will, because past some point higher rates smother effort, push activity informal and collect less. So the honest answer is: borrow for downswings and durable assets, tax for ongoing services, and fear the moment debt-service costs start outgrowing the education budget — which is precisely the moment South Africa's February speeches keep warning about.

**Mandy:** Next one: my cousin says just privatise everything — Eskom, the Post Office, all of it — because business does everything better. Is that the exam answer?

**Tutor:** It is half an exam answer, and halves score halves. Run the argument properly in both directions. The case FOR privatising rests on the provisioning problems we listed: a private owner faces the daily vote of customers and the discipline of profit, so waste gets punished, capital gets raised on markets instead of from taxpayers, and the fiscus is relieved of bailouts. Where a genuine competitive market exists for the product, that case is strong. But now the other direction. First ask WHY the state owned the thing at all. If the enterprise is a natural monopoly — one national grid, one rail network — privatising does not create competition; it creates a private monopoly, which will price like one unless heavily regulated, and then you have traded provisioning problems for regulation problems. Second, the social mandates: the distant village wired at a loss, the rural post route. A private owner sheds these first, rationally — so either they go unserved or government must pay the private firm to serve them, which is not obviously cheaper. Third, a fire sale in a weak bargaining position can hand a public asset to connected buyers cheaply — swapping public sector failure for a different corruption. So the distinction answer: privatise where competition is possible and the mandate is commercial; regulate or restructure where monopoly and social goals dominate; and in every case the real variable is not ownership but ACCOUNTABILITY. State both sides, then judge the specific case — that is what the twenty marks are for.

**Mandy:** This one always causes a fight: VAT is the same fifteen percent for everyone, so surely it's the fairest tax we have. Why do the notes call it regressive?

**Tutor:** Because fairness measured in RATES and fairness measured in BURDEN are different things, and economists measure burden — the share of a person's income a tax takes. Walk the numbers. A domestic worker earning R4 000 a month spends essentially all of it — she must, to live. If her whole R4 000 passes through the till, VAT touches nearly every rand, so the tax claims close to its full fifteen percent of her INCOME. An executive earning R200 000 a month might spend half and save or invest the rest; VAT touches only the spent half, so as a share of income the same fifteen percent rate takes roughly seven and a half percent from him against nearly fifteen from her. The rate is flat; the burden falls as income rises — that is the definition of regressive. It is why the system zero-rates maize meal, brown bread, rice and other basics: to pull the poorest household's essential basket out of the net. Contrast this with personal income tax, which is progressive by design — higher brackets pay higher rates — and you see why a tax SYSTEM is judged as a whole: the progressive income tax and the grants it funds are meant to more than offset VAT's regressive bite. In an essay, define regressive precisely, do the two-household comparison, mention zero-rating, and you will have turned the class argument into an eight-mark answer.

**Mandy:** Last one: the Laffer curve seems to say cutting taxes raises revenue. So why doesn't the Minister just cut all the rates every February and collect more?

**Tutor:** Because the curve says that only on ONE side of the hump, and the whole argument turns on which side you are standing — which nobody can observe directly. Read the logic again. Revenue rises with the rate while the discouragement effects are small; it peaks at some optimum; beyond that, higher rates so shrink the taxed activity — less work offered, more avoidance, more evasion, more emigration of skills, more business done informally — that the government's slice of a shrinking pie gets smaller in absolute terms. A cut raises revenue ONLY if the current rate sits beyond the optimum. If you are below the crest, cutting rates simply collects less, and the deficit widens. So the Minister's real task is empirical, not geometric: look for the fingerprints of the far side. Are avoidance schemes multiplying? Is the emigration of high earners visible in the returns? Did the last rate increase bring in materially less than projected? South Africa's own Treasury reported exactly that warning sign after a top-bracket increase in the late 2010s — collections underperformed, suggesting the top of the personal income tax hump is nearer than assumed at the highest incomes. But at fifteen percent, VAT is nowhere near any such ceiling. That is the mature exam sentence: the Laffer curve is a caution about the EXISTENCE of a revenue-maximising rate, not a promise that every cut pays for itself — and its location differs by tax, by taxpayer, and by how easy escape is.
