# Part 1 — Expert

The circular flow placed government inside the economy as one of the four participants; this session opens that participant up. The PUBLIC SECTOR takes roughly one rand in every three that moves through South Africa's economy, and Paper 1 asks four things about it: what it is made of and why markets need it, why providing public services is genuinely difficult, how the budget and fiscal policy work — including the one curve this topic owns, the Laffer curve — and what happens when the state itself fails. This is also the topic where current South African debates — state-owned enterprises, the deficit, the grant system — become examinable material.

## Subtopic: Composition and Necessity of the Public Sector

Composition first, in three tiers plus one outer ring. NATIONAL government: parliament and the national departments — education, health, police, defence — making policy for the whole country. PROVINCIAL government: the nine provinces, delivering most schooling and hospital care. LOCAL government: the municipalities, responsible for water, electricity distribution, refuse and municipal roads. Around them stand the STATE-OWNED ENTERPRISES — Eskom generating electricity, Transnet running rail and ports, the SABC, the Post Office — public entities producing goods and services directly.

Now necessity: WHY must this machinery exist in a market economy at all? Because markets, left alone, fail in known ways, and each failure maps to a public-sector role — learn them as pairs. Markets cannot supply PUBLIC GOODS — goods that are NON-RIVALROUS, one person's use leaving no less for another, and NON-EXCLUDABLE, non-payers cannot be fenced out. Street lighting and national defence protect everyone or no one, so no private firm can charge for them: the FREE-RIDER problem. Government must provide them from taxes. Markets underprice EXTERNALITIES, so government taxes harms and subsidises goods with social spillovers, such as basic education and vaccination — MERIT GOODS that markets under-provide and DEMERIT goods, alcohol and tobacco, that they over-provide. Markets tolerate MONOPOLY, so the state regulates competition. And markets distribute income by productivity and property, not need, so government REDISTRIBUTES — progressive taxation on one side, social grants on the other, reaching a large share of all South African households. Add stabilisation — the fiscal policy of the business-cycles topic — and the state's economic roles are complete.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: the three tiers with their functions, state-owned enterprises named, public goods defined by their two properties, the free-rider problem, and each market failure paired with the public-sector role that answers it.

## Subtopic: Problems of Provisioning and the Objectives of the Public Sector

The syllabus insists that public provisioning is HARD, and lists the reasons — learn them as five recurring problems.

First, ACCOUNTABILITY. The link between paying and receiving is cut: taxpayers fund services they may never use, and officials spend money that is not theirs. Voters discipline governments only once every five years, where customers discipline a shop daily. Hence auditors-general, parliamentary committees and public protests as substitute disciplines. Second, EFFICIENCY. Without competition or a profit test, there is no automatic penalty for waste; a department that overspends is often rewarded with a larger budget. Third, ASSESSING NEEDS. A market reads demand off prices; the state must GUESS need through surveys, censuses and politics — and the loudest voice is not always the greatest need. Fourth, PRICING. What should a clinic visit, a unit of municipal water or a university year cost the user? Free provision invites over-use and starves supply of funds; full-cost pricing excludes the poor. South Africa's answer is often a stepped compromise — free basic water and electricity, then rising block tariffs. Fifth, the PARASTATAL problem: state-owned enterprises straddle both worlds, expected to run commercially yet serve social goals, and the tension shows — Eskom's debt and load-shedding are the standing example. This is where the PRIVATISATION debate lives: selling state assets promises efficiency and fiscal relief but risks higher prices and lost social mandates; NATIONALISATION promises control and social goals but concentrates the very provisioning problems just listed.

Against the problems, the OBJECTIVES the public sector pursues — the macroeconomic scoreboard: ECONOMIC GROWTH, real GDP rising; FULL EMPLOYMENT, work for all who seek it; PRICE STABILITY, inflation inside the three-to-six percent target; EXCHANGE-RATE STABILITY, a rand steady enough for traders to plan; and ECONOMIC EQUITY, narrowing the gap between rich and poor. Memorise all five: listing and explaining them is a standing eight-mark question.

Stop for this section's questions now — the five provisioning problems each with its mechanism, the parastatal tension with its current example, the privatisation argument both ways, and the five objectives.

## Subtopic: The Budget, Fiscal Policy and the Laffer Curve

The public sector's master document is the NATIONAL BUDGET, tabled by the Minister of Finance each February: a plan of REVENUE — where the money comes from — and EXPENDITURE — where it goes.

Revenue: PERSONAL INCOME TAX, the largest single source, levied progressively; VAT at fifteen percent, broad and reliable but regressive in effect since the poor spend a larger share of their income, softened by zero-rating basics such as maize meal and brown bread; COMPANY TAX on profits; excise duties on fuel, alcohol and tobacco; customs duties. Distinguish DIRECT taxes, levied on income — personal and company tax — from INDIRECT taxes, levied on spending — VAT and excise. Expenditure: the largest allocations run to social development and grants, basic education, health, debt-service costs, and policing — with DEBT-SERVICE COSTS the fastest-growing item, crowding the rest.

Three budget balances. A BALANCED budget: revenue equals spending. A SURPLUS: revenue exceeds spending. A DEFICIT: spending exceeds revenue — the normal South African case, financed by issuing government bonds, which accumulate as PUBLIC DEBT. A deficit in a downswing is expansionary fiscal policy doing its work; a deficit that persists through every phase of the cycle is a STRUCTURAL deficit, and it is why debt service now competes with classrooms for rands.

Now the topic's signature diagram, described in words: the LAFFER CURVE. Draw tax RATE on the horizontal axis, from zero to one hundred percent, and tax REVENUE on the vertical. At a rate of zero, revenue is zero — nothing is levied. At one hundred percent, revenue is again zero — no one works or declares income to be taxed away entirely. Between the two ends the curve rises from zero to a single hump and falls back: revenue climbs with the rate up to some optimum, then declines as ever-higher rates smother effort, drive activity into the informal economy and reward avoidance and evasion. The examinable reading: raising rates raises revenue ONLY on the rising side of the hump; past the optimum, CUTTING rates can raise revenue. The honest caveat, worth a mark: nobody knows precisely where the optimum sits, so the curve is an argument for caution, not a precise instruction.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — main revenue sources with direct against indirect, the big spending items, the three balances with deficit financing, and the Laffer curve drawn in words and read correctly on both sides of its hump.

## Subtopic: Public Sector Failure

Markets fail — that is why the public sector exists. But the state fails too, and the syllabus requires the concept, its causes and its effects.

PUBLIC SECTOR FAILURE occurs when government intervention, meant to improve on the market, instead allocates resources LESS efficiently — when the cure performs worse than the disease. The causes flow from the provisioning problems, sharpened. MANAGEMENT failure: officials appointed without the skills the job demands — the municipal engineer post filled by patronage, the water plant that no one can maintain. APATHY and lack of motivation: no competition, no consequence for poor service, so the queue moves at the pace of indifference. BUREAUCRACY: rules layered on rules until compliance replaces purpose — the licence that takes a year, the tender process that outlasts the need. POLITICS overriding economics: projects sited for votes, not returns; spending timed for elections. CORRUPTION: public money diverted to private pockets — procurement fraud and state capture as the recent South African record names it. And UNINTENDED CONSEQUENCES even of well-meant policy: a price control that empties shelves, a subsidy that entrenches the firms it was meant to wean.

The EFFECTS complete the essay. Resources are misallocated — money spent where it produces least. Services fail the people who depend on them most, since the wealthy buy private substitutes for schooling, health and security while the poor cannot. Taxpayers lose willingness to pay, feeding avoidance and shrinking the base — the Laffer logic arriving by the back door. Investment is deterred, because firms will not build where electricity, rail and licensing cannot be relied on. And the state's own credibility erodes, making every future intervention harder.

Balance the conclusion, because balance is what the twenty-mark essays reward: market failure justifies a public sector; public sector failure disciplines it. The question is never simply state or market, but which failure is worse in the case at hand, and how each can be corrected — competition and regulation for the market's failures; transparency, accountability, skills and consequence management for the state's.

The final questions of this part are with you now: public sector failure defined, its causes with concrete mechanisms, its effects traced to the people who bear them, and the balanced conclusion stated.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same topic from one street in one town — because the public sector is not an abstraction; it is the water in the tap, the light over the road, the grant on the second of the month.

## Subtopic: What the Street Cannot Buy for Itself

Walk down a single street on a weekday morning and sort everything you see into two piles: things somebody bought, and things nobody COULD buy alone.

The bread in the bakery, the taxi ride, the airtime — private goods. You pay, you get; you don't pay, you don't. The market handles these beautifully, because the seller can exclude non-payers.

Now the streetlight. When it burns, it burns for everyone — the woman walking to the early shift, the shopkeeper opening up, the learner catching the first taxi. Nobody can be fenced out of its light, and one person's share leaves no less for the next. So ask: who would voluntarily pay for it? Each neighbour reasons the same way — if the others pay, I get the light free; if they don't, my contribution is wasted. Everyone waits for everyone. Economists call it the FREE-RIDER problem, and the streetlight stays dark until something with the power to make everyone pay — government, through taxes — switches it on. Same logic for the police patrol, the storm-water drain, the road itself.

Then the pile in between. The clinic and the school COULD charge fees, and private ones do. But a child's vaccination protects the whole street from the disease, and an educated street earns more, commits fewer crimes, votes more wisely. The benefits spill past the buyer — so left to the market alone, the street buys too little of them. That is why government provides them anyway, and calls them merit goods.

And the grant that arrives on the second of the month: not a purchase at all, but the market's income distribution corrected — because the market pays what your labour fetches, not what your family needs.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the street are coming to you right now: the two piles told apart, the streetlight's two properties, the free-rider standoff in your own words, and why the clinic sits in between.

## Subtopic: Why the Tap Runs Slow — the Trouble with Providing

If government must provide these things, why is it so often done badly? Not because officials are uniquely wicked — because the job is missing the disciplines that keep the bakery sharp.

The bakery faces a daily vote: bad bread today, empty till tomorrow. The licensing office faces no such vote — its customers cannot go elsewhere, its funding arrives whether the queue moves or not, and the next real election is years away. Nobody chose laziness; the PENALTY for it is simply missing.

The bakery also knows exactly what to bake, because yesterday's sales tell it. The department must guess: how many houses does this district need, how many nurses, how much water? No price tells it; surveys, censuses and politicians' promises must, and the loudest ward is not the neediest one.

Then the pricing puzzle, visible in every municipal bill. Give water away free and people water driveways while the reservoir empties and there is no money for pipes. Charge full cost and grandmothers cannot cook. So the compromise: the first six kilolitres free, then each extra block dearer — free basic need, expensive luxury use. Every stepped tariff you have ever seen is this puzzle, answered.

And the hybrid creatures, the parastatals. Eskom must run like a business — sell electricity, cover costs — while serving goals no business would choose, like wiring distant villages. Serve the social goal and the balance sheet bleeds; serve the balance sheet and the villages go dark. When the tension breaks, the whole country sits in the dark with it — that is why load-shedding turns up in this chapter, not just in the news.

Your questions for this part are up now: the missing daily vote, guessing need without prices, the water-tariff compromise explained, and the parastatal tension in one sentence.

## Subtopic: February's Envelope and the Tax That Bites Back

Last piece — the budget, made small enough to hold.

Every February the Minister of Finance stands up with one giant envelope: what will come in, and where it will go. In: mostly income tax from workers, VAT from every till slip, tax on company profits, and the extra duties hiding in fuel, beer and cigarettes. Out: grants, schools, clinics, police — and a line that should worry you, INTEREST on old borrowing, one of the fastest-growing items of all. When the outgo beats the income — most years — the difference is the DEFICIT, and it is borrowed. Borrowing is not sin: borrowing to build a port can pay for itself. But borrowing every year just to pay salaries and interest means this year's classroom is funded by next year's taxpayer — you.

Now the famous curve, as a story. Suppose the street's spaza is taxed at zero percent: government collects nothing. Tax it at one hundred percent: the owner shuts the shop or sells at the back door — government again collects nothing. Somewhere between sits the rate that collects the most; push past it and each extra percent brings in LESS, because people work less, hide more, emigrate, or slip into the informal economy. Picture the hump: revenue climbing as the rate rises, cresting, then sliding. That is the Laffer curve, and its one honest lesson: a higher rate is not automatically higher revenue. Where exactly the crest sits, nobody knows — say that too, it earns the evaluation mark.

And when the envelope is spent badly — the tender inflated, the project unfinished, the skilled post filled by a friend — remember the topic's closing balance: markets fail, which is why the state exists; states fail, which is why the state must be watched. An answer that sees both failures, and says how each is disciplined, is a distinction answer.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: the envelope's ins and outs, the deficit and who repays it, the spaza-tax story told over the hump, and the closing balance in your own words. Carry this topic with you — it feeds directly into fiscal policy questions, the growth essays, and every headline about the February budget.
