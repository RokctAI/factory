**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! Here's the perennial one: if government can tax us for whatever it needs, why does it borrow at all? Why not just raise taxes and skip the deficit?

**Tutor:** Because taxing and borrowing are different instruments that reach into different pockets at different moments, and each has a ceiling the other lacks. Consider timing first. A deficit run during a downswing is deliberate medicine: raising taxes in a recession would siphon off spending at the exact moment the multiplier needs feeding, deepening the very trough policy is trying to fill. Borrowing lets the state spend into the slump and repay — in principle — out of the fat years. That is expansionary fiscal policy doing its designed job. Second, match the financing to the lifetime of the thing bought. A port or a power station delivers benefits for half a century, so there is real fairness in letting fifty years of taxpayers share the bill through loan repayments rather than dumping the entire cost on this year's workers. The rot sets in when borrowing pays for CONSUMPTION — wages, and interest on the previous borrowing — every year, through booms and busts alike. That is the structural deficit, and it turns borrowing from a bridge into a treadmill. Third, remember the Laffer discipline from today: tax rates cannot simply be cranked upward at will, because beyond some point higher rates shrink the activity being taxed and collect less. So the composed answer runs: borrow for downswings and durable assets, tax for recurring services, and treat it as an alarm bell when the interest line starts growing faster than the education line — which is precisely the warning inside recent February speeches.

**Mandy:** Next: my uncle insists the fix for everything is to privatise the lot — power, ports, post. Sell it all and let business run it. Is he right?

**Tutor:** He is holding one half of a two-handed argument, and half-arguments earn half-marks. Build the case FOR first, honestly. Every provisioning problem we listed is an argument for private ownership somewhere: a private owner faces the daily election of customers and the discipline of profit, so waste is punished instead of budgeted for; capital comes from markets rather than taxpayers; and the fiscus escapes the bailout cycle. Where a competitive market for the product genuinely exists, that case is powerful. Now the other hand. Begin by asking WHY the state owned the enterprise in the first place. If it is a natural monopoly — one national grid, one rail network — selling it does not conjure competition; it merely creates a private monopoly, which will price like one unless a strong regulator sits on it, and then you have swapped provisioning problems for regulating problems. Next, the social mandates: the village electrified at a loss, the rural mail run. A private owner rationally drops these first, so either they vanish or the state must pay the private firm to keep them — not obviously cheaper than doing it in-house. And a rushed sale from a weak fiscal position can pass public assets cheaply to connected buyers, which trades one form of failure for another. So the distinction-level answer: privatise where competition can actually exist and the mandate is commercial; regulate or restructure where monopoly and social goals dominate; and observe that the deciding variable is rarely ownership itself — it is accountability. Argue both hands, then judge the specific case in front of you.

**Mandy:** This one causes a fight every time: VAT is fifteen percent for everybody, rich or poor. Flat rate, same rules — how is that not the fairest tax in the system?

**Tutor:** Because fairness has two measurements — the RATE and the BURDEN — and economists judge taxes by burden: the share of a person's income the tax ends up taking. Put two households side by side. A cleaner earning four thousand rand a month must spend virtually all of it to live, so nearly her whole income passes through tills where VAT waits; the tax claims something close to its full fifteen percent of her INCOME. A director earning two hundred thousand a month might spend half and bank the rest; VAT touches only the spent half, so as a share of income it takes roughly seven and a half percent from him — half the bite it takes from her. Identical rate, unequal burden, and burden falling as income rises is precisely the definition of REGRESSIVE. That is also why the basket of basics — maize meal, brown bread, rice and their companions — is zero-rated: it lifts the poorest household's essential spending out of the net entirely. Then complete the picture, because a tax SYSTEM is judged whole, not line by line: personal income tax is progressive by design, with higher brackets facing higher rates, and the grants it funds flow downward — so the system's progressive parts are intended to more than cancel VAT's regressive bite. In an answer: define regressive by burden, run the two-household comparison, cite zero-rating, then judge the system as a whole. That sequence turns a family argument into full marks.

**Mandy:** Last one: the Laffer curve says cutting tax rates can raise revenue. So why doesn't the Minister cut every rate every February and watch the money roll in?

**Tutor:** Because that promise holds on only ONE slope of the curve, and the entire practical question is which slope the country is standing on — something no one can read directly off any chart. Rehearse the logic. While rates are modest, raising them raises revenue, because the discouragement is small. Somewhere a crest is passed, and beyond it higher rates so shrink the taxed activity — fewer hours worked, more schemes of avoidance, more outright evasion, skills emigrating, business drifting informal — that government takes a bigger slice of a much smaller pie and ends up with less. A cut raises revenue ONLY if you are past the crest. Cut while you are still on the rising slope and you simply collect less and widen the deficit. So the Minister's problem is detective work, not geometry: hunt for fingerprints of the far slope. Are avoidance schemes multiplying at the top brackets? Did the last rate increase deliver materially less than projected? South Africa saw exactly that signal after a top-bracket increase in the late twenty-tens — collections undershot, hinting that the crest for the highest personal incomes is closer than once assumed. Meanwhile VAT at fifteen percent sits nowhere near any ceiling of that kind. The polished sentence for an essay: the Laffer curve establishes that a revenue-maximising rate EXISTS; it does not promise that any particular cut pays for itself — and the crest's position differs by tax, by taxpayer, and by how easily the taxed activity can escape.
