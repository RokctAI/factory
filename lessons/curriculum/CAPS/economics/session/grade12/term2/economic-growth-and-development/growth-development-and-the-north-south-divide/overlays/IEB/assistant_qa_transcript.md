**Mandy:** Class questions before we finish the capstone! First up: if the Human Development Index measures what actually matters — health, learning, living standards — why does anyone still bother with GDP at all?

**Tutor:** Keep both instruments, because each one sees what the other misses — and knowing WHY earns evaluation marks. GDP's virtues first. It is measured often — quarterly, not yearly — so it catches a recession while there is still time to respond; the HDI moves too slowly to steer by. It is comparable, built on the same accounting logic almost everywhere. And it measures the RESOURCE BASE: the taxes that pay for every clinic and classroom the HDI celebrates are levied on the income GDP counts. A country cannot fund development out of an index; it funds development out of output. Now GDP's blindness, which the HDI was invented to correct: GDP counts production, not distribution — a billionaire's gain and a million small wage rises look identical; it counts bads as goods — a crime wave that boosts spending on security counts as growth; it misses unpaid work — the grandmother raising three children adds nothing to it; and it says nothing about whether the income buys longer, healthier, freer lives. The HDI's own limits are worth one sentence too: three dimensions cannot carry everything — it omits inequality, safety and freedom unless extended versions are used. So the working method, and write this: GDP tells you the size of the engine; the HDI tells you whether the passengers are arriving. Any data question deserves both dials read together — and the countries where the two dials disagree are exactly where the interesting essays live.

***

**Mandy:** Next question, and it's the one everyone argues about at home: the grant system reaches millions of people. Is that the fire that saves us, or is it money we should be spending on the stove instead?

**Tutor:** Refuse the either-or — that refusal IS the economics. Start with what the evidence says grants actually do. They are the country's most direct poverty instrument: they cut the depth of poverty measurably, they keep children fed and in school — a grant that keeps a learner through matric is quietly a STOVE investment wearing fire's clothing — and they stabilise demand in every small-town economy where grant day is the busiest trading day of the month. The multiplier logic is genuine: poor households spend nearly every rand, locally. Now the limits, honestly. A grant is relief, not employment; it cannot train, hire or build. Financed from a strained budget, grants compete with the infrastructure and schooling that create the jobs that would make grants less necessary — that is the real tension in every budget speech. And the deeper point from the lesson: the fire-only household stays warm and stays poor; if grants substitute for stove-building rather than accompanying it, the dependency is structural, not personal. So the mature verdict runs in three sentences. One: in a country with our unemployment, withdrawing the fire would be both cruel and economically destabilising — the grants stay. Two: the fire alone transforms nothing — the test of policy is whether stove-building advances ALONGSIDE it, and for a decade the honest answer has been too slowly. Three: design matters — grants tied to school attendance and clinic visits turn relief into investment, which is exactly the proportion principle the lesson taught. Fire AND stove, deliberately linked: that is the answer.

**Mandy:** Third one: every plan since 1994 seems to say the same things — infrastructure, skills, capable state. If everyone already agrees on the diagnosis, why doesn't the economy just get fixed?

**Tutor:** Because diagnosis is the cheap half of medicine — and the gap between naming a constraint and removing one is where this country's last two decades actually happened. Take the constraints one at a time and ask what removal REQUIRES. Electricity: not a policy paper but a decade of engineering — plants maintained, grids extended, new capacity procured and connected — through institutions that were themselves being weakened while the papers were being written; capacity hollowed out by cadre appointments and procurement capture does not rebuild because a plan requests it. Skills: the failure sits in the foundations — a child who cannot read for meaning by age ten is largely lost to the artisan pipeline before any college can help — so the repair takes a school generation, longer than any political cycle, which is precisely why it keeps losing to projects that ribbon-cut before the next election. Logistics: rail and ports decayed through deferred maintenance and theft; reversing that needs sustained money, security and management, none of which a target in a document supplies. Notice the common thread: every constraint is slow, unglamorous, and requires the STATE CAPACITY that is itself the fourth constraint — the plans ask a weakened machine to strengthen itself, which is the hardest ask in public administration. So the exam-grade sentence: South Africa's growth problem long ago stopped being a mystery of diagnosis and became a challenge of implementation — plans converged on the same constraints precisely because the constraints were never removed, and the binding constraint beneath the others is the capability of the state itself. Then add the fairness clause: where implementation was protected — revenue collection rebuilt, treatment programmes scaled, stability defended — delivery followed, which proves the machine can work when it is allowed to.

**Mandy:** Last question: the north-south divide sounds like the south is simply doomed by history. Is there actually a way out — has anyone truly crossed from the street to the suburb?

**Tutor:** Yes — and the crossings are recent enough that your parents lived through them, which is what makes this the most hopeful section of the syllabus. South Korea in the 1950s was war-ruined and poorer than most of Africa; within two generations it moved from exporting wigs and plywood to exporting ships, cars and semiconductors, and today it is a fully developed economy. Singapore made the same crossing; Taiwan likewise. China lifted hundreds of millions of people out of poverty in the fastest development episode in recorded history, and Vietnam is running the same route now. So the gap is not destiny. Now extract the method, because the exam wants principles, not anecdotes. Every successful crossing shares four moves. First, education pushed hard and early — the crossers built schooling AHEAD of their income level, so human capital was ready when investment arrived. Second, an outward tilt: they earned their way up through exports, forcing their firms to meet world standards rather than sheltering behind walls forever — though note, most used temporary, disciplined protection on the way, shelter with expiry dates and export targets. Third, high savings and relentless investment in infrastructure — the stove built year after year regardless of politics. Fourth, a state capable enough to deliver all three consistently for decades. And be honest about the headwinds the early crossers did not face: today's rules leave less room for the industrial tricks they used, automation shrinks the low-wage factory ladder they climbed, and climate constraints tighten. That is exactly why the AfCFTA matters in your essays: scale is the one advantage the south can still build for itself — a continental market big enough to grow industries that no single small economy could raise alone. The closing line worth memorising: the divide is a fact, not a fate — but crossing it has never been an accident. It is built, deliberately, over decades, by exactly the fire-and-stove proportion this lesson taught.
