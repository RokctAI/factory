**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First: the news said our GDP grew last quarter, and everyone at home cheered. But you're telling me growth can happen while people get poorer. Which number should I actually watch?

**Tutor:** Watch three adjustments, in order, and you will read the news better than most of the people reporting it. Adjustment one: REAL, not nominal. If GDP in rands rose five percent while prices rose six, production actually shrank — always ask whether the figure quoted is after inflation. Adjustment two: PER PERSON. South Africa adds roughly one and a half percent more people each year, so real growth below that line means the average share is falling — and this is not a technicality: it is the story of our last decade, in which headlines cheered modest growth while real income per person quietly declined. A quarter of one percent growth with one and a half percent more mouths is a pay cut, nationally. Adjustment three, the deepest: DISTRIBUTION and content. GDP is an average over one of the world's most unequal income distributions, so the average can rise while the median household feels nothing; and growth driven by, say, a commodity price windfall can lift the total without touching employment. That is why you pair the payslip with the report card — employment figures, poverty rates, the HDI — before judging a year. So the disciplined answer to a data question: state the real growth rate, subtract population growth to get the per-capita truth, then ask who received the growth and what it was made of. Three sentences, and you have done what the syllabus means by distinguishing growth from development.

**Mandy:** Next: demand-side versus supply-side sounds like a boxing match — grants versus infrastructure, spending versus reform. In an essay, are we supposed to pick a winner?

**Tutor:** No — you are supposed to referee, and the refereeing has a structure you can learn. First round: state each approach's mechanism honestly. Demand-side works through spending and the multiplier: grants and public works into the pockets with the highest propensity to consume, low interest rates into credit, infrastructure spending doing double duty. In an economy with idle people and machines, it converts spending into jobs — that is not ideology, it is the arithmetic you have used all year. Supply-side works through capacity: electricity, logistics, schooling, competition, policy certainty — it shifts the frontier out rather than pushing spending against it. Second round: state each one's failure mode in South African conditions. Demand-side hits four walls — inflation when the constraint is supply, import leakage that shrinks the multiplier, debt service that eats the budget, and structure: spending cannot conjure unbuilt ports or untrained artisans. Supply-side has three: it is slow, its short-run costs land on the poorest first, and it must be executed by the same state whose capacity is part of the problem. Third round — and this is where the top marks live — refuse the either-or, on TIMING grounds. The two approaches answer different clocks: people must survive and spend through the present, which only demand-side protects; the future must be bigger than the present, which only supply-side delivers. A country doing only the first eats its seed corn; only the second starves the farmer before harvest. So the verdict is proportion and sequence: sustain demand enough to hold society together, while the supply-side building — which every South African plan since ASGISA has correctly named and incompletely done — actually happens. Write that, with the walls and the clocks named, and you have refereed rather than cheered.

**Mandy:** This one is uncomfortable but real: why is the gap between rich and poor countries so stubborn? Colonialism ended generations ago — shouldn't the gap have closed by now?

**Tutor:** The honest answer has two layers: the mechanisms that keep the gap open without needing anyone's malice, and the evidence that the gap is nonetheless closable — both belong in your essay. The mechanisms first, because the syllabus names them. Start where a poor country starts: exporting raw materials. Commodity prices swing violently and, over long stretches, drift down relative to the price of the manufactures the country must import — the terms-of-trade problem: the same tons of ore buy fewer machines each decade. Try to industrialise, and tariff escalation waits at the rich world's gate: raw cocoa enters nearly free, chocolate pays duty — the processing, where the value lives, is quietly discouraged. Borrow to build anyway, and the debt is denominated in currency you cannot print, so every depreciation doubles the burden, and servicing it eats the education budget — the very stove-building money. Meanwhile your best-trained people, whose schooling your taxes funded, emigrate to where their skills price higher — the brain drain, a subsidy from poor to rich perversely flowing uphill. Add rule-setting: the institutions that govern money and trade weight votes by wealth. None of this requires a villain; each mechanism is individually defensible, and together they form a track that runs downhill against the South. Now the second layer: the track can be beaten, because it has been. Korea and Taiwan were poorer than much of Africa in the 1950s; China moved hundreds of millions out of poverty in one generation — through disciplined supply-side building, conditional protection of infant industries, and ferocious investment in schooling. Their existence changes the question in your essay from whether the gap can close to what it costs to close it — and it is also why south-south trade, BRICS and the AfCFTA matter: they are the South changing the track rather than just running harder on it.

**Mandy:** Last one: after thirty years of RDP, GEAR, ASGISA and the NDP, my gran says the plans change but the potholes stay. For the exam, how critical are we allowed to be about South Africa's own endeavours?

**Tutor:** As critical as the evidence — which is very — provided the criticism is specific and two-sided, because an examiner rewards judgment, not mood. Structure it as achievements, failures, diagnosis. Achievements, stated without embarrassment: macroeconomic stability was won and defended — a credible Reserve Bank, functioning revenue collection, no hyperinflation, no default; and the social wage is among the developing world's largest — millions of grants paid monthly, houses built, electrification and water massively extended, the treatment programme that turned around life expectancy. Those are development achievements your gran's report card actually shows, and leaving them out is as inaccurate as leaving out the potholes. Failures, equally specific: growth has trailed population for over a decade, so real income per person fell; unemployment, especially for the young, sits among the highest measured anywhere; and inequality has not meaningfully narrowed. Then the diagnosis, which is where your gran's point about plans becomes an analytical sentence rather than a sigh: the plans were not wrong — ASGISA in 2006 and the NDP in 2012 both correctly named electricity, logistics, skills and state capacity as the binding constraints — but naming a constraint is not removing it, and in the years after the naming, load-shedding worsened, the ports and rail deteriorated, and the skills system underdelivered. So the failure is less of diagnosis than of EXECUTION, which points directly at the public-sector failure machinery you studied: procurement, patronage, hollowed capability. Close with the balanced verdict in one line: South Africa planned like the North and delivered like neither — the strategy documents earned their marks, and the potholes are the marks the delivery earned. That sentence is critical, specific, two-sided — and it is exactly the register the highest essay levels describe.
