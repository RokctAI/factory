# Part 1 — Expert

Everything in this course converges here. The circular flow measured the economy; the multiplier and business cycles explained its motion; the public sector and trade policy supplied the levers. This capstone topic asks the question the levers exist for: how does a country grow RICHER, and — a different question — how do its people come to live BETTER? The syllabus wants four things: growth distinguished from development and both measured; the demand-side approaches to raising both; the supply-side approaches, with an evaluation of the two; and South Africa's own endeavours, set against the global divide between north and south. This topic spans the June and trial examinations and stands behind the biggest essays on Paper 1.

## Subtopic: Growth Against Development — Two Different Questions

ECONOMIC GROWTH is the increase in a country's production of goods and services — measured as the annual percentage change in REAL GDP, real so that inflation cannot masquerade as progress. Growth per person requires real GDP to outrun population: with the population growing around one and a half percent a year, South African growth below that number means the average person is getting POORER even as the economy "grows" — a discipline every essay should state.

ECONOMIC DEVELOPMENT is broader: the process by which the QUALITY OF LIFE of a population rises — incomes, yes, but also health, education, housing, security, freedom of choice. Development is measured by indicators that look past production: life expectancy, infant mortality, literacy, access to water and electricity — and the composite HUMAN DEVELOPMENT INDEX, the HDI, which averages three dimensions: a long and healthy life, knowledge, and a decent standard of living.

The two usually travel together — growth funds the clinics and classrooms development needs — but they can and do part company, and the partings are examinable. Growth WITHOUT development: an economy expanding through capital-intensive mineral exports can lift GDP while employment, schooling and life expectancy stand still — the resource-enclave pattern, familiar across the developing world and visible in South Africa's own history, where growth long coexisted with exclusion. Development indicators can also move without growth: Cuba's health outcomes, or South Africa's own post-2004 rise in HDI driven partly by grants, housing and treatment programmes even through weak-growth years. The examiner's favourite line of attack: given GDP figures and social indicators for two countries, decide which is growing and which is developing — so always ask BOTH questions of any data set.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: both definitions with their measures, the real-GDP-against-population discipline, the HDI's three dimensions, and growth and development parting company in both directions.

## Subtopic: The Demand-Side Approaches

Demand-side approaches raise growth by raising SPENDING — the C plus I plus G plus X minus M of the circular flow — trusting the multiplier to translate spending into income and jobs. The instruments are the macroeconomic ones you already command. EXPANSIONARY FISCAL policy: infrastructure programmes, public employment, grants that flow straight into consumption — recall that the poor's high marginal propensity to consume makes transfers to them powerful spending fuel. EXPANSIONARY MONETARY policy: lower interest rates, easier credit, investment and durables responding. Plus export demand, courted through trade agreements and competitive exchange rates.

The case FOR, in South African terms, is strong on its face: with mass unemployment, spare capacity should exist, so extra demand should call forth output and jobs rather than prices — the multiplier argument in policy dress. Public employment programmes and the grant system demonstrably reduce poverty NOW, and infrastructure spending doubles as demand today and capacity tomorrow.

Now the EVALUATION, which is where the marks concentrate. Demand-side stimulus meets four South African walls. The INFLATION wall: if the constraint is not demand but supply — electricity, rail, skills — extra spending pushes prices, and the Reserve Bank answers with higher rates that cancel the stimulus. The IMPORT wall: our high marginal propensity to import means a large slice of any stimulus leaks abroad — the small-multiplier problem from term one. The DEBT wall: with debt service already among the budget's fastest-growing items, borrowing-financed stimulus competes with the very social spending it means to protect, and bond markets price the risk. And the STRUCTURE wall: demand cannot conjure the electrician who was never trained or the port that was never maintained. Summary sentence for essays: demand-side measures treat the symptoms quickly and visibly, but in an economy whose binding constraints are on the supply side, they buy relief, not transformation.

Stop for this section's questions now — the instruments mapped onto C, I, G and X, the spare-capacity case, and the four walls each in a sentence.

## Subtopic: The Supply-Side Approaches

Supply-side approaches raise the economy's CAPACITY to produce — shifting the production possibility frontier outward rather than pushing spending against it. Group the measures as the syllabus does.

The COST environment: reliable, affordable electricity, transport and communication; efficient ports and rail; less red tape and regulatory delay — every improvement lowers every producer's costs simultaneously, which is why infrastructure failure taxes the whole economy at once. HUMAN capital: schooling quality, TVET colleges, universities, health — a healthier, better-schooled workforce produces more per hour; skills shortages, conversely, cap every expansion plan. NATURAL and physical capital: investment incentives, research and development, technology adoption. EFFICIENCY of markets: competition policy against monopoly pricing, labour-market institutions that match workers to jobs, financial markets that fund small enterprise. And INCENTIVES: tax structures that reward production and investment — the Laffer logic applied gently — plus certainty of policy itself, since investment is a bet on the future and uncertainty is a tax on betting.

The case FOR: only capacity growth is durable — it raises the trend line rather than riding the cycle around it — and it attacks South Africa's actual binding constraints by name. The EVALUATION: supply-side measures are SLOW — a reformed school system pays off in a generation, a port in a decade; they can be SOCIALLY REGRESSIVE in the short run — labour-market flexibility and fiscal discipline have costs borne first by workers and grant recipients; and they depend on STATE CAPACITY — the same public-sector machinery whose failures you studied — so announcing reform is not performing it.

The syllabus asks you to EVALUATE THE APPROACHES against each other, and the mature answer is sequencing, not choice: demand-side measures protect people and sustain the economy through the present; supply-side measures decide whether the future is bigger than the present. A country doing only demand-side buys today at tomorrow's expense; only supply-side sacrifices the poor to a future they may not survive to see. Policy is the art of doing both in proportion — which is what the phrase used in the business-cycles topic, narrow the amplitude and raise the trend, means at national scale.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — the five measure-groups with examples, the durability case, the three weaknesses, and the sequencing evaluation.

## Subtopic: South Africa's Endeavours and the North-South Divide

South Africa's post-1994 growth strategies make a short, examinable history. The RDP — Reconstruction and Development Programme, 1994 — led with basic needs: housing, water, electrification; demand-side in spirit. GEAR — Growth, Employment and Redistribution, 1996 — swung supply-side: fiscal discipline, deficit reduction, trade opening, seeking investor confidence; it stabilised the budget but growth and jobs undershot its name. ASGISA, 2006, targeted binding constraints — infrastructure, skills — naming the supply-side diagnosis explicitly. The NDP — National Development Plan, 2012, the current framework — aims at 2030: eliminate poverty, cut inequality, through infrastructure, education, a capable state and faster growth. Judge the record honestly: macroeconomic stability achieved and defended; the social wage — grants, housing, water, electrification, treatment — a genuine development achievement; but growth itself has trailed population for over a decade, unemployment is among the world's highest, and the constraints named by every plan since ASGISA — electricity, logistics, skills, state capacity — remain the constraints. The plans diagnosed; delivery lagged. That two-sided verdict, stated with examples, is the essay.

Now widen to the world: the NORTH-SOUTH DIVIDE, the shorthand for the gap between the developed economies — the global NORTH: North America, Western Europe, Japan, Australia — and the developing SOUTH: most of Africa, Latin America and southern Asia. The North holds a minority of world population and a large majority of world income, technology, and financial power. Characteristic contrasts: the North exports manufactures, services and capital and sets the rules in the institutions it dominates — IMF, World Bank, WTO voting weight; the South exports primary commodities whose prices swing violently, carries external debt in currencies it cannot print, receives aid with conditions, and sends the North its emigrating skills. The syllabus wants the mechanisms that keep the gap open: terms-of-trade deterioration for commodity exporters; debt-service burdens that eat development budgets; tariff structures that tax processed goods more than raw ones, discouraging southern industrialisation; and the brain drain. Also the counter-movements: the rise of China, India and Brazil blurring the map; south-south trade and BRICS as alternative alignments; the AfCFTA as the South building its own scale; and remittances and technology transfer running north to south. South Africa sits astride the line — southern in geography, poverty and history; northern in financial sophistication and industrial base — which is exactly why its dual character anchors data questions.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the four strategies in order with one clause each, the honest verdict, the divide's contrasts and mechanisms, and South Africa's straddle.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the whole capstone in one family — because a country growing and developing is a household writ large, and the difference between the two is visible across one supper table.

## Subtopic: The Payslip and the Report Card

Two families on one street. The first family's income has risen every year — overtime, a second job, a lodger in the back room. The payslip says: growing. But walk inside: the eldest still could not finish school because fees ran out; the youngest coughs all winter because the clinic queue defeats a working mother; nobody sleeps well because the street is unsafe. Money up, life not.

The second family earns less and their income has barely moved. But the children are in school and staying there; everyone is on the clinic's books; the stokvel paid for the burglar bars; supper happens together. The payslip says: standing still. The lives say: moving.

That is the whole distinction. GROWTH is the payslip — the rand total of what gets produced and earned, and for a country you measure it honestly: strip out inflation, then divide by the number of mouths, because a payslip that grows slower than the family does is a pay cut wearing a smile. DEVELOPMENT is the report card — are the children learning, is anyone sick who needn't be, is life longer, safer, freer than last year? Countries carry exactly this report card: it is called the Human Development Index, and it grades three subjects — how long people live, how much they learn, and what they can afford.

Ask both questions of every country, because the answers split: an oil state can be rich with a shameful report card; a poor country that vaccinates and schools everyone can out-develop its own payslip. And South Africa? A middling payslip that has barely kept up with the family's size — and a report card better than the payslip explains, because grants, clinics and housing did real work. Both facts at once. That is the honest sentence.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the two families are coming to you right now: payslip against report card, the divide-by-mouths discipline, the HDI's three subjects, and the two facts about South Africa held together.

## Subtopic: Feed the Fire or Build the Stove

How does a family — or a country — do better next year than this year? Two schools of advice, and you know both from your own kitchen.

The first school says: the problem is that too little is being spent. Put money in pockets — the grant, the public works wage — and it gets spent at the spaza, and the spaza owner spends it again: the fire of buying and selling burns hotter, and warmth spreads. That is the demand side, and in a street full of people with empty pockets and idle hands, it genuinely works — you have watched the multiplier do it since term one.

But try to cook a feast on that fire and you meet its limits — count them on four fingers. Pour money in when the pots are already full and you get not more food but higher prices. In this family, much of any windfall is spent at shops whose goods come from far away — money that warms someone else's street. If the money is borrowed, the lender is at the door by month-end, eating the next windfall. And no amount of spending turns on a stove that was never wired — if the daughter was never trained as an electrician, demand for electricians does not make her one.

The second school says: fix the STOVE — the family's power to produce. Wire the house. Fix the van that takes goods to market. Keep the children in school to matric and beyond, because every year of schooling raises every future payslip. Make the street safe enough to open a shop on. Nothing about this is quick — the stove is built over years, and the building costs suppers along the way. But when it is built, it cooks every day, forever: that is the supply side, and its slowness is the price of its permanence.

So which school is right? Wrong question — they answer different clocks. The fire is for THIS winter: people must eat while the stove is being built, or they will not be there to use it. The stove is for ALL winters. A family that only feeds the fire stays warm and stays poor; a family that only builds the stove goes hungry in the building years. The wisdom is proportion: feed the fire enough to hold everyone through the present, and never stop building the stove.

Your questions for this part are up now: the fire and its four limits retold, the stove's parts named, the two clocks, and the proportion sentence.

## Subtopic: The Street and the Suburb

Last scene — lift the two families to the scale of the world.

There is a suburb of nations — call it the north — with wired houses, insured cars, and children who inherit not money so much as WORKING SYSTEMS: schools that teach, courts that rule, grids that stay on. And there is a street of nations — the south — that supplies the suburb: the fruit, the ore, the coffee, and, most quietly, its cleverest children, who qualify and move. The suburb buys the street's raw goods cheap, works them up, and sells them back dear. When the street borrows for a stove, it borrows in the suburb's money at the suburb's rates, so bad years double the debt. And at the suburb's gate a strange tariff applies: raw things enter almost free, but anything the street MANUFACTURES pays at the gate — as if the suburb preferred the street never to own a stove at all. Meetings about the rules happen in the suburb's boardroom, where votes are weighted by wealth. None of this needs a villain; it is how the gap, once opened, keeps itself open — and naming these mechanisms, calmly, is what the exam calls the north-south divide.

But the map is redrawing itself. Some street families built stoves against the odds — east Asia most famously — and now sell finished goods to the suburb. The street has begun trading with itself: that is what the continental free-trade agreement is, the street's families opening their doors to each other so their small shops can finally grow. Money wired home by the street's children in the suburb outweighs the suburb's aid. The gap remains; the story is no longer only the gap.

And one house sits exactly on the corner between street and suburb: South Africa. Suburb-grade banks, stock exchange, motorways and universities — street-grade unemployment, inequality and townships, sometimes in the same square kilometre. That corner house is why our exams keep asking both questions: our payslip needs the stove built — the electricity, the trains, the schools every plan since 1994 has promised — and our report card needs the fire kept burning under everyone while the building drags on. Hold both, in every essay and every argument after school: growth to afford the future, development so everyone arrives there.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: the suburb's four gap-keeping mechanisms in street language, the redrawn map with its examples, the corner house explained, and the closing sentence in your own words. This capstone reaches back through every topic this year — spend it wherever the paper lets you.
