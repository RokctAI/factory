# Part 1 — Expert

The perfect market was the benchmark; now take its assumptions down one by one and watch what grows in their place. Remove all rivals and you have MONOPOLY. Leave only a few and you have OLIGOPOLY. Restore many sellers but let each differentiate its product and you have MONOPOLISTIC COMPETITION. These three imperfect structures fill most of the real economy — and most of Paper 2's micro-economics section. The examiners' method is always comparison: every feature of an imperfect market is measured against the perfect one, so keep last lesson's machinery — MR equals MC, the cost curves, normal against economic profit — running underneath everything today.

## Subtopic: Monopoly — One Seller, and the Price Maker's Diagram

A MONOPOLY is a market with a SINGLE seller of a product with NO close substitutes, protected by BLOCKED entry. The barriers matter as much as the definition, so list them: legal barriers — patents, licences, exclusive franchises; ownership of a key resource — historically De Beers and diamonds; NATURAL monopoly, where one national grid or pipe network is genuinely cheaper than two, the Eskom transmission case; and sheer economies of scale that no entrant can match.

Because the monopolist IS the industry, the downward-sloping market demand curve is its own demand curve — and everything follows from that single fact. The monopolist is a PRICE MAKER: it can choose any point on the demand curve, high price with few sales or low price with many, but it must accept the trade-off the curve dictates.

Here is the crucial technical step, described so you can draw it. When the demand curve slopes down, MARGINAL REVENUE runs BELOW average revenue. Why: to sell one more unit, the monopolist must drop the price — not only on the extra unit but on ALL units. Sell ten units at twenty rand, or eleven at nineteen: the eleventh unit brings in nineteen rand but costs one rand off each of the ten others, so marginal revenue is nineteen minus ten — nine rand, far below the nineteen on the price tag. So draw the picture: a downward-sloping demand curve labelled D equals AR; below it a steeper falling MR curve, starting at the same point on the vertical axis and falling twice as fast; the same tick-shaped MC and U-shaped AC as before.

Equilibrium keeps the universal rule: produce where MR equals MC. But now read PRICE off the DEMAND curve, straight up from that quantity — never off the MR curve; that error fails the question. Then compare price to AC at that quantity for the profit verdict, exactly as before. The differences from perfect competition: price sits ABOVE marginal cost, output is SMALLER than the competitive market would deliver, and — because entry is blocked — economic profit can PERSIST into the long run. No entry, no erosion. That persistence, plus the restricted output, is the welfare complaint against monopoly: consumers pay more for less, and the gap between price and marginal cost measures resources misallocated.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: the definition with its barriers, why MR runs below AR with the numbers, equilibrium output and where price is read, and the three contrasts with perfect competition.

## Subtopic: Oligopoly — the Few, the Kink, and the Cartel Temptation

An OLIGOPOLY is a market dominated by a FEW large, mutually dependent sellers — banking's big names, the fuel companies, mobile networks, the two-firm special case being a DUOPOLY. Products may be homogeneous, like cement, or differentiated, like phones. Entry is difficult: scale, branding, licences.

The defining feature is INTERDEPENDENCE: each firm's best move depends on its rivals' responses, so oligopolists behave strategically — the only structure where firms watch each other rather than just the market. Theory captures the result in the KINKED demand curve, described for drawing: the firm's demand curve bends at the current price. ABOVE the kink the curve is flat-ish — elastic — because if one firm RAISES its price, rivals do NOT follow, and its customers desert; below the kink the curve is steep — inelastic — because if it CUTS its price, rivals MUST match, and everyone's volumes barely change while everyone's revenue falls. Raise and you lose alone; cut and you gain nothing. Conclusion: prices in oligopoly are STICKY — firms avoid price wars, and the kink even breaks the MR curve with a vertical gap at the current output, so modest cost changes shift nothing.

If price competition is mutually destructive, competition migrates to NON-PRICE weapons: branding and advertising, loyalty programmes, product features, service, sponsorships — the reason cellphone advertising saturates your screen while the tariffs barely move.

And there is a darker exit from interdependence: stop competing. COLLUSION. Overt collusion — a CARTEL — has the few agree on price or carve up the market, jointly acting as a monopolist and splitting monopoly profit; OPEC does it lawfully between states, but within South Africa the bread and construction cartels met the Competition Commission and Tribunal. Tacit collusion is subtler — price leadership, where one dominant firm moves and the rest follow without a word. Cartels carry their own seed of failure: each member profits by secretly shading price below the agreement, so cartels are inherently unstable — a point evaluators reward. Where they hold, consumers face monopoly outcomes wearing a competitive mask, which is why collusion is the Competition Act's first target.

Stop for this section's questions now — the features of oligopoly, the kink explained side by side, sticky prices and non-price competition, and collusion in both forms with its instability.

## Subtopic: Monopolistic Competition — Many Sellers, Each Slightly Special

Now the hybrid that fills every high street: MONOPOLISTIC COMPETITION — MANY sellers, FREE entry and exit, but each selling a DIFFERENTIATED product: hair salons, takeaways, guesthouses, clothing labels. Differentiation — real or created by branding, location, service, packaging — gives each seller a small monopoly over its own variant: customers who prefer this salon will tolerate a slightly higher price before defecting.

That loyalty tilts the firm's demand curve: no longer horizontal, but gently downward-sloping — far flatter than a monopolist's, because close substitutes wait next door. With a sloping demand curve comes an MR curve below it, and the short-run diagram is a small-scale monopoly picture: produce where MR equals MC, read price off the demand curve, compare with AC. Short-run economic profit is possible.

But here entry is FREE — and this is the structure's signature move. Profit attracts imitators: another chicken franchise opens across the road, the new salon undercuts. Each entrant slices away part of the incumbent's customers, shifting its demand curve LEFTWARD until that curve just TOUCHES the average cost curve — tangent to it — at the chosen output. At the tangency: price equals average cost, normal profit only. Monopoly's diagram, perfect competition's long-run result.

Two efficiency verdicts at that tangency, and both are examinable. Because the demand curve slopes, the tangency lands on the FALLING part of the AC curve, left of its minimum: the firm operates with EXCESS CAPACITY, producing less than the output that would minimise cost — the half-empty restaurant as a permanent condition. And price still sits above marginal cost, so allocative efficiency fails too, mildly. The compensation: VARIETY. Forty identical tomato tables give efficiency without choice; forty different restaurants give choice without full efficiency. The consumer pays a little more and gets a menu.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — features and examples, the gently sloping demand curve, the entry-until-tangency story, excess capacity, and the variety trade-off.

## Subtopic: The Four Structures Side by Side

Examiners love the comparison table; build it in words as a fixed recitation, structure by structure across five tests.

NUMBER OF SELLERS: perfect competition, very many; monopolistic competition, many; oligopoly, few; monopoly, one. NATURE OF PRODUCT: homogeneous; differentiated; homogeneous or differentiated; unique with no close substitutes. ENTRY: free; free; difficult; blocked. CONTROL OVER PRICE: none — price taker; slight; considerable but constrained by rivals; full — price maker, constrained only by demand. DEMAND CURVE FACING THE FIRM: horizontal; gently downward-sloping; kinked at the ruling price; the steep market demand curve itself.

Then the performance row, the one essays are marked on. LONG-RUN PROFIT: normal only; normal only — entry erodes it; economic profit possible — entry is hard; economic profit persists — entry is blocked. EFFICIENCY: perfect competition alone delivers both allocative and productive efficiency; monopolistic competition fails both mildly, paying for variety with excess capacity; oligopoly restricts output and diverts resources into persuasion, though scale and innovation partly redeem it; monopoly restricts most, prices furthest above marginal cost — and its verdict carries the famous qualification: where scale is genuinely natural, one producer IS cheapest, and regulation rather than break-up is the remedy; and large protected profits can fund research no small competitor could attempt.

Practical exam craft to finish. Every structure answers the same three questions with the same machinery: WHERE to produce — always MR equals MC; WHAT price — read off the firm's demand curve above that output; WHAT profit — compare price with AC there, remembering normal profit lives inside the cost curves. The structures differ only in the SHAPE of the demand curve the firm faces and the EASE of entry that decides whether profit survives the long run. Master those two dials — slope and entry — and every diagram in this section becomes one diagram with different settings.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the five-test recitation, the long-run profit row explained by entry, the efficiency verdicts with monopoly's qualification, and the two dials that generate all four structures.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the whole zoo at street level — one town, four businesses — because you already shop in all four market structures every week without naming them.

## Subtopic: The Only Bridge in Town

The town sits on a river, and one company owns the only bridge. Cross, or swim.

Feel the difference from the tomato street immediately. The tomato seller who tried twelve rand watched buyers step to the next table. The bridge company has no next table: raise the toll and most people grumble and pay. It chooses its price — a price MAKER — though not without limits, because at fifty rand some people really will swim, cycle the long way, or just stay home. The bridge picks its spot on the town's willingness to pay: a high toll and fewer crossings, or a low toll and many. That downward slope IS its constraint — the only one it has.

But notice a wrinkle the exam loves. When the bridge cuts its toll from twenty to nineteen to attract one more crosser, EVERYONE pays nineteen — including all the people who happily paid twenty. The extra crosser brings nineteen rand in, but the discount to everyone else eats into it, so the real gain from that extra crossing is much less than nineteen. Selling more always costs a monopolist something invisible. That invisible arithmetic is why its marginal revenue sits below its price.

Why does no rival build a second bridge? Sometimes the law forbids it; sometimes the first company owns the only narrow crossing point; and sometimes a second bridge would genuinely be wasteful — one bridge can carry everyone at lower cost than two half-empty ones. That last case is why nobody wants two electricity grids down every street: some monopolies are natural, and the sensible response is not to break them but to watch their tolls — a regulator at the tollgate.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the bridge are coming to you right now: price maker against price taker, the invisible cost of the discount, why the toll still has a ceiling, and the natural-monopoly case for a regulator instead of a rival.

## Subtopic: Three Petrol Stations and a Staring Contest

Drive out of town: three petrol stations on the same stretch. Watch how differently they behave from both the tomato street and the bridge.

Station A considers raising its price ten cents. It knows exactly what happens: B and C keep theirs, every regular drifts two hundred metres down the road, and A stands alone with empty pumps. So A does not raise. Now A considers CUTTING ten cents to steal customers. It knows that too: B and C match within the hour — they must — and by evening all three sell at the lower price to the same customers as before, each simply poorer. So A does not cut. Up loses alone; down gains nothing. The price stands still for months — not because anyone agreed, but because each station is playing chess against the other two. That staring contest is oligopoly, and the stuck price is the kink drawn in real life.

So where does the fighting go? Everywhere except price. Cleaner forecourts, loyalty cards, a better shop, coffee, sponsorship of the local team — the war of everything-but-the-price. Next time you wonder why banks and networks advertise relentlessly yet charge nearly the same, you are looking at the same contest.

And the temptation: one rainy evening the three owners share a table. Why fight? Fifteen cents up, all three, from Monday. Suddenly the stretch of road behaves like one big bridge — customers pay more with nowhere to go. Two things eventually break the party. Each owner is tempted to quietly shave the price after dark and steal volume — cheating built into every cartel. And in South Africa that table is a crime scene: the Competition Commission raids it, the Tribunal fines it up to a tenth of turnover — ask the bread companies. The staring contest is legal; the handshake is not.

Your questions for this part are up now: why the price sticks without any agreement, where the competition migrates, the dinner-table temptation, and the two forces that break it.

## Subtopic: A Street Full of Salons

Back in town, one street carries eleven hair salons — and every one is busy being different. One is cheapest; one has the best braider; one has air-conditioning and gospel; one stays open late; one has the owner everyone tells their troubles to.

Each salon owns a tiny bridge: HER customers will pay twenty rand more before walking, because to them the difference is real. So each sets a price slightly her own — a small price maker. But the bridge is short: thirty rand more and even loyal customers try next door, because ten close substitutes wait within two hundred metres. A gently sloping curve, not a cliff.

Now the tomato street's old logic returns, because entry is FREE. Suppose salon three's chairs are full and her margins fatten. Within six months two new salons open on the same street — a certainty you can set your watch by. Each newcomer slices away a few customers; the incumbents' curves creep left; and the fattening stops exactly where every salon again earns what its owner's hands could earn elsewhere. The special trick is only special until it is copied.

Look down the street at the result. Nobody rich; nobody starving; and notice — every salon has empty chairs some afternoons. Eleven half-busy salons where seven full ones could cut costs. That idle chair is what economists call excess capacity, the price of a street where everyone is slightly different. What do customers get for it? CHOICE — the braider, the gospel, the late hours, the listening ear. The tomato street was efficient and identical; the salon street is slightly wasteful and human. Both are markets working exactly as their structures dictate.

So carry the whole zoo home in one sweep: forty identical tables — no power, no profit that lasts. One bridge — full power, profit that lasts as long as the entry stays blocked. Three stations staring — power held carefully, frozen prices, war by advertisement. Eleven salons — a pinch of power each, melted away by the next arrival, paid out to you as variety.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: the salon's short bridge, entry as the fat-melter, the idle chair named and priced, and the four businesses matched to their four structures. Hold the zoo firmly — market failure, the next topic, begins exactly where these structures leave consumers paying more than the last unit truly cost.
