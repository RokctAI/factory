**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First, the one that derails everyone's diagram: why is the monopolist's marginal revenue below its price? In perfect competition they were the same line, and suddenly they split apart.

**Tutor:** The split has one cause: the slope of the demand curve, and once you see the mechanism you will never draw it wrong again. In perfect competition the firm sells every unit at the going price, so the extra unit brings in exactly the price — MR and price coincide on the flat line. The monopolist faces the whole market's downward-sloping demand, which means one thing: to sell MORE, it must charge LESS — and not just to the new customer. There is one price on the board for everyone. Run the numbers slowly. Ten crossings sold at twenty rand is two hundred rand. To win the eleventh crossing the toll must drop to nineteen — for all eleven. New revenue: eleven times nineteen, two hundred and nine. The eleventh crossing changed total revenue by nine rand, even though its price tag says nineteen. Where did the other ten rand go? Into the discount handed to the ten crossers who would have paid twenty. So marginal revenue equals the new unit's price MINUS the revenue sacrificed on all previous units, and that subtraction grows as output grows — which is why the MR curve falls twice as steeply as demand and eventually goes negative. Negative MR, note, is where cutting price actually shrinks total revenue: the inelastic region of demand, connecting straight back to your Grade 11 elasticity work. And the exam consequence you must never violate: the decision happens where MR meets MC, but the PRICE is read off the demand curve above that quantity — because the demand curve, not MR, tells you what buyers will pay for that output.

**Mandy:** Next: if cartels are illegal and unstable, why do the petrol stations near me always charge exactly the same price and change it on the same day? Isn't that proof of collusion?

**Tutor:** It is proof of interdependence, which produces identical prices with or without a crime — and telling the two apart is genuinely hard, which is why competition authorities employ economists. Start with the innocent explanations. In a homogeneous-product oligopoly, any station charging visibly more loses its customers, and any station charging less gets matched at once — the kinked-demand logic — so prices converge and freeze without a word exchanged. Same-day changes have an even simpler cause in South Africa: the pump price of petrol is REGULATED, adjusted monthly by government formula, so that particular example is neither collusion nor coincidence. Then there is tacit price leadership: one large firm moves, the rest follow within days because following is individually rational. None of that involves an agreement, and none of it is prosecutable in itself. What the Competition Act attacks is the AGREEMENT — evidence of contact: meetings, phone calls, emails, industry-association gatherings where prices were discussed. That is what sank the bread cartel: not matching prices, but proof the bakers coordinated increases and divided territories. The economic difference matters too. Tacit parallelism still leaves each firm afraid to raise prices unilaterally — the kink still binds — so prices stay near the competitive level. A cartel removes the fear: all raise together, and price migrates toward the monopoly level. So the careful sentence for an essay: identical prices are consistent with fierce oligopolistic rivalry; it is coordination of price CHANGES through agreement that turns oligopoly into a cartel — and even then, each member's incentive to cheat means the authorities are often tipped off by the cartel's own unravelling.

**Mandy:** This one sounds like a trick: my aunt's takeaway earns just enough to pay her what she'd earn working elsewhere — you call that normal profit and long-run equilibrium. But she works brutally hard being different from the other takeaways. If differentiation earns her nothing extra in the end, why bother being different at all?

**Tutor:** Because the alternative to being different is not normal profit — it is being undercut into leaving. Her differentiation is not a bonus generator; it is her admission ticket to the market. Think about what the gently sloping demand curve gives her: customers who prefer her chilli sauce, her cooking, her corner. Without that slope — if her food were identical to every rival's — she would be a price taker in a street where someone is always hungrier for volume, and any cost disadvantage would finish her. The slope is her cushion. Now, why does the cushion not become permanent riches? Free entry. The moment her recipe fattens her margins, imitators arrive — that is the tangency story, and it grinds every LASTING excess away. But notice three things the long-run equilibrium quietly pays her. First, normal profit for her includes the return to her particular skills — if her cooking is genuinely scarce, the market wage for that scarce skill is inside her costs, and it is higher than a bland competitor's. Second, the run TO the long run is long: every improvement she makes — new dish, longer hours, delivery — earns real economic profit until it is copied, and a proprietor who keeps innovating lives permanently in that profitable gap, exactly like the entrepreneurs of the perfect-market lesson. Third, differentiation buys her survival insurance: loyal customers ride out her bad months, where a price taker's demand vanishes at the first undercut. So the honest answer to your aunt: being different does not guarantee riches, but being the same guarantees worse — and the customers, meanwhile, collect the whole street's variety as their dividend.

**Mandy:** Last one: between a monopoly and an oligopoly, which is actually worse for consumers? The monopoly seems obviously worse, but the notes hedge.

**Tutor:** The notes hedge because the honest answer is: it depends on what the monopoly is and what the oligopoly is doing — and building that dependence is exactly what a discussion question wants. Take the clean comparison first. A textbook monopoly restricts output further and prices further above marginal cost than a rivalrous oligopoly, faces no competitive pressure to innovate or please anyone, and its profits persist untouched. On those terms monopoly is worse. Now disturb the comparison from both sides. If the oligopoly COLLUDES, it simply becomes a monopoly with more shareholders — bread, construction — and consumers get monopoly outcomes with none of monopoly's occasional excuses. A colluding oligopoly can therefore be every bit as bad, and harder to detect. From the other side, some monopolies carry redeeming features a fragmented market cannot: a natural monopoly like a transmission grid genuinely minimises cost with one network — the right answer there is regulation of its price, not competition — and a patent monopoly is the deliberate, temporary reward society pays for innovation that might otherwise never be funded. Meanwhile a NON-colluding oligopoly often serves consumers surprisingly well: the kink keeps prices stable, scale keeps costs low, and rivalry — pushed out of prices — pours into quality, features and innovation, which is why phones improve yearly while the tariffs crawl. So rank them like this in an essay: competitive oligopoly, then regulated natural monopoly, then unregulated monopoly and colluding oligopoly at the bottom together. The structure's NAME matters less than two questions: is entry genuinely possible, and are the few actually fighting? Those two dials — the same two that generate the structures — also rank their harm.
