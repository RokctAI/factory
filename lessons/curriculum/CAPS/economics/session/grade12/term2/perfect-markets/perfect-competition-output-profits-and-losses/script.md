# Part 1 — Expert

Paper 2 opens with micro-economics, and micro-economics opens here: the PERFECT MARKET, the benchmark against which every real market is measured. Grade 11 built the tools — demand, supply, and the cost and revenue curves; Grade 12 now assembles them into the full machine: what perfect competition assumes, how the individual business relates to its industry, how output, profit and loss are determined on the cost-revenue diagram, and how South Africa's competition authorities try to push real markets toward the benchmark. Every curve in this lesson must live in your head as a picture you can draw and a sentence you can say.

## Subtopic: Perfect Competition — Assumptions, and the Industry Against the Business

Perfect competition holds when five conditions are met, and examiners award a mark per condition. VERY MANY buyers and sellers, each too small to influence the price. A HOMOGENEOUS product — every unit identical, so no buyer prefers one seller's. FREE ENTRY AND EXIT — no legal, financial or technical barrier stops a newcomer or traps a leaver. PERFECT KNOWLEDGE — all participants know all prices. And MOBILE factors of production, with no collusion and no government interference in price. No real market meets all five; fresh-produce markets and the JSE come close, which is why they are the standing examples.

The consequence of the assumptions is the topic's central idea: the INDUSTRY sets the price, the BUSINESS accepts it. Hold two pictures side by side. Picture one, the industry: the market demand curve sloping downward, the market supply curve sloping upward, crossing at the equilibrium price — say ten rand a unit. Picture two, the individual business: it can sell any quantity it likes at ten rand, but nothing at ten rand and one cent, because buyers with perfect knowledge simply step to the identical rival. So the demand curve FACING THE BUSINESS is a horizontal line at the market price. The business is a PRICE TAKER; its only decision is QUANTITY.

That horizontal line does triple duty, and this identity unlocks the whole topic: it is the demand curve, the AVERAGE REVENUE curve — since every unit sells at ten rand, revenue per unit is ten rand — and the MARGINAL REVENUE curve, since each extra unit adds exactly ten rand. In perfect competition, D equals AR equals MR equals P, one flat line.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: the five assumptions, the price-taker consequence, the two side-by-side pictures, and the flat line's three names.

## Subtopic: Costs, Revenue and the Profit-Maximising Output

Now lay the cost curves under that flat revenue line — all of this described precisely, so you can draw it from the words. The MARGINAL COST curve, MC, plots the cost of producing one more unit: it dips at first as specialisation helps, then rises steeply as diminishing returns bite — a tick-shaped curve, falling then climbing. The AVERAGE COST curve, AC, plots total cost per unit: a wide U, high at low output where fixed costs are spread over few units, falling to a minimum, rising again as inefficiency sets in. The MC curve always cuts the AC curve at AC's LOWEST point — while the marginal unit costs less than the average it drags the average down; the moment it costs more, it pulls the average up.

The business chooses output where PROFIT is maximised, and the rule is universal across every market structure this term: produce where MARGINAL REVENUE EQUALS MARGINAL COST — MR equals MC — with MC rising. The logic: any unit whose extra revenue exceeds its extra cost adds to profit, so keep going; the first unit whose extra cost exceeds its extra revenue subtracts, so stop exactly at the crossing. In perfect competition MR is the flat price line, so the rule becomes: produce where rising MC cuts the price line. Price fifteen rand and the MC of the two-hundredth unit fifteen rand: two hundred is the output.

Whether that output yields profit depends on where AVERAGE cost sits at that quantity, and here the syllabus wants exact vocabulary. NORMAL PROFIT: the minimum earnings needed to keep the entrepreneur in this industry — the opportunity cost of capital and enterprise — and it is INCLUDED in the cost curves. When price equals average cost at the chosen output, the business earns normal profit only; accountants would call it profitable, economists call it break-even. ECONOMIC PROFIT — supernormal profit — exists when price exceeds average cost at the chosen output: the firm earns more than its next-best alternative. On the diagram, a rectangle between the price line and the AC curve, its width the output, its height the per-unit surplus. Price fifteen, average cost twelve, output two hundred: economic profit of three rand times two hundred, six hundred rand.

Stop for this section's questions now — the shapes of MC and AC in words, why MC cuts AC at its minimum, the MR-equals-MC rule with its logic, and normal against economic profit calculated from figures.

## Subtopic: Losses, the Shut-Down Decision, and the Long Run

Symmetry: when price sits BELOW average cost at the best output, the firm makes an ECONOMIC LOSS — the rectangle now lies between the AC curve above and the price line below. The examinable question: should a loss-making firm close immediately? Not necessarily, and the reasoning is a guaranteed mark-earner.

Split costs into FIXED — rent, insurance, loan instalments, owed even at zero output — and VARIABLE — materials, casual wages, electricity for the machines, avoidable by stopping. In the SHORT RUN, the fixed costs are sunk: close and you still owe them. So the true test is the AVERAGE VARIABLE COST curve, a shallower U nested below AC. If price covers average variable cost, each unit sold pays its own way and contributes something toward the rent — produce, and lose less than by closing. If price falls below even average variable cost, every unit deepens the loss: SHUT DOWN. The point where price equals minimum AVC is the SHUT-DOWN point; below it the firm stops, which is why the firm's SUPPLY curve is its rising MC curve above minimum AVC.

Now let time pass — the LONG RUN, where entry and exit do their work, and where perfect competition earns its title as benchmark. Suppose firms are earning economic profit. Free entry plus perfect knowledge means newcomers flood in; industry supply shifts right; the market price falls; each firm's flat revenue line slides down — until it just touches the bottom of the AC curve. Economic profit: gone. Suppose instead losses: firms exit, supply shifts left, price rises to the same resting place. LONG-RUN EQUILIBRIUM is therefore price equal to marginal cost equal to minimum average cost — every firm producing at the cheapest possible cost per unit, earning normal profit only, with price equal to the true resource cost of the last unit. That is ALLOCATIVE efficiency — resources follow consumers' wishes, since P equals MC — and PRODUCTIVE efficiency — output at minimum average cost — achieved together. No other market structure this term reaches both, which is exactly why this model is the measuring stick.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — the loss rectangle, fixed against variable costs, the shut-down test on AVC, the MC-above-AVC supply curve, and the long-run entry-exit story ending at both efficiencies.

## Subtopic: Competition Policy — Pushing Real Markets Toward the Benchmark

Real South African markets are not perfect, and the state maintains machinery to push them closer. The framework is the COMPETITION ACT of 1998, and its three institutions must be named with their functions.

The COMPETITION COMMISSION investigates: it screens mergers, probes cartels and studies whole markets. The COMPETITION TRIBUNAL adjudicates: it rules on the Commission's cases and can block mergers, impose conditions, and fine offenders up to ten percent of annual turnover. The COMPETITION APPEAL COURT hears appeals from the Tribunal.

What the Act attacks, with the cases that make essays concrete. HORIZONTAL restrictive practices — collusion between rivals: price fixing, market division, bid rigging. South Africa's record supplies the examples: the bread cartel, where major bakers fixed the price of the national staple; the construction bid-rigging exposed around the 2010 World Cup stadiums, settled with hundreds of millions in penalties. VERTICAL restrictive practices — abuses along the supply chain, such as minimum resale price maintenance. ABUSE OF DOMINANCE — a dominant firm pricing predatorily to kill entrants or refusing rivals access to essential facilities. And MERGER CONTROL — blocking or conditioning combinations that would substantially lessen competition; South African merger law is distinctive in also weighing PUBLIC INTEREST: employment, small-business access, and ownership spread.

Why does the state bother? Tie it back to the model: collusion converts many competing price takers into one price MAKER — the market drifts toward monopoly outcomes, price above marginal cost, output restricted, the efficiencies lost. Competition policy is the attempt to restore, by law, the discipline that free entry and many rivals provide automatically in the model. Evaluate honestly for the top marks: enforcement is slow, fines can be passed on to customers, and policy must sometimes balance competition against industrial policy goals — but the deterrent is real, and the bread on your table is cheaper for it.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the Act and its three institutions with functions, the practices attacked with South African cases, merger control's public-interest test, and the model-based reason competition policy exists.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same machine at street level — one tomato seller in a row of forty — because perfect competition is not an abstraction; it is what a market looks like when nobody is in charge of the price.

## Subtopic: Forty Tables of Tomatoes

Market day. Forty tables down the taxi-rank street, every table piled with the same red tomatoes. A woman arrives to sell hers.

She tries to charge twelve rand a kilo when every other table asks ten. Buyers glance, walk two steps, buy identical tomatoes for ten. She sells nothing. She tries eight rand to steal the crowd — she sells out fast, but she was ALREADY able to sell everything at ten, so she has simply thrown away two rand on every kilo. By mid-morning she has learnt the street's one law: the price is ten because the WHOLE street's tomatoes and the whole street's buyers made it ten, and no single table can move it. Her only real decision left: HOW MANY kilos to bring.

That is a price taker, and the flat line from the theory is just her morning drawn as a graph: whatever quantity she chooses, each kilo fetches ten rand — so ten is her price, her revenue per kilo, and the revenue from one more kilo. One number wearing three names.

Notice what makes the street work this way: forty tables, identical tomatoes, buyers who can see every table, and nothing stopping table forty-one from setting up tomorrow. Remove any one — make her tomatoes branded, blindfold the buyers, fence the street — and she gets price power. That is why the assumptions matter: each one is a wall that keeps the price honest.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the street are coming to you right now: why twelve fails and eight is foolish, the one decision left to her, the flat line's three names, and which walls keep the price honest.

## Subtopic: How Many Kilos? — the One Rule

So how many kilos should she bring? Answer like an economist: kilo by kilo.

Each kilo sells for ten rand. Each kilo also COSTS her something to supply — and the cost per extra kilo is not constant. The first few are cheap: her own garden, her own morning. More kilos mean buying from neighbours at rising prices, hiring a bakkie, paying her cousin to help — each extra kilo costs more than the last. Somewhere, the cost of one more kilo hits exactly ten rand.

Stop there. Every kilo before that point brought in more than it cost — profit in the pocket. Every kilo past it costs more than the ten rand it fetches — profit leaking out. The rule, in street language: keep adding kilos while one more kilo earns more than it costs, stop when the next kilo would cost more than it earns. That is all MR equals MC says. It is not clever algebra; it is the arithmetic of not being silly, applied one kilo at a time.

Then the day's verdict. Add up ALL her costs — including paying herself what she could have earned at her other option, sewing at the factory, because a morning here is a morning's wages lost. Suppose the whole operation costs her nine rand a kilo, all-in, and she sells at ten: one rand a kilo of pure surplus — economic profit, more than her best alternative. If all-in cost is exactly ten: she is not rich, but she is earning exactly what her time and money could earn anywhere — normal profit, and staying makes sense. If all-in cost is eleven: she is subsidising the street, and her sewing machine calls.

Your questions for this part are up now: the kilo-by-kilo rule in your own words, why her own wage belongs in the costs, and the three verdicts — surplus, break-even, and the sewing machine.

## Subtopic: The Street Corrects Itself

Last piece — what happens NEXT, because the street never stands still.

Word gets out that tomato tables are clearing one rand a kilo above all costs. Nothing stops newcomers — that is the free-entry wall — so next month there are fifty tables, then sixty. More tomatoes chase the same buyers; the street price slips from ten toward nine. The surplus shrinks table by table until a tomato table earns exactly what the same effort earns elsewhere — and the arrivals stop. Profit was the invitation; entry was the RSVP; the party ends when the food runs out.

Run it backwards for the drought year: costs above price, tables losing money. Some sellers quit for the factory — free EXIT — fewer tomatoes, price climbs, until the survivors break even. The street breathes in and out around one resting point: price equal to the cheapest possible cost per kilo, every seller earning a fair return and not a cent more.

See what the street achieved with nobody in charge: tomatoes produced as cheaply as they can be, sold at what the last kilo truly cost society, by exactly as many sellers as the demand justifies. That is why economists keep this model on the wall like a plumb line — not because real markets look like this, but to measure how far they lean.

And the lesson's final turn: what if the forty sellers meet on Sunday and agree — nobody sells below fifteen? The street becomes one big seller; buyers pay more for fewer tomatoes; the honest walls are down. That agreement is illegal in South Africa, and there is a Commission that investigates it, a Tribunal that fines it up to ten percent of turnover, and a case file that already includes the bread you ate this morning. When forty tables become one voice, the law exists to break up the meeting.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: profit as invitation and entry as RSVP, the drought year in reverse, the resting point in one sentence, and the Sunday meeting with its legal consequences. Master this benchmark — next lesson the walls come down one by one, and monopoly, oligopoly and monopolistic competition are measured against everything you built today.
