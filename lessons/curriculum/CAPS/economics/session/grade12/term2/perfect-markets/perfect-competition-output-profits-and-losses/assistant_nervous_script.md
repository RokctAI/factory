Hey, it's Mandy — I know the perfect markets diagram, with five curves crossing each other, is the single scariest picture in Economics Paper 2. So let's build it in layers, the way you'd actually draw it in the exam, because drawn in order it is only ever one line at a time.

Layer one: the flat line. The business is a price taker, so draw one horizontal line at the market price and label it four times — D, AR, MR, P. Four labels, one line, and usually a mark just for that.

Layer two: the tick and the U. Marginal cost dips then climbs like a tick; average cost is a wide U. One rule about how they meet: MC always cuts AC at AC's lowest point. If your sketch has them crossing anywhere else, fix it before you fix anything else — markers look there first.

Layer three: the decision. Find where rising MC crosses the flat line. That crossing IS the answer to "how much should the firm produce" in every question, every time: MR equals MC. Drop a line down to the quantity axis and you're done deciding.

Layer four: the verdict. At that quantity, look UP to the AC curve. Price above AC: economic profit — shade the rectangle between them. Price ON the minimum of AC: normal profit, and remember the sentence that earns its mark — normal profit is already inside the cost curves, because the owner's own wage and capital return are costs. Price below AC: loss — and before you write "shut down", check the shallower U, average variable cost. Covering AVC? Stay open, contribute to the rent. Below AVC? Close.

And for the long run, one sentence covers every version: profits invite entry, losses force exit, and the price comes to rest on the bottom of the AC curve — normal profit only, price equal to marginal cost, both efficiencies achieved.

Flat line, tick and U, crossing, verdict. Four layers, drawn in order, never all at once. You've got this!
