**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! The first is the one everyone whispers: why are we studying a market that doesn't exist? No real market has perfect knowledge and identical products, so what's the point?

**Tutor:** The point is the same as the point of a perfectly straight ruler: nothing in nature is perfectly straight either, yet you cannot measure crookedness without one. Perfect competition is the measuring instrument of micro-economics. It tells us what a market delivers when no one has power over price: output where price equals marginal cost, so resources go exactly where consumers direct them; production at minimum average cost, so nothing is wasted; and profits competed down to normal in the long run, so no one earns more than their contribution. Now you hold that ruler against real markets, and the CROOKEDNESS becomes visible and measurable. Why does a monopolist's price sit above marginal cost, and by how much? How far does a cartel restrict output below the competitive level? Those questions only have meaning relative to the benchmark. And the model earns its keep in policy: the Competition Commission's entire logic is to identify where real markets have drifted from the benchmark — collusion, blocked entry, abused dominance — and force them back toward it. Notice too that some markets come close enough for the model to predict well: fresh-produce markets, maize at the silo, shares on the JSE all behave nearly as the model says, with sellers as price takers. So write this in an essay: the model is not a description, it is a STANDARD — and the next three market structures you will study are each defined by exactly which of its assumptions they break.

**Mandy:** Next: I can calculate MR equals MC in the exam, but I don't believe it. No spaza owner draws marginal cost curves. How can a rule nobody uses decide what real businesses do?

**Tutor:** Nobody calculates it, and nearly everyone obeys it — that is the beautiful part. The rule is not a procedure businesses follow; it is a description of what survival forces them toward. Watch the spaza owner decide whether to stock a second fridge of cold drinks. She asks herself: what will this fridge cost me each month — the stock, the extra electricity, maybe a small loan instalment? And what will it bring in? If the answer is costs eight hundred, brings in twelve hundred, she does it. If the next fridge after that would cost nine hundred and bring in seven hundred, she stops at two fridges. She has just equated marginal revenue and marginal cost to the nearest fridge, using no curve and no Greek letters — because the QUESTION she asked, is one more worth it, IS the marginal question. Every trader deciding whether to open on Sundays, every farmer deciding whether to plant the last field, every taxi owner weighing one more vehicle is running the same comparison on the same logic. The curves in the textbook are simply that everyday question drawn smoothly, kilo by kilo instead of fridge by fridge. And there is a harder edge: businesses that persistently get the comparison wrong — producing units that cost more than they earn — bleed money and exit, while those that get it right expand. Competition does the calculating for the market even where individuals guess. So in the exam, state the rule formally, but understand it as the arithmetic of not being silly — and if an evaluation question asks whether real firms use it, say: not consciously, but market survival enforces it anyway.

**Mandy:** This one seems like a contradiction: how can a firm be making a loss and still be advised to keep producing? Surely a loss means stop.

**Tutor:** It means stop EVENTUALLY, but not necessarily NOW, and the reason is that in the short run, some of the bleeding continues whether you produce or not. Separate the two kinds of cost with a concrete case. A guesthouse pays rent of twenty thousand a month on a lease with a year to run — fixed, owed even if every room stands empty. Its variable costs — cleaning, laundry, breakfasts, casual staff — run at three hundred per occupied room-night, avoidable by closing. Suppose the market only bears five hundred a night, and all-in average cost including the rent works out at six hundred: the guesthouse loses money on the books. Should it lock the doors? Compare the two futures. Closed: it earns nothing and still owes twenty thousand — a twenty-thousand-rand loss, guaranteed. Open: every five-hundred-rand room-night covers its own three hundred of variable cost and contributes two hundred toward that unavoidable rent. Sixty room-nights contribute twelve thousand, shrinking the monthly loss to eight thousand. Producing at a loss is here the loss-MINIMISING choice. The rule falls out cleanly: stay open while price covers average variable cost; the moment price falls below even the variable cost per unit — say to two hundred and fifty — every guest deepens the hole, and closing wins. That is why the shut-down point sits at minimum AVC and why the supply curve is MC above that point. And keep the time horizon honest: when the lease expires, all costs become avoidable, and a price below full average cost means exit. Short run: cover your variable costs. Long run: cover everything, or leave.

**Mandy:** Last one: if competition pushes every firm's profit down to normal in the long run, why would anyone bother starting a business at all?

**Tutor:** Because normal profit is not nothing — and because the road to the long run is where the rewards live. First, be precise about what normal profit contains: it is the full opportunity cost of the owner's capital, time and risk-bearing. A shopkeeper earning normal profit is earning as much as her money would earn invested elsewhere PLUS as much as her labour would fetch employed elsewhere, with the independence thrown in. Calling that break-even is economists' vocabulary, not misery — millions of sound businesses live exactly there, and staying is rational, which is precisely what the concept is built to show. Second, the model's long run takes real time to arrive, and entrepreneurship is the art of living in the meantime. The first seller to bring cherry tomatoes to the street, the first guesthouse in the newly fashionable town, the farmer who adopts drip irrigation before the neighbours — each earns genuine economic profit for as long as imitation takes. Competition then erodes it; the entrepreneur's answer is to innovate again. Economic profit in a competitive economy is not a permanent address, it is a moving target — and chasing it is what drags the whole economy's costs down and quality up, which is the deep reason the model celebrates entry rather than mourning the profits it erases. Third, remember what protects a genuine cost advantage: a farmer whose land is simply better earns what looks like surplus even in the long run — economists fold that into rent on the superior resource. So: start the business for normal profit at worst, temporary economic profit at best, and the chance that your innovation keeps you one step ahead of the entry that is always coming.
