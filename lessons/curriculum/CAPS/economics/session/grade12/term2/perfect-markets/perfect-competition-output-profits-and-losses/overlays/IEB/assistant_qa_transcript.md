**Mandy:** Class questions before we finish! Here's the one half the room asked: if perfect competition exists nowhere in the real world, why do we spend a whole lesson learning a market that isn't real?

**Tutor:** For the same reason a builder carries a spirit level: no wall on earth is perfectly level, but you cannot see how far a wall leans without an instrument that shows you level. Perfect competition is the instrument. It tells us what a market delivers when nobody holds power over the price: output where price equals marginal cost, so resources land exactly where consumers point them; production at the lowest achievable cost per unit; and profits worn down to normal in the long run, so nobody collects more than their contribution. With that instrument in hand, the lean of every real market becomes measurable. How far above marginal cost does a monopolist price? How much output does a cartel withhold compared with the competitive quantity? Those questions have no meaning without the benchmark. The model also powers real policy: the Competition Commission's whole approach is to find where a market has drifted from the benchmark — collusion, blocked entry, dominance abused — and push it back. And a few markets get close enough for the model to predict well: fresh-produce floors, grain at the silo, shares changing hands on the exchange all behave nearly as the model describes, sellers as price takers. So the essay sentence is this: perfect competition is not a description of the world, it is a STANDARD held against the world — and each market structure you study next is defined by exactly which of its assumptions gets broken.

**Mandy:** Next one: MR equals MC feels like something only textbooks believe. No stall owner at any market has ever drawn a marginal cost curve. How can a rule nobody actually calculates explain real decisions?

**Tutor:** Nobody draws it, and almost everybody obeys it — that is what makes the rule deep rather than silly. It is not a procedure firms perform; it is the shape of the question survival forces on them. Watch a bakkie trader decide whether to add a second load of oranges this week. She asks: what will the extra load cost me — the stock, the fuel, my nephew's help? And what will it bring in? Costs six hundred, brings in nine hundred: she adds it. If a third load would cost seven hundred and bring in five hundred, she stops at two. Without a single curve she has just balanced marginal revenue against marginal cost, load by load — because the question is one more load worth it IS the marginal question. The farmer weighing one more hectare, the salon owner weighing one more chair, the taxi owner weighing one more vehicle: all running the same comparison. The textbook curves are that ordinary question drawn smoothly, kilo by kilo instead of load by load. And there is a sharper mechanism behind it: businesses that persistently answer wrongly — producing units that cost more than they earn — leak money and disappear, while those answering rightly grow. The market performs the calculation even for owners who never do. In an essay, state the rule formally, then add the mature line: real firms rarely compute it, but competition enforces it on them anyway.

**Mandy:** This one confused our whole study group: the lesson said a business making a loss should sometimes carry on producing. How can producing at a loss ever beat closing the doors?

**Tutor:** Because in the short run some costs bleed whether the doors are open or shut, and the clever question is which future bleeds LESS. Take a small bus operator with a vehicle on finance: the monthly instalment of fifteen thousand rand is owed no matter what — a fixed cost. The running costs — diesel, the driver's daily wage, tyres — come to about two hundred rand per trip and stop the moment the bus parks. Now suppose fares have fallen so each trip brings in only three hundred rand, while the full cost per trip, instalment included, works out at three hundred and eighty. On paper: losses. Should the bus park? Compare the futures. Parked: income zero, and the fifteen thousand instalment still arrives — a guaranteed fifteen-thousand-rand hole. Running: every three-hundred-rand trip covers its own two hundred of running costs and leaves one hundred toward the instalment; eighty trips contribute eight thousand, shrinking the monthly hole to seven thousand. Running at a loss is the loss-MINIMISING move. The rule falls straight out: keep producing while price covers average variable cost; the moment fares drop below even the running cost per trip — say to one hundred and eighty — every trip digs deeper, and parking wins. That is why the shut-down point sits at minimum AVC, and why the supply curve is MC above it. And mind the horizon: when the finance term ends, all costs become avoidable, and fares below full average cost mean leaving the route. Short run, cover the variable costs; long run, cover everything or go.

**Mandy:** Last question: the long run erodes everyone's profit down to normal. If that is where every business ends up, why does anyone open one in the first place?

**Tutor:** Two answers, and both belong in your evaluation paragraphs. First, normal profit is a floor worth standing on, not a punishment. Be precise about its contents: it is the full opportunity cost of the owner's money, hours and risk. A trader earning normal profit earns as much as her savings would earn banked PLUS as much as her labour would fetch employed — with independence as a bonus. Economists call that break-even; a household calls it a living, and millions of perfectly sound businesses live exactly there. The concept exists precisely to show that staying is rational. Second — and this is the part entrepreneurs actually chase — the long run takes time to arrive, and the meantime pays. The first stall to sell peeled and packed butternut chunks, the first guesthouse in a town the tourists just discovered, the farmer running drip irrigation before the neighbours catch on: each collects genuine economic profit for as long as imitation takes. Entry then eats it — so the entrepreneur innovates again. In a competitive economy, economic profit is never a home; it is a moving target, and the chasing of it is what drives costs down and quality up across the whole economy. That is why the model treats entry as a hero, not a thief. One refinement for top marks: a producer with a genuinely superior resource — better soil, a better location — keeps earning what looks like surplus even in the long run; economists count that as rent on the superior resource, not disappearing profit. So: open the business for a floor of normal profit, a window of economic profit, and the chance that your next idea keeps you ahead of the entry that never stops coming.
