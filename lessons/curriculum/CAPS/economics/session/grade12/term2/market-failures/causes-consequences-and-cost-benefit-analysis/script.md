# Part 1 — Expert

The perfect market delivered both efficiencies; the imperfect structures chipped away at them. This session completes the argument: even a market with many sellers and free entry can misallocate resources systematically. MARKET FAILURE is when the price mechanism, left to itself, produces the WRONG quantities — too much of some things, too little of others, none at all of a few — judged against what society actually values. The topic supplies the intellectual foundation for the whole public-sector half of the syllabus, and it carries two workhorse tools: the distinction between private and social costs and benefits, and cost-benefit analysis. Paper 2 loves this topic in both essay and data-response form.

## Subtopic: The Concept and Causes of Market Failure

Define with precision: market failure exists when markets fail to allocate resources EFFICIENTLY — when the quantity produced and consumed differs from the socially optimal quantity, so society's welfare could be improved by a different allocation. Note what the definition does NOT say: it does not say prices are high, or firms are greedy. It says the QUANTITIES are wrong.

The syllabus lists the causes; learn them as six, each with its mechanism. One: EXTERNALITIES — costs or benefits that land on third parties who were never part of the transaction; the price ignores them, so the quantity is wrong. Full machinery in the next section. Two: MISSING MARKETS — public goods. Recall the pair of properties from the public-sector topic: non-rivalry and non-excludability. No firm can charge for streetlights, so the market for them simply never forms — failure at its most absolute. Merit goods are under-supplied for a softer reason: buyers undervalue education, vaccination, insurance, or cannot afford them; demerit goods — alcohol, tobacco, gambling — are over-consumed. Three: IMPERFECT COMPETITION — last lesson's structures. A monopolist prices above marginal cost and restricts output below the social optimum; the misallocation is the gap. Four: IMPERFECT INFORMATION — buyers who do not know a product is dangerous, workers who do not know a job's risks, lenders who know less than borrowers; wrong information, wrong quantities. Five: IMMOBILITY of factors — resources that cannot move to their best use: miners in a dying coal town who cannot retrain or relocate; capital sunk in the wrong province; structural unemployment as the standing South African case. Six: INEQUALITY in income distribution — markets weight demand by purchasing power, so the rand votes of the rich pull resources toward luxuries while essential needs backed by no money go unmet. A market can be perfectly efficient and still leave a nation hungry; efficiency and equity are different tests.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: the definition centred on wrong quantities, and the six causes each tied to its mechanism.

## Subtopic: Externalities — Private Against Social Costs and Benefits

Externalities carry the topic's diagrams, so build the vocabulary first. PRIVATE costs are the producer's own: wages, materials, electricity. EXTERNAL costs fall on bystanders: the smoke, the noise, the poisoned river. SOCIAL COST is the sum: private plus external. Same on the other side: private benefit to the buyer, external benefit to third parties, SOCIAL BENEFIT the sum. The market equates demand and supply — which reflect only PRIVATE benefit and PRIVATE cost. Society wants output where social benefit equals social cost. Whenever externals exist, those two quantities differ: there is the failure.

Negative production externality first, described for drawing. A paper mill on the Vaal discharges waste. Draw quantity across, price up. The demand curve slopes down. The supply curve — the mill's marginal PRIVATE cost — slopes up. But every ton of paper also imposes downstream costs: sick livestock, water treatment, dead fish. Add that external cost on top of private cost and you get a second, higher supply curve — the marginal SOCIAL cost curve, running parallel above the private one, the vertical gap between them being the external cost per ton. The market settles where demand crosses the PRIVATE cost curve: a large output at a low price. Society's best point is where demand crosses the SOCIAL cost curve: a smaller output at a higher price. The market OVER-produces the polluting good and under-prices it — the town subsidises the mill with its lungs and its river.

Positive consumption externality, mirror image. Vaccination: my jab protects me — private benefit — and also everyone I never infect — external benefit. The market demand curve counts only private benefit; the social benefit curve runs parallel ABOVE it. The market settles where private demand crosses supply; society wants the larger quantity where SOCIAL benefit crosses supply. The market UNDER-produces vaccination, education, research — every good whose benefits leak generously onto others.

One sentence to rule the section: goods with external costs are over-produced, goods with external benefits are under-produced, and in each case the market price fails to tell the truth about what the good really costs or really gives.

Stop for this section's questions now — the four cost-benefit terms, both diagrams narrated with their two crossing points, and the over- and under-production conclusions.

## Subtopic: Consequences and the State's Toolkit

Consequences first, because the syllabus asks for them by name. Resources misallocate: too much paper, too little vaccination — society's welfare below its possibility. The environment degrades where external costs go unpriced — the free river absorbs what no one is billed for. Public and merit goods go missing or scarce: the unlit street, the unbuilt clinic. Inequality compounds: those least able to move or protect themselves live nearest the smoke. And macro-costs follow micro-failures: structural unemployment from immobile factors, health burdens from demerit goods, growth foregone from under-built human capital.

Now the toolkit, instrument by instrument, each aimed at a specific failure. TAXES on external costs: levy on each ton of pollution or packet of cigarettes a tax equal to the external cost — the polluter now pays the social cost, the private cost curve lifts up onto the social cost curve, and the market itself chooses the right smaller quantity. South Africa's carbon tax, the fuel levy, sin taxes on alcohol and tobacco, the sugar-sweetened beverages levy — all this instrument. SUBSIDIES on external benefits: pay schools, clinics and researchers the value of the benefits they scatter, pulling private incentives up to social ones — no-fee schools, free primary healthcare, housing subsidies. DIRECT PROVISION where markets are missing entirely: the state supplies public goods — defence, street lighting, the courts — from tax revenue. REGULATION where prices are too blunt: emission standards, banning lead in petrol, zoning, licensing, minimum information rules — the label that must list ingredients answers imperfect information directly. PRICE INTERVENTIONS: maximum prices to keep essentials affordable — with the standard side effect of shortages and queues when set below equilibrium; minimum prices, like the minimum wage, to protect suppliers of labour — with the standard risk of surpluses, here unemployment, if set above equilibrium. And REDISTRIBUTION — progressive tax and grants — answering the inequality failure directly.

Every instrument has a cost, and stating it is the evaluation mark: taxes can be set at the wrong level, subsidies must be financed, direct provision meets the provisioning problems of the public-sector topic, controls distort quantities, and every intervention risks the public-sector failure you already know. The state corrects the market with imperfect tools held by imperfect hands — which is why each intervention needs its own justification, not a blanket one.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — the consequences named, each instrument matched to the failure it targets with a South African example, and the cost of each stated.

## Subtopic: Cost-Benefit Analysis

When the state decides whether to build the dam, the highway or the power station, it cannot read profit off a market — many of the costs and benefits are external, unpriced, or decades away. The tool for the decision is COST-BENEFIT ANALYSIS, CBA: a disciplined comparison of ALL social costs against ALL social benefits of a project, private and external alike.

The method in five steps. One: identify every cost — construction and operation, but also the farmland flooded, the households relocated, the wetland lost. Two: identify every benefit — electricity generated, floods prevented, jobs created, travel time saved, lives lengthened. Three: attach money values, including to the unpriced — and here lives the craft: time saved valued at the wage rate, a wetland at the water purification it performs, risk to life through statistical willingness to pay. Four: DISCOUNT future values to the present — a rand of benefit in twenty years is worth less than one today, so distant flows are shrunk by a discount rate before adding. Five: compare — proceed if social benefits exceed social costs, or rank projects by their benefit-cost ratios when budgets are scarce.

Why is CBA the right tool for public choices? Precisely because it internalises on paper what markets ignore in practice: the externalities, the unpriced goods, the third parties. A private investor asks what the toll road earns; CBA asks what the road does to everyone — including the villages bypassed and the accidents avoided.

State the limits like an examiner. Valuing the unpriceable is contestable — what is a wetland or a life worth, and who decides? The discount rate is a moral choice disguised as arithmetic: a high rate silences future generations, which is why climate projects hinge on it. Benefits are routinely overstated and costs understated by projects' own champions — optimism bias, with South Africa's own mega-projects as cautionary tales. And CBA sums rands without asking WHOSE rands — a project can pass while hurting the poorest; distribution must be judged alongside the total. Used honestly, CBA disciplines politics with arithmetic; used cynically, it dresses politics in arithmetic. Both sentences belong in a top essay.

The final questions of this part are with you now: why public projects need CBA, the five steps in order, discounting explained, and the four limits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the whole topic from one riverbank — because market failure is not a theory first; it is a smell in the air, a bill nobody sent, and a clinic queue, and the theory only names what the riverbank already knows.

## Subtopic: The Bill Nobody Sends

A chicken farm sits upstream; a village sits downstream. The farm sells chickens at forty rand each, and at that price it sells thousands — the market working, apparently.

Now stand in the village. The river arrives carrying the farm's runoff. The village boils its water — paraffin costs money. Children get stomach bugs — clinic visits, taxi fare, missed school. The vegetable gardens along the bank yield less. Add it up honestly and every chicken costs the village perhaps five rand in coping. But no bill for that five rand ever reaches the farm or the chicken buyer. The shopper in the city pays forty rand and thinks that is what a chicken costs. It is not. A chicken COSTS forty-five rand — forty paid by the buyer, five paid by the village in paraffin, taxi fare and lost spinach. The missing five rand is what economists call an external cost, and the forty-rand price is a lie of omission.

And see what the lie does: because chickens LOOK five rand cheaper than they truly are, people buy MORE of them than they would at the honest price. The market over-produces exactly the thing that hurts the village — not from malice, but from a missing bill.

Flip it. The village teacher runs evening classes at fifty rand a month. Her students gain — they pay, so that is counted. But the whole village gains too: students who read help parents with forms, start small businesses, stay out of trouble. Those gains land on people who never paid fifty rand. Counted honestly, a month of classes is worth far more than fifty rand — so at fifty, too few people enrol and the classes stay half-empty. The village under-buys exactly the thing that helps it most.

One missing bill makes too many chickens; one missing thank-you makes too few classes. That is market failure.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the riverbank are coming to you right now: the honest cost of a chicken, why the lie increases sales, the evening classes mirror-case, and the one-sentence pattern.

## Subtopic: Fixing the Price Until It Tells the Truth

If the problem is a lying price, the fix is to make the price tell the truth — and almost every government tool you have ever heard of is a version of that.

Send the missing bill: put a five-rand levy on each chicken, sized to the village's real coping costs. Now the farm's own arithmetic includes the river. Chickens sell at forty-five; buyers buy fewer; the runoff shrinks — and the levy money can pay for the village's water treatment. That is all a carbon tax is; that is what the tobacco excise is: the hospital bill, delivered in advance at the till. Notice the elegance — government never orders the farm to do anything; it just corrects the price and lets the market do the rest.

Send the missing thank-you: subsidise the evening classes down to twenty rand, because the village gains more than the student does. Enrolment fills. That is every no-fee school and free clinic: society paying for the part of the benefit that society keeps.

Some things need more than a corrected price. The streetlight from the public-sector topic — no bill CAN be sent, so the state buys it outright for everyone. And sometimes the price is left alone and the BEHAVIOUR is ruled directly: no dumping within fifty metres of the river, full stop — regulation, for when the harm is too grave to merely tax. Poison has no correct price.

But keep last topic's warning in your pocket: the same state that sets the levy can set it wrong, spend it badly, or award the water-treatment tender to a cousin. Fixing a lying price with a lying process helps nobody. The honest sentence: markets fail, and states fail at fixing them — good policy is choosing the smaller failure, case by case.

Your questions for this part are up now: the levy as a delivered bill, the subsidy as a delivered thank-you, when direct provision and outright rules beat both, and the smaller-failure sentence.

## Subtopic: Should the Dam Be Built?

Final scene. Government proposes a dam upstream: electricity for the region, water for the dry years — and the village's fields drowned. Shouting fills the community hall. How does anyone DECIDE such a thing? Not by market prices — half of what matters here has no price. The grown-up tool is to count everything, on both sides, honestly. Watch it work.

Costs: the concrete and cranes, yes — but also the eighty households moved, the fields lost, the fish that no longer run, the wetland that used to clean the water for free. Benefits: the electricity sold, yes — but also the floods that no longer drown the lower valley, the clinics that can refrigerate vaccines, the jobs, the boreholes no longer needed. Money values go on all of it — even the awkward ones: the wetland is worth at least what a treatment plant doing the same job would cost; the travel time saved is worth the wages of the hours.

Then the step people miss: most costs land NOW, most benefits arrive over forty years — and a promise of a rand in forty years is not worth a rand today. So future benefits are shrunk before counting — discounted. Choose the shrink-rate carelessly and you can make any dam look brilliant or worthless; that dial deserves the argument it causes.

Count, shrink, compare: if all-in benefits beat all-in costs, build; if not, don't — and if three projects compete for one budget, rank them by benefit per rand. That is cost-benefit analysis: the missing bills and missing thank-yous, finally all on one page.

Close with the caution that earns the last marks. The counting can be gamed — champions of the dam will round its benefits up and its costs down. The page adds rands without asking whose: a dam can pass the total test while the eighty drowned households carry the whole cost — so ask about the losers, and about compensating them, before signing. Done honestly, this page is the closest thing economics has to fairness on a big decision. Done dishonestly, it is a lie with a spreadsheet. Your job — in the exam and after it — is to know the difference.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: why the dam cannot be judged by market prices, the counting on both sides, the shrinking of future rands, ranking by benefit per rand, and the two-line caution.
