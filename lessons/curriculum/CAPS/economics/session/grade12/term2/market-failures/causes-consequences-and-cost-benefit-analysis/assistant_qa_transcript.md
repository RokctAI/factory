**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First one, and it's the objection every class raises: if the paper mill's pollution really costs the village so much, why doesn't the village just sue the mill, or the mill pay the village to stop complaining? Why does government need to be involved at all?

**Tutor:** That objection has a famous history, and taking it seriously actually deepens the case for intervention. In principle, if the village owned a clear legal right to a clean river, it could demand payment for every ton of discharge — and the mill, forced to buy pollution like any other input, would cut back to exactly the efficient quantity. Private bargaining would fix the externality with no tax in sight. Now count what that requires in practice. The village is hundreds of households: who negotiates, and who stops each household from free-riding on the others' efforts? Proving WHICH farm or mill upstream caused WHICH illness downstream takes scientists and years. Court cases cost more than most villages hold, and the mill knows it. And the deepest problem: many of those harmed cannot bargain at all — children, downstream towns not yet built, the next generation inheriting the river. Economists call all of this transaction costs, and when they are high, the private fix never happens; the externality just sits there. That is precisely when the state earns its place: a pollution levy or an emission standard is society doing, at one stroke and at law, the bargain the village could never organise itself. So in an essay, concede the elegant case — with clear property rights and cheap bargaining, private deals can internalise externalities — then show why rivers, air and climate flunk those conditions, which is why the toolkit exists.

**Mandy:** Next: sin taxes on cigarettes and alcohol keep rising, but people keep smoking and drinking. Doesn't that prove the tax approach just doesn't work?

**Tutor:** It proves something subtler — that the tax is working exactly as much as demand allows, and that the syllabus's elasticity tools from Grade 11 decide the outcome. A corrective tax has two possible jobs, and it cannot do both fully at once. Job one: shrink the harmful quantity. Job two: make remaining users pay the social cost — fund the hospitals their choices burden. Which job dominates depends on price elasticity of demand. Cigarettes are addictive, so demand is inelastic: a big price rise cuts consumption only modestly. Result: consumption falls a little — and it genuinely does fall, especially among the young, whose demand is far more elastic because they are not yet addicted and have thin wallets — while revenue rises a lot, which finances the external costs. That is not failure; that is the inelastic-demand version of success. The evaluation marks live in the honest complications. Push the tax high enough and you feed an illicit market — South Africa saw exactly this when cigarette sales were banned outright in 2020: smoking barely stopped, but the trade went underground, untaxed and unregulated, and some of that market never came back to the books. There is also a distribution worry: sin taxes take a larger share of poor households' budgets. So the mature judgment: corrective taxes reliably deliver revenue matched to harm always, quantity reduction in proportion to elasticity, best results among the not-yet-addicted — and they must stop short of the rate at which the illegal market takes over. A tax is a dial, not a switch.

**Mandy:** This one about the dam: you said the discount rate is a moral choice disguised as arithmetic. That sounds dramatic for a percentage. Can you show what it actually changes?

**Tutor:** Watch the arithmetic do its quiet work, because two reasonable-sounding numbers can flip the same project between yes and no. Take a wetland the dam would destroy, providing water purification worth ten million rand a year, effectively forever. How much is that stream of future benefit worth today? At a three percent discount rate, a perpetual ten million a year is valued around three hundred and thirty million rand — a huge cost of building the dam, likely decisive against it. At ten percent, the identical wetland is worth about one hundred million — perhaps less than the dam's electricity, so the dam proceeds. Same wetland, same science, same honesty: the choice of rate alone decided. Now see what the rate MEANS. Discounting says benefits arriving later count less. For private money there is a market logic — funds tied up have alternative uses earning interest. But stretch across generations and the logic turns ethical: a benefit in fifty years accrues to people not yet born, and a high rate values their welfare at nearly nothing. Discount at ten percent and a catastrophe a century away is worth almost zero today — which is why climate economics fights precisely over this number, and why serious analyses of long-horizon projects use LOW rates for far-future effects. For the exam, hold the two-sided sentence: discounting is legitimate — a rand today truly is worth more than a rand next year — but the rate chosen for public projects embeds a judgment about how much the future matters, so a CBA should always be shown at more than one rate. If the answer flips between rates, the arithmetic has told you the decision is really about values, and it should be argued as one.

**Mandy:** Last one: our notes list inequality as a market failure, but my brother's textbook from varsity says inequality isn't inefficiency and shouldn't be on the list. Who's right for our exam?

**Tutor:** Both, once you see they are answering different questions — and CAPS has made a defensible choice you should understand rather than just obey. The purist position: efficiency asks whether resources go where they create most value GIVEN the pattern of demand; a market can pass that test perfectly while the pattern of demand itself reflects a brutal distribution of income. Nothing in the machinery malfunctioned — so, says the purist, call it an equity problem, not a market failure. The broader position, which your syllabus takes: judge the market by whether it delivers what society actually needs, and the price mechanism has a built-in bias — it counts rands, not people. A luxury clinic for the wealthy outbids a basic clinic for a township not because society values it more, but because the rands behind it are thicker. Demand backed by no money is invisible to the market entirely, however urgent the need behind it. On that view the misallocation is real: resources flow to low-urgency wants over high-urgency needs, and the state's redistribution — progressive tax, grants, free basic services — is a correction of the mechanism's blind spot, exactly parallel to correcting a missing bill. There is also a bridge between the camps worth one line: severe inequality CAUSES textbook failures — the poor cannot finance education or insurance even when the returns are enormous, which is a capital-market failure with inequality as its engine. For the exam: list inequality among the causes as CAPS does, explain the rand-votes mechanism, and if you want the distinction-level flourish, add that some economists class it as an equity rather than efficiency failure — showing you know where the line is drawn and why your syllabus draws it where it does.
