**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we wrap! First: the lesson keeps saying the quantities are wrong. But who decides what the right quantity is? Isn't whatever the market produces just... the answer?

**Tutor:** Sharpest question of the day, because the whole topic stands or falls on it — and the answer is that the market's own logic supplies the standard, not some official's taste. Here is the reasoning. A transaction between a willing buyer and a willing seller makes both better off; that is why free exchange creates value. The right quantity is therefore the quantity at which every unit that creates more value than it destroys gets produced, and none beyond — where the benefit of the last unit to EVERYONE affected equals its cost to EVERYONE affected. Notice the word everyone. When only the buyer and seller feel the effects, the market's demand and supply curves capture the full story, and the market quantity IS the right quantity — no failure, no case for interference. The failure begins when people OUTSIDE the deal feel effects that the price never counts: the household downwind of the coal dust never agreed to anything, yet pays in soap and inhalers. Then the market equates the wrong curves — private ones — and lands on a quantity that a full accounting rejects. So the social optimum is not somebody's preference; it is the market's own efficiency logic, extended to include the bystanders the price forgot. Put it in one exam sentence: the market fails by its own standard — voluntary exchange should make society better off, and where spillovers exist, some exchanges quietly make third parties worse off than the deal makes its parties better.

**Mandy:** Next one: taxes on cigarettes and alcohol are huge, but people carry on smoking and drinking anyway. If the quantity barely moves, hasn't the corrective tax just failed?

**Tutor:** Not necessarily — but your question exposes exactly the tension a top answer should name. A corrective tax has two jobs that pull in different directions. Job one: shrink the quantity toward the social optimum. Job two: make the users who remain carry the social cost of their habit, so the price finally tells the truth. Now bring in a tool from earlier in the year — price elasticity of demand. Addictive goods have famously inelastic demand: a large price rise trims consumption only modestly. So the quantity does fall — decades of excise increases have genuinely reduced smoking, especially among teenagers, whose thinner wallets make their demand the most elastic in the market — but it falls slowly, and meanwhile the tax collects heavily from those who continue. Is that failure? Partly no: even when quantity barely moves, the revenue is the hospital bill collected in advance, which is fairer than sending it to non-smokers through the health budget. Partly yes, and say so: with very inelastic demand, tax alone cannot reach the optimum, which is why policy stacks instruments — excise PLUS advertising bans, age limits, plain packaging, public smoking rules. And add the honest complication: push the tax too high and the market dives underground — illicit cigarettes already claim a serious share of South African sales, paying no tax and obeying no rules. So the evaluation sentence: corrective taxes on inelastic goods change behaviour slowly, raise revenue certainly, and beyond some level feed the illicit market — which is why regulation walks beside them.

**Mandy:** Here's one from the back row: if the smoke from the kiln harms the neighbours, why do we need a clever tax at all? Why doesn't the community just make the kiln pay them directly?

**Tutor:** That question has a famous history, and exploring it wins evaluation marks. In principle, private bargaining CAN solve an externality: if the neighbours own the right to clean air, the kiln must pay them for every ton of soot, and the smoke instantly enters the kiln's arithmetic — no government needed. The insight is that externalities persist only where such deals fail to happen. So ask why the deal fails, and you discover exactly when the state must step in. First obstacle: numbers. One kiln, three hundred affected households — someone must organise them, agree on shares, negotiate, enforce; the costs of transacting swallow the gains, so the bargain never forms. Second: proof. Whose asthma came from whose smoke? Which kiln, which coal fire, which dust road? Damage that is real can still be unattributable. Third: rights. Until the law says whether the kiln owns the right to burn or the neighbours own the right to breathe, there is nothing to bargain over — and the poor rarely win the fight over who holds the right. Fourth: power. A big employer negotiating with the households that depend on its wages is not a fair table. Where a spill involves two firms and a fence line, private deals genuinely do fix it — that happens between farms and factories more often than people think. Where it involves smoke drifting over hundreds of unorganised households, the state's tax or rule is simply the bargain those households could never negotiate for themselves. That is the deep reading: government intervention is not the opposite of the market solution; it is the market solution carried out on behalf of those the market could not gather into the room.

**Mandy:** Last one: cost-benefit analysis puts a money value on a person's life. That feels wrong. How can any of this be legitimate?

**Tutor:** Your discomfort is correct — hold onto it — and yet the alternative is worse, and a mature answer walks that line carefully. Start with what CBA does NOT claim: it never prices a named person, and no serious economist claims your life is worth some figure in rand. What it prices is RISK. When a barrier is built on a lethal stretch of road, nobody knows who would have died; we know only that the risk of death falls a little for everyone who drives it. Society reveals, through its own choices — the extra pay demanded for dangerous work, what households spend on safety — roughly what a small reduction in risk is worth. Multiply a tiny risk reduction across millions of trips and you get the value of a statistical life: a tool for comparing safety projects, not a price tag on a person. Now the uncomfortable defence: refusing to value risk does not protect life — it hands the decision to accident. A budget can build the barrier, staff the clinic, or light the intersection, never all three. Choose without numbers and you choose by loudest lobby or newest headline, and more people die than the budget needed to permit. The number, for all its coldness, lets the same money prevent the most harm. Then close with the limit, because the lesson demands it: the method must never become a licence to impose deadly risk on the powerless because compensation looks cheap — that is where the distribution question, whose costs and whose risks, must override the total. Say both things in the exam: valuing risk is how scarce safety money saves the most lives, and asking who bears the risk is how the method stays moral. The technique and the conscience must arrive together.
