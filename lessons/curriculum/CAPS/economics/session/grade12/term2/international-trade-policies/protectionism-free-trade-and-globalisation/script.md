# Part 1 — Expert

Term one established WHY nations trade — comparative advantage — and HOW the payments settle. This session takes up the political question that follows: how OPEN should a country's borders be? Every government stands between two poles: FREE TRADE, letting goods cross borders untaxed and unhindered, and PROTECTIONISM, shielding home producers from foreign competition. The syllabus wants the instruments of each, the arguments for and against both, South Africa's two export-oriented strategies, the machinery of globalisation — the WTO and the major protocols — and the ladder of economic integration. This is a Paper 1 essay topic with a guaranteed evaluation component: no side wins outright, and the marks live in weighing.

## Subtopic: The Instruments of Protection

Protection is practised through a toolkit; learn each tool with its mechanism. TARIFFS — customs duties on imports: a tax added at the border, raising the import's shelf price so home products compete more easily, and yielding revenue as a bonus. Import QUOTAS — quantity limits: only so many tons or units may enter per year; beyond price effects, quotas hand import licences to favoured firms, which is why economists dislike them more than tariffs. SUBSIDIES to domestic producers — payments or tax breaks that lower home costs, letting local firms undercut imports without any visible border tax; the protection is real but hidden in the budget. EXCHANGE CONTROL — rationing foreign currency so would-be importers simply cannot pay abroad. Physical and administrative barriers: outright import bans on some goods, health and technical STANDARDS applied more zealously to foreign goods, deliberate red tape at the port. And EXPORT incentives — the mirror image, subsidising sales abroad.

Two terms complete the vocabulary. DUMPING: selling exports at below cost or below the home-market price, often to kill off competitors in the target market before raising prices — predatory pricing across borders. Anti-dumping duties are the WTO-sanctioned response, and South Africa applies them, for instance on some imported chicken portions and steel. INFANT INDUSTRY: a new domestic industry that cannot yet match established foreign rivals' costs but could, once grown to scale — the classic candidate for temporary protection.

Note the incidence, because it anchors the evaluation later: every one of these instruments raises prices or narrows choice for CONSUMERS at home. Protection is never free; it is a transfer from consumers, who pay a little more each, to protected producers and their workers, who each gain a lot. That asymmetry — losses spread thin, gains concentrated — explains why protection is politically easy and economically expensive.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: each instrument with its mechanism, why quotas offend more than tariffs, dumping and the infant industry defined, and the thin-spread cost, concentrated-gain asymmetry.

## Subtopic: The Case for Protection Against the Case for Free Trade

Arguments FOR protection, as the syllabus lists them — state each fairly, then its standard rebuttal, because that paired structure IS the essay. Infant industries: new industries need shelter until scale lowers their costs; rebuttal — infants rarely admit to growing up, and protection that never lapses breeds permanent inefficiency. EMPLOYMENT: protection saves home jobs from import competition; rebuttal — it saves visible jobs in the protected industry at the cost of invisible ones elsewhere, since dearer inputs burden every downstream producer, and retaliation kills export jobs. Anti-dumping and unfair competition: shelter against predatory pricing and against imports produced under standards home firms may not breach; this is the strongest card — even free traders accept anti-dumping action. DIVERSIFICATION and self-sufficiency: a country over-dependent on a few exports or on foreign food, medicine or defence supply is strategically fragile; protection buys resilience — an argument that returned worldwide after the pandemic disrupted supply chains. Balance of payments support and revenue: tariffs curb imports and fund the state — though better instruments usually exist for both.

The case FOR free trade is comparative advantage cashed out: specialisation raises total output; consumers gain lower prices and wider choice; competition disciplines domestic monopolies and forces efficiency; scale opens — a firm confined to sixty million South Africans can serve a market of billions; and technology and ideas travel with goods. The case AGAINST free trade is the mirror of protection's case: exposed industries shed workers faster than new industries absorb them; the gains are unevenly shared; and a developing country locked into exporting raw materials may never build the industries where future advantage lies.

The syllabus asks for the DESIRABLE MIX, and the honest answer: no country practises either pole purely. The defensible combination is broadly open trade — for the efficiency and the discipline — plus targeted, temporary, conditional protection where a genuine infant, a genuine dumping case or a genuine strategic need exists, with sunset clauses so the shelter expires. Protection as scalpel, never as blanket.

Stop for this section's questions now — each protection argument with its rebuttal, the free-trade case in five points, and the desirable mix with its conditions.

## Subtopic: Export Promotion, Import Substitution, and South Africa's Path

Developing countries have run two grand strategies for industrialising behind or through trade, and South Africa has tried both.

IMPORT SUBSTITUTION: replace imports with home production, behind tariff walls — the inward path. Identify what the country imports, protect that market, and let domestic industry fill it. Benefits: industry starts, jobs appear, imports fall, and the home market is guaranteed. The record, though, is a caution: industries built behind walls tend to stay small — the home market is the ceiling — and inefficient, since the wall removes the pressure to match world costs; scarce foreign exchange goes on importing machinery for the protected factories; and consumers fund it all through higher prices. Apartheid-era South Africa ran exactly this model, sanctions reinforcing tariffs, and produced industries that struggled the day the walls came down.

EXPORT PROMOTION: the outward path — help firms SELL to the world rather than hide from it. Instruments: export incentives and finance, infrastructure that lowers exporters' costs, skills, trade missions, and industrial financing tilted toward exporting sectors. Benefits: the world market has no ceiling, so scale economies open; competing globally forces efficiency rather than excusing its absence; and export earnings ease the balance of payments. The East Asian economies industrialised this way. Costs and conditions: export subsidies collide with WTO rules; success depends on infrastructure that actually works — ports, rail, electricity — and the strategy exposes the economy to world demand swings.

Democratic South Africa swung outward: tariffs cut sharply from the 1990s, trade agreements pursued, and current policy runs through the Industrial Policy Action Plans and sector master plans — automotive the flagship, where production incentives keep global manufacturers assembling and exporting vehicles from the Eastern Cape and Gauteng; clothing, poultry and steel remain the contested cases where tariff protection is still actively argued and applied. The examinable judgment: South Africa officially favours export-led growth, retains selective protection, and its export performance is throttled less by trade policy than by the logistics and electricity constraints you met in earlier topics.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — both strategies with mechanisms, benefits and costs, the East Asian and apartheid-era contrasts, and South Africa's current mix with its examples.

## Subtopic: Globalisation, the WTO and the Ladder of Integration

GLOBALISATION is the accelerating integration of national economies — goods, services, capital, technology, information and people moving across borders in volumes and speeds without precedent. Its engines: container shipping and cheap transport, telecommunications and the internet, financial liberalisation, and the deliberate dismantling of trade barriers through successive agreements — the major trade PROTOCOLS. The line runs from the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade — GATT, 1947 — through eight rounds of tariff-cutting to the Uruguay Round, which in 1995 created the WORLD TRADE ORGANISATION.

The WTO, with over 160 members including South Africa, administers the rules: non-discrimination — a benefit granted to one trading partner extends to all, the most-favoured-nation principle; national treatment — imported goods, once inside, are treated like local ones; bound tariffs that members may not raise at will; and a DISPUTE SETTLEMENT system in which members litigate rather than retaliate. TRADE LIBERALISATION is the ongoing result: world tariffs a fraction of their 1947 levels and world trade multiplied many times over. The critiques are examinable too: rich countries kept protecting agriculture — the very sector where developing countries hold advantage; rules constrain the industrial policies today's rich countries themselves used while developing; and gains have been uneven between and within countries.

Finally the LADDER OF ECONOMIC INTEGRATION, rung by rung. A FREE TRADE AREA: members abolish tariffs among themselves but keep their own external tariffs — the African Continental Free Trade Area, AfCFTA, now being phased in across the continent, is the world's largest by membership. A CUSTOMS UNION adds a COMMON external tariff — the Southern African Customs Union, SACU, linking South Africa, Botswana, Namibia, Lesotho and Eswatini, is the world's oldest, sharing customs revenue by formula. A COMMON MARKET adds free movement of labour and capital. An ECONOMIC UNION adds harmonised policies and possibly one currency — the European Union with the euro the standing example. South Africa stands on several rungs at once: SACU member, SADC free-trade participant, AfCFTA signatory — and the AfCFTA is the syllabus's live example of Africa attempting scale: a single market of over a billion people intended to let African industries grow beyond their national ceilings.

The final questions of this part are with you now: globalisation with its engines, GATT to WTO with the core rules, the liberalisation critiques, and the four rungs with their African examples.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the whole debate at the school gate — because protection and free trade is not an abstract quarrel; it is the tuckshop, the imported sweets, and the question of who gets to sell lunch.

## Subtopic: The Tuckshop and the Vendors at the Gate

A school tuckshop has sold pies and cooldrink for years. Then vendors appear at the gate selling sweets and kotas — cheaper, and honestly, sometimes better. Learners stream out at break. Tuckshop takings halve.

The tuckshop pleads with the principal, and notice — its requests are the entire toolkit of protection, in miniature. Charge each vendor twenty rand a day to trade at the gate: a TARIFF, raising the outsiders' costs so the insider competes more easily. Allow only three vendors, chosen by list: a QUOTA — and watch how the three chosen vendors suddenly love the rule, because the licence itself is now worth money. Let the tuckshop use the school's kitchen free: a SUBSIDY, invisible to learners but real. Ban sweets at the gate as unhealthy: a STANDARD, sincere or convenient. And the tuckshop's darkest suspicion — that one vendor sells below cost on purpose, planning to raise prices once the tuckshop dies: DUMPING, the one complaint even the vendors' friends take seriously.

Now stand where the principal stands and count both sides. Protect the tuckshop and its two workers keep their jobs, the school keeps its rent — visible, concentrated, grateful gains. But eight hundred learners pay two rand more every single day — a loss so thinly spread nobody marches over it, yet added up it dwarfs the gain. That is protection's arithmetic everywhere: the saved shop is on the news; the eight hundred slightly lighter pockets never are.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the gate are coming to you right now: each request matched to its instrument, why the licensed vendors switch sides, the dumping complaint, and the visible-versus-thin-spread arithmetic.

## Subtopic: Raising the Young Shop Without Spoiling It

Now the strongest argument the tuckshop has — and what it takes for that argument to be honest.

Suppose the tuckshop is NEW: opened last term, learning to bake, buying small and dear while the gate vendors buy from big wholesalers cheap. Give it one sheltered year, says the principal, and its costs will fall to match anyone's. That is the infant-industry argument, and it is respectable: nearly every industrial country sheltered its young industries once — you cannot learn to swim while drowning.

But watch what the shelter does to incentives. With the gate taxed, the tuckshop's pies sell regardless. Why improve the pastry? Why cut costs? The year passes; the costs have not fallen; and the shop that called itself an infant at one year old calls itself an infant at ten. The protection meant as a ladder has become a hammock — and everyone in it fights to keep it. That is the true record of walls kept too long: industries that stay small, dear and loud.

So the honest version has rules, and they convert straight into exam sentences. The shelter must have an EXPIRY DATE, written down, believed. It must come with PRESSURE — targets for cost and quality, or the subsidy dies early. And it must protect only genuine infants with a real prospect of growing — not old shops pleading young. Shelter with a clock and a test: that is the desirable mix in one line — trade mostly open, protection rare, temporary and conditional.

And the two strategies from the Expert half are just the tuckshop's two futures. Import substitution: bar the gate and sell to your own eight hundred learners forever — safe, small, and the ceiling is the school roll. Export promotion: get the pastry so good you sell to the OTHER schools — no ceiling, but you must survive real competition to get there, and the school van better start every morning. South Africa, note, keeps choosing the second future while its van — the ports, the trains, the power — keeps refusing to start.

Your questions for this part are up now: the infant case stated fairly, the hammock danger, the three honesty rules, and the two futures with the van.

## Subtopic: One Big Playground

Last scene — widen the gate until it disappears.

Imagine the schools of the whole district agreeing: any learner may buy from any school's vendors, no gate fees anywhere. Suddenly the brilliant kota stand at one school sells to five schools — it buys bigger, its costs drop, everyone's lunch improves. The mediocre stands feel the squeeze — some improve, some close, and their owners are the deal's real cost, standing at their empty tables. That district agreement is a free trade area, and Africa has just signed one across an entire continent — the AfCFTA — betting that vendors confined to small national playgrounds can never grow, but a stand serving a billion customers can.

Climb the ladder rung by rung. The district schools also charge OUTSIDERS one common gate fee: a customs union — southern Africa has run one for a century, sharing the takings. Let vendors themselves move and set up at any school, with their savings: a common market. One district lunch card, one set of rules: an economic union — Europe went that far, one currency and all.

And who referees when schools quarrel — when one school claims another's vendors dump below cost, or sneaks in a secret gate fee? A district office where all the schools sit, with written rules and a complaints desk: the WTO, in a blazer. Its rules in playground terms: whatever privilege you give one school you give all — no favourites; once a vendor is inside, treat her like your own; no raising gate fees you promised to freeze; and quarrels go to the desk, not to fistfights at the fence. Imperfect — the biggest schools still shield their own vegetable gardens while preaching open gates, and the small schools notice — but the desk beats the fistfight, and the district's lunches, on the whole, have never been better or cheaper.

That is globalisation from your own gate: bigger playgrounds, better and cheaper lunches, real losers at the empty tables who deserve real help, and a rulebook forever arguing with itself. An exam answer that holds all four of those in one paragraph is a distinction answer.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: the district deal named, the four rungs climbed in order with their African examples, the referee's four rules, and the four-part honest summary. Carry the gate with you into the growth and development topic — the north-south quarrel there is this playground, drawn on a world map.
