**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! Here's the first, straight from the news: South Africa puts duties on imported chicken portions, and the debate gets furious — farmers say it saves the industry, everyone else says chicken is the nation's protein and the duty makes it dearer. Who's right?

**Tutor:** Both are stating facts; the argument is about whose facts weigh more, and a Grade 12 answer earns marks by weighing rather than picking a side by temperament. Set out the farmers' case fairly first. Poultry is South Africa's largest agricultural sector, employing tens of thousands, many in rural towns with no alternative employer — the immobility problem from market failure, in real life. The imports in question are often bulk frozen portions from producers who sell dark meat cheaply abroad because their home markets prefer breast meat — which is at least arguably dumping, the strongest card in the protection deck, and the duties South Africa applies are formally anti-dumping and safeguard measures, WTO-sanctioned instruments. Without them, say the farmers, the industry shrinks, and once slaughterhouses and hatcheries close, they do not reopen when world prices normalise. Now the other side, with the incidence analysis from the lesson: chicken is the primary protein of lower-income households, so a duty on it operates like a regressive tax — the cost is spread across millions of consumers, each paying more at every meal, and summed across the country that thin-spread loss is enormous precisely because chicken is bought so often. Higher input costs also hurt every business built on chicken, from street braaiers to takeaways. So the honest weighing: if the pricing abroad is genuinely predatory, temporary defended duties have a real justification; but the more permanent the protection becomes and the less the industry invests in matching world costs, the more it resembles the infant that never grows up — with the nation's cheapest protein as the ongoing bill. Conclude with the desirable-mix discipline: duties with expiry dates, tied to industry investment commitments — which is in fact what the poultry master plan attempts — and you have written the balanced essay the examiner wants.

**Mandy:** Next: if import substitution failed everywhere, why did every rich country use tariffs when THEY were developing? It feels like the ladder got kicked away.

**Tutor:** That is one of the sharpest critiques in development economics, and you can deploy it in an essay provided you state both halves accurately. The historical record is real: Britain protected its wool manufacturing for centuries before preaching free trade; the United States ran some of the world's highest industrial tariffs through the whole nineteenth century; Germany, Japan, South Korea and Taiwan all sheltered and subsidised their way up before liberalising. So when today's rich countries, through the WTO rules they largely wrote, restrict the subsidies and infant-industry protections that poorer countries might use, critics say the ladder was climbed and then kicked away — and rich-world agricultural protection, which persists to this day, sharpens the injustice, because farming is exactly where many developing countries hold comparative advantage. That is half the answer, and it is examinable under the critiques of trade liberalisation. But the other half matters just as much: the successful climbers did not use protection the way the failures did. Korea and Japan protected CONDITIONALLY — firms received shelter and cheap credit only while they hit export targets, so world-market discipline was imported even behind the wall; the shelter had teeth and expiry. The failures — much of protected import substitution elsewhere, including apartheid South Africa's version — protected unconditionally and permanently, and grew industries that could not survive an open door. So the mature conclusion: history vindicates neither blanket free trade nor blanket protection; it vindicates disciplined, temporary, performance-linked protection inside a broadly outward orientation — and it convicts the current global rules of hypocrisy at the margins without proving that permanent walls would have served the poor world better.

**Mandy:** This one comes up every year: what's the actual point of the AfCFTA? Africa's countries barely trade with each other anyway — most of our exports go overseas.

**Tutor:** You have just stated the problem that IS the point. Intra-African trade sits somewhere around a sixth of the continent's trade, against well over half within Europe or Asia — and the composition differs even more than the volume. Africa's exports to the rest of the world are dominated by raw materials: ores, oil, unprocessed crops. But look at what African countries sell to EACH OTHER, and the share of manufactured and processed goods jumps dramatically. That is the prize. The reason African industry stays small is the tuckshop ceiling: a factory confined to a market of five, twenty, even sixty million consumers cannot reach the scale at which costs fall to world levels, so the continent imports manufactures it could, at scale, make. Stitching fifty-plus small playgrounds into one market of well over a billion people is the only route to that scale that does not depend on the goodwill of markets elsewhere. For South Africa specifically the stakes are direct: we are the continent's most industrialised economy, and our vehicle, food, chemical and retail sectors are precisely what a continental market buys. Now the honest caveats, because the AfCFTA is a bet, not a result. Tariffs are the smaller half of the barrier: the true costs are borders that take days, roads and rail that do not connect, ports that queue, and paperwork in duplicate — reduce tariffs without fixing logistics and little changes. Rules of origin must stop outside goods being trans-shipped through the lowest-tariff member. Revenue-dependent governments fear losing customs income. And the gains will concentrate in the industrialised members first — which is why the agreement carries development provisions, and why smaller members negotiated slower phase-ins. So frame it for the exam: the AfCFTA attacks the right problem — scale — with the right instrument — integration — and its success will be decided by the unglamorous work of borders, roads and rules, not by the signing ceremony.

**Mandy:** Last one: globalisation clearly makes things cheaper — but my uncle lost his job when the clothing factory in his town closed against imports. When I write the essay, how do I say globalisation is good when I've seen what it costs?

**Tutor:** Write the essay with your uncle in it — because an answer that holds both truths is not weaker, it is exactly what the top of the mark scheme describes. Start with the aggregate truth, stated without flinching: openness has lowered the price of clothing, phones, food and machinery for every South African household; it disciplines our monopolies; it gave our vehicle industry and our fruit farmers markets no domestic demand could offer. Those gains are diffuse, continuous and real — the eight-hundred-learners side of the arithmetic. Then state your uncle's truth with the same precision: when the clothing quota walls came down in the 2000s and Asian imports surged, factories in specific towns closed, and the workers were not abstractions — they were concentrated in places with no second employer, holding skills the growing sectors did not want, unable to move for all the reasons the immobility failure describes. The gains were spread across fifty million people; the losses landed on a few valleys. Economic theory itself — not sentiment — says the winners could compensate the losers and still stay ahead; the moral and political failure is that compensation mostly never arrives. So your essay's judgment writes itself in three sentences. Openness is kept, because reversing it would make the many poorer without resurrecting the factories. The losers are owed real compensation — retraining that leads somewhere, social insurance, and deliberate re-industrialisation of the towns that carried the cost, which is what the clothing master plan and localisation drives attempt, late. And trade policy earns legitimacy only when the second sentence is honoured, because a system whose costs always land on the same people will eventually be voted down by them. Bring your uncle into the conclusion, and the examiner will see what the syllabus calls critical evaluation — and what everyone else calls telling the whole truth.
