**Mandy:** Class questions before we close! First: if free trade makes the whole economy richer, why does almost every country still protect something? Are governments just bad at economics?

**Tutor:** No — they are good at politics, and the lesson's arithmetic explains why the two pull apart. Protection's gains are CONCENTRATED: a few thousand workers in one industry, one region, a handful of firms — visible people with names, unions and marginal constituencies. Its costs are DIFFUSED: millions of consumers each paying a few rand more, spread so thin that nobody organises a march over the price of tinned tomatoes. Now run the politics: the protected group knows exactly what it stands to lose and lobbies with everything it has; the paying majority barely notices it is paying. A democratic government hears one side loudly and the other not at all — so protection persists even where an economist's ledger says it destroys more than it saves. That is not stupidity; it is an incentive structure, and naming it earns evaluation marks. Add the two honest qualifications. First, some protection IS defensible on the lesson's own terms — genuine anti-dumping cases, genuine infants with expiry dates, genuine strategic supplies — so not every barrier is capture. Second, the workers whose jobs vanish under open trade are real, concentrated losers of LIBERALISATION, and pointing at aggregate gains does not retrain a single one of them; the credible free-trade position always carries adjustment help — retraining, relocation support, time — in its other hand. So the full answer: countries protect because the politics of concentrated gains beats the economics of diffuse losses, and the repair is not pretending otherwise but designing policy — sunset clauses, compensation, independent tariff review — that forces the quiet losses into the open where they can be counted.

**Mandy:** Next one: the lesson said quotas are worse than tariffs. But both of them block imports — why does it matter which wall you build?

**Tutor:** It matters enormously, and the difference is one of the most elegant points in the topic. Both raise the home price, yes. Follow the money after that. Under a tariff, the gap between the world price and the home price flows to the STATE as customs revenue — money that can build clinics or cut other taxes. Under a quota, imports are scarce not because they are taxed but because they are rationed — and the right to import becomes a golden ticket. Whoever holds a licence buys at the world price, sells at the inflated home price, and pockets the difference privately. The same rands that a tariff would deliver to the public purse flow instead to whoever was lucky, connected or persuasive enough to be allocated a licence. That creates a second disease: firms now invest in WINNING LICENCES rather than in making better products — lobbying, relationships, sometimes bribery — what economists call rent-seeking, effort burnt chasing transfers instead of creating value. A tariff at least keeps the corruption surface small: the rate is published, the revenue is audited. Quotas breed discretion, and discretion breeds favourites. There is also a market-discipline difference worth one more sentence: under a tariff, a foreign producer who cuts costs can still climb over the wall and compete; under a quota, no amount of foreign efficiency adds a single extra unit — the door is simply shut, so the pressure on home firms to improve dies completely. Same wall from the consumer's side; very different machinery behind it. If you must protect, tax at the border transparently — never hand out tickets.

**Mandy:** This one is from our debate team: South Africa signed the AfCFTA, but our factories already struggle against imports. Doesn't opening the whole continent just invite more competition to finish them off?

**Tutor:** Hold both halves of the answer, because the question is genuinely two-sided. The fear is legitimate: openness cuts both ways, and a factory that cannot match rivals at home will not magically beat them across a border. But now look at what the AfCFTA actually changes for a South African producer. Today, our manufacturers sell mainly into a home market of sixty-odd million people — comfortable, but a low ceiling — while African neighbours often buy their manufactured goods from Asia and Europe, partly because tariffs and paperwork between African countries have historically been HIGHER than tariffs between Africa and the rest of the world. Absurd, and exactly what the agreement attacks. A continental market of over a billion people raises the ceiling for precisely the goods South Africa is relatively good at within Africa: vehicles and components, processed foods, chemicals, machinery, financial and retail services. We are, by continental standards, an industrial heavyweight — the AfCFTA opens far more doors for our factories than it opens into them, which is why our industrial lobbies pushed FOR it. Now the honest cautions, because they earn the evaluation marks. Paper tariffs are not the binding constraint: goods reach the continent through our ports and rail, and if the logistics fail, the treaty cannot help. Rules of origin — deciding what counts as African-made — will decide whether the zone builds African industry or merely re-labels imports. And some sectors will genuinely lose to Egyptian, Moroccan or Kenyan competitors — the adjustment-help principle applies inside the continent too. So the balanced sentence: the AfCFTA is South Africa's single biggest market-opening opportunity, and it will be won or lost not at the negotiating table but at the harbour.

**Mandy:** Last one: with all the criticism of the world trade rules — rich countries protecting their farmers and all of it — would poor countries be better off just walking away from the rulebook?

**Tutor:** Ask what the rulebook replaced, and the answer sharpens. Before rules, trade ran on raw power: the strong imposed terms, closed markets on a whim, and settled disputes by escalation — the tariff wars of the 1930s deepened a world depression and taught exactly that lesson. A rules-based system, whatever its flaws, gives SMALL countries the two things power politics never grants them: predictability and a forum. Predictability: bound tariffs mean a rich importer cannot suddenly slam the door on your fruit exports because of a domestic election — investments in export industries become possible because the door is contractually held open. The forum: dispute panels let a small economy argue a case against a giant and sometimes win — not always, and enforcement is imperfect, but compare the alternative, where a small economy's complaint against a giant is simply ignored. Walking away means trading purely on the giant's terms. That is why developing countries, South Africa loudly among them, choose the second strategy: stay inside and fight to REWRITE — pushing against agricultural protection in the north, defending policy space for industrialisation, and building their own scale through regional blocs so they negotiate as continents rather than as single small economies; the AfCFTA is exactly that logic. Note also what the system has already conceded: special and differential treatment gives developing members longer timelines and gentler obligations — imperfectly, but the principle is inside the rulebook, and principles inside rulebooks can be enlarged. So the essay verdict: the criticisms are true, and the conclusion does not follow. A flawed rulebook that binds the strong is worth more to the weak than no rulebook at all — reform beats exit, and scale is the reformer's best argument.
