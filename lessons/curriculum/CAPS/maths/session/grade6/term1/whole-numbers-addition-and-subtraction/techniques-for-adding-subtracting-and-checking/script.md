# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers techniques for adding and subtracting whole numbers and for checking the answers. The curriculum asks you to use at least two techniques for every calculation: one to work it out and one to check it. The anchor is a stokvel in Gqeberha whose members saved R34 526 last year and R23 251 this year, a car dealer whose cars cost amounts like R99 999 and R19 998, and number lines with big jumps. The governing idea is that numbers can be broken into parts, rounded to friendly numbers and put back together, and that every answer can be checked with an estimate or with the inverse operation. Breaking down and building up, using the number line, rounding off and compensating, estimating, using inverse operations and using a calculator only to check are examinable.

## Subtopic: Breaking Down and Building Up

Breaking down means splitting each number into its place value parts. The stokvel saved R34 526 and then R23 251. Break both numbers down: 30 000 plus 4 000 plus 500 plus 20 plus 6, and 20 000 plus 3 000 plus 200 plus 50 plus 1. Now add the same places together: 30 000 plus 20 000 is 50 000. 4 000 plus 3 000 is 7 000. 500 plus 200 is 700. 20 plus 50 is 70. 6 plus 1 is 7. Building up the parts gives 57 777 rand.

You can also break down only the second number and add it in jumps. 145 300 plus 28 600: first add 20 000 to get 165 300, then add 8 000 to get 173 300, then add 600 to get 173 900. On a number line this is three jumps from 145 300: a big jump, a middle jump and a small jump.

For subtraction, building up means counting on from the smaller number to the bigger one. A member needs R50 000 and has saved R37 850. How much more is needed? Count on from 37 850: add 150 to reach 38 000, add 2 000 to reach 40 000, then add 10 000 to reach 50 000. The jumps are 150, 2 000 and 10 000, which add up to 12 150. So R12 150 more is needed.

Counting on is often easier than borrowing, especially when the bigger number has lots of zeros. Jump to the next friendly number, then the next, and add the jumps at the end.

The questions for this section are with you now: breaking numbers down, adding in jumps, and counting on to subtract.

## Subtopic: Rounding Off and Compensating

Some numbers are just next to a friendly number. A car costs R99 999, which is R1 less than R100 000. To add R248 635 and R99 999, add R100 000 instead, to get R348 635. But you added R1 too much, so take it away again: R348 634. This is called rounding off and compensating. You round to make the sum easy, then you fix the answer.

It works for subtraction too, but be careful with which way you fix it. 76 412 take away 19 998. Take away 20 000 instead: 76 412 take away 20 000 is 56 412. But you took away 2 too many, so you must give 2 back: 56 414.

Here is a rule to remember. When you add too much, take the extra away. When you subtract too much, add the extra back. Another example: 365 000 take away 49 000. Take away 50 000 to get 315 000. You took away 1 000 too many, so add 1 000 back: 316 000.

Rounding and compensating works best when a number ends in 9s or 8s, or is close to a round number. It is a mental method: with practice you can do these sums in your head, without writing columns.

The questions for this section are with you now: rounding off and compensating in addition and subtraction.

## Subtopic: Checking with Inverse Operations and Estimation

Addition and subtraction are inverse operations, which means one undoes the other. To check that 34 526 plus 23 251 is 57 777, subtract: 57 777 take away 23 251 should give 34 526. It does, so the sum is right. To check a subtraction, add: if 50 000 take away 37 850 is 12 150, then 12 150 plus 37 850 should be 50 000, and it is.

An estimate is a quick check before or after the exact sum. 248 635 plus 99 999 is about 250 000 plus 100 000, which is 350 000. Our answer, 348 634, is close, so it is sensible. If you had written 3 486 340, the estimate would warn you that something is wrong.

A calculator is only used to check an answer, never instead of working it out. You must be able to work it out yourself in a test, and you must know whether the calculator's answer makes sense. A finger can slip on a calculator key, and a quick estimate catches that.

The two-technique habit is simple: work it out one way, check it another way. Columns and inverse, jumps and estimate, compensating and calculator: any two will do.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: compensating the wrong way, so 76 412 take away 19 998 becomes 56 410; you took away 2 too many, so add 2 back to get 56 414. Two: breaking down and adding the parts wrongly, such as writing 30 000 plus 20 000 as 5 000. Three: checking 57 777 take away 23 251 by subtracting again instead of adding. Four: trusting a calculator answer without an estimate.

The questions for this section are with you now: inverse operations, estimating, and using a calculator to check.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the stokvel, the car prices and the number lines — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Split It by Place

Split numbers into parts: ten thousands, thousands, hundreds, tens and units.

Add the same parts together. 34 526 plus 23 251 is 57 777.

To subtract, count on in jumps: from 37 850 to 50 000 is 12 150.

Keep the parts in mind and try the questions for this part: breaking down and building up.

## Subtopic: Use a Friendly Number

99 999 is nearly 100 000. Add 100 000, then take 1 away.

19 998 is nearly 20 000. Take 20 000 away, then give 2 back.

Too much added? Take it off. Too much taken? Put it back.

Hold the friendly number in mind and try this part's questions: rounding off and compensating.

## Subtopic: Undo It to Check

Adding undoes subtracting. Subtracting undoes adding.

Estimate first: about 250 000 plus 100 000 is about 350 000.

Use the calculator only to check.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Break numbers down by place and build them up again. Jump along a number line or count on to subtract. Round to a friendly number and compensate: take back what you added too much, give back what you subtracted too much. Always check with a second technique: an estimate, the inverse operation or a calculator.

Hold the two-technique habit in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: checking answers.
