### subtopic_1 — Changing the Order: The Commutative Property
**Assistant:** How do I remember which property is which?

**Tutor:** Link each name to an everyday word. Commutative sounds like commute, which is travelling to work or school. The numbers travel: they swap places. So 3 456 plus 12 544 becomes 12 544 plus 3 456. Associative sounds like associate, which means to spend time with someone, like friends who form a group. The numbers change which friends they are grouped with, shown by the brackets. 2 375 plus 1 625 is grouped first, or 1 625 plus 4 890 is grouped first. Distributive sounds like distribute, which means to hand out, the way a teacher hands out books to each learner. The multiplier is handed out to every part of the sum: 4 times 1 000, 4 times 200 and 4 times 50. So commute means move, associate means group, distribute means hand out. You will mostly be asked to use the properties, not just name them, so the most important thing is to know what you are allowed to do. If you can explain why 1 998 plus 5 467 plus 2 002 is easier as 1 998 plus 2 002 first, you already understand the commutative and associative properties working together.

### subtopic_2 — Changing the Grouping: The Associative Property
**Assistant:** Why do the swapping rules work for adding but not for subtracting?

**Tutor:** Because adding is putting together, and subtracting is taking away from a starting amount. When you put two piles of sweets together, it does not matter which pile you pour in first: you end up with the same big pile. That is why 3 456 plus 12 544 and 12 544 plus 3 456 give the same total. But subtraction has a starting amount and an amount removed, and they play different roles. 500 take away 200 starts with 500 packets of chips and sells 200, leaving 300. 200 take away 500 starts with only 200 packets and tries to sell 500, which is impossible with whole numbers. The two numbers are not equal partners. Grouping fails for the same reason. In 100 take away 50 take away 20, both 50 and 20 are being taken away from 100, so altogether 70 is taken away, leaving 30. If you put brackets round 50 take away 20, you suddenly make the 20 work against the 50 instead of against the 100, and you get the wrong answer of 70. So with subtraction, keep the order and work from left to right.

### subtopic_3 — The Distributive Property and Zero
**Assistant:** When is the distributive property actually useful?

**Tutor:** Whenever one of the numbers you multiply is awkward but can be split into friendly parts. 6 times 235 looks hard, but 235 is 200 plus 30 plus 5. 6 times 200 is 1 200, 6 times 30 is 180, and 6 times 5 is 30, so the answer is 1 410. That is exactly what you do in your head when you work out 6 times R235 at a shop. It is also useful with numbers just below a round number. 8 times 99 is 8 times 100 take away 8 times 1, which is 800 take away 8, which is 792. You will use the distributive property all through high school too, especially in algebra, where you will multiply brackets by numbers and letters. The property also works backwards to make sums shorter. If you need 7 times 48 plus 7 times 52, you can notice that both parts are multiplied by 7 and write it as 7 times 100, which is 700. Learning to spot this now is like learning a shortcut through your neighbourhood: once you know it, you use it all the time.
