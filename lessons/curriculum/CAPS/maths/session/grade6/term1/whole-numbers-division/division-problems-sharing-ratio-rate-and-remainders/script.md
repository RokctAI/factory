# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers solving problems with division in financial and measurement contexts, including ratio, rate, and grouping and equal sharing with remainders. The anchor is a school in Polokwane taking 1 350 learners on a trip in buses that seat 65, a sweet-sharing at the end of a party, two friends who share a R2 400 prize in the ratio 1 to 3, and a family car that travels 12 km on each litre of petrol. The governing idea is that division answers two kinds of question, how many in each group and how many groups, and that the story decides what to do with any remainder. Equal sharing and grouping, remainders that are left over, rounded up or ignored, unit prices, sharing in a ratio and finding rates are examinable.

## Subtopic: Equal Sharing and Grouping with Remainders

Division solves two kinds of problem. In sharing, you know the number of groups and want to know how many go in each. In grouping, you know the size of each group and want to know how many groups. Both are division.

Grouping: 1 350 learners go on a trip, and each bus seats 65. How many buses? 1 350 divided by 65. 65 times 20 is 1 300, which leaves 50 learners. So the answer is 20 remainder 50. But 50 learners cannot be left behind, so the school needs one more bus: 21 buses. Here the remainder makes you round up.

Sharing: 1 000 sweets are shared equally among 24 learners. 24 times 40 is 960, which leaves 40, and 24 fits into 40 once more, leaving 16. So 1 000 divided by 24 is 41 remainder 16. Each learner gets 41 sweets and 16 are left over. Here the remainder is simply left over.

Sometimes the remainder is ignored. Packets of chips cost R45 and you have R500. 45 times 11 is 495, so you can buy 11 packets and have R5 left. You cannot buy part of a packet, so the answer is 11 packets.

Before you write the final answer, read the question again and ask: does the remainder stay as a leftover, push the answer up, or get ignored?

The questions for this section are with you now: sharing and grouping, and deciding what to do with the remainder.

## Subtopic: Division in Money and Measurement

Money problems often ask for an equal share or a price for one item. Twelve families share the cost of a R4 680 minibus to the Kruger Park. Each family pays R4 680 divided by 12, which is R390. Check: 390 times 12 is 4 680.

A unit price is the price of one item. A school buys 144 pens for R1 008. The price of one pen is R1 008 divided by 144, which is R7. Unit prices help you compare deals. A pack of 6 juices costs R54, which is R9 each. A pack of 4 costs R40, which is R10 each. The pack of 6 is better value.

Measurement problems divide lengths, masses or volumes. A farmer has 2 500 m of rope and cuts it into pieces of 125 m. 2 500 divided by 125 is 20 pieces. If a 1 000 m roll is cut into 3 m pieces, 1 000 divided by 3 is 333 remainder 1. There are 333 pieces and 1 m left over, which is too short to use.

Always check that the units make sense. Rand divided by a number of families gives rand for each family. Metres divided by metres in each piece gives a number of pieces, with no unit.

The questions for this section are with you now: sharing money, unit prices and dividing measurements.

## Subtopic: Ratio and Rate with Division

Sharing in a ratio means sharing unequally, but by a fixed rule. Thabo and Lindiwe win R2 400 and agree to share it in the ratio 1 to 3. That means for every R1 Thabo gets, Lindiwe gets R3. Add the parts of the ratio: 1 plus 3 is 4 parts. Divide the prize into 4 equal parts: R2 400 divided by 4 is R600. Thabo gets 1 part, R600. Lindiwe gets 3 parts, 3 times R600, which is R1 800. Check: R600 plus R1 800 is R2 400.

Rates are found by dividing too. A car travels 450 km in 5 hours. Its speed is 450 divided by 5, which is 90 km per hour. The family car travels 12 km on each litre of petrol. For a trip of 756 km, it needs 756 divided by 12, which is 63 litres.

To find a rate, divide the total by the number of units. To find the number of units, divide the total by the rate. Writing the rate with per helps: 12 km per litre, so kilometres divided by kilometres per litre gives litres.

Rates can be combined with money. If petrol costs R23 per litre, the 63 litres for the trip cost 63 times 23, which is R1 449. So one rate tells you how many litres, and a second rate tells you how many rand. Work one step at a time and write the unit after every answer.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: saying 20 buses are enough for 1 350 learners; 50 learners would be left behind, so 21 buses are needed. Two: sharing R2 400 in the ratio 1 to 3 by dividing by 3; first add the parts to get 4. Three: writing a remainder bigger than the divisor. Four: giving the unit price of 144 pens costing R1 008 as R144; divide the cost by the number of pens to get R7.

The questions for this section are with you now: sharing in a ratio and finding rates.
