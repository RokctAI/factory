### subtopic_1 — Doubling and Halving
**Assistant:** Why does doubling one number and halving the other keep the answer the same?

**Tutor:** Because you are only rearranging the same objects, not adding or removing any. Picture 48 trays with 25 rolls on each. That is a big rectangle of rolls: 48 rows and 25 columns. Now cut the rectangle in half, across the rows, and put the two halves side by side. You now have 24 rows, but each row is twice as long, 50 rolls. The number of rolls has not changed, so 25 times 48 must equal 50 times 24. You can do it again: 12 rows of 100. Now the sum is easy: 1 200. In number language, doubling multiplies by 2 and halving divides by 2, and multiplying by 2 and then dividing by 2 cancel each other out. Doubling and halving only helps when one number is even, so it can be halved neatly, and when the doubled number becomes friendlier. 25 becomes 50 and then 100, which is very friendly. But 37 times 48 becomes 74 times 24, which is no easier. So look at the numbers first and use the trick only when it helps. Fives, twenty-fives and one hundred and twenty-fives are the best candidates.

### subtopic_2 — Properties of Multiplication, Including 1
**Assistant:** Why does multiplying by 1 leave a number the same, but adding 1 changes it?

**Tutor:** Because multiplying and adding are different actions. Adding 1 means putting one more thing onto a pile, so the pile grows by one: 4 875 plus 1 is 4 876. Multiplying by 1 means taking one group of the number. 4 875 times 1 means one lot of 4 875, which is just 4 875. You have not added any new groups, so nothing changes. In the same way, 1 times 4 875 means 4 875 groups of 1, which is still 4 875. This is why 1 is called the identity for multiplication: it keeps the number's identity. Zero is the identity for addition, because adding 0 keeps a number the same. It is easy to mix these up, so here is a short table to remember. Adding 0: no change. Multiplying by 1: no change. Multiplying by 0: the answer is 0. Imagine a box with 4 875 sweets. One box of them is 4 875 sweets. No boxes of them is no sweets at all. The property of 1 is useful later with fractions, where multiplying by a fraction equal to 1, like 2 over 2, changes how a number looks without changing its value.

### subtopic_3 — Estimating and Checking with Division
**Assistant:** How do I check a multiplication if I do not have a calculator?

**Tutor:** Use the inverse, division, or a different method of multiplying. Say you worked out 125 times 48 and got 6 000. You can check by dividing 6 000 by 48. That is a long division, so it might be easier to divide 6 000 by 125: 125 goes into 1 000 eight times, so it goes into 6 000 six times eight, which is 48 times. That matches, so the answer is right. Another way is to use a different multiplication method. If you used the column method, check with doubling and halving: 125 times 48 is 250 times 24, which is 500 times 12, which is 6 000. Both methods agree. A third way is to estimate. 125 is between 100 and 150, so 125 times 48 should be between 4 800 and 7 200. 6 000 is inside that range, so it is sensible. An estimate cannot prove that an answer is exactly right, but it can quickly show when an answer is badly wrong. If you got 60 000 or 600, the estimate would warn you at once.
