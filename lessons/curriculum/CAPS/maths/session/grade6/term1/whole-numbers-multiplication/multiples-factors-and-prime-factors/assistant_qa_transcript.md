### subtopic_1 — Multiples of Two-Digit and Three-Digit Numbers
**Assistant:** How do I know if a problem needs a common multiple or a common factor?

**Tutor:** Ask whether the answer should be bigger or smaller than the numbers in the problem. Common multiple problems are about things that repeat or come in packs, and you want to know when they match up. The answer is usually bigger than the numbers you start with. Taxis every 15 minutes and buses every 20 minutes meet again after 60 minutes, and 60 is bigger than both 15 and 20. Hot dog rolls in packs of 6 and sausages in packs of 8 match up at 24, which is bigger than both. Common factor problems are about splitting things into equal groups with nothing left over. The answer is usually smaller than the numbers you start with, because it has to fit into them. 120 sopranos and 84 altos split into 12 groups, and 12 is smaller than both. Words like every, again and together often point to a common multiple. Words like share, split, equal groups and as many as possible often point to a common factor. If you are still unsure, try both and see which answer makes sense in the story.

### subtopic_2 — Factors of Two-Digit and Three-Digit Numbers
**Assistant:** Does it matter which pair I use to start a factor tree?

**Tutor:** No. You will always end with the same prime factors, just drawn in a different shape. For 60 you can start with 6 and 10, 4 and 15, 2 and 30, or 3 and 20. Try 2 and 30: 2 is prime, so circle it. 30 splits into 5 and 6, and 6 splits into 2 and 3. The circled primes are 2, 5, 2 and 3, which is the same as 2, 2, 3 and 5. Try 3 and 20: 3 is prime, and 20 splits into 4 and 5, and 4 splits into 2 and 2. Again 2, 2, 3 and 5. This is a very important fact in mathematics: every whole number bigger than 1 has exactly one set of prime factors. That is why primes are called the building blocks of numbers, like the bricks that make up a house. A good habit is to write the prime factors in order from smallest to largest at the end, so that it is easy to compare answers: 60 equals 2 times 2 times 3 times 5. To check, multiply them back together: 2 times 2 is 4, times 3 is 12, times 5 is 60.

### subtopic_3 — Prime Factors
**Assistant:** What is the difference between factors and prime factors?

**Tutor:** Factors are all the numbers that divide exactly into a number. Prime factors are only the factors that are prime numbers. Take 60. Its factors are 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 10, 12, 15, 20, 30 and 60. That is twelve factors. But most of them are not prime: 4 is 2 times 2, 6 is 2 times 3, and so on. Only 2, 3 and 5 are prime, so the prime factors of 60 are 2, 3 and 5. When we write 60 as a product of prime factors, we show how many times each prime is used: 60 is 2 times 2 times 3 times 5, because 2 is used twice. All the other factors of 60 can be made by multiplying some of these primes together. For example, 12 is 2 times 2 times 3, and 30 is 2 times 3 times 5. So the prime factors are like a recipe for the number, and the ordinary factors are all the different dishes you can make from parts of that recipe. Remember that 1 is a factor of every number, but it is never a prime factor, because 1 is not prime.
