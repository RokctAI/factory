# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers techniques for multiplying whole numbers and for checking the answers, and the properties of multiplication, including 1 in terms of its multiplicative property. The anchor is a bakery in Mbombela that bakes 48 trays of 25 rolls, packs 125 muffins in each crate, and sells loaves at R19,99 each, and a teacher who orders 236 exercise books for each class. The governing idea is that a multiplication can be made easier by doubling one number and halving the other, by regrouping, or by breaking a number up, and that every answer can be checked by estimating or by dividing. Doubling and halving, the commutative, associative and distributive properties, multiplying by 1 and by 0, estimating, using division as the inverse, and using a calculator only to check are examinable.

## Subtopic: Doubling and Halving

The bakery bakes 48 trays with 25 rolls on each tray. 25 times 48 looks awkward, but there is a trick. If you double one number and halve the other, the answer stays the same. Double 25 to get 50, and halve 48 to get 24: 50 times 24. Do it again: 100 times 12. That is 1 200 rolls.

Why does it work? Imagine the rolls laid out in 48 rows of 25. If you cut the rows in half and put the halves side by side, you get 24 rows of 50. You have not added or removed any rolls, so the total is still the same.

Doubling and halving works best when one number is even and the other becomes a friendly number when doubled, like 5, 25, 50 or 125. 16 times 35 becomes 8 times 70, which is 560. 45 times 14 becomes 90 times 7, which is 630. 125 times 64 becomes 250 times 32, then 500 times 16, then 1 000 times 8, which is 8 000.

Doubling on its own is useful too. To multiply by 4, double twice. To multiply by 8, double three times. The teacher orders 236 books for each of 4 classes: double 236 to get 472, and double again to get 944 books. For 8 classes, double once more: 1 888 books.

The questions for this section are with you now: doubling and halving, and multiplying by 4 and 8 by doubling.

## Subtopic: Properties of Multiplication, Including 1

Multiplication has some properties that make calculations easier. The commutative property: you can swap the order. 6 times 250 is the same as 250 times 6, which is 1 500. The associative property: you can change the grouping. 4 times 37 times 25 is easier as 4 times 25 first, which is 100, and then 100 times 37, which is 3 700.

The distributive property: you can break one number into parts, multiply each part and add. 7 times 1 999 is 7 times 2 000 take away 7 times 1. That is 14 000 take away 7, which is 13 993. This is the same as rounding and compensating, but for multiplication.

The number 1 has a special property. Any number multiplied by 1 stays exactly the same: 4 875 times 1 is 4 875. This is the multiplicative property of 1. It is like a mirror: what goes in comes out unchanged. Dividing by 1 also leaves a number unchanged.

The number 0 is special in a different way. Any number multiplied by 0 is 0. If the bakery bakes 0 trays, it has 0 rolls, no matter how many rolls fit on a tray. Be careful not to mix the two up: adding 0 leaves a number the same, but multiplying by 0 makes it 0. It is multiplying by 1 that leaves a number the same.

The questions for this section are with you now: the commutative, associative and distributive properties, and multiplying by 1 and by 0.

## Subtopic: Estimating and Checking with Division

Always check a multiplication with a second technique. The first check is an estimate. A loaf costs R19,99, which is about R20, so 236 loaves cost about 236 times 20. Double 236 to get 472, then multiply by 10: about R4 720.

For bigger numbers, round both numbers. 236 books for each of 48 classes is about 200 times 50, which is 10 000 books. The exact answer, 11 328, is a little more, because we rounded 236 down by quite a lot. An estimate is not meant to be exact. It is meant to tell you the size of the answer, so that you know the answer should have five digits and start with a 1.

The second check is the inverse operation. Multiplication and division undo each other. The bakery packs 125 muffins in each of 48 crates, which is 6 000 muffins. To check, divide: 6 000 divided by 48 should give 125, and 6 000 divided by 125 should give 48. If you work out 2 352 divided by 8 and get 294, check by multiplying: 294 times 8 is 2 352.

A calculator is only used to check. Work the answer out first, then use a calculator to confirm it. If the calculator shows something very different from your estimate, a key may have been pressed wrongly, so check again.

Using two techniques means using two different ways. If you used doubling and halving to get 25 times 48 equals 1 200, you could check with the column method or with breaking down: 25 times 40 is 1 000 and 25 times 8 is 200, which makes 1 200.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: doubling both numbers, so 25 times 48 becomes 50 times 96; double one and halve the other. Two: saying 4 875 times 1 is 4 876; multiplying by 1 leaves the number the same. Three: saying 4 875 times 0 is 4 875; multiplying by 0 gives 0. Four: compensating the wrong way, so 7 times 1 999 becomes 14 007; you multiplied 7 times 1 too many, so take 7 away.

The questions for this section are with you now: estimating, checking with division, and using a calculator only to check.

## Subtopic: Rules That Help

Swap the order. Change the grouping. Break a number into parts.

4 times 25 is 100, so 4 times 37 times 25 is 3 700.

Times 1: no change. Times 0: always 0.

Hold the rules in mind and try this part's questions: properties.

## Subtopic: Undo It with Division

Estimate first: 236 times about 20 is about 4 720.

Check with division: 6 000 divided by 48 is 125.

Calculator only to check.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Double one number and halve the other to make friendly numbers. Swap, regroup and break up numbers to make multiplying easier. Multiplying by 1 changes nothing; multiplying by 0 gives 0. Estimate first, then check with division or a second method.
