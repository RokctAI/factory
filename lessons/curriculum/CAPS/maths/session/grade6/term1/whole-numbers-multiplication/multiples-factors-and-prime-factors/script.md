# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers multiples and factors of two-digit and three-digit numbers, and prime factors of numbers to at least 100. It builds on multiples and factors in Grade 5 and on prime numbers earlier this term. The anchor is a choir festival in Pietermaritzburg with 120 sopranos and 84 altos who must be split into equal groups, a taxi rank where taxis leave every 15 minutes and buses every 20 minutes, and factor trees drawn on the board. The governing idea is that a multiple is in the times table of a number, a factor divides into a number exactly, and every whole number bigger than 1 can be broken down into prime factors. Listing multiples, finding common multiples, listing factors in pairs, finding common factors and the highest common factor, and writing a number as a product of prime factors are examinable.

## Subtopic: Multiples of Two-Digit and Three-Digit Numbers

A multiple of a number is the number times any whole number. You find multiples by skip-counting. The multiples of 125 are 125, 250, 375, 500, 625, 750, 875, 1 000 and so on. The multiples of 15 are 15, 30, 45, 60, 75 and so on. Multiples go on for ever.

To test whether a number is a multiple, divide and see if there is a remainder. Is 875 a multiple of 125? 125 times 7 is 875, so yes. Is 900 a multiple of 125? 125 times 7 is 875 and 125 times 8 is 1 000, so 900 is between them and is not a multiple.

Common multiples are numbers that are multiples of two numbers at the same time. At the taxi rank, a taxi leaves every 15 minutes and a bus every 20 minutes. Both leave together at 6:00. When do they next leave together? List the multiples. 15, 30, 45, 60 and 20, 40, 60. The first number in both lists is 60. They next leave together 60 minutes later, at 7:00. The smallest common multiple is called the lowest common multiple.

Another example: the multiples of 6 are 6, 12, 18, 24 and the multiples of 8 are 8, 16, 24. The lowest common multiple of 6 and 8 is 24. Notice that it is not 6 times 8, which is 48. 48 is a common multiple, but it is not the lowest.

The questions for this section are with you now: listing multiples, testing for multiples, and common multiples.

## Subtopic: Factors of Two-Digit and Three-Digit Numbers

A factor divides into a number exactly, with nothing left over. Factors come in pairs that multiply to make the number. The festival organisers want to split 120 sopranos into equal groups. Search for factor pairs, starting at 1: 1 and 120, 2 and 60, 3 and 40, 4 and 30, 5 and 24, 6 and 20, 8 and 15, 10 and 12. 7 and 9 do not divide 120 exactly. After 10 and 12, the next pair would start with 12, which you already have, so stop. 120 has 16 factors: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 10, 12, 15, 20, 24, 30, 40, 60 and 120.

The factors of 84 are found the same way: 1 and 84, 2 and 42, 3 and 28, 4 and 21, 6 and 14, 7 and 12. So the factors of 84 are 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 7, 12, 14, 21, 28, 42 and 84.

Now the organisers want the sopranos and the altos in the same number of groups, with each group the same. The number of groups must be a factor of 120 and a factor of 84. The common factors are 1, 2, 3, 4, 6 and 12. The biggest is 12, which is called the highest common factor. With 12 groups, each group has 120 divided by 12, which is 10 sopranos, and 84 divided by 12, which is 7 altos.

Quick divisibility tests help when you search for factors. A number is divisible by 2 if it is even, by 5 if it ends in 0 or 5, by 10 if it ends in 0, and by 3 if its digits add up to a multiple of 3. For 150, the digits add to 6, so 3 is a factor: 3 times 50 is 150.

The questions for this section are with you now: factor pairs, common factors and the highest common factor.

## Subtopic: Prime Factors

A prime factor is a factor that is also a prime number. The factors of 60 include 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 10, 12, 15, 20, 30 and 60, but only 2, 3 and 5 are prime. So the prime factors of 60 are 2, 3 and 5.

A factor tree breaks a number down into primes. Start with 60 at the top. Split it into any factor pair, such as 6 and 10. Neither is prime, so split again: 6 is 2 times 3, and 10 is 2 times 5. Now every branch ends in a prime. So 60 is 2 times 2 times 3 times 5. Circle the primes at the ends of the branches.

It does not matter which pair you start with. If you start 60 as 4 and 15, then 4 is 2 times 2 and 15 is 3 times 5. You still end with 2, 2, 3 and 5. Every number has only one set of prime factors. More examples: 84 is 2 times 2 times 3 times 7. 100 is 2 times 2 times 5 times 5. 72 is 2 times 2 times 2 times 3 times 3.

Another way is repeated division by primes. Divide 90 by the smallest prime that works: 90 divided by 2 is 45. 45 divided by 3 is 15. 15 divided by 3 is 5. 5 is prime, so stop. 90 is 2 times 3 times 3 times 5.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: calling 4 a multiple of 120; 4 is a factor of 120, and 120 is a multiple of 4. Two: giving 6 times 8, which is 48, as the lowest common multiple of 6 and 8; the lowest is 24. Three: including 1 as a prime factor; 1 is not prime. Four: stopping a factor tree at a branch like 6 or 15 that is not prime; keep splitting until every branch is prime.

The questions for this section are with you now: prime factors, factor trees and repeated division.

## Subtopic: Factor Pairs

Factors divide in exactly. Find them in pairs, starting at 1.

120: 1 and 120, 2 and 60, 3 and 40, 4 and 30, 5 and 24, 6 and 20, 8 and 15, 10 and 12.

The highest common factor of 120 and 84 is 12: 12 groups of 10 sopranos and 7 altos.

Hold the pairs in mind and try this part's questions: factors.

## Subtopic: Break It into Primes

Split the number into a factor pair. Keep splitting until every branch is prime.

60 is 2 times 2 times 3 times 5.

1 is not a prime factor.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Multiples come from skip-counting; the first match of two lists is the lowest common multiple. Factors come in pairs; the biggest one shared by two numbers is the highest common factor. Prime factors are factors that are prime. A factor tree breaks a number down until every branch is prime.

Hold the factor tree in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: prime factors.
