### subtopic_1 — Breaking Up the Three-Digit Number
**Assistant:** Why do I need to write a zero at the start of the tens row?

**Tutor:** Because in that row you are multiplying by tens, not by units. In 3 408 times 125, the 2 in 125 is really 20. When you multiply 3 408 by 2 you get 6 816, but you need 3 408 times 20, which is ten times bigger: 68 160. Writing a 0 in the units column first moves every digit one place to the left, which multiplies by 10. So the placeholder zero turns 3 408 times 2 into 3 408 times 20. The hundreds row works the same way. The 1 in 125 is really 100, so you write two zeros first and then 3 408, giving 340 800, which is 3 408 times 100. If you forget the zeros, you add 17 040, 6 816 and 3 408 and get 27 264, which is far too small. An estimate would warn you: 3 408 is about 3 400 and 125 is more than 100, so the answer must be more than 340 000. Some learners like to write the zeros in a different colour, or to say aloud tens row, one zero; hundreds row, two zeros, before they start each row.

### subtopic_2 — Long Multiplication in Columns
**Assistant:** Do I have to use long multiplication, or can I use breaking down?

**Tutor:** You may use any method that works, and the curriculum asks you to use at least two techniques: one to calculate and one to check. Breaking down the three-digit number into hundreds, tens and units and then multiplying each part is a perfectly good method, and it shows clearly why the answer is right. Long multiplication in columns does exactly the same steps, but in a shorter layout, so it is usually faster once you know it well. Some learners break down both numbers. For 1 245 times 236, you could multiply 1 000, 200, 40 and 5 each by 200, 30 and 6, giving twelve small products, and add them all. That is correct but slow, and with so many numbers to add it is easy to lose one. A good plan is to calculate with the column method and then check with an estimate, or to calculate by breaking down the multiplier and check with the column method. If the two methods agree, you can be confident. If they disagree, go through both slowly to find where the slip happened.

### subtopic_3 — Zeros in the Numbers
**Assistant:** How do I estimate a big multiplication quickly?

**Tutor:** Round each number to one or two non-zero digits, multiply those, and then count the zeros. For 1 245 times 236, round 1 245 to 1 200 and 236 to 240. Now 12 times 24 is 288. You removed two zeros from 1 200 and one zero from 240, so put three zeros back: 288 000. The exact answer, 293 820, is close. For a very quick estimate you can round more: 1 000 times 200 is 200 000. That is rougher, but it still tells you the answer has six digits, so an answer of 29 382 or 2 938 200 would be obviously wrong. Rounding one number up and the other down tends to keep the estimate closer, because the errors partly cancel. For 2 006 times 304, both numbers are very close to 2 000 and 300, so 600 000 is a very good estimate. Remember that an estimate is not the answer. It is a target to aim near. Write it down before you start, then compare your final answer with it.
