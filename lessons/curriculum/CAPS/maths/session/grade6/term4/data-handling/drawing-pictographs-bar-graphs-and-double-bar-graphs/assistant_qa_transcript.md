### subtopic_1 — Pictographs with a Key
**Assistant:** How do I choose a good key for a pictograph?

**Tutor:** Look at the numbers you need to show and pick a key that makes them easy to draw. Grade 6A's numbers are 40, 60, 35 and 70. If one picture stood for 1 can, you would need 70 pictures in one row, which would take ages and be hard to count. If one picture stood for 100 cans, every row would be less than one picture, which is hard to draw and to read. A key of 10 cans per picture works well: 40 is 4 pictures, 60 is 6 pictures and 70 is 7 pictures. The numbers that are not multiples of 10, like 35, are multiples of 5, so you can show them with half a picture: 3 whole pictures and a half. That is why the key should usually divide nicely into most of your numbers. If the data were numbers like 12, 18 and 24, a key of 2 or 6 might be better. Whatever you choose, write the key clearly next to the graph, for example: one can picture stands for 10 cans. Then anyone reading the graph can multiply the number of pictures by 10 to get the real number.

### subtopic_2 — Drawing Bar Graphs
**Assistant:** Why must the scale on a bar graph start at 0 and go up in equal steps?

**Tutor:** Because the height of each bar must be fair, so that a bar twice as tall really shows twice as much. If the scale goes 0, 10, 20, 30, 40, 50, 60, 70, every step is the same, and 70 cans really does look twice as tall as 35 cans. Now imagine someone uses uneven steps, like 0, 10, 20, 50, 60, 70. The gap from 20 to 50 is drawn the same size as the gap from 10 to 20, even though it is three times bigger. Bars above 20 would look much shorter than they should, and the graph would give a false picture. Starting at 0 matters too. If the scale started at 30, April's 35 cans would be a tiny stub and May's 70 cans would be a tall tower, as if 6A collected eight times as much in May, when really it was twice as much. Some adverts use tricks like this to make small differences look huge. A scale starting at 0 with equal steps keeps your graph honest, so readers can trust what they see.

### subtopic_3 — Double Bar Graphs
**Assistant:** Why is a double bar graph useful?

**Tutor:** It lets you compare two sets of data at a glance, category by category. If you drew two separate bar graphs, one for Grade 6A and one for Grade 6B, you would have to look back and forth between them and read every bar carefully to see who collected more each month. In a double bar graph, the two bars for each month stand side by side on the same scale. You can see straight away that 6A's bar is taller in February, March and May, while 6B's bar is taller in April. You can also see how much taller: in April, 6B's bar reaches 50 and 6A's reaches 35, a difference of 15 cans. To make it work, the two classes must have different colours or patterns, and the graph needs a legend showing which is which. The bars in each pair touch, and there is a gap between one month's pair and the next, so the months stay clear. Double bar graphs are used in newspapers to compare things like rainfall in two cities or sales in two years.
