# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers collecting data by asking questions, including simple yes or no questionnaires, and by observing; recording data with tally marks and frequency tables; grouping data into intervals; and ordering data from the smallest to the largest group. It builds on data handling in earlier grades and on ordering whole numbers. The anchor is a Grade 6 class of 36 learners at a school in Soweto. They survey their own class: how each learner travels to school, which sport each likes best, whether each eats breakfast every day, and how many minutes each takes to get to school. The governing idea is that good data starts with a clear question, is recorded carefully with tallies that are easy to count, is checked by making sure the total matches the number of people asked, and is then organised so that patterns are easy to see. Writing good survey questions, using tallies and frequency tables, grouping data into intervals, and ordering groups are examinable.

## Subtopic: Asking Questions and Collecting Data

Data is information collected to answer a question. The class wants to know how learners travel to school, so it collects data by asking each learner. Collecting data by asking people is called a survey, and a list of written questions is a questionnaire.

A good survey question is clear and fair, and gives choices when that helps. How do you usually travel to school: walk, taxi, bus, car or bicycle? Every learner can answer that question with exactly one choice. A poor question pushes people towards an answer, like: soccer is the best sport, isn't it? People feel they must agree, so the data is not honest.

The simplest questions have only two answers, yes or no. Do you eat breakfast every day? These are quick to ask and easy to count.

We can also collect data by observing. Learners stand at the school gate and count how many cars, taxis and buses pass in 10 minutes. Data can also come from records, like the school's attendance register.

Before starting, decide who to ask and make sure you ask each person only once.

A class list helps. The class secretary reads out each name, the learner answers, and she ticks the name. When every name is ticked, the survey is complete. Data about people should be treated with respect: the class records the answers, not who gave each answer, so nobody feels embarrassed about not eating breakfast.

## Subtopic: Tally Marks and Frequency Tables

As each learner answers, make a tally mark next to their choice. Tally marks are grouped in fives: draw four upright lines, and then draw the fifth line across them. Groups of five are quick to count: two groups and three more lines is 13.

When the tallying is finished, write a frequency table. The frequency is how many times each answer appears. For transport, the class finds: walk 15, taxi 9, bus 6, car 4 and bicycle 2.

Always check the total. 15 plus 9 plus 6 plus 4 plus 2 is 36, which matches the 36 learners in the class. If the total were 37, someone would have been counted twice; if it were 35, someone would have been missed.

For favourite sport, the frequencies are: soccer 14, netball 8, rugby 6, cricket 5 and athletics 3. That also adds up to 36.

For breakfast, 27 learners said yes and 9 said no. 27 plus 9 is 36.

A frequency table usually has three columns: the choice, the tally and the frequency. For transport, the first row reads walk, then three bundles of five, then 15. The bottom row shows the total. Keeping the tally column in the table means anyone can check your counting later.

## Subtopic: Organising and Ordering Data

To make data easier to read, order the groups from the smallest to the largest. For transport: bicycle 2, car 4, bus 6, taxi 9, walk 15. Now it is easy to see that the largest group walks to school and the smallest group rides a bicycle.

For sport, from smallest to largest: athletics 3, cricket 5, rugby 6, netball 8, soccer 14. Soccer has 6 more learners than netball.

You can also order data from largest to smallest, which puts the most popular choice at the top of a list. Either way, ordering helps you compare the groups quickly and spot the biggest and smallest.

Some data has too many different values to list one by one, like the number of minutes each learner takes to get to school. Then we group the values into intervals of equal size: 0 to 9 minutes, 10 to 19 minutes, 20 to 29 minutes, and 30 to 39 minutes. The class finds 7, 13, 10 and 6 learners in these intervals, a total of 36. The intervals must not overlap, so that each value fits in exactly one interval. A learner who takes 20 minutes goes in the 20 to 29 interval.

Organised data starts to answer the class's questions. More than a third of the class walks to school, so the school decides to paint a new pedestrian crossing. Three quarters of the class eats breakfast every day, but 9 learners do not, so the school starts a breakfast club.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: drawing five upright lines instead of four lines with a fifth across; the group is hard to count. Two: not checking the total, so a learner counted twice goes unnoticed. Three: asking a question that pushes people to an answer, like soccer is the best sport, isn't it. Four: using overlapping intervals like 0 to 10 and 10 to 20; a value of 10 would fit in both.

Smallest to largest: bicycle, car, bus, taxi, walk.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Collect data with a clear, fair question, by survey, observation or records. Record answers with tally marks in groups of five. Write the frequencies in a table and check that the total matches the number of people asked. Order the groups from smallest to largest to see the pattern, and use equal intervals that do not overlap when there are many different values.
