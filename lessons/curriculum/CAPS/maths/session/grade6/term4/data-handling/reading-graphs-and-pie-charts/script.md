# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers reading and interpreting data shown in words, pictographs, bar graphs, double bar graphs and pie charts, answering questions about categories, totals and differences, and reading graphs critically by thinking about the source, the context and the scale. It builds on drawing graphs in the previous lesson and on fractions of whole numbers from Term 2. The anchor is the newsletter of a primary school in Makhanda. It shows a bar graph of the town's rainfall from January to April, 60 mm, 70 mm, 80 mm and 50 mm, and a pie chart of the favourite fruit of 40 Grade 6 learners: half chose oranges, a quarter chose apples, an eighth chose bananas and an eighth chose mangoes. A local advert shows a graph with a scale that starts at 50. The governing idea is that a graph is a summary of data, so we read it carefully, we work out real numbers from it, and we ask who collected the data, how, and whether the graph is drawn fairly. Reading values, comparing and totalling, interpreting pie charts as fractions of a whole, and spotting misleading graphs are examinable.

## Subtopic: Reading Bar Graphs and Pictographs

To read a bar graph, find the bar, follow its top across to the scale, and read the number. In the rainfall graph, January's bar reaches 60 mm, February's 70 mm, March's 80 mm and April's 50 mm.

Now answer questions. The wettest month is March, with the tallest bar. The driest is April. March had 80 minus 50, which is 30 mm more rain than April. February had 10 mm more than January. Altogether, the four months had 60 plus 70 plus 80 plus 50, which is 260 mm of rain.

If the same data were shown in a pictograph with one umbrella for 20 mm, January would have 3 umbrellas and February 3 and a half. To read a pictograph, count the pictures and multiply by the key.

A double bar graph lets you compare two sets of data, such as this year's rainfall and last year's, month by month. Read each bar separately, then compare the pair.

Data can also be described in words. A sentence such as March was the wettest month, with 80 mm of rain, is a summary of what the graph shows.

Some questions need more than one step. How much rain fell in the first two months together? 60 plus 70 is 130 mm. Was that more or less than in the last two months? 80 plus 50 is also 130 mm, so it was exactly the same.

The questions for this section are with you now: reading bar graphs and pictographs.

## Subtopic: Reading Pie Charts

A pie chart is a circle divided into slices. The whole circle stands for the whole group, and each slice shows what fraction of the group is in each category. Pie charts are good for showing parts of a whole.

In the newsletter's pie chart, the whole circle stands for all 40 learners. Half the circle is oranges, so half of 40, which is 20 learners, chose oranges. A quarter of the circle is apples: a quarter of 40 is 10 learners. An eighth is bananas: an eighth of 40 is 5 learners. The last eighth is mangoes, another 5 learners. Check: 20 plus 10 plus 5 plus 5 is 40.

To find the number in a slice, find that fraction of the total. Divide the total by the denominator and multiply by the numerator.

Pie charts are often labelled with percentages. Half is 50%, a quarter is 25%, and the whole circle is 100%. The slices always add up to the whole: one half plus one quarter plus one eighth plus one eighth is one whole.

The size of a slice tells you the fraction, not the number of people. A half slice in a pie chart of 40 learners is 20 learners, but a half slice in a pie chart of 400 learners is 200 learners.

You can also work backwards. If the oranges slice is half of the pie and stands for 20 learners, the whole pie must stand for 2 times 20, which is 40 learners.

The questions for this section are with you now: reading pie charts.

## Subtopic: Reading Graphs Critically

Reading critically means asking questions about a graph before you believe it.

Ask about the source: who collected the data, and how? The rainfall data comes from the town's weather station, which measures rain every day, so it is reliable.

Ask about the context: how many were asked, and who? The fruit survey asked 40 learners in Grade 6 at one school. It tells us about those learners. It does not tell us what every child in South Africa likes best, because they were not asked.

Ask about the scale. A shop advert shows bars for sales of 60 and 80 on a scale that starts at 50, so the 80 bar looks three times as tall as the 60 bar, even though 80 is only a third more than 60. A scale that does not start at 0 can make small differences look huge.

Ask about the date too. Rainfall from one year may be very different from the next, so a graph from a drought year should not be used to plan for every year.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: reading a pie slice as a number of people, not a fraction of the total; an eighth of 40 learners is 5 learners. Two: comparing slices from two pie charts with different totals; half of 400 is more than half of 40. Three: trusting a bar graph whose scale starts at 50; read the numbers, not just the heights. Four: saying all children like oranges best because 20 learners in one class did; a small survey speaks only for the people asked.

The questions for this section are with you now: reading graphs critically.
