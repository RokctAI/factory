# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers drawing pictographs with many-to-one representations, drawing bar graphs with a title, labelled axes and an equal scale, and drawing double bar graphs that compare two sets of data. It builds on collecting and organising data in the previous lesson. The anchor is a recycling competition at a school in Durban. Grade 6A and Grade 6B collect cans for four months. Grade 6A collects 40 cans in February, 60 in March, 35 in April and 70 in May. Grade 6B collects 30, 45, 50 and 65. The governing idea is that a graph is a picture of data, so it must be honest and easy to read: a pictograph needs a key saying how many each picture stands for, a bar graph needs a scale that starts at 0 and goes up in equal steps, and a double bar graph needs a key to show which bar is which. Drawing and labelling pictographs, bar graphs and double bar graphs are examinable.

## Subtopic: Pictographs with a Key

A pictograph shows data with small pictures. If one picture stood for one can, Grade 6A would need 70 pictures for May, which is far too many. So we use a key in which one picture stands for many. Here, one can picture stands for 10 cans.

With that key, Grade 6A's 40 cans in February is 4 pictures, and 60 cans in March is 6 pictures. For April's 35 cans, draw 3 whole pictures for 30 cans and half a picture for the other 5. May's 70 cans is 7 pictures.

Grade 6B's row for February has 3 pictures for 30 cans, and its row for March has 4 and a half pictures for 45 cans. Putting both classes in one pictograph lets you compare them row by row.

Choose a key that suits the numbers. All of Grade 6A's numbers are multiples of 5, so a key of 10 works, using half pictures for 5. A key of 1 would need too many pictures, and a key of 100 would make every row a tiny part of a picture.

To read a pictograph, count the pictures and multiply by the key. A row with 7 pictures shows 7 times 10, which is 70 cans.

Every pictograph needs a title, a label for each row, and the key. Without the key, nobody knows what each picture is worth.

Draw the pictures the same size and line them up neatly in rows, so that a longer row really means more cans. If some pictures were big and some small, the rows could not be compared fairly.

The questions for this section are with you now: drawing and reading pictographs.

## Subtopic: Drawing Bar Graphs

A bar graph uses bars to show data. Draw two axes: a horizontal one along the bottom and a vertical one up the side. Label the horizontal axis with the categories, here the months February, March, April and May. Label the vertical axis with what is being counted, here the number of cans.

Choose a scale for the vertical axis. It must start at 0 and go up in equal steps. The biggest number for Grade 6A is 70, so a scale of 0, 10, 20 and so on up to 80 works well.

Draw one bar for each month. All bars must be the same width, with equal gaps between them. The top of each bar lines up with its number on the scale. February's bar reaches 40. March's reaches 60. April's reaches 35, which is halfway between 30 and 40. May's reaches 70.

Give the graph a title that says what it shows: Cans collected by Grade 6A.

To read a bar graph, follow the top of a bar across to the scale. A bar whose top is halfway between 60 and 70 shows 65.

Bars can also be drawn across the page instead of up it. Then the categories go down the side and the scale goes along the bottom. Both kinds of bar graph follow the same rules.

Graph paper or a ruler makes the bars neat. If each small square on the vertical axis stands for 5 cans, a bar for 35 cans is 7 squares tall.

The questions for this section are with you now: drawing bar graphs.

## Subtopic: Double Bar Graphs

A double bar graph shows two sets of data on the same axes, so they can be compared. For each month, draw two bars side by side, touching each other, one for Grade 6A and one for Grade 6B. Use a different colour or pattern for each class, and add a key, also called a legend, that shows which colour stands for which class. Leave a gap between one month's pair of bars and the next month's pair.

Now the comparison is easy to see. In February, 6A's bar is taller: 40 against 30. In March, 6A again: 60 against 45. In April, 6B's bar is taller: 50 against 35, so 6B collected 15 more cans. In May, 6A wins: 70 against 65. Over the four months, 6A collected 40 plus 60 plus 35 plus 70, which is 205 cans, and 6B collected 30 plus 45 plus 50 plus 65, which is 190 cans.

The double bar graph also shows patterns over time. Both classes collected the most cans in May, perhaps because the competition was ending and everyone tried harder.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: drawing a pictograph without a key; nobody can tell what each picture is worth. Two: using a scale with uneven steps like 0, 10, 20, 50; the bars become misleading. Three: starting the scale at 30 instead of 0; small differences look huge. Four: drawing a double bar graph with no legend; you cannot tell which bar belongs to which class.

The questions for this section are with you now: drawing and comparing double bar graphs.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the can collection — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: One Picture, Many Cans

Key: one picture is 10 cans.

35 cans: 3 and a half pictures.

Always give the key.

Keep the key in mind and try the questions for this part: pictographs.

## Subtopic: Bars and a Scale

Start the scale at 0. Go up in equal steps.

Same width bars, equal gaps.

Title and labels on both axes.

Hold the scale rules in mind and try this part's questions: bar graphs.

## Subtopic: Two Bars Side by Side

One bar for each class, with a legend.

April: 6B 50, 6A 35. 6B wins by 15.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A pictograph uses pictures with a key that says how many each picture stands for; half a picture shows half the key. A bar graph needs a title, labelled axes, a scale that starts at 0 and goes up in equal steps, and bars of the same width with equal gaps. A double bar graph puts two bars side by side for each category, with a legend, so two sets of data can be compared.

Hold the side-by-side bars in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: double bar graphs.
