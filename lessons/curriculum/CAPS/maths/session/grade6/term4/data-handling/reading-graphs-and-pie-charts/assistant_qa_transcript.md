### subtopic_1 — Reading Bar Graphs and Pictographs
**Assistant:** How do I work out how many people are in a slice of a pie chart?

**Tutor:** Find what fraction of the circle the slice is, then find that fraction of the total number of people. The whole circle always stands for the whole group. In the newsletter's pie chart, the whole circle is 40 learners. The oranges slice is half the circle, so it stands for half of 40, which is 20 learners. The apples slice is a quarter of the circle: 40 divided by 4 is 10 learners. The bananas slice is an eighth: 40 divided by 8 is 5 learners. If a slice were three eighths, you would divide 40 by 8 to get 5, and then multiply by 3 to get 15. If the slices are labelled with percentages, change them to fractions first: 50% is a half and 25% is a quarter. Always check that your numbers add up to the total. Here, 20 plus 10 plus 5 plus 5 is 40, so every learner is accounted for. Remember that you cannot read the number of people from the size of a slice alone. You must know the total, because the same half slice means 20 people in a group of 40 and 200 people in a group of 400.

### subtopic_2 — Reading Pie Charts
**Assistant:** Why should I check where the data in a graph came from?

**Tutor:** Because a graph is only as good as the data behind it. If the data was collected carelessly, or from the wrong people, the graph can look neat and official but still be wrong or misleading. Ask three questions. Who collected the data? The Makhanda rainfall comes from the weather station, which measures rain carefully every day, so we can trust it. How was it collected? A survey that asks clear, fair questions is better than one that pushes people to an answer. Who was asked, and how many? The fruit survey asked 40 Grade 6 learners at one school. It is useful for planning that school's tuck shop, but it cannot tell us what all South African children like, because most children were never asked. If a toothpaste advert says that 9 out of 10 dentists recommend it, you might ask how many dentists were asked and who paid for the survey. Being a critical reader does not mean refusing to believe any graph. It means checking first, so that you believe the good ones and are careful with the weak ones.

### subtopic_3 — Reading Graphs Critically
**Assistant:** How can a graph be misleading if all its numbers are correct?

**Tutor:** The numbers can be correct while the picture gives the wrong impression. The most common trick is the scale. Suppose a shop sold 60 items one month and 80 the next. On a fair bar graph, with a scale starting at 0, the 80 bar is a third taller than the 60 bar, which is honest. Now suppose the scale starts at 50. The 60 bar is only 10 units tall, and the 80 bar is 30 units tall. The second bar now looks three times as tall, as if sales tripled, when really they went up by only a third. The numbers on the scale are correct, but the picture misleads. Other tricks include uneven steps on the scale, pictures of different sizes in a pictograph, and pie charts with slices that do not add up to a whole. To protect yourself, always read the actual numbers on the scale before you judge by the heights, check that the scale starts at 0 and goes up in equal steps, and check that pie slices add up to 100%. Then you will see what the data really says.
