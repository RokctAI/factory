### subtopic_1 — Units, Benchmarks and Instruments
**Assistant:** How do I work out what each small mark on a dial is worth?

**Tutor:** Look at two numbers next to each other on the dial and count the spaces between them, not the marks. On Mrs Jacobs's kitchen scale, the numbers go 0, 100, 200, 300 and so on, so each gap between numbers is 100 g. Between 300 and 400 there are 4 small marks. Those 4 marks cut the gap into 5 equal spaces, because there is a space before the first mark, between each pair of marks, and after the last mark. So each space is worth 100 divided by 5, which is 20 g. Now count up from 300: the first mark is 320 g, the second is 340 g, the third is 360 g and the fourth is 380 g. Then comes 400. Many learners count the marks instead of the spaces and divide by 4, getting 25 g, which is wrong. Others treat each mark as 1 g. Every scale is different, so do this check every time you meet a new one. A bathroom scale might have a mark for each kilogram, while another kitchen scale might have a mark for every 10 g. Once you know the value of a mark, reading the scale is just counting on.

### subtopic_2 — Reading Scales Accurately
**Assistant:** Why should I press the zero button before weighing flour in a bowl?

**Tutor:** Because otherwise the scale weighs the bowl as well as the flour. A scale shows the total mass of everything on it. If the bowl is 300 g and you add 500 g of flour, the scale shows 800 g. If Mrs Jacobs's recipe needs 500 g of flour and she watches for 500 g on the scale, she will only add 200 g of flour, and her cake will flop. Pressing zero, which is sometimes called tare, tells the scale to ignore what is already on it. Put the empty bowl on the scale, press zero, and the screen shows 0 g. Now add flour until it shows 500 g, and that is the flour alone. You can then press zero again and add the next ingredient, like sugar, straight into the same bowl. On an analogue scale with no zero button, you can usually turn a small knob to move the needle back to 0. If you cannot zero the scale at all, weigh the bowl first, then weigh the bowl with the flour, and subtract: 800 g minus 300 g is 500 g of flour.

### subtopic_3 — Recording, Comparing and Ordering Mass
**Assistant:** Why is 1,2 kg heavier than 950 g when 950 is a bigger number?

**Tutor:** Because the two numbers are in different units. A kilogram is 1 000 times as heavy as a gram, so a small number of kilograms can be much heavier than a big number of grams. Change them to the same unit before you compare. 1,2 kg is 1 kg and 0,2 of a kilogram. 1 kg is 1 000 g, and 0,2 kg is two tenths of 1 000 g, which is 200 g. So 1,2 kg is 1 200 g. Now compare 1 200 g with 950 g: 1 200 is more, so the flour is heavier, by 250 g. Think about it with real objects. 950 g is a bit less than a bag of sugar. 1,2 kg is a bag of sugar plus a bit more. You would feel the difference if you held one in each hand. The same idea works when you order a list. Change everything into grams, because then there are no decimals, put the numbers in order, and then write each mass in its original form. This is the same rule we used for length: same units first, then compare.
