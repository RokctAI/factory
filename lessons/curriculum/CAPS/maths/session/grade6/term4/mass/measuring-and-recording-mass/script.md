# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers estimating and measuring mass using analogue and digital kitchen scales, bathroom scales and balances, reading scales accurately, and recording, comparing and ordering masses in grams and kilograms. It builds on measuring length and capacity in earlier grades and on decimals from Term 2. The anchor is Mrs Jacobs, who bakes for a church bazaar in Paarl. She weighs 250 g of butter, a 2,5 kg bag of flour and eggs of about 60 g each on her kitchen scales, compares parcels on an old pan balance, and her grandson Ethan, who is in Grade 6, finds that he has a mass of 38 kg on the bathroom scale. The governing idea is that grams are for light things and kilograms for heavy things, that 1 000 g make 1 kg, that every scale must be read by first working out what each mark is worth, and that masses must be in the same unit before you compare them. Choosing units and instruments, estimating, reading scales, and comparing and ordering masses are examinable.

## Subtopic: Units, Benchmarks and Instruments

Mass tells us how heavy something is. We measure it in grams, written g, and kilograms, written kg. A gram is very light: a paper clip has a mass of about 1 g. A kilogram is 1 000 grams: a bag of sugar or a 1 litre bottle of water has a mass of about 1 kg.

Use grams for light things: a block of butter is 250 g, an egg is about 60 g and a slice of bread is about 30 g. Use kilograms for heavy things: a bag of flour, a pumpkin or a person.

Benchmarks help you estimate. If something feels about as heavy as a bag of sugar, estimate 1 kg. If it is lighter than a block of butter, it is less than 250 g.

Each instrument suits a different job. A kitchen scale measures food in grams, and some go up to a few kilograms. It can be analogue, with a needle moving around a dial, or digital, with numbers on a screen. A bathroom scale measures people in kilograms. A pan balance has two pans; you put the object in one pan and mass pieces in the other until the arm is level. Then the object's mass equals the total of the mass pieces.

Bigger does not always mean heavier. A pillow is much bigger than a brick, but the brick is much heavier.

Before weighing, Mrs Jacobs always estimates. She holds the pumpkin in both hands and says it feels like about three bags of sugar, so about 3 kg. The scale shows 3,2 kg, so her estimate was close.

## Subtopic: Reading Scales Accurately

On an analogue scale, first work out what each small mark is worth. Mrs Jacobs's kitchen scale has numbers every 100 g, and there are 4 small marks between each pair of numbers. The 4 marks split the 100 g into 5 equal spaces, so each mark is worth 100 divided by 5, which is 20 g. When the needle points to the third small mark after 300, the reading is 300 plus 60, which is 360 g.

On another scale, the numbers go up in 50 g. A needle exactly halfway between 400 g and 450 g shows 425 g.

On a bathroom scale, the numbers might go up in 10 kg with a mark for every kilogram. Ethan's needle stops at the eighth mark after 30, so his mass is 38 kg.

On a digital scale, read the number on the screen and check the unit: g or kg. Most digital kitchen scales have a zero or tare button. Put the empty bowl on the scale, press zero, and then add the flour. The scale then shows only the mass of the flour.

If you cannot zero the scale, subtract. The bowl alone is 300 g. The bowl with flour is 800 g. So the flour is 800 minus 300, which is 500 g.

On the pan balance, Mrs Jacobs puts a parcel in one pan. It balances a 200 g piece and a 50 g piece, so the parcel has a mass of 250 g.

## Subtopic: Recording, Comparing and Ordering Mass

Always record a mass with its unit. 250 on its own could mean 250 g or 250 kg, and that is a big difference.

To compare masses, first change them into the same unit. Which is heavier, 1,2 kg of flour or 950 g of sugar? 1,2 kg is 1 200 g, which is more than 950 g, so the flour is heavier, even though 950 looks like the bigger number.

Fractions of a kilogram are common: half a kilogram is 500 g, a quarter of a kilogram is 250 g and three quarters is 750 g. Mrs Jacobs's 2,5 kg bag of flour is 2 500 g.

To order masses, change them all to grams. Order 750 g, 1,5 kg and 1 250 g from lightest to heaviest. In grams they are 750 g, 1 500 g and 1 250 g. So the order is 750 g, 1 250 g, 1,5 kg.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: treating each small mark on a dial as 1 g; work out what the marks are worth first, 20 g on Mrs Jacobs's scale. Two: weighing flour in a bowl without zeroing the scale, and recording the bowl's mass as part of the flour. Three: saying 950 g is heavier than 1,2 kg because 950 is bigger than 1,2; change to the same unit first. Four: thinking the bigger object is always heavier; a pillow is bigger than a brick but much lighter.
