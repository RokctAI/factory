### subtopic_1 — Units and Benchmarks
**Assistant:** What is the difference between capacity and volume?

**Tutor:** Capacity is how much a container can hold when it is full. Volume is how much space something actually takes up. Think of the Mokoenas' 1,5 l bottles. Each bottle has a capacity of 1,5 l, because that is the most it can hold. When they fill a bottle to the top, the volume of ginger beer inside is 1,5 l, the same as the capacity. But if a bottle is only half full, the volume of ginger beer inside is 0,75 l, or 750 ml, while the capacity is still 1,5 l. The container has not changed, only how much is in it. The words are closely linked, and in everyday speech people often mix them up. In maths, use capacity when you talk about a container, like a jug, a bottle or a tank, and use volume when you talk about the amount of liquid or the size of an object. Both are measured in millilitres, litres and kilolitres for liquids. For solid objects, volume is often measured in cubic centimetres, and 1 cubic centimetre is the same as 1 ml.

### subtopic_2 — Measuring with Spoons, Cups and Jugs
**Assistant:** Why must I read a measuring jug at eye level?

**Tutor:** Because if your eyes are above or below the surface of the liquid, you see the level against the wrong mark on the scale. The marks are printed on the outside of the jug, and the liquid is inside, a little distance behind them. If you look down from above, the liquid surface lines up with a mark that is lower than the true level. If you look up from below, it lines up with a mark that is higher. Either way, your reading is wrong. When you bend down so that your eyes are exactly level with the surface, the liquid lines up with the correct mark. Put the jug on a flat table first, because if you hold it in your hand, it may tilt and the level will slope. You may also notice that the surface of water curves up a tiny bit where it touches the side of the jug. Read the level at the flat part in the middle, not at the edge. Scientists measure liquids in exactly the same way in the laboratory. It only takes a few seconds, and it makes your measurement accurate.

### subtopic_3 — Recording, Comparing and Ordering
**Assistant:** How many cups or spoons make a litre?

**Tutor:** Use the benchmarks. A standard measuring cup holds 250 ml. 1 litre is 1 000 ml, and 1 000 divided by 250 is 4, so 4 cups make 1 litre. Half a litre is 2 cups, and a quarter of a litre is 1 cup. A tablespoon holds 15 ml and a teaspoon holds 5 ml, so 3 teaspoons fill 1 tablespoon. Spoons are too small for measuring litres, but they are perfect for small amounts in recipes, like ginger, spices or medicine. 1 litre would need about 67 tablespoons, which is why we use cups and jugs for larger amounts. For the ginger beer recipe, the Mokoenas use spoons for the ginger, cups for the sugar and the 2 l jug for the water. This is like choosing a ruler for small lengths and a tape measure for long ones. Knowing the benchmarks also helps you estimate. If a recipe says 750 ml of water and you have no jug, you can measure 3 cups instead. And if a medicine bottle says take 10 ml, you know that is 2 teaspoons.
