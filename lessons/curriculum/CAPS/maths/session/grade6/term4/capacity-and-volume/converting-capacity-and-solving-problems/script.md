# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers converting between kilolitres, litres and millilitres, including common fraction and decimal fraction forms to two decimal places, and solving problems in contexts involving capacity and volume. It builds on measuring capacity in the previous lesson and on converting mass and length. The anchor is a water-saving project at a primary school in Cape Town, a city that has faced serious droughts. The school has a rain tank that holds 5 kl. On sports days, 400 learners each drink about 2,5 l. The class measures a dripping tap, compares a shower with a bath, and shares juice from 1,5 l bottles and 330 ml cans. The governing idea is that 1 000 ml make 1 l and 1 000 l make 1 kl, so we multiply by 1 000 to change into the smaller unit and divide by 1 000 to change into the bigger unit, and that amounts must be in the same unit before we calculate with them. Converting in both directions, writing capacities as decimals and fractions, and solving water problems are examinable.

## Subtopic: Bigger Units to Smaller Units

To change litres into millilitres, multiply by 1 000. To change kilolitres into litres, also multiply by 1 000. The answer must be a bigger number, because the units are smaller.

A juice bottle holds 1,5 l. 1 l is 1 000 ml and 0,5 l is half a litre, which is 500 ml. So 1,5 l is 1 500 ml. A 2,25 l bottle holds 2 250 ml, because 25 hundredths of 1 000 ml is 250 ml. A 0,25 l glass holds 250 ml.

Fractions work the same way. 1/2 l is 500 ml. 1/4 l is 250 ml. 3/4 l is 750 ml. 1/5 l is 200 ml. 2 and 1/2 l is 2 500 ml.

The rain tank holds 5 kl, which is 5 000 l. A smaller tank of 2,5 kl holds 2 500 l, and a tank of 0,25 kl holds 250 l.

On a place value chart, multiplying by 1 000 moves every digit three places to the left. In 1,5 the 1 unit becomes 1 thousand and the 5 tenths become 5 hundreds: 1 500.

Always ask whether the answer is sensible. A 1,5 l bottle is a medium cooldrink bottle. 1 500 small millilitres fit in it, so a big number makes sense. If you got 150 ml, that would be less than a cup, which cannot be right.

The questions for this section are with you now: changing into smaller units.

## Subtopic: Smaller Units to Bigger Units

To change millilitres into litres, divide by 1 000. To change litres into kilolitres, also divide by 1 000. The answer must be a smaller number.

2 500 ml is 2 000 ml, which is 2 l, and 500 ml, which is half a litre. So 2 500 ml is 2,5 l, or 2 and 1/2 l. A can of 330 ml holds 330 thousandths of a litre, which is 0,33 l to two decimal places. 750 ml is 0,75 l, or 3/4 l.

7 500 l is 7,5 kl. 600 l is 0,6 kl, which is 3/5 kl in simplest form.

Writing an amount in two units is also useful: 2 500 ml is 2 l 500 ml.

Check every conversion by going back the other way. 2,5 l times 1 000 should give 2 500 ml again.

Labels on shop shelves use both units. A 500 ml bottle of water might also say 0,5 l, and a 2 l bottle of milk might be compared with four 500 ml bottles to see which is the better buy.

The questions for this section are with you now: changing into bigger units, as decimals and fractions.

## Subtopic: Solving Water Problems

How long will the rain tank last if the school uses 1 000 l a day? The tank holds 5 kl, which is 5 000 l. 5 000 divided by 1 000 is 5, so the water lasts 5 days.

On sports day, 400 learners each drink 2,5 l. 400 times 2 l is 800 l, and 400 times half a litre is 200 l. Altogether that is 1 000 l, or 1 kl.

How many 250 ml cups can be poured from a 1,5 l bottle? 1,5 l is 1 500 ml, and 1 500 divided by 250 is 6 cups.

The class buys 12 cans of juice of 330 ml each. 12 times 330 ml is 3 960 ml, which is 3,96 l.

A tap drips 5 l every hour. In a day of 24 hours it wastes 24 times 5, which is 120 l. In a week, it wastes 7 times 120, which is 840 l. That is almost one whole kilolitre, just from one dripping tap.

A shower uses 9 l a minute. A 4-minute shower uses 36 l. A bath uses about 150 l. Taking a short shower instead of a bath saves 150 minus 36, which is 114 l.

For every capacity problem, follow the same steps. Read the question and note the units. Change them into the same unit. Choose the operation. Estimate, calculate and check.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: writing 1,5 l as 150 ml by multiplying by 100; there are 1 000 ml in a litre, so it is 1 500 ml. Two: writing 2 500 ml as 25 l by dividing by 100; divide by 1 000 to get 2,5 l. Three: writing 3/4 l as 75 ml; three quarters of 1 000 ml is 750 ml. Four: adding 2 l and 500 ml to get 502; change to the same unit first, and the total is 2,5 l.

The questions for this section are with you now: solving water problems.

Pocket summary of the lesson. 1 000 ml make 1 l, and 1 000 l make 1 kl. To change into a smaller unit, multiply by 1 000; to change into a bigger unit, divide by 1 000.
