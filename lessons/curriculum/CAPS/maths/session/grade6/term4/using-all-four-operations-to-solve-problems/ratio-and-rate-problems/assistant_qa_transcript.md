### subtopic_1 — Ratio
**Assistant:** How is a ratio different from a rate?

**Tutor:** A ratio compares quantities of the same kind, measured in the same unit. The juice mix of 1 cup of concentrate to 4 cups of water is a ratio, 1 : 4, because both amounts are in cups. Because the units are the same, they cancel out, and a ratio has no units at all. You would never say the ratio is 1 : 4 cups. A rate compares quantities of different kinds, measured in different units. R18 for 6 rolls is a rate, because one quantity is money and the other is a number of rolls. The speed of 80 km per hour is a rate, because one quantity is distance and the other is time. A rate always needs its units, and we usually write it as one amount per one of the other, such as R3 per roll. A quick test is to look at the units. If they match, you have a ratio. If they are different, you have a rate. Both are ways of comparing, and both are solved with multiplication and division.

### subtopic_2 — Rate
**Assistant:** How do I share an amount in a ratio such as 3 : 4?

**Tutor:** Think of the ratio as parts. In the ratio 3 : 4, the first person gets 3 parts and the second gets 4 parts, so there are 3 plus 4, which is 7 parts altogether. When the bakers share 35 rolls, those 7 parts must make up all 35 rolls, so one part is 35 divided by 7, which is 5 rolls. The first baker gets 3 parts, which is 3 times 5, or 15 rolls. The second baker gets 4 parts, which is 4 times 5, or 20 rolls. Always check by adding the shares: 15 plus 20 is 35, which is the total we started with. A common mistake is to share the rolls equally, because sharing usually means equal shares. But a ratio of 3 : 4 tells you the shares are not equal: the second baker gets more. Another mistake is to give the bakers just 3 and 4 rolls. The ratio tells you the size of the shares compared with each other, not the actual number of rolls. Drawing 7 boxes, 3 for one baker and 4 for the other, with 5 rolls in each box, is a good way to see it.

### subtopic_3 — Solving Ratio and Rate Problems
**Assistant:** Why is the bigger pack not always the better buy?

**Tutor:** Because a bigger pack costs more money as well as giving you more items, so you have to compare fairly. The fair way is to find the price of one item in each pack. The pack of 6 rolls costs R18, so one roll costs R18 divided by 6, which is R3. The pack of 10 rolls costs R25, so one roll costs R25 divided by 10, which is R2,50. Here the bigger pack is the better buy, because each roll is cheaper. But it does not always work that way. If the pack of 10 rolls cost R35, each roll would cost R3,50, and the small pack would be the better buy. Shops sometimes make big packs look like bargains when they are not. Another fair way is to compare the same number of items. For 30 rolls, you would need 5 small packs at R18, which is R90, or 3 big packs at R25, which is R75. Either way, working out a rate tells you the truth. Of course, the better buy is only better if you need that many rolls before they go stale.
