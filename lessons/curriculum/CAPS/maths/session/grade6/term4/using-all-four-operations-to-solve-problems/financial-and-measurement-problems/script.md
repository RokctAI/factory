# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers solving problems with whole numbers and fractions in financial and measurement contexts, using all four operations, and using fractions for grouping and equal sharing. It brings together the operations, fractions and measurement work of the whole year. The anchor is the Grade 6 class at a school in Vryheid, which runs a vetkoek and juice stall at the spring market. The class buys ingredients, measures flour, oil and juice, sells its food and shares the results. The governing idea is that every problem in context follows the same steps: read carefully, decide which operations are needed, make sure the units match, calculate, and check that the answer makes sense in the story. Solving financial and measurement problems with whole numbers and fractions, including grouping and sharing problems, is examinable.

## Subtopic: Money Problems

In money problems, the words tell you which operation to use. A total cost means adding. Change means subtracting. Many items at the same price means multiplying. Sharing an amount equally means dividing.

The class buys flour for R89, cooking oil for R65 and mince for R146. The total cost is R89 plus R65 plus R146. R89 plus R65 is R154, and R154 plus R146 is R300. So the ingredients cost R300.

On market day, the class sells 120 vetkoek at R5 each. The income is 120 times R5, which is R600.

Profit is the money that is left after the costs are paid. Profit equals income minus costs. Here the profit on vetkoek is R600 minus R300, which is R300. If the costs were more than the income, the class would make a loss instead of a profit.

The juice also makes money. The juice concentrate costs R32, and the class sells 20 cups of juice at R4 each, which is R80. The profit on juice is R80 minus R32, which is R48. So the total profit for the day is R300 plus R48, which is R348.

Change is a subtraction problem. A customer buys 7 vetkoek with a R50 note. 7 times R5 is R35, and R50 minus R35 is R15, so the change is R15.

Before you calculate, it helps to estimate. 120 vetkoek at about R5 is about R600, and costs of about R90, R70 and R150 come to about R310. So a profit of about R300 makes sense. If your answer were R3 000 or R30, the estimate would warn you that something went wrong.

The questions for this section are with you now: money problems.

## Subtopic: Measurement Problems with Fractions

Measurement problems often ask for a fraction of a quantity. To find a fraction of an amount, divide by the denominator and multiply by the numerator. It is usually easiest to change to a smaller unit first, so that you work with whole numbers.

The vetkoek recipe uses three quarters of a 2 kg bag of flour. 2 kg is 2 000 g. One quarter of 2 000 g is 2 000 divided by 4, which is 500 g. Three quarters is 3 times 500 g, which is 1 500 g. So the class uses 1 500 g, which is one and a half kilograms, of flour.

The class has a 2 litre bottle of oil and uses three fifths of it for frying. 2 litres is 2 000 ml. One fifth of 2 000 ml is 400 ml, so three fifths is 1 200 ml. The oil left over is 2 000 ml minus 1 200 ml, which is 800 ml.

Units must match before you divide. The class mixes 5 litres of juice and pours it into cups that each hold 250 ml. You cannot divide 5 by 250, because the units are different. Change 5 litres to 5 000 ml. Then 5 000 ml divided by 250 ml is 20, so the class can fill 20 cups.

Time is a measurement too. The market runs from 08:30 to 13:00. From 08:30 to 12:30 is 4 hours, and from 12:30 to 13:00 is another half an hour, so the market runs for four and a half hours.

When you finish a measurement problem, check that the unit in your answer fits the question. A mass answer needs grams or kilograms, a capacity answer needs millilitres or litres, and a time answer needs hours or minutes.

## Subtopic: Grouping and Equal Sharing

Fractions come up naturally when we share things equally or put things into equal groups.

In an equal sharing problem, we know how many shares there are and we find the size of each share. At the end of the day, 3 melktert tarts are left over, and the 4 helpers share them equally. Each tart can be cut into quarters, giving 12 quarters altogether. 12 quarters shared among 4 helpers is 3 quarters each. So each helper gets three quarters of a tart. In general, 3 things shared equally among 4 people gives each person 3 divided by 4, which is three quarters.

The helpers also share 5 litres of leftover juice equally. Each gets 5 divided by 4 litres, which is one and a quarter litres, or 1 250 ml.

In a grouping problem, we know the size of each group and we find how many groups there are. The class has 3 litres of juice and pours it into cups of a quarter litre each. Each litre fills 4 quarter-litre cups, so 3 litres fill 3 times 4, which is 12 cups. If the bottles hold half a litre each instead, 6 litres of juice fill 12 bottles, because each litre fills 2 half-litre bottles.

Fractions of money work the same way. The class gives one third of its R348 profit to the school library. One third of R348 is R348 divided by 3, which is R116. The rest is R348 minus R116, which is R232. The 4 helpers share the rest equally, so each gets R232 divided by 4, which is R58.

Check sharing answers by working backwards. 4 helpers with R58 each is R232, and R232 plus R116 is R348, which matches the profit.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: adding the income and the costs to get the profit, R600 plus R300 is R900; profit is income minus costs, R300. Two: saying three quarters of a 2 kg bag is three quarters of a kilogram; three quarters of 2 kg is 1 500 g. Three: dividing 5 by 250 to find the number of cups; change 5 litres to 5 000 ml first, and 5 000 divided by 250 is 20 cups. Four: saying that 3 tarts shared among 4 helpers gives each four thirds of a tart; each gets 3 divided by 4, which is three quarters.

The questions for this section are with you now: grouping and equal sharing.
