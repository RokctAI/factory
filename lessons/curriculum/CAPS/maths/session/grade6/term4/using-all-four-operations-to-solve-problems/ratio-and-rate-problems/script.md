# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers comparing two or more quantities of the same kind, which is called ratio, and comparing two quantities of different kinds, which is called rate, and solving problems with both. It builds on multiplication, division and fractions from earlier in the year. The anchor is a family bakery in Ladysmith. The bakery mixes juice for its customers, shares bread rolls between its two bakers, sells rolls in packs of different sizes and sends a delivery bakkie to nearby towns. The governing idea is that a ratio compares quantities measured in the same unit, while a rate compares quantities measured in different units and is usually written with the word per. Writing, using and solving problems with ratios and rates is examinable.

## Subtopic: Ratio

A ratio compares two or more quantities of the same kind. We write it with a colon, and we read the colon as to.

The bakery mixes its juice using 1 cup of concentrate to 4 cups of water. The ratio of concentrate to water is 1 : 4, read as 1 to 4. Both quantities are measured in cups, so the ratio has no units.

Order matters in a ratio. The ratio of concentrate to water is 1 : 4, but the ratio of water to concentrate is 4 : 1. Always write the numbers in the same order as the words.

A ratio stays the same when you multiply both numbers by the same number. To make a bigger batch with 2 cups of concentrate, the bakery needs 2 times 4, which is 8 cups of water. The ratio 2 : 8 gives the same taste as 1 : 4. With 3 cups of concentrate, it needs 12 cups of water.

You can also go the other way and divide both numbers by the same number. This is called simplifying a ratio. The bakery employs 12 men and 18 women. The ratio of men to women is 12 : 18. Both numbers can be divided by 6, giving 2 : 3. So for every 2 men there are 3 women.

A ratio can compare more than two quantities. A bread mix uses 5 cups of flour, 2 cups of water and 1 cup of milk. The ratio of flour to water to milk is 5 : 2 : 1.

A ratio also tells you about the whole. In the juice, there are 1 plus 4, which is 5 parts altogether. So the concentrate is 1 part out of 5, which is one fifth of the juice, and the water is four fifths.

## Subtopic: Rate

A rate compares two quantities of different kinds, such as rands and rolls, or kilometres and hours. A rate has units, and we usually write it with the word per, which means for each.

The bakery sells a pack of 6 rolls for R18. The rate is R18 for 6 rolls. To find the price per roll, divide: R18 divided by 6 is R3. So the rate is R3 per roll. Once you know the price per roll, you can find the price of any number of rolls: 10 rolls at R3 per roll cost R30.

The delivery bakkie travels 240 km in 3 hours. Its speed is a rate: 240 km divided by 3 hours is 80 km per hour, often written as 80 km/h. At that speed, the bakkie travels 400 km in 5 hours, because 80 times 5 is 400.

The oven also works at a rate. It bakes 48 rolls in 20 minutes. There are 3 lots of 20 minutes in an hour, so the oven bakes 3 times 48, which is 144 rolls per hour.

Rates can describe many things: litres of petrol per 100 km, words per minute when typing, heartbeats per minute, or rands per kilogram at the shop.

## Subtopic: Solving Ratio and Rate Problems

Many problems ask you to share an amount in a given ratio. At the end of the day, the two bakers share 35 leftover rolls in the ratio 3 : 4. First find the total number of parts: 3 plus 4 is 7 parts. Then find the size of one part: 35 divided by 7 is 5 rolls. The first baker gets 3 parts, which is 3 times 5, or 15 rolls. The second baker gets 4 parts, which is 4 times 5, or 20 rolls. Check: 15 plus 20 is 35.

Rates help us find the better buy. The bakery sells packs of 6 rolls for R18 and packs of 10 rolls for R25. Which is better value? Find the price per roll for each. The small pack costs R18 divided by 6, which is R3 per roll. The big pack costs R25 divided by 10, which is R2,50 per roll. The big pack is the better buy. Another way is to compare the same number of rolls: 30 rolls cost 5 small packs, which is R90, or 3 big packs, which is R75.

Recipes use rates too. The bakery uses 500 g of flour to make 10 rolls. That is 50 g of flour per roll. To make 35 rolls, it needs 35 times 50 g, which is 1 750 g of flour.

A good habit is to ask whether the two quantities are the same kind or different kinds. Cups and cups, or rolls and rolls, give a ratio with no units. Rands and rolls, or kilometres and hours, give a rate, and the answer needs a unit with per.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: writing the ratio of concentrate to water as 4 : 1; the words say concentrate first, so it is 1 : 4. Two: sharing 35 rolls equally, seventeen and a half each, when the ratio is 3 : 4; there are 7 parts of 5 rolls, so the bakers get 15 and 20. Three: thinking the bigger pack is always the better buy; compare the price per roll, R3 against R2,50. Four: multiplying 240 by 3 to find the speed and getting 720; speed is distance divided by time, so it is 80 km per hour.
