### subtopic_1 — Counting Faces, Vertices and Edges of Prisms
**Assistant:** How do I count the edges of a cube without missing the hidden ones?

**Tutor:** Count in layers, and say where each group is. Picture the cube sitting on a table. First, count the edges around the top face: there are 4. Next, count the edges around the bottom face, the one touching the table: there are 4 more, even though you cannot see all of them in a picture. Finally, count the upright edges that join the top to the bottom, one at each corner: there are 4. Add the layers: 4 plus 4 plus 4 is 12 edges. In a drawing of a cube, usually only 9 edges are visible, and the other 3 are hidden behind. Some drawings show the hidden edges as dotted lines to remind you. You can check your count with a model. When the maths club built their cube frame, they needed exactly 12 straws, one for each edge, and no straw was left over. The same layer method works for vertices: 4 on top and 4 at the bottom make 8. It also works for faces: the top, the bottom and the 4 sides make 6. Counting in an organised way is much more reliable than pointing at a picture and hoping.

### subtopic_2 — Counting and Comparing Pyramids
**Assistant:** Why does a pyramid always have the same number of faces as vertices?

**Tutor:** Because of how a pyramid is built from its base. Suppose the base has a certain number of sides. Each side of the base has one triangle rising from it, so there are as many triangles as base sides. Add the base itself, and the number of faces is one more than the number of base sides. Now count the vertices. The base has as many corners as sides, and there is one more vertex at the apex. So the number of vertices is also one more than the number of base sides. Both counts are the base sides plus one, so they are always equal. Check with a square-based pyramid. Its base has 4 sides, so it has 4 triangles plus the base, 5 faces, and 4 base corners plus the apex, 5 vertices. A tetrahedron has 4 faces and 4 vertices, and a hexagonal pyramid has 7 of each. The edges follow a pattern too: each base side is an edge, and each base corner has an edge running up to the apex, so the number of edges is double the number of base sides. A hexagonal pyramid has 12 edges.

### subtopic_3 — Making 3D Models
**Assistant:** Why do some arrangements of six squares not fold into a cube?

**Tutor:** Because when you fold them, some faces land on top of each other and other places are left open. A cube needs one square for each of its 6 faces: top, bottom, front, back, left and right. In a working net, each square folds into a different position. Take the cross-shaped net: a row of 4 squares makes the front, the right side, the back and the left side as you fold them around, and the squares above and below fold over to make the top and the bottom. Every face is covered exactly once. Now try two rows of 3 squares side by side. When you fold them, two squares try to become the same face and overlap, and the top and bottom are left empty. So it cannot make a cube. There are 11 different nets that do work for a cube, and many arrangements of 6 squares that do not. A good way to test a net is to imagine one square as the bottom and fold the others up in your mind, naming each face as it lands. If two squares land in the same place, the net does not work. Cutting and folding real paper is the best check of all.
