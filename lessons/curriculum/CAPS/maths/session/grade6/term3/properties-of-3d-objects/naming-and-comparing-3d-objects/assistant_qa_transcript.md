### subtopic_1 — Prisms and Cubes
**Assistant:** Why is a tetrahedron a pyramid if it does not look like the pyramids of Egypt?

**Tutor:** Because a pyramid is defined by its parts, not by one famous example. A pyramid is any 3D object with one polygon as its base and triangular faces that rise from each side of the base and meet at a single point, the apex. The pyramids of Egypt have a square base, so they have four triangles meeting at the top. Now picture a pyramid with a triangle as its base. The base has three sides, so three triangles rise from it and meet at the apex. Count all the faces: the base triangle plus three side triangles makes four triangles. That object is a triangular-based pyramid, and because it has exactly four faces, we also call it a tetrahedron. It looks a bit different from the Egyptian pyramids because its base is a triangle, not a square. But it follows exactly the same rule. The pyramid tea bags at the depot are tetrahedrons. Pyramids come in a whole family: triangular, square, pentagonal, hexagonal and so on, each named by its base. The tetrahedron is simply the member of the family with the fewest faces.

### subtopic_2 — Pyramids and Tetrahedrons
**Assistant:** How is a cube different from other rectangular prisms?

**Tutor:** A cube is a rectangular prism in which every face is a square. All rectangular prisms have six faces, and opposite faces are identical rectangles. In a cereal box, the front and back are big rectangles, the sides are tall thin rectangles, and the top and bottom are short thin rectangles. The length, width and height are all different. In a cube, the length, width and height are all the same, so every face is the same square. A dice is a cube, and so is a sugar cube. Remember that a square is a special rectangle, with four right angles and all its sides equal. In the same way, a cube is a special rectangular prism, with six faces and all its edges equal. So every cube is a rectangular prism, but not every rectangular prism is a cube. This is the same idea as squares and rectangles in 2D, just one dimension up. When you sort objects at the depot, you could put the dice in the rectangular prism bin, but it is more exact to give them their own special name: cubes.

### subtopic_3 — Comparing and Visualising 3D Objects
**Assistant:** How can I see a 3D object in my mind from different sides?

**Tutor:** Practise with real objects first, then picture them. Hold a square-based pyramid, like the gift box, at eye level and look straight at the front. You see a triangle, because one triangular face points at you and the base is hidden below. Now hold it below your eyes and look straight down from above. You see a square, the outline of the base, with four lines running from its corners to the middle, where the apex sticks up towards you. The same object gives a triangle from the front and a square from above. Try the triangular prism, the chocolate box. Look at its end and you see a triangle. Look at its long side and you see a rectangle. A cube shows a square from every side, which is one way to recognise it. When you are given a picture of a 3D object, imagine walking around it and looking down on it. Ask which faces would face you and what shapes they are. Drawing the views helps too. Architects and engineers draw objects from the front, the side and the top, so that builders know exactly what to make.
