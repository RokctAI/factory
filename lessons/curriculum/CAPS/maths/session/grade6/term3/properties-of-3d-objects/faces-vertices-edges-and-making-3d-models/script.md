# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers describing, sorting and comparing 3D objects by the number and shape of their faces, the number of their vertices and the number of their edges, and making 3D models using drinking straws, toothpicks and nets. It builds on naming prisms and pyramids in the previous lesson. The anchor is the maths club at a school in Thohoyandou, preparing a display of 3D objects. They build skeleton frames using drinking straws and toothpicks for the edges and small balls of clay for the vertices, and they make solid models by cutting nets from old cereal boxes and folding them. The governing idea is that every face, vertex and edge can be counted in an organised way, and that a model makes the count visible: each straw is an edge, each ball of clay is a vertex, and each flat part of a net is a face. Counting faces, vertices and edges, comparing objects with a table, and planning models and nets are examinable.

## Subtopic: Counting Faces, Vertices and Edges of Prisms

A face is a flat surface. An edge is a line where two faces meet. A vertex is a corner where edges meet; the plural is vertices.

Count in an organised way, so you do not miss the faces, edges and vertices at the back. For a cube, count the top, the bottom and the four sides: 6 faces. Count the vertices: 4 around the top and 4 around the bottom, 8 vertices. Count the edges: 4 around the top, 4 around the bottom and 4 upright edges joining them, 12 edges.

A rectangular prism, like a cereal box, has the same counts as a cube: 6 faces, 8 vertices and 12 edges. The difference is in the shape of the faces. A cube's faces are all squares; a rectangular prism's faces are rectangles, and not all of them are squares.

A triangular prism has 2 triangular faces and 3 rectangular faces, which is 5 faces. It has 3 vertices on each triangle, which is 6 vertices. It has 3 edges around each triangle and 3 edges joining them, which is 9 edges.

Notice the pattern for prisms. Count the sides of the base. A triangular prism's base has 3 sides; it has 3 times 2 vertices and 3 times 3 edges. A rectangular prism's base has 4 sides; it has 4 times 2 vertices and 4 times 3 edges.

The questions for this section are with you now: counting faces, vertices and edges of prisms.

## Subtopic: Counting and Comparing Pyramids

A square-based pyramid has 1 square face and 4 triangular faces, which is 5 faces. It has 4 vertices around the base and 1 at the apex, which is 5 vertices. It has 4 edges around the base and 4 edges running up to the apex, which is 8 edges.

A tetrahedron has 4 triangular faces. It has 3 vertices around its base and 1 at the apex, which is 4 vertices. It has 3 edges around the base and 3 up to the apex, which is 6 edges.

A pentagonal pyramid has 6 faces, 6 vertices and 10 edges. A hexagonal pyramid has 7 faces, 7 vertices and 12 edges.

A table makes comparing easy. Write the name of each object down the side and faces, vertices and edges across the top. The table shows patterns. In every pyramid, the number of faces equals the number of vertices: one more than the number of sides of the base. And the number of edges is double the number of sides of the base.

We can sort objects using the table. Objects with only triangular faces: the tetrahedron. Objects with exactly 5 faces: the square-based pyramid and the triangular prism. Objects with the same number of faces as vertices: all the pyramids.

The questions for this section are with you now: counting and comparing pyramids.

## Subtopic: Making 3D Models

A skeleton model shows the edges and vertices. Each straw or toothpick is one edge, and each ball of clay is one vertex. So to plan a model, count the edges and vertices first.

A cube frame needs 12 straws of equal length and 8 balls of clay. A rectangular prism frame also needs 12 straws and 8 balls, but in three different lengths: 4 long, 4 medium and 4 short. A tetrahedron needs 6 toothpicks and 4 balls. A square-based pyramid needs 8 straws and 5 balls. A triangular prism needs 9 straws and 6 balls.

A net is a flat shape that folds up to make a 3D object. Each part of the net becomes a face. The net of a cube is 6 squares joined edge to edge. A common one is a cross: a row of 4 squares with 1 square above and 1 below the second square. But not every arrangement of 6 squares works. Two rows of 3 squares will not fold into a cube, because some faces overlap and others are missing. The net of a square-based pyramid is 1 square with a triangle on each side. Add small tabs on some edges to glue the model together.

Skeleton models and nets show different things. A straw frame shows the edges and vertices clearly, and you can look right through it to see the hidden edges at the back. A folded net shows the faces, so you can see their shapes and colour them. The maths club makes one of each for every object in their display.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: counting only the edges you can see, giving a cube 9 edges; count the hidden edges too, and there are 12. Two: mixing up vertices and edges; edges are the lines, vertices are the corners. Three: thinking any 6 squares fold into a cube; two rows of 3 do not. Four: saying a square-based pyramid has 4 faces; do not forget the square base, so it has 5.

The questions for this section are with you now: planning models and nets.
