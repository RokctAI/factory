### subtopic_1 — Same Shape, New Size
**Assistant:** Why can I not just add the same amount to every side to make a shape bigger?

**Tutor:** Because adding changes the shape, while multiplying keeps it. Take the 3 by 2 rectangle. If you add 2 to each side, you get 5 by 4. Compare the shapes: the first rectangle is one and a half times as long as it is wide, because 3 is one and a half times 2. The new one is only one and a quarter times as long as it is wide, because 5 is one and a quarter times 4. So the new rectangle is much more square-like. It is bigger, but it is not the same shape. Now multiply each side by 2 instead, to get 6 by 4. 6 is one and a half times 4, just as 3 was one and a half times 2. The shape has kept its proportions, so it looks exactly the same, only bigger. You can see the difference with a photo. If a printer added the same number of centimetres to the width and the height, people's faces would look squashed or stretched. Multiplying keeps everything in proportion, so the picture looks right.

### subtopic_2 — Drawing on a Grid
**Assistant:** Do the angles change when I enlarge a triangle?

**Tutor:** No, the angles stay exactly the same. An enlargement only changes the lengths of the sides. Think about what an angle measures: the amount of turn between two sides. When you enlarge a triangle, each side gets longer, but the sides still meet each other at the same amount of turn. Draw a right-angled triangle with a base of 4 squares and a height of 3 squares, and then enlarge it so that the base is 12 squares and the height is 9 squares. The right angle is still a right angle, and the two acute angles are still the same size as before. If you cut out both triangles, you could lay the small one into each corner of the big one and see that the corners match exactly. This is actually the best test for whether a shape is a true enlargement. If the angles have changed, the shape has changed, and it is not an enlargement. Remember that longer arms never make an angle bigger. That is why the logo looks the same on the badge and on the banner: every angle in the design is unchanged, and only the lengths are bigger or smaller.

### subtopic_3 — Comparing Size and Shape
**Assistant:** Why does a rectangle cover 4 times as many squares when its sides are doubled?

**Tutor:** Because the doubling happens in two directions at once. The 3 by 2 rectangle has 2 rows of 3 squares, which is 6 squares. When you double every side, it becomes 6 by 4. Doubling the length means each row now has 6 squares instead of 3, which is twice as many in each row. Doubling the width means there are now 4 rows instead of 2, which is twice as many rows. Twice as many squares in each row, and twice as many rows, gives 2 times 2, which is 4 times as many squares altogether: 24 instead of 6. You can see this on grid paper. The enlarged rectangle can be cut into exactly four copies of the original rectangle. If the sides are made 3 times as long, the space inside becomes 3 times 3, which is 9 times as big. This matters for the print shop. The banner logo is 5 times as wide and 5 times as high as the original, so it needs 25 times as much ink to fill it. That is why big posters cost much more to print than small ones.
