### subtopic_1 — Slide, Flip and Turn
**Assistant:** How can I tell whether a shape was translated, reflected or rotated?

**Tutor:** Compare the new shape with the old one and ask two questions. First, does it face the same way? Pick a feature, such as the right angle on a triangle paver or a dot drawn in one corner, and see where it is. If the new shape faces exactly the same way and has simply moved, it was translated. Every point moved the same distance in the same direction. Second, if it does not face the same way, is it a mirror image or is it turned? A mirror image looks like the reflection of the shape in a mirror: what was on the left is now on the right, like your left and right hands. You could find a mirror line halfway between the two shapes. That is a reflection. If instead the shape looks as if it has been spun around a point, like the hands of a clock, it was rotated. A good test is to trace the shape on thin paper. If you can slide the tracing onto the new shape, it was translated. If you have to flip the paper over, it was reflected. If you have to turn it, it was rotated.

### subtopic_2 — Making Composite Shapes
**Assistant:** Why do regular hexagons tessellate but regular pentagons do not?

**Tutor:** Because of the angles at the points where the corners meet. For shapes to fit with no gaps and no overlaps, the angles that meet at each point must add up to exactly one full revolution, all the way around. Each angle of a regular hexagon is a third of a revolution. So three hexagons meet at each point, and their three angles fill the revolution exactly. That is why a honeycomb has no gaps. Each angle of a regular pentagon is a bit bigger than a right angle. Three pentagons meeting at a point do not quite fill the revolution, so a thin gap is left. Four pentagons are too much, so they would overlap. There is no whole number of pentagons that fits exactly, so regular pentagons cannot tessellate on their own. The same check works for other shapes. Four squares fill a revolution, because four right angles make a full turn. Six equilateral triangles fill a revolution. Regular octagons leave gaps, but Mrs Mokoena fills them with small squares, so octagons and squares together tessellate.

### subtopic_3 — Tessellations
**Assistant:** Why does reflecting a shape always give a composite shape with line symmetry?

**Tutor:** Because a reflection creates a mirror image, and a line of symmetry is exactly a line with a mirror image on each side. When Mrs Mokoena reflects a right-angled triangle paver across its upright side, every point of the new triangle is the same distance from the upright side as its partner in the old triangle, just on the opposite side. So if you fold the composite shape along that upright side, the new triangle lands exactly on the old one. That is the fold test for symmetry, and it passes. The mirror line of the reflection becomes the line of symmetry of the whole shape. The result here is an isosceles triangle, which has one line of symmetry. If she reflects the triangle across its long slanted side instead, she gets a kite, and again the mirror line is the line of symmetry. Translations and rotations do not always give symmetry. Two triangles side by side that both face the same way, after a translation, may not have any line of symmetry at all. So if a design must be symmetrical, reflection is the move to use.
