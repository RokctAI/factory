# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the three transformations, translation, reflection and rotation, using them to make composite 2D shapes, including shapes with line symmetry, and making tessellated patterns. It builds on lines of symmetry and on the angles in 2D shapes from earlier this term. The anchor is Mrs Mokoena's paving business in Kimberley. She lays square pavers, rectangular bricks, right-angled triangle pavers and hexagonal pavers in driveways and courtyards, and she plans each pattern on grid paper first. The governing idea is that a translation slides a shape, a reflection flips it over a line and a rotation turns it about a point, and that none of these changes the size or shape of the shape, only its position or the way it faces. Naming and doing transformations, making composite shapes, and making and recognising tessellations are examinable.

## Subtopic: Slide, Flip and Turn

A transformation moves a shape. In Grade 6 we use three.

A translation is a slide. Every point of the shape moves the same distance in the same direction. The shape does not turn or flip, so it still faces the same way. Mrs Mokoena slides a brick 4 squares to the right on her plan. That is a translation.

A reflection is a flip. The shape is flipped over a mirror line, so the new shape is a mirror image of the old one. It faces the opposite way, like your left hand and your right hand. Every point is the same distance from the mirror line as its partner.

A rotation is a turn. The shape turns about a fixed point, called the centre of rotation. It can turn a quarter turn, a half turn, or any amount, clockwise or anticlockwise. A paver turned a half turn about one corner ends up upside down on the other side of that corner.

In all three transformations, the size and shape stay exactly the same. The sides keep their lengths and the angles keep their sizes. Only the position, and sometimes the direction the shape faces, change.

On grid paper, a translation is described by how far the shape moves and in which direction, such as 3 squares right and 2 squares up. A reflection is described by its mirror line, such as the vertical line through the middle of the grid. A rotation is described by its centre, the amount of turn and the direction, such as a quarter turn clockwise about the bottom left corner.

You can see transformations everywhere. A sliding door translates. Your reflection in a shop window is a reflection. The wheels of a taxi and the hands of a clock rotate.

## Subtopic: Making Composite Shapes

A composite shape is a shape made by putting two or more shapes together. Transformations are a neat way to build them.

Translate a square paver one paver-width to the right, and the two squares together make a rectangle.

Take a right-angled triangle paver and reflect it across its upright side. The triangle and its mirror image together make an isosceles triangle. Because one half is the reflection of the other, the new shape has a line of symmetry along the mirror line. Reflecting always gives a composite shape with line symmetry.

Reflect the same triangle across its long slanted side instead, and the two triangles make a kite. Again, the mirror line is a line of symmetry.

Rotate a right-angled triangle a half turn about the middle of its long side, and the two triangles together make a rectangle.

Four squares can be joined in many ways, but they must touch along whole sides. Some arrangements, like a straight row or a big square, have line symmetry. Others, like an S-shape, have none.

Mrs Mokoena designs a border for a driveway by taking one triangle paver and reflecting it, then translating the pair along the edge again and again. The border has a line of symmetry at every reflection and repeats all the way along.

## Subtopic: Tessellations

A tessellation is a pattern of shapes that covers a flat surface completely, with no gaps and no overlaps, and could go on forever in every direction. Floor tiles, brick walls, paving and honeycombs are tessellations.

Squares tessellate: translate a square again and again in rows and columns. Rectangular bricks tessellate in a brick pattern, where each row is translated half a brick sideways. Equilateral triangles tessellate: rotate each triangle a half turn to fit it between its neighbours. Regular hexagons tessellate like a honeycomb.

They work because, at every point where corners meet, the angles fill a full revolution exactly. Four right angles make a revolution. Six equilateral triangle angles make a revolution. Three regular hexagon angles make a revolution.

Some shapes cannot tessellate on their own. Regular pentagons leave gaps, because three of their angles are less than a revolution and four are more. Circles always leave gaps. Regular octagons leave square-shaped gaps, but Mrs Mokoena fills those gaps with small square pavers, making a pattern of octagons and squares.

Many tessellations have lines of symmetry, especially ones made by reflecting the tiles.

To make your own tessellation, choose a shape that tessellates, such as a square. Cut a small piece out of one side and stick it onto the opposite side by translating it straight across. The new shape looks unusual, but it still tessellates, because every bump fits into a matching dent. Artists use this trick to make tessellations of birds, fish and lizards.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: calling a flip a slide; if the shape faces the opposite way, it was reflected, not translated. Two: thinking a rotation makes a shape bigger or smaller; transformations never change the size. Three: saying regular pentagons tessellate; they leave gaps. Four: saying circles tessellate; there are always gaps between them.

The questions for this section are with you now: tessellations.
