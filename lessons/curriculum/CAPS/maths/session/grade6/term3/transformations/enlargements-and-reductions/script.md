# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers drawing enlargements and reductions of 2D shapes, mainly triangles and quadrilaterals, on grid paper, and comparing the original and the new shape in size and in shape. It builds on transformations and on the properties of 2D shapes from earlier this term, and on multiplying and finding fractions of numbers. The anchor is a print shop in Mthatha. A school brings in its logo, a design made of a rectangle and a triangle that is 4 cm wide. The shop enlarges it to 20 cm wide for a banner, and reduces a large poster version for a small badge. The governing idea is that in an enlargement or a reduction, every length is multiplied by the same number, so the shape stays the same while the size changes, and the angles do not change at all. Drawing enlargements and reductions on grids, finding the new lengths, and deciding whether one shape is an enlargement of another are examinable.

## Subtopic: Same Shape, New Size

An enlargement makes a shape bigger without changing its shape. A reduction makes a shape smaller without changing its shape. Think of a photo printed small for a wallet and large for a frame: the people in it look exactly the same, just a different size.

To keep the shape the same, every side must be multiplied by the same number. A rectangle 3 cm long and 2 cm wide, enlarged so that every side is twice as long, becomes 6 cm long and 4 cm wide. A rectangle 8 cm long and 4 cm wide, reduced so that every side is half as long, becomes 4 cm long and 2 cm wide.

The angles stay exactly the same. A rectangle still has four right angles after it is enlarged. A triangle with one right angle and two acute angles keeps those same angles. Only the lengths of the sides change.

The print shop enlarges the logo from 4 cm wide to 20 cm wide. Every length is multiplied by 5, because 4 times 5 is 20. So if the logo is 2 cm high, the banner version is 10 cm high.

For the badge, the shop starts with a poster version of the logo that is 40 cm wide and 20 cm high, and reduces it so that every length is a tenth as long. The badge logo is 4 cm wide and 2 cm high, the same as the school's original drawing. A reduction is just an enlargement in reverse.

The questions for this section are with you now: what enlargements and reductions are.

## Subtopic: Drawing on a Grid

Grid paper makes enlargements easy, because you can count squares.

Draw a right-angled triangle with a base of 4 squares and a height of 3 squares. To enlarge it so that every side is 3 times as long, multiply each count by 3. The new base is 12 squares and the new height is 9 squares. Draw the base along a grid line, draw the height straight up from one end, and join the ends with the slanting side. The slanting side is also 3 times as long as before, even though it does not follow the grid lines.

A square with sides of 2 squares, enlarged 5 times, has sides of 10 squares.

For a reduction, divide each count. A triangle with a base of 6 squares and a height of 4 squares, reduced to half, has a base of 3 squares and a height of 2 squares.

Another way to enlarge a drawing is to copy it from a small grid onto a bigger grid. Draw the shape on a grid of 1 cm squares. Then draw a grid of 2 cm squares and copy the drawing square by square. Each line crosses the same squares as before, so the copy is twice as big in every direction. Artists use this method to paint large murals from small sketches.

For a quadrilateral, enlarge each side in turn. A parallelogram with a base of 3 squares, whose slanting sides go across 1 and up 2, becomes, when every length is doubled, a parallelogram with a base of 6 squares whose slanting sides go across 2 and up 4.

The questions for this section are with you now: drawing enlargements and reductions on a grid.

## Subtopic: Comparing Size and Shape

To check whether one shape is an enlargement of another, compare matching sides. Divide each new side by its matching old side. If every answer is the same number and the angles are the same, it is an enlargement or reduction.

A 6 by 4 rectangle is an enlargement of a 3 by 2 rectangle: 6 is 2 times 3, and 4 is 2 times 2. But a 6 by 3 rectangle is not, because 6 is 2 times 3, while 3 is only 1 and a half times 2. That rectangle has been stretched; it is longer and thinner, not just bigger.

Notice what happens to the space inside. The 3 by 2 rectangle covers 6 squares. The enlargement with sides twice as long, 6 by 4, covers 24 squares. That is 4 times as many, not 2 times. When the sides are doubled, the space inside becomes 4 times as big, because it doubles in length and doubles in width.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: adding 2 to every side instead of multiplying, turning 3 by 2 into 5 by 4; the shape changes, so it is not an enlargement. Two: doubling only one side, turning 3 by 2 into 6 by 2; the shape is stretched. Three: thinking the angles get bigger in an enlargement; the angles stay the same. Four: thinking the space inside doubles when the sides double; it becomes 4 times as big.

The questions for this section are with you now: comparing shapes and their enlargements.

Base 4, height 3, times 3: base 12, height 9.

## Subtopic: Same Number for Every Side

6 by 4 is an enlargement of 3 by 2. 6 by 3 is not.

Double the sides, four times the squares.

Pocket summary of the lesson. An enlargement or a reduction changes the size but not the shape. Multiply every side by the same number; do not add. The angles stay exactly the same. On a grid, count the squares along each side and multiply or divide. To check, divide each new side by the old side: every answer must be the same. When the sides double, the space inside becomes four times as big.
