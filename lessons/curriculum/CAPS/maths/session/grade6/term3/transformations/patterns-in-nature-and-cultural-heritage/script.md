# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers describing patterns in nature, in our cultural heritage and in modern everyday life, using the words lines, 2D shapes, 3D objects, symmetry, translation, reflection and rotation. It builds on lines of symmetry and on transformations and tessellations from the previous lessons. The anchor is a Grade 6 class trip in Mpumalanga. In the morning the class walks a nature trail past a beehive, spider webs, proteas and a rock pool with starfish. In the afternoon they visit a Ndebele cultural village with painted walls and beadwork, and see a Basotho hat and woven baskets in the craft shop. On the bus home they spot patterns in brick walls, wheels and logos. The governing idea is that the maths of patterns is the same everywhere: a pattern is made of shapes that are repeated by sliding, flipping or turning, and we can describe any pattern by naming its shapes, its transformations and its symmetry. Recognising and describing these features in real patterns is examinable.

## Subtopic: Patterns in Nature

Nature is full of shapes and symmetry, although it is rarely perfect.

Many leaves and every butterfly have one line of symmetry down the middle. The left half is roughly a reflection of the right half.

The starfish in the rock pool has five arms. It has five lines of symmetry, one through each arm. It also has rotation in its pattern: if you turn it a fifth of a full turn about its centre, it looks the same as before.

The beehive is a tessellation of regular hexagons. Bees build their cells this way because hexagons fit together with no gaps, so no wax is wasted.

A spider web has straight threads running out from the centre like the spokes of a wheel, and threads going around that form polygons, one inside another.

A snowflake usually has six arms and six lines of symmetry. The protea flower and the sunflower have petals and seeds arranged around a centre, turning a little each time, which is a kind of rotation.

Animals show patterns too. A zebra's stripes are roughly reflected across the middle of its back. The shell of a tortoise is covered in shapes that are close to hexagons and pentagons fitted together. A snail's shell turns around and around in a spiral, growing a little bigger with each turn.

Look closely at a pineapple or a pine cone, and you will see the same kind of spiral pattern, with scales arranged in rows that curve around the fruit.

When you describe a natural pattern, say what shapes you see, whether it has line symmetry and how many lines, and whether it is repeated by sliding, flipping or turning.

## Subtopic: Patterns in Our Cultural Heritage

South Africa has a rich tradition of geometric art.

Ndebele wall paintings use bold black outlines filled with bright colours. The designs are made of straight lines, rectangles, triangles and steps. Many designs have a vertical line of symmetry down the middle, so the left side is a reflection of the right side. Borders along the bottom of a wall repeat the same design again and again; each copy is a translation of the one before it.

Zulu and Ndebele beadwork uses small triangles, diamonds and stripes. A diamond is a rhombus, and a row of diamonds is often made by translation. Triangles that point up and down in turn are made by reflection or by a half-turn rotation.

Litema is a Basotho tradition of patterns pressed into the clay walls of houses. Many litema designs are made of square tiles, each turned a quarter turn from its neighbour, so they use rotation and reflection.

The Basotho hat is a 3D object, a cone, with a knob on top. A woven grass basket seen from above shows rings around a centre, like concentric circles.

These patterns are part of our heritage, passed from one generation to the next. Ndebele women traditionally paint the walls of their homes, and the patterns can show important events, such as a wedding. The artists plan their designs carefully, using symmetry and repeated shapes, which shows that geometry has been part of South African art for a very long time.

## Subtopic: Patterns in Everyday Life

Patterns are all around us in modern life too. A brick wall is a tessellation of rectangles made by translation. A tiled floor in a shopping centre may use squares, or octagons and squares together. A car wheel or hubcap looks the same after a small turn, so it shows rotation. Many company logos have line symmetry, or have three or four identical parts arranged around a centre by rotation. Road markings, such as the white stripes of a pedestrian crossing, are translations of one rectangle.

Even the things you use every day are full of patterns. The squares on a chessboard are translated in rows, with the colours changing each time. The keys on a keyboard are translations of one small square. A soccer ball is a 3D object covered with pentagons and hexagons fitted together.

To describe any pattern well, use three steps. Name the shapes: lines, triangles, squares, hexagons, circles, or 3D objects such as cones and cylinders. Name the transformation that repeats them: translation, reflection or rotation. Then describe the symmetry: how many lines of symmetry, and whether the pattern looks the same after a turn.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: calling a row of identical triangles that all point the same way reflections; they face the same way, so they are translations. Two: saying a starfish has one line of symmetry; it has five, one through each arm. Three: calling the Basotho hat a triangle; it is a 3D object, a cone, and only its side view looks like a triangle. Four: saying a pattern is symmetrical because it is colourful and beautiful; use the fold test to check.
