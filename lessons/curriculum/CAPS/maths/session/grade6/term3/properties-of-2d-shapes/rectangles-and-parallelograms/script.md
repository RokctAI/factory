# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the properties of rectangles and parallelograms, the similarities and differences between them, and how they relate to squares, rhombuses, trapeziums and kites. It builds on naming polygons and on regular and irregular shapes from the previous lesson. The anchor is Mr Dlamini's workshop in Soweto. He builds a rectangular bookshelf frame that is 80 cm wide and 50 cm high, and a folding security gate made of crossing metal strips that opens and closes. When the bookshelf frame is not braced, it leans to one side, and the rectangle becomes a parallelogram. The governing idea is that a parallelogram is any quadrilateral with two pairs of parallel sides, and a rectangle is a special parallelogram whose angles are all right angles. Describing rectangles and parallelograms by their sides and angles, saying how they are the same and different, and naming special quadrilaterals are examinable.

## Subtopic: Properties of Rectangles

First, a key word. Parallel lines are straight lines that stay the same distance apart all the way along and never meet, however far you extend them. Railway tracks and the lines on a page of a writing book are parallel. On a drawing, parallel sides are marked with matching small arrows.

A rectangle is a quadrilateral with four right angles. Because of those right angles, its opposite sides are parallel: the top is parallel to the bottom and the left side is parallel to the right side. Its opposite sides are also equal in length. Mr Dlamini's bookshelf frame is 80 cm wide at the top and 80 cm wide at the bottom, and 50 cm high on both sides.

So a rectangle has two pairs of parallel sides, two pairs of equal opposite sides and four right angles. If you draw both diagonals, from corner to opposite corner, they are equal in length.

A square has all these properties, and its four sides are also equal. So a square is a special rectangle.

To check that a corner is a right angle, hold the square corner of a book or a set square against it. If it fits exactly, it is a right angle. Builders do the same check with a tool called a square.

Mr Dlamini also checks his frame by measuring the two diagonals. If both diagonals are the same length, the frame is a true rectangle. If one is longer than the other, the frame is leaning a little and the corners are not quite square. This is a trick that builders and carpenters use every day.

## Subtopic: Properties of Parallelograms

A parallelogram is a quadrilateral with two pairs of parallel sides. Its opposite sides are equal in length, just like a rectangle's. Its opposite angles are equal. But its angles do not have to be right angles. A parallelogram that is not a rectangle has two acute angles, smaller than a right angle, and two obtuse angles, bigger than a right angle. The acute angles are opposite each other, and so are the obtuse angles.

Watch what happens to the bookshelf frame before it is braced. Mr Dlamini pushes the top sideways and the frame leans. Each side keeps its length: the top and bottom are still 80 cm and the sides are still 50 cm. The top is still parallel to the bottom and the sides are still parallel to each other. But the right angles have gone. Two corners have closed up into acute angles and two have opened into obtuse angles. The rectangle has become a parallelogram.

The security gate does the same thing. Its metal strips cross to make diamond shapes. As the gate closes, the diamonds squash into thinner and thinner parallelograms, but each strip keeps its length.

This shows that every rectangle is a parallelogram, because it has two pairs of parallel sides. But not every parallelogram is a rectangle, because most parallelograms do not have right angles. To stop the shelf from leaning, Mr Dlamini nails a diagonal brace across the back. That makes triangles, which cannot be pushed out of shape.

You can draw a parallelogram on grid paper. Draw a bottom side 5 squares long. Go up 3 squares and 2 squares to the right, and draw a top side 5 squares long. Join the ends. Both pairs of sides are parallel, because each pair follows the same path across the grid.

## Subtopic: Comparing Quadrilaterals

Here is how rectangles and parallelograms compare. The same: both have four sides, two pairs of parallel sides and opposite sides equal; in both, opposite angles are equal. Different: a rectangle always has four right angles, while a parallelogram usually has two acute and two obtuse angles.

Other quadrilaterals fit into the same family. A rhombus is a parallelogram with all four sides equal. Each diamond in the security gate is a rhombus. A square is both a rectangle and a rhombus: four right angles and four equal sides. A trapezium has exactly one pair of parallel sides. A kite has two pairs of equal sides that are next to each other, not opposite, and it usually has no parallel sides.

A good way to sort them is to ask questions in order. How many pairs of parallel sides? Two pairs means a parallelogram, one pair means a trapezium. Are there right angles? Are all the sides equal?

The error museum, four exhibits. One: saying a parallelogram has four right angles; only a rectangle does, and a parallelogram usually has two acute and two obtuse angles. Two: saying a rectangle is not a parallelogram; it has two pairs of parallel sides, so it is a special parallelogram. Three: saying a square is not a rectangle; it has four right angles and opposite sides equal, so it is a special rectangle. Four: saying all four angles of a parallelogram are equal; only the opposite angles are equal.
