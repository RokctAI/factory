### subtopic_1 — Naming Polygons
**Assistant:** Why is a rectangle not a regular polygon when all its angles are right angles?

**Tutor:** Because a regular polygon needs two things to be true at the same time: all the sides must be equal, and all the angles must be equal. A rectangle passes the angle test, because all four of its angles are right angles. But it fails the side test. The shop window in Durban is 6 m wide and 3 m high, so two of its sides are 6 m and two are 3 m. The sides are not all equal, so the rectangle is irregular. A square passes both tests: four equal sides and four right angles. That is why a square is the regular quadrilateral. The opposite can happen too. A rhombus has four equal sides, so it passes the side test. But its angles are not all equal, because two are small and two are big, so it fails the angle test and is irregular. Whenever you decide whether a shape is regular, check the sides and then check the angles. Only if both are all equal is the shape regular. A good example of a regular shape you see every day is a stop sign, a regular octagon.

### subtopic_2 — Regular and Irregular Polygons
**Assistant:** How can an L-shape be a hexagon when it does not look like one?

**Tutor:** Because a polygon's name depends only on how many sides it has, not on what it looks like. Most pictures of hexagons show a regular hexagon, like a honeycomb cell, with six equal sides and six equal angles. So we start to think that only that shape is a hexagon. But the word hexagon simply means a polygon with six sides. Take the L-shaped paving area. Start at one corner and walk around the edge, counting each straight side. Along the bottom is one side. Up the right is two. Across the step is three. Up again is four. Across the top is five. Down the left back to the start is six. So the L-shape has six sides and six vertices, which makes it a hexagon. Its sides are different lengths, so it is an irregular hexagon. The same is true for other polygons. A pentagon does not have to look like a house shape, and an octagon does not have to look like a stop sign. Always count the sides carefully, and put your finger on each one so that you do not miss any.

### subtopic_3 — Describing, Sorting and Comparing Shapes
**Assistant:** What is the best way to describe a shape so someone else can draw it?

**Tutor:** Give three kinds of information: the number of sides, which sides are equal, and the sizes of the angles. Imagine describing the kite in the sky to a friend on the phone. If you only say it has four sides, your friend might draw a square, a rectangle or any other quadrilateral. Add that it has two pairs of equal sides, and that the equal sides are next to each other, not opposite. Now your friend knows it is a kite and not a parallelogram. Then add something about the angles: one pair of opposite angles is equal, and none of the angles is a right angle. Now the picture is clear. Using the correct words helps. Say vertex for a corner, side for an edge of a flat shape, right angle for a square corner, and regular or irregular. In drawings, mark equal sides with matching dashes and right angles with a small square. These marks make a description quick and exact. When you sort shapes, use the same three properties to decide which group each shape belongs in.
