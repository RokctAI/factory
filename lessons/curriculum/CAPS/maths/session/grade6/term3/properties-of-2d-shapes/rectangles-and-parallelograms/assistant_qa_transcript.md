### subtopic_1 — Properties of Rectangles
**Assistant:** How can a rectangle be a parallelogram if they look so different?

**Tutor:** Because a parallelogram is defined by its parallel sides, not by its angles. A parallelogram is any quadrilateral with two pairs of parallel sides. Check a rectangle against that rule. Mr Dlamini's bookshelf frame has a top and bottom that are parallel, and two sides that are parallel. That is two pairs of parallel sides, so it passes the test. A rectangle is a parallelogram that happens to have four right angles. It is a special kind, just as a square is a special kind of rectangle. Think of it like groups of people. All Grade 6 learners are learners, but not all learners are in Grade 6. In the same way, all rectangles are parallelograms, but not all parallelograms are rectangles. When most people draw a parallelogram, they draw a slanted one, because it shows the parallel sides clearly without any right angles. That is why it looks different. But if you push that slanted parallelogram upright until its angles are right angles, it becomes a rectangle, and it never stops being a parallelogram along the way.

### subtopic_2 — Properties of Parallelograms
**Assistant:** What happens to the shelf frame when Mr Dlamini pushes it sideways?

**Tutor:** The side lengths stay the same, the sides stay parallel, but the angles change. The frame is made of four wooden pieces joined at the corners with single nails, so the corners can turn. When he pushes the top to the side, the top and bottom pieces are still 80 cm long and the two side pieces are still 50 cm long, because wood does not stretch. The top is still parallel to the bottom, and the two sides are still parallel to each other. So the frame is still a parallelogram. But the corners are no longer right angles. Two of them close up and become acute, and the other two open out and become obtuse. The frame has stopped being a rectangle. It also gets lower, because the sides are leaning. This is why builders brace frames with a diagonal piece. The diagonal divides the frame into two triangles. A triangle cannot change shape if its sides keep their lengths, so the brace holds the frame rigid and keeps its corners square. You can test this at home with four strips of card and paper fasteners.

### subtopic_3 — Comparing Quadrilaterals
**Assistant:** How do I tell a trapezium, a kite and a parallelogram apart?

**Tutor:** Look for parallel sides first, then for equal sides. A parallelogram has two pairs of parallel sides: the top is parallel to the bottom, and the left side is parallel to the right side. Its opposite sides are equal. A trapezium has exactly one pair of parallel sides. Picture the side view of a bucket or a lampshade: the top and bottom are parallel, but the two slanted sides are not parallel, because they get closer together as they go down. A kite usually has no parallel sides at all. Instead, it has two pairs of equal sides, and the equal sides are next to each other, not opposite. The two short sides meet at the top and the two long sides meet at the bottom, like the kites flown at the beach. So a quick test is to count the pairs of parallel sides. Two pairs means a parallelogram, which includes rectangles, rhombuses and squares. One pair means a trapezium. None, with equal sides next to each other, means a kite. Use small arrows on your drawing to mark parallel sides as you check.
