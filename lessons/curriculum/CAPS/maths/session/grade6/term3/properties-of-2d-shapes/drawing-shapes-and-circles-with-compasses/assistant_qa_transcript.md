### subtopic_1 — Drawing Shapes on Grid Paper
**Assistant:** What is the difference between the radius and the diameter?

**Tutor:** The radius is the distance from the centre of a circle to its edge. The diameter is the distance right across the circle, from one side of the edge to the other, passing through the centre. Picture a bicycle wheel. Each spoke goes from the hub in the middle to the rim, so each spoke is like a radius. If you take two spokes that point in exactly opposite directions, together they stretch right across the wheel; that is a diameter. So a diameter is made of two radii, and it is always twice as long as the radius. If the radius is 4 cm, the diameter is 8 cm. If the diameter is 10 cm, the radius is 5 cm. This matters when you use compasses, because the opening of the compasses is always the radius. If a question asks for a circle with a diameter of 8 cm, open the compasses to 4 cm, not 8 cm. A good habit is to read the question twice and underline the word radius or diameter before you open the compasses. Then halve the diameter if you need to.

### subtopic_2 — Circles and Compasses
**Assistant:** How do I stop my compasses from slipping when I draw a circle?

**Tutor:** There are a few simple tricks. First, make sure the hinge at the top is firm, so the arms do not open or close while you turn. If they move, the radius changes and the circle will not join up. Second, press the point gently into the paper so that it bites. It helps to have a few sheets of paper or a piece of cardboard underneath, so the point has something soft to grip. Third, hold the compasses only by the small handle at the top, not by the arms. Holding the arms squeezes them and changes the opening. Fourth, lean the compasses slightly in the direction you are turning, and turn in one smooth movement rather than in little jerks. Many people find it easier to turn the paper slowly with the other hand while the compasses stay still. Finally, keep your pencil sharp, so that the line is thin and exact. When you finish, the end of the line should meet the start exactly. If it does not, check whether the arms moved, reset the radius with your ruler and try again.

### subtopic_3 — Patterns in and with Circles
**Assistant:** Why does the radius fit around the circle exactly six times?

**Tutor:** Because of the triangles it makes. When you put the compass point on the circle and draw an arc through the centre, the arc crosses the circle at a point that is exactly one radius from where you started. Join that point, the starting point and the centre of the circle. All three sides of that triangle are one radius long: two of them go from the centre to the edge, and the third is the step you just made. So it is an equilateral triangle. Six equilateral triangles fit together perfectly around a point, just like six slices of a cake cut exactly the same. That is why six steps of the radius take you all the way around the circle and back to the start. If you join the six points with straight lines, you get a regular hexagon, made of those six equilateral triangles, with each side equal to the radius. This is also why the cells in a honeycomb are hexagons: they fit together with no gaps. The flower pattern shows this hidden hexagon in a beautiful way, and it was used in many old designs, from tiles to fabric.
