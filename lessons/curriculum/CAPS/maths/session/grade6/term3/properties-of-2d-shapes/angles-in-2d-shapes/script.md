# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers recognising and naming acute, right, obtuse, straight and reflex angles and revolutions, finding these angles inside 2D shapes, and describing, sorting and comparing shapes by the size of their angles. It builds on right angles and on the properties of rectangles and parallelograms from the previous lessons. The anchor is Sizwe at a skate park in Cape Town. He does quarter turns, half turns and full spins on his skateboard, rides a gentle ramp and a steep one, and checks the time on the big clock above the gate. The skate park also has an L-shaped deck and railings made of triangles and parallelograms. The governing idea is that an angle measures an amount of turn, and that we name an angle by comparing it with two benchmarks: the right angle, which is a quarter turn, and the straight angle, which is a half turn. Naming the six kinds of angles, recognising them in shapes and in everyday life, and sorting shapes by their angles are examinable.

## Subtopic: Naming Angles

An angle is made where two straight arms meet at a point called the vertex. The size of the angle is the amount of turn from one arm to the other.

When Sizwe turns a quarter of the way around, he turns through a right angle. A right angle is a square corner, like the corner of a page. When he turns halfway around, so that he faces the opposite way, he turns through a straight angle. The two arms make a straight line. When he spins all the way around and faces the same way again, he turns through a revolution, a full turn. A revolution is four right angles, or two straight angles.

The other angles are named by where they fit between these benchmarks. An acute angle is smaller than a right angle. The gentle ramp meets the ground at an acute angle. An obtuse angle is bigger than a right angle but smaller than a straight angle. A door opened wide, but not flat against the wall, makes an obtuse angle. A reflex angle is bigger than a straight angle but smaller than a revolution. If Sizwe turns three quarters of the way around, he turns through a reflex angle.

So in order from smallest to biggest: acute, right, obtuse, straight, reflex, revolution.

The clock helps. At 3 o'clock the hands make a right angle. At 6 o'clock they make a straight angle. At 1 o'clock they make an acute angle. At 5 o'clock they make an obtuse angle. The other side of each of these, going the long way around, is a reflex angle.

Look for angles around you. The corner of a desk is a right angle. A pair of scissors opened a little makes an acute angle. A laptop screen pushed back past upright makes an obtuse angle. The minute hand of a clock sweeps through a full revolution every hour.

## Subtopic: Angles Inside 2D Shapes

Every polygon has an angle inside it at each vertex. Name each one by comparing it with a right angle.

A square and a rectangle have four right angles. An equilateral triangle has three acute angles. The triangles in the railings each have one right angle and two acute angles. A triangle can have at most one right angle or one obtuse angle, never two, because the three angles of a triangle together make only a straight angle.

A parallelogram that is not a rectangle has two acute angles and two obtuse angles. A regular hexagon, like a honeycomb cell, has six obtuse angles. A regular octagon, like a stop sign, has eight obtuse angles.

Some shapes have a reflex angle inside them. The L-shaped deck has six sides. Five of its inside angles are right angles, but at the inside corner, where the L turns in, the angle is bigger than a straight angle. That is a reflex angle. An arrowhead shape, like a dart, also has one reflex angle inside it. Shapes like these look as if a piece has been pushed in.

To test angles, make a right-angle tester. Fold a piece of paper in half, then fold it in half again so that the folded edges meet. The corner is a right angle. Hold it in the corner of a shape. If the shape's angle is smaller, it is acute. If it is bigger, it is obtuse or bigger.

## Subtopic: Comparing and Sorting by Angles

To compare two angles, look at the amount of turn, not the length of the arms. An angle drawn with arms 2 cm long and the same angle drawn with arms 10 cm long are equal. The longer arms just make the angle easier to see.

We can sort shapes by their angles. Shapes with only right angles: squares and rectangles. Shapes with only acute angles: equilateral triangles. Shapes with only obtuse angles: regular pentagons, regular hexagons and regular octagons. Shapes with at least one reflex angle: the L-shape and the arrowhead.

When you describe a shape, add its angles to its sides. A full description of the deck says it is an irregular hexagon with five right angles and one reflex angle.

You can also compare angles in two shapes. The angles of a regular octagon are bigger than the angles of a regular hexagon, which are bigger than the angles of a square. The more sides a regular polygon has, the bigger each of its angles.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: saying an angle with longer arms is bigger; the size is the turn, not the arm length. Two: saying a straight line cannot be an angle; a half turn makes a straight angle. Three: calling the inside corner of the L-shape a right angle by looking at the outside of the shape; inside the shape, that angle is reflex. Four: saying a triangle can have two right angles; the three angles together make only a straight angle, so there is room for at most one.

## Subtopic: Six Kinds of Turn

Quarter turn: right angle. Half turn: straight angle. Full turn: revolution.

Smaller than right: acute. Between right and straight: obtuse.

Between straight and full: reflex.
