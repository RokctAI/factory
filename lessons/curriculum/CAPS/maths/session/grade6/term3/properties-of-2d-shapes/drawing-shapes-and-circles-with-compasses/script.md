# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers drawing 2D shapes accurately on grid paper, naming the parts of a circle, drawing circles of a given radius or diameter with a pair of compasses, and drawing patterns in and with circles. It builds on the properties of polygons and quadrilaterals from the previous lessons. The anchor is a Grade 6 class in East London designing a mural for their school wall. They plan it on grid paper first: a square, a rectangle, a right-angled triangle and a parallelogram along the bottom, a target of circles in one corner, and a large six-petal flower in the middle. The governing idea is that grid lines give us straight sides and right angles for free, and that a pair of compasses keeps every point of a circle the same distance from the centre, so the opening of the compasses is the radius. Drawing shapes on grids, using compasses to draw circles of a given size, and drawing circle patterns are examinable.

## Subtopic: Drawing Shapes on Grid Paper

Grid paper is covered in small squares. Its lines are straight, they are parallel in each direction, and they cross at right angles. That makes it ideal for drawing polygons accurately.

To draw a square with sides of 4 units, start at a point where two grid lines cross. Count 4 spaces to the right along a line and mark the point. Count 4 spaces up from both points and mark them. Join the four points with a ruler. All the corners are right angles, because the grid lines cross at right angles. Inside the square there are 4 rows of 4 small squares, which is 16 small squares.

Count spaces, not dots. A side 4 units long goes from one dot to the dot 4 spaces away, so it touches 5 dots. If you count only 4 dots, your side will be just 3 units long.

A rectangle 6 units long and 3 units high is drawn the same way. A right-angled triangle is easy too: draw one side along a horizontal line, one side up a vertical line from the same point, then join the two ends with a slanting line.

For a parallelogram, draw a bottom side 5 units long. Then draw the top side 5 units long as well, but start it 2 units further to the right and 3 units higher. Join the ends. The slanting sides are parallel because each one goes across 2 and up 3.

## Subtopic: Circles and Compasses

A circle is a round shape where every point on the edge is the same distance from the centre. The centre is the point in the middle. The radius is the distance from the centre to the edge. The diameter is a straight line from the edge to the edge, passing through the centre. A diameter is made of two radii, so the diameter is twice the radius. The edge of the circle is called the circumference.

A pair of compasses has two arms joined at the top. One arm has a sharp point and the other holds a pencil. The distance between the point and the pencil is the radius of the circle you will draw.

To draw a circle with a radius of 4 cm, lay the compasses on a ruler. Put the point on 0 and open the arms until the pencil tip is on 4 cm. Now put the point on the paper where you want the centre, press it down gently and hold the compasses by the top. Turn it all the way around, leaning it slightly in the direction you are turning, so that the pencil draws the circle without the arms moving. The circle has a radius of 4 cm and a diameter of 8 cm.

If a question gives you the diameter, halve it first. For a circle with a diameter of 12 cm, open the compasses to 6 cm.

The questions for this section are with you now: the parts of a circle and drawing circles with compasses.

## Subtopic: Patterns in and with Circles

The target in the corner of the mural is made of concentric circles. Concentric circles share the same centre but have different radii. Keep the point in the same hole and draw circles with radii of 1 cm, 2 cm and 3 cm. The gap between each circle and the next is 1 cm. The biggest circle has a diameter of 6 cm.

The six-petal flower uses one opening of the compasses all the way through. Draw a circle with a radius of 3 cm. Without changing the opening, put the point anywhere on the circle and draw an arc inside the circle, from one side of the circle to the other. It passes through the centre. Now put the point where the arc meets the circle and draw another arc. Keep stepping around. The radius fits around the circle exactly six times, and the arcs make six petals.

The six points where the arcs meet the circle are equally spaced. Join them with a ruler and you get a regular hexagon, with every side equal to the radius.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: opening the compasses to the diameter when the radius was asked for; the circle comes out twice as wide. Two: saying a circle with a diameter of 8 cm has a radius of 16 cm; the radius is half the diameter, so it is 4 cm. Three: letting the point slip or the arms move while turning; the circle does not close up, so press the point in and hold the compasses by the top. Four: counting dots instead of spaces on grid paper; 4 dots make a side only 3 units long, so count 4 spaces.

The questions for this section are with you now: drawing circle patterns.
