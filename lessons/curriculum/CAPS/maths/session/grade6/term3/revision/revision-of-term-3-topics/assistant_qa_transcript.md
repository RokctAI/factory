### subtopic_1 — Length, Perimeter, Area and Volume
**Assistant:** How do I remember the difference between perimeter, area and volume?

**Tutor:** Think about what each one measures and the unit that goes with it. Perimeter is a distance around the edge, like the fence around the sandpit. It is a length, so it is measured in metres or centimetres. For the 6 m by 4 m sandpit, you add the sides: 2 times 10, which is 20 m. Area is the flat space inside a shape, like the sand on the surface of the sandpit. You measure it by counting squares, so it is in square metres or square centimetres. For a rectangle, multiply the length by the width: 6 times 4 is 24 square metres. Volume is the space a 3D object takes up, like the space inside the storage box. You measure it by counting cubes, so it is in cubic metres or cubic centimetres. For a rectangular prism, multiply the length, the width and the height: 2 times 1 times 1 is 2 cubic metres. So perimeter adds, area multiplies two lengths, and volume multiplies three. A quick check of the unit at the end of your answer will often catch a mix-up between them.

### subtopic_2 — 2D Shapes, Angles and Symmetry
**Assistant:** How do I check whether a shape is regular and how many lines of symmetry it has?

**Tutor:** For regular, check two things: are all the sides equal, and are all the angles equal? Both must be true. The stop sign passes both tests, so it is a regular octagon. The sandpit has four right angles, so its angles pass, but its sides are 6 m and 4 m, so it fails the side test and is irregular. For lines of symmetry, imagine folding the shape so that one half lands exactly on the other. Try every possible fold: down the middle, across the middle and along each diagonal. The sandpit folds neatly down the middle and across the middle, but folding along a diagonal makes the corners stick out, so it has only 2 lines of symmetry. A square passes all four folds, so it has 4. For regular polygons there is a shortcut: the number of lines of symmetry is the same as the number of sides. A regular hexagon has 6 and the stop sign has 8. A circle has more than you can count, because every diameter works. A parallelogram that is not a rectangle or a rhombus has none, because no fold makes the halves match.

### subtopic_3 — Transformations and 3D Objects
**Assistant:** How do I count the faces, vertices and edges of the climbing frame?

**Tutor:** Count in an organised way, one group at a time, and do not forget the parts at the back or the bottom. The climbing frame is a square-based pyramid. Start with the faces. There is the square base on the ground, and one triangle rising from each of its four sides. That is 1 plus 4, which is 5 faces. Next, the vertices. There are 4 corners around the square base and 1 at the very top, the apex. That is 5 vertices. Finally, the edges. There are 4 edges around the base and 4 edges running from the base corners up to the apex. That is 8 edges. So the builders need 8 metal poles, one for each edge, and 5 joints, one for each vertex. You can check with the pyramid patterns from this term: in any pyramid, the number of faces equals the number of vertices, and the number of edges is double the number of sides of the base. The base is a square with 4 sides, so there are 8 edges. The same careful counting works for prisms: count the top, then the bottom, then the parts that join them.
