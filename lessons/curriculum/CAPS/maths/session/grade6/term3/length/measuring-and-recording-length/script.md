# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers estimating and measuring lengths using rulers, metre sticks, tape measures and trundle wheels, recording lengths in millimetres, centimetres, metres and kilometres, and comparing and ordering lengths. It builds on measuring length in earlier grades and on decimals from Term 2. The anchor is a Grade 6 class in Rustenburg planning a school fun run. They measure an eraser that is 45 mm long, a desk that is 1,2 m long, a classroom that is 8,4 m long, and a fun run route of 2,5 km around the school grounds and nearby streets. The governing idea is that you choose the unit and the instrument that fit the size of the thing you measure, you start measuring at zero, you always write the unit, and you change lengths into the same unit before you compare them. Choosing units and instruments, estimating lengths, reading scales accurately, recording lengths with units, and comparing and ordering lengths are examinable.

## Subtopic: Choosing Units and Instruments

We measure length in four main units. Millimetres, written mm, are for very small lengths, like the thickness of a coin or the length of an eraser. Centimetres, written cm, are for things you can hold, like a pencil or a book. Metres, written m, are for rooms, desks, people and sports fields. Kilometres, written km, are for long distances, like the fun run route or the road from Rustenburg to Pretoria.

The units are linked. 10 mm make 1 cm. 100 cm make 1 m. 1 000 m make 1 km.

Each unit has a good instrument. A 30 cm ruler measures short, straight lengths in centimetres and millimetres. A metre stick measures longer straight lengths, like the width of a door. A tape measure bends, so it can measure curved lengths, like your waist or the outside of a tin, and it can stretch for several metres. A trundle wheel is a wheel on a handle that measures long distances on the ground. Each time it goes around once, it rolls 1 m and makes a click. You count the clicks.

Before measuring, estimate. Use benchmarks, which are lengths you already know. The width of your little fingernail is about 1 cm. A big step is about 1 m. A classroom door is about 2 m high. Walking 1 km takes about 12 to 15 minutes. If the class estimates the classroom at about 8 big steps, they expect about 8 m.

## Subtopic: Measuring and Recording Accurately

On a ruler, the numbered marks are centimetres and the small marks between them are millimetres. There are 10 small spaces in each centimetre.

To measure the eraser, put one end exactly on the 0 mark, not on the edge of the ruler, because many rulers have a gap before 0. The other end reaches the fifth small mark after 4. So the eraser is 4 cm and 5 mm long. We can record this as 45 mm, because 4 cm is 40 mm and 40 plus 5 is 45. We can also record it as 4,5 cm, because 5 mm is 5 tenths of a centimetre.

If you cannot start at 0, subtract. A pencil that starts at the 1 cm mark and ends at the 15 cm mark is 14 cm long, not 15 cm.

The desk is longer than one metre stick. The class lays the metre stick down once, marks the end, and then measures 20 cm more. The desk is 1 m and 20 cm, which is 1,2 m or 120 cm.

For the route, a learner pushes the trundle wheel and counts the clicks. One straight part of the route gives 84 clicks, so it is 84 m long. The whole route gives 2 500 clicks, so it is 2 500 m, which is 2,5 km.

Always write the unit. A record that says only 45 could mean 45 mm, 45 cm or 45 m, and those are very different lengths.

## Subtopic: Comparing and Ordering Lengths

To compare lengths, first change them into the same unit. Which is longer, the desk at 1,2 m or a bookshelf at 115 cm? It is tempting to say 115 is bigger than 1,2. But 1,2 m is 120 cm, and 120 cm is more than 115 cm, so the desk is longer.

To order lengths, change them all into the smallest unit in the list. Order 2 m, 150 cm and 1 800 mm from shortest to longest. In millimetres they are 2 000 mm, 1 500 mm and 1 800 mm. So the order is 150 cm, then 1 800 mm, then 2 m.

Another example: 45 mm, 5 cm and 4,2 cm. In millimetres they are 45 mm, 50 mm and 42 mm. From longest to shortest: 5 cm, 45 mm, 4,2 cm.

For long distances, compare in metres. Is a 3 km route longer than a 2 900 m route? 3 km is 3 000 m, so yes, by 100 m.

The class also compares the four parts of the route. The track is 400 m, the path past the hall is 0,6 km, the road section is 1 100 m and the last stretch is 0,4 km. In metres these are 400 m, 600 m, 1 100 m and 400 m. The road section is the longest, and the track and the last stretch are equal. Together they make 2 500 m, the full route.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: placing the end of an object at the edge of the ruler instead of at 0; on many rulers this adds a few millimetres, so line the object up with 0. Two: writing 4 cm 5 mm as 4,05 cm; 5 mm is 5 tenths of a centimetre, so it is 4,5 cm. Three: saying 115 cm is longer than 1,2 m because 115 is bigger than 1,2; change to the same unit, and 1,2 m is 120 cm. Four: writing a length with no unit; always write mm, cm, m or km.

The questions for this section are with you now: comparing and ordering lengths in different units.

## Subtopic: Pick the Right Tool

Small things: mm or cm, with a ruler.

Rooms and desks: m, with a metre stick or tape measure.

Long routes: km, with a trundle wheel.

Keep the right tool in mind and try the questions for this part: units and instruments.

## Subtopic: Start at Zero

Line up one end with the 0 mark.

4 cm and 5 mm is 45 mm, or 4,5 cm.

Always write the unit.
