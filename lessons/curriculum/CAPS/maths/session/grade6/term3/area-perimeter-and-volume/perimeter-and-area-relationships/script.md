# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers investigating the relationship between the perimeter and the area of rectangles and squares, and between the surface area and the volume of rectangular prisms. It builds on perimeter, area and volume from the previous lessons. The anchor is Mr Botha, a farmer in the Karoo, who has exactly 24 m of fencing for a rectangular chicken run, and a small gift shop in his town that folds gift boxes from cardboard. The governing idea is that perimeter and area are different measurements that do not change together: rectangles with the same perimeter can have very different areas, and rectangles with the same area can have very different perimeters. In the same way, boxes with the same volume can need very different amounts of cardboard. Investigating these relationships with tables, finding surface area and comparing shapes are examinable.

## Subtopic: Same Perimeter, Different Areas

Mr Botha has 24 m of fencing. Every rectangle he makes must have a perimeter of 24 m, so the length and the width must add up to half of 24, which is 12 m. Let us try whole numbers in a table.

A run 1 m wide and 11 m long has an area of 11 square metres. 2 m by 10 m gives 20 square metres. 3 m by 9 m gives 27 square metres. 4 m by 8 m gives 32 square metres. 5 m by 7 m gives 35 square metres. 6 m by 6 m gives 36 square metres.

Every one of these runs uses exactly 24 m of fencing, but the areas are very different. The long, thin run gives the chickens only 11 square metres, while the square gives them 36 square metres, more than three times as much.

The pattern is clear: the closer the rectangle is to a square, the bigger its area. For a fixed perimeter, the square has the biggest area. So Mr Botha builds a 6 m by 6 m square run.

If he builds the run against the wall of his barn, the wall can be one side, and the fencing only needs to cover the other three sides. Then 24 m of fencing can make a run 6 m deep and 12 m along the wall, with an area of 72 square metres.

The questions for this section are with you now: rectangles with the same perimeter.

## Subtopic: Same Area, Different Perimeters

Now turn the question around. Suppose the chicken run must have an area of exactly 36 square metres. How much fencing is needed? The length times the width must be 36.

A run 1 m by 36 m needs a perimeter of 74 m. 2 m by 18 m needs 40 m. 3 m by 12 m needs 30 m. 4 m by 9 m needs 26 m. 6 m by 6 m needs only 24 m.

The areas are all the same, but the perimeters are very different. Long, thin rectangles have big perimeters, and the square has the smallest perimeter. For a fixed area, the square uses the least fencing, which also makes it the cheapest to fence.

So perimeter and area are not tied together. Knowing one does not tell you the other unless you also know the shape.

What happens when you double every side? A 3 m by 3 m square has a perimeter of 12 m and an area of 9 square metres. Double the sides to 6 m by 6 m: the perimeter doubles to 24 m, but the area becomes 36 square metres, which is 4 times as big. Doubling the sides doubles the perimeter but makes the area 4 times as big.

The questions for this section are with you now: rectangles with the same area.

## Subtopic: Surface Area and Volume

The surface area of a rectangular prism is the total area of all six of its faces. It tells you how much cardboard you need to make a box. The volume tells you how much the box holds.

A gift box is 4 cm long, 3 cm wide and 2 cm high. Its faces come in three matching pairs. The top and bottom are each 4 by 3, which is 12 square centimetres. The front and back are each 4 by 2, which is 8 square centimetres. The two ends are each 3 by 2, which is 6 square centimetres. The surface area is 24 plus 16 plus 12, which is 52 square centimetres. Its volume is 4 times 3 times 2, which is 24 cubic centimetres.

A long thin box 24 cm by 1 cm by 1 cm also has a volume of 24 cubic centimetres. But its surface area is 24 plus 24 plus 24 plus 24 plus 1 plus 1, which is 98 square centimetres. It needs almost twice as much cardboard to hold the same amount.

So, just like rectangles, boxes with the same volume can have different surface areas, and the closer a box is to a cube, the less cardboard it needs.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking rectangles with the same perimeter have the same area; 2 m by 10 m and 6 m by 6 m both have 24 m, but areas of 20 and 36. Two: thinking a bigger area always means a bigger perimeter; the 1 m by 36 m run has the same area as the square but a much bigger perimeter. Three: finding the surface area of the gift box as 12 plus 8 plus 6, which is 26; there are six faces, so the answer is 52. Four: mixing up surface area and volume; surface area is in square units, volume is in cubic units.

The questions for this section are with you now: surface area and volume.
