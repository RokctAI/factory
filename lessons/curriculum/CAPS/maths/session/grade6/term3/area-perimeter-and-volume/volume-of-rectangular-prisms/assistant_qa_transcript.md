### subtopic_1 — Packing and Filling
**Assistant:** Why is the volume of a box the length times the width times the height?

**Tutor:** Because a box fills up in equal layers, and multiplying is the quickest way to count equal layers of equal rows. Take the 5 cm by 4 cm by 3 cm box at the packhouse. Start filling the bottom with 1 cm cubes. Along the length, 5 cubes fit in a row. Along the width, 4 rows fit. So the bottom layer holds 4 rows of 5, which is 20 cubes. That is length times width, the same as the area of the base. Now put a second layer on top. It holds another 20 cubes. The box is 3 cm high, so exactly 3 layers fit. 3 layers of 20 cubes is 60 cubes. So the volume is 60 cubic centimetres. Length times width tells you how many cubes are in one layer, and the height tells you how many layers there are, so length times width times height counts every cube exactly once. It works for any rectangular prism, from a matchbox to a shipping container. And because multiplication can be done in any order, it does not matter which edge you call the length, the width or the height.

### subtopic_2 — Layers and the Rule
**Assistant:** What is the difference between volume and capacity?

**Tutor:** Volume is how much space an object takes up. Capacity is how much a container can hold inside it. A solid wooden block has a volume, because it takes up space, but it has no capacity, because you cannot pour anything into it. A hollow box has both. For a thin box, like a cardboard carton, the volume and the capacity are nearly the same, because the walls are so thin. For a thick-walled container, like a stone trough, the capacity is less than the outside volume, because the walls take up some of the space. We usually measure volume in cubic centimetres or cubic metres, and capacity in millilitres and litres. The two are linked. A cube with edges of 10 cm has a volume of 10 times 10 times 10, which is 1 000 cubic centimetres, and it holds exactly 1 litre of water. So 1 cubic centimetre holds 1 millilitre. That is why the washing tank, which is 100 cm by 50 cm by 40 cm, has a volume of 200 000 cubic centimetres and a capacity of 200 litres.

### subtopic_3 — Packing Problems
**Assistant:** Why can I not just count the cubes I see in a picture?

**Tutor:** Because a picture of a stack of cubes only shows the outside, and many cubes are hidden at the back, at the bottom and in the middle. Look at a drawing of a cube made of smaller cubes, 3 long, 3 wide and 3 high. From the front you see 9 cube faces, from the top you see 9 more, and from the side you see 9 more. But some cubes are counted in two or three views, because they sit on corners and edges. And one cube, right in the very centre, cannot be seen at all. Counting faces in the picture gives a wrong answer. Instead, think in layers. The bottom layer has 3 rows of 3, which is 9 cubes. There are 3 layers, so there are 3 times 9, which is 27 cubes altogether. That includes every hidden cube. The same is true for the crates in the truck. From the back doors you only see the last row, but the rule, 6 times 4 times 3, counts all 72 crates. So whenever you find volume, use the layers and the rule, not just what you can see.
