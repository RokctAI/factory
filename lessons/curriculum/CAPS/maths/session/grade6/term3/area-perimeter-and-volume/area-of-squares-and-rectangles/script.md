# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers finding the area of regular and irregular shapes by counting squares on a grid, developing and using the rule for the area of squares and rectangles, and using area to solve problems with composite shapes, tiles and costs. It builds on perimeter from the previous lesson and on multiplication. The anchor is the Naidoo family in Pietermaritzburg. They re-tile their kitchen floor, which is a rectangle 4 m long and 3 m wide, using square tiles 50 cm on each side, and they plant a new lawn 6 m by 5 m with grass that costs R25 per square metre. They also measure an L-shaped patio, and their daughter traces a leaf on grid paper for a school project. The governing idea is that area is the amount of flat surface inside a shape, measured by counting how many unit squares cover it, and that for a rectangle the count is the number of rows times the number of squares in each row: length times width. Counting squares, estimating irregular areas, using the rule for rectangles and squares, and solving area problems are examinable.

## Subtopic: Counting Squares

Area is the amount of flat surface inside a shape. We measure it by counting how many squares of a fixed size cover the shape with no gaps and no overlaps. Those squares are called square units.

On a grid of 1 cm squares, each square has an area of 1 square centimetre. A shape that covers 15 whole squares has an area of 15 square centimetres. For bigger surfaces like floors, we use square metres: a square 1 m long and 1 m wide.

Area is always measured in square units, never in plain centimetres or metres. Perimeter is a length; area is a number of squares.

When a shape covers some half squares, put two halves together to make one whole. A shape that covers 12 whole squares and 4 half squares has an area of 12 plus 2, which is 14 square centimetres.

Irregular shapes, like the leaf, have curved edges, so the squares at the edge are only partly covered. To estimate the area, count every whole square. Then count each square that is more than half covered as a whole one, and ignore each square that is less than half covered. If the leaf covers 20 whole squares and 9 squares more than half, its area is about 29 square centimetres.

## Subtopic: The Rule for Rectangles and Squares

Draw a rectangle on a grid, 5 squares long and 3 squares wide. Instead of counting all the squares one by one, notice that they sit in rows. There are 3 rows with 5 squares in each row. So the area is 3 times 5, which is 15 square units.

This gives us a rule. The area of a rectangle is the length multiplied by the width.

The Naidoos' kitchen floor is 4 m long and 3 m wide. Its area is 4 times 3, which is 12 square metres. The lawn is 6 m by 5 m, so its area is 30 square metres.

A square has the same length and width, so its area is the side multiplied by itself. A square with sides of 5 cm has an area of 25 square centimetres. A square with sides of 9 m has an area of 81 square metres.

You can work backwards too. A rectangle with an area of 24 square metres and a length of 6 m has a width of 24 divided by 6, which is 4 m. A square with an area of 49 square centimetres has sides of 7 cm, because 7 times 7 is 49.

Different shapes can have the same area. A rectangle 8 cm by 2 cm and a square with sides of 4 cm both have an area of 16 square centimetres.

Before you calculate, estimate. The lawn is a bit more than 5 m by 5 m, so its area should be a bit more than 25 square metres. Our answer of 30 square metres makes sense.

## Subtopic: Using Area to Solve Problems

The L-shaped patio has a bottom side of 10 m, a left side of 8 m, a top side of 4 m and a short right side of 3 m. To find its area, split it into two rectangles. The bottom part is 10 m by 3 m, which is 30 square metres. The top part is 4 m wide and 8 minus 3, which is 5 m, high, so it is 20 square metres. The total area is 50 square metres. Another way: the whole 10 m by 8 m rectangle is 80 square metres, and the missing corner is 6 m by 5 m, which is 30 square metres. 80 minus 30 is also 50 square metres.

Now the tiles. Each tile is 50 cm by 50 cm, so 2 tiles fit along each metre, and 2 rows of 2 tiles fill one square metre. That is 4 tiles per square metre. The kitchen floor is 12 square metres, so the Naidoos need 12 times 4, which is 48 tiles.

The lawn is 30 square metres at R25 per square metre. The grass costs 30 times R25, which is R750.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: adding the length and the width, giving the area of the kitchen as 4 plus 3, which is 7; area is length times width, 12 square metres. Two: writing the area in metres instead of square metres; area always needs square units. Three: counting half squares as whole squares; two halves make one whole. Four: finding the area of the L-shape by multiplying 10 by 8 to get 80; that includes the missing corner, so the area is 50 square metres.
