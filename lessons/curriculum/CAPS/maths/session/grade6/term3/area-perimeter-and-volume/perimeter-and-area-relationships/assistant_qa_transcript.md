### subtopic_1 — Same Perimeter, Different Areas
**Assistant:** How can two rectangles with the same perimeter have different areas?

**Tutor:** Because the perimeter only measures the edge, and the same length of edge can be bent into different shapes that hold different amounts of space. Think of Mr Botha's 24 m of fencing as a long piece of string tied in a loop. If he stretches it into a long, thin rectangle, 1 m wide and 11 m long, the inside is very narrow: just 11 square metres. If he pulls the sides apart into a 6 m by 6 m square, the inside opens up to 36 square metres. The string is the same length every time, so the perimeter is always 24 m, but the area changes a lot. Try it yourself with a loop of string on grid paper. A useful rule comes out of this: for a fixed perimeter, the closer a rectangle is to a square, the bigger its area, and the square gives the biggest area of all. That is why Mr Botha chooses a square run. So never assume that two shapes have the same area just because they have the same perimeter. Always work out each one separately.

### subtopic_2 — Same Area, Different Perimeters
**Assistant:** Why does doubling the sides make the area four times as big?

**Tutor:** Because doubling happens in two directions at once, but the perimeter only goes around the edge once. Take a 3 m by 3 m square. Its perimeter is 12 m, and its area is 9 square metres. Now double each side to make a 6 m by 6 m square. The perimeter is the four sides added up, and each side is twice as long, so the perimeter is twice as long: 24 m. The area is different. The square is now twice as long and twice as wide. Twice as many squares fit in each row, and there are twice as many rows. 2 times 2 is 4, so the area is 4 times as big: 36 square metres. You can see it on grid paper: the big square can be cut into exactly four copies of the small square. This is why a bigger chicken run gives much more space than you might expect. Doubling the fencing gives the chickens four times the room. The same idea from enlargements applies: lengths multiply by the scale, but areas multiply by the scale twice.

### subtopic_3 — Surface Area and Volume
**Assistant:** What is surface area, and how do I find it?

**Tutor:** Surface area is the total area of all the faces of a 3D object. For a box, it is the amount of cardboard you would need to make it, with no overlaps or flaps. A rectangular prism has six faces, in three matching pairs. Find the area of one face from each pair, double each one, and add them. For the 4 cm by 3 cm by 2 cm gift box: the top is 4 by 3, which is 12 square centimetres, and the bottom matches it, so that pair is 24. The front is 4 by 2, which is 8, and the back matches, so that pair is 16. Each end is 3 by 2, which is 6, so that pair is 12. Add the pairs: 24 plus 16 plus 12 is 52 square centimetres. A good check is to draw the net of the box: six rectangles joined together. The area of the net is the surface area. The most common mistake is to add only three faces, the ones you can see in a drawing, and get 26. Remember that the hidden faces at the back, bottom and other end need cardboard too. Surface area is measured in square units, not cubic units.
