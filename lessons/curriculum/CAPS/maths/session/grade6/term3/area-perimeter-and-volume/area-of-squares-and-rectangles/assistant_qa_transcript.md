### subtopic_1 — Counting Squares
**Assistant:** Why is the area of a rectangle the length times the width?

**Tutor:** Because the squares inside a rectangle are arranged in neat rows, and multiplying is a quick way to count equal rows. Draw the Naidoos' kitchen floor on grid paper, 4 squares long and 3 squares wide, where each square stands for one square metre. Look at the bottom row: it has 4 squares, one for each metre of the length. The width is 3 m, so there are 3 rows stacked up. So the total is 3 rows of 4 squares, which is 4 plus 4 plus 4, or 4 times 3, which is 12 squares. Each square is one square metre, so the area is 12 square metres. The length tells you how many squares are in each row, and the width tells you how many rows there are. Multiplying them counts every square exactly once. This works for any rectangle, even a very big one, where counting the squares one by one would take far too long. A soccer field 100 m long and 60 m wide has 60 rows of 100 squares, which is 6 000 square metres. Nobody wants to count those one at a time.

### subtopic_2 — The Rule for Rectangles and Squares
**Assistant:** Why do I write square metres and not just metres for area?

**Tutor:** Because area and length measure different things, and the unit tells the reader which one you mean. A metre is a length: a line one metre long. A square metre is an area: a square, one metre long on each side, that covers a patch of floor. When you say the kitchen floor is 12 square metres, you mean that 12 of those squares would cover it. If you just wrote 12 m, someone might think it was a line 12 m long, which is something quite different. Perimeter is measured in metres, because it is a length around the edge. The kitchen's perimeter is 4 plus 3 plus 4 plus 3, which is 14 m. Its area is 12 square metres. Those are two different numbers with two different units, and both are correct for their own question. The same goes for smaller shapes: a shape on 1 cm grid paper has a perimeter in centimetres and an area in square centimetres. In books you may see square metres written as m with a small 2 above it. You read it as square metres. Always check your units before you write your final answer.

### subtopic_3 — Using Area to Solve Problems
**Assistant:** How do I estimate the area of a leaf on grid paper?

**Tutor:** Trace the leaf on grid paper with 1 cm squares, then count in a careful, organised way. First, count all the squares that are completely inside the leaf's outline. Tick each one as you count it, so that you do not count any square twice. Next, look at the squares along the edge, which the outline cuts through. If a square is more than half inside the leaf, count it as a whole square. If it is less than half inside, do not count it. The bits you leave out and the bits you count extra roughly balance each other. Add the two counts. If the leaf covers 20 whole squares and 9 squares that are more than half covered, the estimate is about 29 square centimetres. The answer is an estimate, not an exact area, so say about 29 square centimetres. A smaller grid gives a better estimate, because the squares at the edge are smaller and the errors are smaller. Scientists use this method to compare the sizes of leaves on different plants, and mapmakers have used it to estimate the area of lakes and farms.
