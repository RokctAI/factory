# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers measuring the perimeter of shapes using rulers and tape measures, calculating the perimeter of polygons by adding their sides, using rules for the perimeter of rectangles, squares and regular polygons, finding missing sides of composite shapes, and solving perimeter problems. It builds on measuring length and on the properties of 2D shapes from earlier this term. The anchor is a school in George building vegetable gardens. One garden is a rectangle 12 m long and 8 m wide, one is a square with sides of 7 m, and one is L-shaped. Fencing costs R35 per metre. The class also frames a picture that is 30 cm by 20 cm for the hall. The governing idea is that perimeter is the total distance around the outside of a shape, so we add the lengths of all its sides, in the same unit, and give the answer in a unit of length. Measuring perimeter, calculating it with rules, finding missing sides, and solving fencing and framing problems are examinable.

## Subtopic: What Perimeter Is

Perimeter is the distance all the way around the outside of a flat shape. If you walked along the fence of a garden from one corner back to the same corner, the distance you walked would be the perimeter.

To measure the perimeter of a small shape, use a ruler to measure each side and add the lengths. A triangle with sides of 5 cm, 7 cm and 9 cm has a perimeter of 5 plus 7 plus 9, which is 21 cm.

For a big shape, like a garden, use a tape measure. Hook the end at one corner, measure along each side, and write down each length.

For a curved shape, like a round table, wrap a tape measure around the edge, or lay a piece of string around it and then measure the string with a ruler.

Before measuring, estimate. The rectangular garden is about one and a half classrooms long and one classroom wide, so its perimeter will be tens of metres, not hundreds.

Perimeter is a length, so it is measured in millimetres, centimetres, metres or kilometres. Always make sure every side is in the same unit before you add. A rectangle 1,2 m long and 80 cm wide has sides of 120 cm, 80 cm, 120 cm and 80 cm, so its perimeter is 400 cm, which is 4 m.

## Subtopic: Rules for Rectangles, Squares and Regular Polygons

A rectangle has two pairs of equal sides, so we can find its perimeter quickly. The vegetable garden is 12 m long and 8 m wide. Adding all four sides: 12 plus 8 plus 12 plus 8 is 40 m. A quicker way: add the length and the width, 12 plus 8 is 20, then double it, because there are two of each. 2 times 20 is 40 m. The rule is: perimeter of a rectangle equals 2 times the sum of the length and the width.

A square has four equal sides, so its perimeter is 4 times one side. The square garden has sides of 7 m, so its perimeter is 28 m.

Any regular polygon has all its sides equal. So its perimeter is the length of one side multiplied by the number of sides. A regular hexagon with sides of 5 cm has a perimeter of 6 times 5, which is 30 cm. A regular octagon with sides of 3 m has a perimeter of 24 m.

You can also work backwards. A square with a perimeter of 36 m has sides of 36 divided by 4, which is 9 m. A rectangle with a perimeter of 30 cm and a length of 10 cm: the length and width together make half of 30, which is 15 cm, so the width is 5 cm.

The picture for the hall is 30 cm by 20 cm. Its frame has a perimeter of 2 times 50, which is 100 cm, or 1 m.

## Subtopic: Composite Shapes and Perimeter Problems

The L-shaped garden needs more thought. Its bottom side is 10 m, its left side is 8 m, its top side is 4 m and its short right side is 3 m. Two sides are not labelled. Find them from the sides you know. The step across is the bottom minus the top: 10 minus 4 is 6 m. The step up is the left side minus the short right side: 8 minus 3 is 5 m. Now add all six sides: 10 plus 3 plus 6 plus 5 plus 4 plus 8 is 36 m.

Now the cost of fencing. The rectangular garden has a perimeter of 40 m, and fencing costs R35 per metre. 40 times R35 is R1 400.

If a gate is fitted, the fence is shorter. The L-shaped garden has a perimeter of 36 m. With a 2 m gate, it needs only 34 m of fencing.

Compare the gardens. The square garden has a perimeter of 28 m and the rectangular garden has 40 m. Fencing the square garden costs 28 times R35, which is R980, so it is R420 cheaper to fence than the rectangular one.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: adding only two sides of a rectangle, giving 12 m plus 8 m as 20 m; there are four sides, so the perimeter is 40 m. Two: multiplying the length by the width, 12 times 8 is 96; that counts the squares inside, not the distance around. Three: leaving out the unlabelled sides of the L-shape; work them out and add all six. Four: adding 1,2 m and 80 cm as if they were the same unit; change them to the same unit first.
