### subtopic_1 — What Perimeter Is
**Assistant:** Why do I double the length plus the width for a rectangle?

**Tutor:** Because a rectangle has two lengths and two widths, and the rule just adds them in a shorter way. Walk around the rectangular vegetable garden. You go along a length of 12 m, then a width of 8 m, then the other length of 12 m, then the other width of 8 m, and you are back at the start. That is 12 plus 8 plus 12 plus 8, which is 40 m. Notice that the walk is really one length and one width, done twice. One length plus one width is 12 plus 8, which is 20 m: that takes you halfway around. Doubling it takes you all the way around: 2 times 20 is 40 m. Both methods give exactly the same answer. The rule is quicker, especially when the numbers are large or have decimals, and it is harder to forget a side. Some people prefer to double each side first and then add: 24 plus 16 is 40 m. That works too. What matters is that every side is counted exactly once. If you only add the length and the width, you have gone halfway around, and your answer is half of the real perimeter.

### subtopic_2 — Rules for Rectangles, Squares and Regular Polygons
**Assistant:** Why is the perimeter not the same as the length times the width?

**Tutor:** Because they measure two different things. Perimeter is the distance around the edge of a shape, so it is a length, measured in metres. Length times width counts how many squares fit inside the shape; that is the area, which you will learn about next. The rectangular garden is 12 m by 8 m. If you multiply 12 by 8, you get 96, which is the number of square metres inside the garden: the space where you plant the vegetables. The fence does not go across the inside, only around the edge. Walking around the edge is 12 plus 8 plus 12 plus 8, which is 40 m. That is how much fencing you need. Mixing these up would be expensive. If you ordered 96 m of fencing for a 40 m perimeter, you would pay for 56 m too much. So before you calculate, ask what the question needs. If it is about a fence, a border, a frame or walking around something, it is perimeter, and you add the sides. If it is about covering the inside, like carpet, paint or grass, it is area.

### subtopic_3 — Composite Shapes and Perimeter Problems
**Assistant:** How do I find the missing sides of an L-shape?

**Tutor:** Use the sides you know, and remember that the opposite sides of the whole shape must match. The L-shaped garden has its bottom side of 10 m. Along the top, the edge is in two pieces: the top side of 4 m and the step across, which is not labelled. Together, those two pieces must stretch the same distance as the bottom, 10 m. So the step across is 10 minus 4, which is 6 m. Now look at the height. The left side is 8 m. On the right, the height is also in two pieces: the short right side of 3 m and the step up, which is not labelled. Together they must make 8 m, so the step up is 8 minus 3, which is 5 m. Now you know all six sides: 10, 3, 6, 5, 4 and 8. Add them to get 36 m. Here is an interesting check: the perimeter of the L-shape is the same as the perimeter of a full 10 m by 8 m rectangle, which is 2 times 18, also 36 m. That is because the steps simply move parts of the edge inwards without changing their total length.
