# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers recognising lines of symmetry in 2D shapes and pictures, counting the lines of symmetry in polygons and circles, and drawing and completing symmetrical shapes on grid paper. It builds on the properties of polygons and quadrilaterals from earlier this term. The anchor is a craft market in Pretoria. A beadwork stall sells panels with matching patterns on the left and right, a stop sign stands at the market gate, and a banner spells MATHS in big capital letters for a school stall. The governing idea is that a line of symmetry divides a shape into two halves that are mirror images of each other, so that if you fold the shape along the line, the halves fit exactly on top of each other. Recognising lines of symmetry, counting them in shapes, and completing a shape so that it is symmetrical about a given line are examinable.

## Subtopic: What Is a Line of Symmetry?

A shape has line symmetry when a straight line can divide it into two halves that match exactly, like an object and its reflection in a mirror. That line is called a line of symmetry, or a mirror line.

There are two easy tests. The fold test: cut out the shape, fold it along the line and see whether the two halves fit exactly on top of each other with nothing sticking out. The mirror test: stand a small mirror upright on the line. If the half you can see, together with its reflection, looks exactly like the whole shape, the line is a line of symmetry.

A line of symmetry can be vertical, going from top to bottom, horizontal, going from side to side, or diagonal, going from corner to corner or at a slant.

The beadwork panel at the stall has the same pattern on its left and right sides, so it has a vertical line of symmetry down the middle. A butterfly has one vertical line of symmetry down its body. Your face is nearly symmetrical about a vertical line, but not perfectly; most things in nature are only roughly symmetrical.

Symmetry is about matching shape and position. Each point on one side must have a partner on the other side, the same distance from the line, straight across.

Colour can matter too. If a shape matches when folded but one half is red and the other half is blue, the picture is not symmetrical, even though its outline is. The beadwork seller makes sure the colours match on both sides as well as the shapes, so that the whole design is symmetrical.

## Subtopic: Lines of Symmetry in 2D Shapes

Different shapes have different numbers of lines of symmetry.

A square has 4 lines of symmetry: one vertical, one horizontal and two diagonals. A rectangle that is not a square has only 2: one vertical and one horizontal, through the middles of its sides. Its diagonals are not lines of symmetry. If you fold a rectangle along a diagonal, the corners stick out.

An equilateral triangle has 3 lines of symmetry, each from a vertex to the middle of the opposite side. An isosceles triangle, with two equal sides, has 1. A scalene triangle, with no equal sides, has none.

Regular polygons follow a pattern: the number of lines of symmetry is the same as the number of sides. A regular pentagon has 5, a regular hexagon has 6, and the stop sign at the market gate, a regular octagon, has 8.

A kite has 1 line of symmetry, along the diagonal that joins the corners between the pairs of equal sides. A rhombus that is not a square has 2, along its diagonals. A parallelogram that is not a rectangle or a rhombus has none at all.

A circle has more lines of symmetry than you can count, because every diameter is a line of symmetry.

To find the lines of symmetry in a shape, try every possible fold in your mind: down the middle, across the middle, and along each diagonal. Then check each fold carefully. Drawing the lines with a ruler as dashed lines helps you keep count, and it helps you see that in regular shapes, all the lines cross at the centre.

## Subtopic: Completing Symmetrical Drawings

To complete a symmetrical drawing on grid paper, reflect each corner across the mirror line. Count how many squares the corner is from the line, then mark its partner the same number of squares on the other side, straight across. Join the new points in the same order.

Suppose a vertical mirror line runs down the middle of the grid. A corner 3 squares to the left of the line has its partner 3 squares to the right, at the same height. A corner that sits on the mirror line stays where it is.

Now the banner. In simple capital letters, M has 1 vertical line of symmetry, A has 1 vertical line, T has 1 vertical line, H has 2 lines, one vertical and one horizontal, and S has no line of symmetry. Letters like B, C, D and E have a horizontal line.

Whole words can be symmetrical too. The word TOOT, written in capitals, has a vertical line of symmetry down the middle, because it reads the same in a mirror. The word BOX, written in capitals, has a horizontal line of symmetry through the middle of every letter.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: saying the diagonals of a rectangle are lines of symmetry; fold along a diagonal and the corners stick out. Two: saying a parallelogram has 2 lines of symmetry; it has none, because no fold makes the halves match. Three: completing a drawing by copying the half without flipping it; the new half must be a mirror image, facing the other way. Four: saying a square has only 2 lines of symmetry; it also has its 2 diagonals, so it has 4.
