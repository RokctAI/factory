### subtopic_1 — What Is a Line of Symmetry?
**Assistant:** Why are the diagonals of a rectangle not lines of symmetry?

**Tutor:** Because when you fold a rectangle along a diagonal, the two halves do not land on top of each other. Try it with a sheet of paper, which is a rectangle. Fold it so that one corner meets the opposite corner along the diagonal. You will see that two flaps stick out past the edges. The two triangles are the same size and shape, but they are turned differently, so one does not cover the other exactly. A line of symmetry needs more than two equal halves; it needs halves that are mirror images across that line. Now try the fold that brings the left edge onto the right edge. The halves match perfectly, so that fold line is a line of symmetry. The fold that brings the top onto the bottom also works. So a rectangle that is not a square has exactly 2 lines of symmetry. A square is different. Because all its sides are equal, folding along a diagonal brings one side exactly onto the next side, and nothing sticks out. That is why a square has 4 lines of symmetry: the vertical, the horizontal and both diagonals.

### subtopic_2 — Lines of Symmetry in 2D Shapes
**Assistant:** How many lines of symmetry does a circle have?

**Tutor:** More than you can count. Every straight line through the centre of a circle, which is every diameter, is a line of symmetry. Fold a paper circle in half along any diameter and the two halves match exactly, because every point on the edge is the same distance from the centre. You could fold it along a vertical diameter, a horizontal one, or one at any slant at all, and it would always work. There is no limit to how many different diameters you can draw, so there is no limit to the lines of symmetry. This fits a pattern with regular polygons. An equilateral triangle has 3 lines, a square has 4, a regular hexagon has 6 and a regular octagon has 8. The more sides a regular polygon has, the more lines of symmetry it has, and the more it looks like a circle. A circle is like a regular polygon with so many tiny sides that you cannot see them. When an answer asks how many lines of symmetry a circle has, you can say it has countless lines of symmetry, or more than can be counted.

### subtopic_3 — Completing Symmetrical Drawings
**Assistant:** How do I complete a symmetrical drawing on grid paper?

**Tutor:** Work one corner at a time. Pick a corner of the half that is already drawn. Count how many squares it is from the mirror line, moving straight towards the line, at a right angle to it. Then count the same number of squares on the other side of the line, in the same straight direction, and mark a dot. That dot is the corner's partner. If a corner is 3 squares to the left of a vertical mirror line, its partner is 3 squares to the right, at exactly the same height. If a corner is on the mirror line, it is its own partner, and it stays where it is. Do this for every corner, then join the new dots with a ruler in the same order as the original corners. Finally, check your work by imagining a fold along the line. Every corner should land on its partner. The most common mistake is to copy the half by sliding it across, so that it faces the same way. A mirror image always faces the opposite way, like your left and right hands, which match but point in opposite directions.
