# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers investigating and extending numeric patterns, including sequences that are not limited to a constant difference or ratio, representing them in tables, and describing the rules in your own words. The anchor is Lebo in Durban, who saves R7 in the first week and then R8 more each week, a chain message that each learner sends to 3 friends, and a supermarket worker in Pietermaritzburg who stacks cans in a triangle. The governing idea is that every pattern follows a rule, and that you find the rule by looking at how each term changes from the one before: by adding the same amount, by multiplying by the same amount, or by changing in a pattern of its own. Finding the rule, extending the pattern, writing it in a table and describing it in words are examinable.

## Subtopic: Patterns with a Constant Difference

Lebo's savings follow a pattern. After week 1 she has R7. After week 2 she has R15. After week 3, R23. After week 4, R31. Look at the gaps between the terms: 15 take away 7 is 8, 23 take away 15 is 8, 31 take away 23 is 8. The gap is always 8. A pattern where the same amount is added each time has a constant difference.

To extend the pattern, keep adding 8: 31, 39, 47, 55. After week 8 she has R63. Describe the rule in words: start at 7 and add 8 each time.

A table shows the pattern neatly. The top row gives the position, or week number: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5. The bottom row gives the term, or amount saved: 7, 15, 23, 31, 39. Reading across, the terms go up by 8 for each step of 1 in the position.

You can jump ahead without writing every term. To reach week 10 from week 1 takes 9 jumps of 8, which is 72. So week 10 is 7 plus 72, which is R79. Patterns can also go down. 500, 475, 450, 425 has a constant difference of 25, subtracted each time. The next terms are 400 and 375.

The questions for this section are with you now: finding a constant difference, extending a pattern and jumping ahead.

## Subtopic: Patterns with a Constant Ratio

A chain message starts with 1 learner, who sends it to 3 friends. Each of them sends it to 3 more, and so on. The numbers of new learners in each round are 1, 3, 9, 27, 81. The gaps are 2, 6, 18 and 54, which are not constant. But look at how each term is made: 1 times 3 is 3, 3 times 3 is 9, 9 times 3 is 27. Each term is the one before multiplied by 3. This pattern has a constant ratio.

Extend it: 81 times 3 is 243, and 243 times 3 is 729. In just seven rounds, 729 learners get the message in that round. Patterns that multiply grow very quickly, much faster than patterns that add.

Another example: 3, 6, 12, 24, 48. Each term is doubled, so the rule is multiply by 2. The next term is 96. Ratio patterns can shrink too. 6 400, 3 200, 1 600, 800: each term is halved, so the rule is divide by 2. The next term is 400.

To decide whether a pattern adds or multiplies, first check the gaps. If the gaps are equal, it is a constant difference. If they are not, try dividing each term by the one before it. If you always get the same number, it is a constant ratio.

The questions for this section are with you now: finding a constant ratio and telling adding patterns from multiplying patterns.

## Subtopic: Patterns with a Changing Difference

The supermarket worker stacks cans in a triangle. The top row has 1 can, the next row 2, then 3, then 4. The total number of cans for 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5 rows is 1, 3, 6, 10, 15. The gaps are 2, 3, 4 and 5. They are not the same, and the terms are not multiplied by the same number either. But the gaps themselves make a pattern: they go up by 1 each time.

To extend this pattern, extend the gaps first. The next gap is 6, so the next term is 15 plus 6, which is 21. Then 21 plus 7 is 28. Describe it in words: start at 1, add 2, then add 3, then add 4, adding one more each time. These are called triangular numbers.

Another example: 2, 3, 5, 8, 12, 17. The gaps are 1, 2, 3, 4, 5. The next gap is 6, so the next term is 23. The square numbers 1, 4, 9, 16, 25 also have a changing difference: the gaps are 3, 5, 7 and 9, the odd numbers. The next square number is 25 plus 11, which is 36, and indeed 6 times 6 is 36.

You can also make up your own patterns. Choose a start number and a rule, such as start at 5 and add one more than last time. Write the terms in a table, then describe the rule so that a friend could continue it.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: calling 1, 3, 9, 27 a pattern that adds 2; the gaps change, but each term is 3 times the one before. Two: extending 1, 3, 6, 10 by adding 4 again to get 14; the gaps grow, so add 5 to get 15. Three: finding week 10 of Lebo's savings as 10 times 8, which is 80; she starts at 7 and makes only 9 jumps of 8, so R79. Four: describing a rule only as it goes up, which does not say by how much.

The questions for this section are with you now: patterns whose differences change, and making up your own patterns.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the savings, the chain message and the cans — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Add the Same Each Time

Find the gaps. All the same? Keep adding that gap.

7, 15, 23, 31: add 8 each time. Next: 39.

Use a table: position on top, term underneath.

Keep the gap in mind and try the questions for this part: constant difference.

## Subtopic: Multiply the Same Each Time

Gaps not the same? Try dividing each term by the one before.

1, 3, 9, 27: times 3 each time. Next: 81.

Times patterns grow very fast.

Hold the times rule in mind and try this part's questions: constant ratio.

## Subtopic: Watch the Gaps Grow

Sometimes the gaps make their own pattern.

1, 3, 6, 10, 15: the gaps are 2, 3, 4, 5. Next gap 6, next term 21.

Say the rule so a friend could carry on.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Look at the gaps between terms. Equal gaps mean a constant difference: keep adding or subtracting. If the gaps are not equal, check for a constant ratio: keep multiplying or dividing. If neither, look for a pattern in the gaps themselves. Show patterns in a table and describe the rule in your own words.

Hold the growing gaps in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: changing differences.
