### subtopic_1 — Flow Diagrams: Input, Rule, Output
**Assistant:** Why do I undo the last step first when I work backwards?

**Tutor:** Because working backwards is like unwrapping a parcel: what was put on last must come off first. In the jumping castle machine, the input is first multiplied by 15 and then 50 is added. So the last thing that happened to the number was adding 50. To get back, you undo that first by taking away 50, and only then undo the multiplying by dividing by 15. Think of getting dressed. You put on socks and then shoes. To undress, you take off your shoes first and then your socks. If you tried to take your socks off first, it would not work. With R350: take away 50 to get 300, then divide by 15 to get 20 hours. If you did it the other way round, dividing first, you would get 350 divided by 15, which is not even a whole number, and then you would subtract 50 and get nonsense. So write the flow diagram, draw arrows going backwards underneath, and next to each backward arrow write the inverse operation: plus becomes minus, times becomes divide. Then always check by running your answer forwards through the machine.

### subtopic_2 — Finding Inputs and Rules
**Assistant:** How do I find the rule when I only have a table?

**Tutor:** Look at how the outputs change when the inputs go up by 1. In the table with inputs 1, 2, 3, 4 and outputs 8, 14, 20, 26, the outputs go up by 6 each time. That jump tells you the multiplying part of the rule: times 6. Then compare the first output with what times 6 gives. 1 times 6 is 6, but the output is 8. The difference is 2, so the rule must add 2. Your rule is times 6, plus 2. Now test it on every pair: 2 times 6 plus 2 is 14, 3 times 6 plus 2 is 20, 4 times 6 plus 2 is 26. All correct. If the inputs do not go up by 1, be careful. If the inputs are 2, 4, 6 and the outputs go up by 10, then each step of 2 in the input adds 10, so each step of 1 would add 5, and the rule starts with times 5. Some rules have only one operation, like times 7, and some subtract, like times 3, minus 1. The method is the same: find the jump, try it, adjust, and test every pair.

### subtopic_3 — Four Ways to Show One Rule
**Assistant:** What does it mean for two descriptions to be equivalent?

**Tutor:** It means they describe exactly the same relationship, so they always give the same output for the same input, even though they look different. It is like saying the same sentence in English, isiZulu and Afrikaans: the words change, but the meaning stays the same. The rule for the jumping castle can be said in words, drawn as a flow diagram, written in a table, or written as a number sentence. They all mean times 15, plus 50. To check whether two descriptions are equivalent, test them with the same inputs. Take the pattern 7, 15, 23, 31 and the rule multiply the position by 8 and subtract 1. For positions 1, 2, 3 and 4, the rule gives 7, 15, 23 and 31. Same outputs, so they are equivalent. One different output is enough to show that they are not equivalent. For example, the rule add 8 to the position gives 9 for position 1, not 7, so it does not match. Being able to move between the four descriptions is powerful, because some questions are easier with a table and others with a number sentence.
