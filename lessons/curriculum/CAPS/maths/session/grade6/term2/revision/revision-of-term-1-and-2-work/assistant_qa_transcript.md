### subtopic_1 — Whole Numbers and Number Sentences
**Assistant:** How do I check the answer to a number sentence?

**Tutor:** Use substitution: put your answer back into the number sentence and see whether it makes a true statement. Take box times 24 equals 1 344. You solved it by dividing 1 344 by 24 and got 56. Now replace the box with 56. Is 56 times 24 equal to 1 344? Work it out: 50 times 24 is 1 200, and 6 times 24 is 144. 1 200 plus 144 is 1 344. Yes, the statement is true, so 56 is correct. If the two sides had not matched, you would know there was a mistake somewhere, either in the division or in the multiplication, and you would look again. Substitution works for every kind of number sentence, whether the box is added, subtracted, multiplied or divided. It is quicker than solving the whole problem again, and it uses a different operation, which makes it a stronger check. It is also a good idea to estimate before you solve. 1 344 is about 1 200, and 1 200 divided by 24 is 50, so the answer should be a bit more than 50. 56 fits.

### subtopic_2 — Numeric and Geometric Patterns
**Assistant:** How do I find the rule for a pattern like 5, 9, 13, 17?

**Tutor:** Start by looking at how the pattern changes from one term to the next. Here it goes up by 4 each time. That tells you the rule probably includes times 4. Now test it. Multiply the position by 4: position 1 gives 4, position 2 gives 8, position 3 gives 12. Compare these with the real terms, 5, 9 and 13. Each real term is 1 more. So the rule is: multiply the position by 4 and add 1. Always test the rule on at least two terms you already know. Position 4: 4 times 4 is 16, plus 1 is 17. Correct. Now you can find any term without counting all the way. The 20th term is 20 times 4, which is 80, plus 1, which is 81. You can also work backwards. If someone asks which position gives 41, undo the steps in reverse order: take away 1 to get 40, then divide by 4 to get 10. The same thinking works for geometric patterns. Count what each new picture adds, use that as the multiplier, and then fix the difference.

### subtopic_3 — Fractions, Decimals and Percentages
**Assistant:** How do I compare a fraction, a decimal and a percentage, like 3/4, 0,8 and 72%?

**Tutor:** Change them all into one form, and decimals are usually the easiest. 0,8 is already a decimal. For 3/4, find an equivalent fraction with denominator 100. Multiply the top and bottom by 25 to get 75/100, which is 0,75. For 72%, remember that per cent means out of 100, so 72% is 72/100, which is 0,72. Now give each decimal two places: 0,75, 0,80 and 0,72. Compare them from the left. All have 0 wholes. The tenths are 7, 8 and 7, so 0,80 is the biggest. The other two both have 7 tenths, so look at the hundredths: 5 and 2. So 0,72 is the smallest. From smallest to biggest the order is 72%, 3/4, 0,8. Then write your answer using the forms the question gave you. This method uses three skills from this term at once: equivalent fractions, place value of decimals and the meaning of per cent. That is what revision is about: seeing how the tools fit together, so that a question that looks new is really made of pieces you already know.
