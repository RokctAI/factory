### subtopic_1 — Three Names for One Number
**Assistant:** How can 1/2, 0,5 and 50% all be the same number?

**Tutor:** Because they are three different ways of writing the same amount, just like R1 and 100 cents are the same money. Imagine a slab of chocolate cut into 100 small squares, and you eat 50 squares. As a common fraction, you ate 50/100, which simplifies to 1/2. As a decimal, 50 hundredths is 0,50, which we write as 0,5. As a percentage, 50 out of 100 is 50%. The amount of chocolate is the same in all three: half the slab. Each form is useful in a different place. Fractions are handy for sharing, like half a pizza. Decimals are handy for money and measurement, like R0,50 or 0,5 m. Percentages are handy for comparing and for shops, like a 50% sale. The bridge between them is the number 100. If you can write a fraction as hundredths, you can read off the decimal and the percentage at once. For example, 1/4 is 25/100, so it is 0,25 and 25%. Learning the common ones by heart, like halves, quarters, fifths and tenths, makes problems much faster.

### subtopic_2 — Converting and Comparing Forms
**Assistant:** How do I compare 3/5, 0,58 and 62%?

**Tutor:** Change them all into the same form first, because it is hard to compare things that are written differently. Decimals are usually the easiest form to compare. 0,58 is already a decimal. For 3/5, find an equivalent fraction with denominator 10 or 100. Multiply the numerator and the denominator by 2 to get 6/10, which is 0,6. Write it as 0,60 so that it has two places like the others. For 62%, remember that per cent means out of 100, so 62% is 62/100, which is 0,62. Now line up 0,58, 0,60 and 0,62. All three have 0 wholes. Look at the tenths: 5, 6 and 6. So 0,58 is the smallest. The other two both have 6 tenths, so look at the hundredths: 0 and 2. So 0,62 is the biggest. The order from smallest to biggest is 0,58, then 3/5, then 62%. When you write your answer, use the numbers in their original forms, as the question gave them. Your working just shows the decimals you used to decide.

### subtopic_3 — Solving Decimal Problems
**Assistant:** How do I know which operation to use in a decimal word problem?

**Tutor:** Read the problem and ask what is happening to the amounts in the story. If amounts are being put together, like adding up the takings from two tables, you add. If something is being taken away, like costs out of takings, or if you want the difference or the change, you subtract. If you have many of the same thing, like 100 cupcakes at R4,50 each, you multiply. Then estimate before you calculate. 100 cupcakes at about R5 is about R500, so R450 makes sense and R4 500 does not. After you calculate, check your answer with the inverse operation. If the profit is R392,10 and the costs were R185,40, then adding them should give back the takings of R577,50. Finally, read the question again and give the answer in the right units, with R in front for money. The decimals do not change these steps at all. You choose the operation in exactly the same way as with whole numbers, and then you remember to line up the commas when you add or subtract.
