# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers multiplying decimal fractions with up to two decimal places by 10 and by 100, and using this to solve money and measurement problems such as changing rand to cents and metres to centimetres. It builds on place value of decimals and on multiplying whole numbers by 10 and 100. The anchor is Mrs Pillay's home bakery in Gqeberha, where one koeksister costs R4,75, a piece of ribbon for a box is 0,35 m long, and orders come in tens and hundreds. The governing idea is that multiplying by 10 makes every digit ten times bigger, so each digit moves one place to the left, and multiplying by 100 moves each digit two places to the left; the comma itself stays still while the digits move. Multiplying decimals by 10 and 100, finding a missing number, and using these facts to convert units and work out costs are examinable.

## Subtopic: Multiplying Decimals by 10

Think about what ten times bigger means for each place. Ten tenths make one whole, so a digit in the tenths place becomes units. Ten hundredths make one tenth, so a digit in the hundredths place becomes tenths. Ten units make one ten, so units become tens. Every digit moves one place to the left.

A school orders 10 koeksisters at R4,75 each. Work out 4,75 times 10. In 4,75 the 4 is units, the 7 is tenths and the 5 is hundredths. After multiplying by 10, the 4 becomes 4 tens, the 7 becomes 7 units and the 5 becomes 5 tenths. The answer is 47,5. In money we write R47,50.

On a place value chart, picture the comma fixed in place and the digits sliding one column to the left. Some learners say the comma moves one place to the right instead. That gives the same answer, and it is a useful shortcut, but remember that what really happens is that the number gets ten times bigger.

More examples. 0,6 times 10: 6 tenths become 6 units, so the answer is 6. 3,08 times 10: the 3 becomes 30, the 0 tenths become 0 units and the 8 hundredths become 8 tenths, so the answer is 30,8. 12,5 times 10 is 125.

Always check that your answer makes sense. Ten koeksisters should cost roughly ten times as much as one. One costs almost R5, so ten should cost almost R50. R47,50 fits. If you got R4,750 or R475, you would know at once that something went wrong.

Each piece of ribbon is 0,35 m long. Ten pieces are 0,35 times 10, which is 3,5 m.

The questions for this section are with you now: multiplying decimals by 10.

## Subtopic: Multiplying Decimals by 100

Multiplying by 100 is the same as multiplying by 10 and then by 10 again. So every digit moves two places to the left. Hundredths become units, tenths become tens and units become hundreds.

A hotel orders 100 koeksisters. Work out 4,75 times 100. The 4 units become 4 hundreds, the 7 tenths become 7 tens and the 5 hundredths become 5 units. The answer is 475, so the order costs R475.

When there are not enough digits, zeros fill the empty places. 2,4 times 100: the 2 becomes 200 and the 4 tenths become 4 tens, so the answer is 240. There is no digit to fill the units place, so we write a 0 there. 0,07 times 100: the 7 hundredths become 7 units, so the answer is 7. 0,35 times 100 is 35, and 0,9 times 100 is 90.

We can also work backwards to find a missing number. What times 10 is 37? Move the digits one place to the right: 3,7. What times 100 is 52? Move the digits two places to the right: 0,52. Check: 0,52 times 100 is 52.

Notice how the two steps build up. 0,25 times 10 is 2,5, and 2,5 times 10 is 25. So 0,25 times 100 is 25.

In money, this pattern is easy to see. A sweet that costs R0,25 is 25 cents. Ten sweets cost R2,50, and a hundred sweets cost R25. Each time we multiply by 10, the amount becomes ten times bigger.

The questions for this section are with you now: multiplying decimals by 100 and finding missing numbers.

## Subtopic: Using ×10 and ×100 in Measurement and Money

Many units are linked by 10 and 100. There are 100 cents in R1, 100 cm in 1 m, and 10 mm in 1 cm. To change a bigger unit into a smaller unit, we multiply, because there will be more of the smaller units.

To change rand to cents, multiply by 100. R3,60 is 3,60 times 100 cents, which is 360 cents. R0,85 is 85 cents.

To change metres to centimetres, multiply by 100. A box is 0,35 m long, which is 35 cm. A table at the bakery is 2,45 m long, which is 245 cm. A length of 6,03 m is 603 cm; the zero tenths become zero tens.

To change centimetres to millimetres, multiply by 10. A koeksister is 12,5 cm long, so it is 125 mm long.

Mrs Pillay needs 100 pieces of ribbon, each 0,35 m long, for a big order. That is 0,35 times 100, which is 35 m of ribbon. If the ribbon costs R2,15 a metre, 100 metres would cost R215, but she only needs 35 m.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: writing 4,75 times 10 as 4,750; a zero at the end of a decimal changes nothing, so the digits must move and the answer is 47,5. Two: writing 2,4 times 100 as 2,400; that is still 2,4, and the answer is 240. Three: writing 0,35 times 100 as 3,5; that only moves the digits one place, so the answer is 35. Four: saying R3,60 is 36 cents; there are 100 cents in a rand, so R3,60 is 360 cents.

The questions for this section are with you now: converting units and working out costs with 10 and 100.

## Subtopic: Rand, Cents and Centimetres

R1 is 100 cents. 1 m is 100 cm. 1 cm is 10 mm.

R3,60 is 360 cents. 2,45 m is 245 cm.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Multiplying by 10 makes a number ten times bigger, so every digit moves one place to the left. Multiplying by 100 moves every digit two places to the left. Fill empty places with zeros. Adding a zero to the end of a decimal does not multiply it. Use times 100 to change rand to cents and metres to centimetres, and times 10 to change centimetres to millimetres.

Hold the moving digits in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: converting with 10 and 100.
