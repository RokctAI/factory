### subtopic_1 — Multiplying Decimals by 10
**Assistant:** Why can I not just add a zero when I multiply a decimal by 10?

**Tutor:** Because adding a zero at the end of a decimal does not change its value at all. With whole numbers, the trick works: 34 times 10 is 340, and the zero lands in the units place, pushing the other digits left. But with decimals, a zero at the end lands after the last decimal place. 4,75 and 4,750 are the same number: 4 units, 7 tenths, 5 hundredths and 0 thousandths. So writing 4,750 does not make the number any bigger. Multiplying by 10 must make the number ten times bigger. Think of the koeksisters. One costs R4,75, so ten of them cannot also cost R4,75. They cost about R50. The real rule is that every digit moves one place to the left, because each digit becomes worth ten times as much: 7 tenths become 7 units, and 5 hundredths become 5 tenths. That gives 47,5, which is R47,50. The adding-a-zero trick is just a shortcut for whole numbers. The moving-digits rule works for every number, whole or decimal, so it is the one to remember.

### subtopic_2 — Multiplying Decimals by 100
**Assistant:** Does the comma move or do the digits move?

**Tutor:** In the real maths, the digits move and the comma stays where it is. The comma always sits between the units and the tenths. When you multiply by 10, each digit becomes ten times bigger, so it moves into the next place to the left. On a place value chart, you can see the 7 in 4,75 slide out of the tenths column and into the units column. But if you look at the answer, 47,5, it looks as if the comma has jumped one place to the right. That is why many people use the shortcut of moving the comma to the right: one place for times 10, two places for times 100. Both ways of thinking give exactly the same answer, so you may use either. The digits moving way helps you understand why the answer is ten times bigger. The comma moving way is quick when you are working fast. Whichever you use, check the size of your answer. Ten koeksisters should cost about ten times as much as one, and a hundred should cost about a hundred times as much.

### subtopic_3 — Using ×10 and ×100 in Measurement and Money
**Assistant:** Why do I multiply when I change metres to centimetres?

**Tutor:** Because centimetres are smaller units, so you need more of them to cover the same length. One metre is the same length as 100 centimetres. So 2 metres is 200 centimetres, and 2,45 metres is 2,45 times 100 centimetres, which is 245 cm. The length of the table has not changed; we are just counting it in smaller pieces, and smaller pieces mean a bigger number. Money works the same way. One rand is 100 cents, so R3,60 is 3,60 times 100, which is 360 cents. The amount of money is the same, but there are more of the smaller coins. A good way to check is to ask whether your answer should be bigger or smaller. Changing from a big unit to a small unit should give a bigger number. If you change 2,45 m to centimetres and get 0,0245, your answer is far too small, and you know you divided instead of multiplying. Later you will learn to change small units into big units, and then you will divide, because you need fewer of the bigger units.
