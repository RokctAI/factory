# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers writing number sentences to describe problem situations. It builds on number sentences in Grade 5 and on the order of operations from Term 1. The anchor is a cricket club in Benoni that sold 1 480 tickets on Friday and 2 365 on Saturday, set out 4 500 seats in rows of 125, and had to share money and equipment among its teams. The governing idea is that a number sentence is the story written in the language of mathematics: it shows the operations, the numbers we know and a box where the unknown number belongs. Choosing the operation, placing the box where the gap in the story is, telling true from false sentences, and writing one sentence for a two-step problem are examinable.

## Subtopic: Choosing the Operation

A number sentence is a mathematical statement with an equals sign. 1 480 plus 2 365 equals 3 845 is a true number sentence. 1 480 plus 2 365 equals 3 745 is a false number sentence. 1 480 plus 2 365 equals box is an open number sentence: the box stands for a number we still have to find.

To write a number sentence, picture what is happening in the story. How many tickets were sold over the two days? Two amounts are joined, so add: 1 480 plus 2 365 equals box. How many more tickets were sold on Saturday than on Friday? Two amounts are compared, so subtract the smaller from the bigger: 2 365 minus 1 480 equals box. The box is 885.

Multiplication and division follow the same thinking. The club sets out 36 rows with 125 seats in each row. Equal groups are joined, so multiply: 36 times 125 equals box, which is 4 500. If we know there are 4 500 seats in rows of 125 and we want the number of rows, a total is split into equal groups, so divide: 4 500 divided by 125 equals box, which is 36.

Clue words help but they are not rules. Altogether and total often mean add. Difference, left and how many more often mean subtract. Each, per and groups of often mean multiply or divide. Always read the whole story and check that your sentence tells the same story.

The questions for this section are with you now: true, false and open number sentences, and choosing the operation.

## Subtopic: Where the Box Goes

The box does not always go at the end. It goes where the gap in the story is. The club had some money in the bank. It spent R3 750 on new nets and now has R8 250 left. The unknown is the starting amount, so the sentence is box minus 3 750 equals 8 250.

1 350 cricket balls are shared equally among 18 teams. How many balls does each team get? You can write 1 350 divided by 18 equals box, or 18 times box equals 1 350. Both sentences tell the same story, and both give a box of 75.

Writing the box in the right place keeps the story clear. A coach buys some bats at R250 each and pays R3 000 altogether. The number of bats is unknown, so box times 250 equals 3 000. The box is 12. If you wrote 3 000 times 250 equals box, you would be multiplying two amounts of money, which makes no sense in the story.

A number sentence can also have operations on both sides of the equals sign. The club sold 1 480 tickets plus 2 365 tickets, which is the same as 2 365 plus box. The box must be 1 480, because adding works in any order. The equals sign means the two sides have the same value, not the answer comes next.

The questions for this section are with you now: placing the box, and sentences with the box at the start or in the middle.

## Subtopic: Two-Step Number Sentences

Some stories have two steps, and you can write them as one number sentence. A family buys 4 adult tickets at R85 and 6 child tickets at R45. The total cost is 4 times 85 plus 6 times 45 equals box. Remember the order of operations: multiply first. 340 plus 270 is 610. The tickets cost R610.

Brackets show which step comes first when the normal order would do something else. The club raises R2 000 at a cake sale, pays R560 for ingredients, and shares the rest equally among 6 teams. The sentence is (2 000 minus 560) divided by 6 equals box. Brackets first: 1 440. Then 1 440 divided by 6 is 240. Each team gets R240. Without the brackets, the sentence would mean 2 000 minus 560 divided by 6, which is a different story.

Read your sentence back as a story to check it. 4 times 85 plus 6 times 45 says four tickets at R85 and six tickets at R45. That matches, so the sentence is right.

Here is one more. The club has R500 and buys 8 caps at R45 each. How much is left? The sentence is 500 minus 8 times 45 equals box. Multiplication comes first, so no brackets are needed: 8 times 45 is 360, and 500 minus 360 is 140. R140 is left.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: writing 1 480 minus 2 365 equals box for how many more were sold on Saturday; put the bigger number first. Two: always putting the box at the end, so the money story becomes 8 250 minus 3 750 equals box, which finds the wrong thing. Three: leaving out the brackets in the cake sale sentence. Four: thinking the equals sign means the answer comes next, so 1 480 plus 2 365 equals 2 365 plus box looks wrong; equals means both sides are the same.

The questions for this section are with you now: writing two-step sentences and using brackets.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the cricket club — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: What Is Happening

Joining? Add. Comparing or taking away? Subtract.

Equal groups? Multiply. Splitting into equal groups? Divide.

1 480 plus 2 365 equals box. The box is 3 845.

Keep the story in mind and try the questions for this part: choosing the operation.

## Subtopic: Box at the Gap

Put the box where the unknown is.

Some money, minus R3 750, leaves R8 250: box minus 3 750 equals 8 250.

Equals means both sides have the same value.

Hold the gap in mind and try this part's questions: placing the box.

## Subtopic: Two Steps in One Sentence

Two steps can go in one sentence.

4 times 85 plus 6 times 45 equals box. The box is 610.

Use brackets for the step that must come first: (2 000 minus 560) divided by 6.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A number sentence has an equals sign, and both sides have the same value. Picture the story to choose the operation. Put the box where the unknown is, even at the start. For two steps, use the order of operations, and add brackets when a step must come first. Read the sentence back as a story to check it.

Hold the two steps in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: two-step sentences.
