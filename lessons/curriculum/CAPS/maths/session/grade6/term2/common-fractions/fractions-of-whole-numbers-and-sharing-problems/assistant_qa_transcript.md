### subtopic_1 — Finding a Fraction of a Whole Number
**Assistant:** Why do I divide by the denominator and then multiply by the numerator?

**Tutor:** Because the denominator tells you how many equal parts the whole is split into, and the numerator tells you how many of those parts you want. Take 3/4 of 240 sheep. The 4 says: split the flock into 4 equal groups. 240 divided by 4 is 60, so each group, each quarter, has 60 sheep. The 3 says: take 3 of those groups. 3 times 60 is 180 sheep. So you divide to find the size of one part and multiply to find how many parts. You could multiply first and then divide, 240 times 3 is 720, and 720 divided by 4 is 180, and you get the same answer. But dividing first keeps the numbers smaller and easier to work with. A good check is to think about the size of your answer. 3/4 is less than 1 whole, so 3/4 of 240 must be less than 240. It is also more than half, so it must be more than 120. 180 fits. If you had mixed up the numbers and divided by 3 then multiplied by 4, you would get 320, which is more than the whole flock, and that is impossible.

### subtopic_2 — Fractions in Sharing and Grouping Problems
**Assistant:** When I share 7 pizzas among 4 friends, why is the answer 7/4 and not 4/7?

**Tutor:** Because a fraction can mean a division: the top number is what is being shared, and the bottom number is how many share it. 7 pizzas shared by 4 people is 7 divided by 4, which is written 7/4. Picture it. Each friend gets one whole pizza first, which uses 4 of the 7. The remaining 3 pizzas are each cut into 4 quarters, and each friend gets one quarter from each of those 3 pizzas, which is 3 quarters. So each friend gets 1 whole and 3 quarters, which is 1 and 3/4, the same as 7/4. If the answer were 4/7, each friend would get less than one pizza, but there are more pizzas than friends, so everyone must get more than one. Checking the size like this is a great way to catch a swapped fraction. Here is another: 5 litres of juice shared by 8 learners is 5/8 of a litre each. Now there are fewer litres than learners, so everyone gets less than 1 litre, and 5/8 is less than 1, which makes sense.

### subtopic_3 — Fraction Problems with Money and Measurement
**Assistant:** How do I know if the question wants the fraction part or what is left over?

**Tutor:** Read the question word by word and underline what it asks for. In a sale, a question might ask how much is taken off, which is the discount, or what the new price is, which is what is left after the discount. For a R450 jacket with 1/5 off, the discount is R90, but the new price is R450 minus R90, which is R360. Both numbers come from the same working, so it is easy to write the wrong one. Words like off, spent, used, eaten and walked usually point to the fraction part. Words like left, remaining, still to go, new price and saved usually point to what is left. A neat trick for what is left is to subtract the fraction from 1 first. If Thandeka spends 2/3 of her R360, then 1 minus 2/3 is 1/3 left. 1/3 of R360 is R120. You can use this as a check: R240 spent plus R120 left is R360, the whole amount. Always finish with a sentence that answers the exact question, like the new price of the jacket is R360, so you can see that you answered what was asked.
