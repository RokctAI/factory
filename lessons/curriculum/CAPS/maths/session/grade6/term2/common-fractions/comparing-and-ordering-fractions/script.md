# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers comparing and ordering common fractions, including tenths and hundredths. It builds on comparing fractions in Grade 5. The anchor is three apple farms near Ceres that have picked different fractions of their orchards, learners at an athletics day in Bloemfontein who have run different fractions of a lap, and a number line from 0 to 1. The governing idea is that a fraction is bigger when it has more pieces of the same size or bigger pieces of the same number, and that fractions with different denominators can be compared by changing them into equivalent fractions with the same denominator. Comparing fractions with the same denominator or numerator, comparing fractions where one denominator is a multiple of the other, comparing tenths and hundredths, and ordering fractions are examinable.

## Subtopic: Same Denominator or Same Numerator

The bottom number of a fraction, the denominator, tells you how many equal pieces the whole is cut into. The top number, the numerator, tells you how many pieces you have. When two fractions have the same denominator, the pieces are the same size, so the one with more pieces is bigger. Farm A has picked 5/8 of its orchard and Farm B has picked 3/8. Five eighths is more than three eighths, so Farm A has picked more.

When two fractions have the same numerator, you have the same number of pieces, so compare the size of the pieces. Lindiwe ran 3/4 of a lap and Kagiso ran 3/8 of a lap. Quarters are bigger than eighths, because the lap is cut into fewer pieces. So 3/4 is bigger than 3/8. The bigger the denominator, the smaller each piece.

This catches many learners. 1/10 is smaller than 1/2, even though 10 is bigger than 2. Think of sharing one pizza among 10 people instead of 2: each slice is much smaller. In the same way, 1/12 of a lap is a shorter run than 1/6 of a lap, and 1/100 is smaller than 1/10.

A third check is to compare with one half. 4/9 is less than a half, because half of 9 is 4 and a half, and 4 is less. 5/9 is more than a half. So 5/9 is bigger than 4/9, and you can see it straight away.

The questions for this section are with you now: comparing fractions with the same denominator, the same numerator, and comparing with one half.

## Subtopic: Using Equivalent Fractions to Compare

When both the numerators and the denominators are different, change one fraction so that both have the same denominator. Farm C has picked 2/3 of its orchard, and Farm D has picked 5/6. Since 6 is a multiple of 3, change thirds into sixths. Multiply the top and bottom of 2/3 by 2 to get 4/6. Now compare 4/6 and 5/6. Five sixths is bigger, so Farm D has picked more.

Another example: 3/4 and 7/12. 12 is a multiple of 4, because 4 times 3 is 12. Multiply the top and bottom of 3/4 by 3 to get 9/12. Nine twelfths is bigger than seven twelfths, so 3/4 is bigger than 7/12.

Tenths and hundredths work the same way. One tenth is ten hundredths, so multiply the top and bottom of a tenth by 10. Compare 3/10 and 27/100: 3/10 is 30/100, which is bigger than 27/100. Compare 7/10 and 72/100: 7/10 is 70/100, so 72/100 is a little bigger.

One more pair: 1/3 and 5/12. 12 is 3 times 4, so multiply the top and bottom of 1/3 by 4 to get 4/12. Now 5/12 is bigger than 4/12, but only by one twelfth. Without matching the denominators, it would be hard to see that these two fractions are so close.

Multiplying the top and bottom by the same number does not change the size of a fraction. It cuts each piece into smaller pieces, but you have more of them, so the amount stays the same.

The questions for this section are with you now: making the denominators the same, and comparing tenths with hundredths.

## Subtopic: Ordering Fractions on a Number Line

To order several fractions, give them all the same denominator, then order the numerators. Order 1/2, 3/8, 3/4 and 5/8 from smallest to biggest. All the denominators divide into 8, so write each in eighths: 1/2 is 4/8, 3/8 stays 3/8, 3/4 is 6/8 and 5/8 stays 5/8. In ascending order: 3/8, 4/8, 5/8, 6/8. So the order is 3/8, 1/2, 5/8, 3/4.

A number line from 0 to 1 cut into eighths shows this clearly. 3/8 is just before the middle, 1/2 is exactly in the middle, 5/8 is just after it and 3/4 is further along. The further right a fraction is, the bigger it is. Fractions that are equal, like 1/2 and 4/8, land on exactly the same point.

Mixed numbers are compared by their whole numbers first. 3 and 1/4 is bigger than 2 and 7/8, because 3 wholes is more than 2 wholes. If the whole numbers are the same, compare the fractions: 2 and 1/3 is bigger than 2 and 1/4, because thirds are bigger than quarters.

Ordering tenths and hundredths: 7/10, 65/100 and 72/100. In hundredths, 7/10 is 70/100. So the order from smallest is 65/100, 7/10, 72/100.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: saying 1/10 is bigger than 1/2 because 10 is bigger than 2; more pieces means smaller pieces. Two: comparing 2/3 and 5/6 by changing only the bottom number, so 2/3 becomes 2/6; multiply the top and bottom by 2 to get 4/6. Three: saying 3/10 is smaller than 27/100 because 3 is smaller than 27; change 3/10 to 30/100. Four: comparing 3 and 1/4 with 2 and 7/8 by looking only at the fractions; compare the wholes first.

The questions for this section are with you now: ordering fractions and mixed numbers, and using the number line.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the apple farms, the running track and the number line — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Same Size Pieces

Same bottom number? More pieces wins: 5/8 is more than 3/8.

Same top number? Bigger pieces win: 3/4 is more than 3/8.

A bigger bottom number means smaller pieces.

Keep the piece size in mind and try the questions for this part: same denominator or numerator.

## Subtopic: Make the Pieces Match

Different bottoms? Make them the same.

2/3 is 4/6, so 5/6 is bigger.

3/10 is 30/100, so it is bigger than 27/100.

Hold the matching pieces in mind and try this part's questions: equivalent fractions to compare.

## Subtopic: Line Them Up

Change them all to the same bottom number, then order the tops.

3/8, 1/2, 5/8, 3/4.

Mixed numbers: compare the wholes first.

Pocket summary of the lesson. With the same denominator, more pieces is bigger. With the same numerator, bigger pieces, meaning a smaller denominator, is bigger. Otherwise, change the fractions to the same denominator by multiplying top and bottom by the same number. One tenth is ten hundredths. Order by giving every fraction the same denominator. Compare mixed numbers by their wholes first.

Hold the number line in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: ordering fractions.
