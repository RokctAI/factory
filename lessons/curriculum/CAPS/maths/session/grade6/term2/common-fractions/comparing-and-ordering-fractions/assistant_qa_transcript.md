### subtopic_1 — Same Denominator or Same Numerator
**Assistant:** Why is 1/10 smaller than 1/2 when 10 is bigger than 2?

**Tutor:** Because the denominator tells you how many pieces the whole is cut into, not how big the pieces are. The more pieces you cut a whole into, the smaller each piece must be. Imagine one big pizza. If you share it between 2 people, each gets half the pizza, a huge slice. If you share the same pizza among 10 people, each gets only one tenth, a thin slice. So 1/10 is much smaller than 1/2. It feels backwards because with whole numbers, bigger numbers always mean more. With fractions, the bottom number works the other way round: a bigger bottom number means smaller pieces. The top number still works the normal way: more pieces means more. So 3/10 is bigger than 1/10, because it is three of the same size piece. A good habit is to picture the fraction before you compare. Ask: how big is each piece, and how many pieces do I have? If you can picture a chocolate slab or a pizza cut into the right number of pieces, you will rarely make a mistake.

### subtopic_2 — Using Equivalent Fractions to Compare
**Assistant:** Why does multiplying the top and bottom by the same number not change the fraction?

**Tutor:** Because you are cutting every piece into smaller equal pieces, and you get more of them, so the amount you have stays exactly the same. Take 2/3 of a slab of chocolate. Now cut every third into 2 equal pieces. The whole slab is now in 6 pieces instead of 3, so the denominator doubles to 6. Your 2 pieces have each been cut in 2, so you now hold 4 pieces, and the numerator doubles to 4. You have 4/6 of the slab, but you have not gained or lost any chocolate. 2/3 and 4/6 are equivalent fractions: different names for the same amount. It is like changing a R10 note into ten R1 coins. You have more coins, but the same amount of money. Multiplying the top and bottom by the same number is the same as multiplying the fraction by 2/2, which is 1 whole, and you know that multiplying by 1 changes nothing. But be careful: if you multiply only the bottom, you make the pieces smaller without giving yourself more of them, and the fraction shrinks. That is why both numbers must change together.

### subtopic_3 — Ordering Fractions on a Number Line
**Assistant:** How do I choose which denominator to use when ordering several fractions?

**Tutor:** Look for a number that all the denominators divide into exactly, and choose the smallest one you can find. For 1/2, 3/8, 3/4 and 5/8, the denominators are 2, 8, 4 and 8. Does 8 work for all of them? 2 goes into 8, 4 goes into 8, and 8 goes into 8. So eighths is a good choice. 1/2 becomes 4/8, because you multiply the top and bottom by 4. 3/4 becomes 6/8, because you multiply the top and bottom by 2. In Grade 6, most questions have one denominator that is a multiple of the others, which makes it easy: use the biggest denominator. With tenths and hundredths, use hundredths, because 10 goes into 100. If the denominators are not multiples of each other, like 3 and 4, you need a number in both times tables, like 12. Make sure that once you have chosen a denominator, you change every fraction to it before you compare. Then ordering is easy: just order the numerators. Finally, write the answer using the original fractions, so 3/8, 1/2, 5/8, 3/4, not the changed ones, unless the question says otherwise.
