# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers recognising and using equivalent forms of common fractions with one-digit and two-digit denominators. It builds on equivalent fractions in Grade 5 and on comparing fractions in the last lesson. The anchor is a slab of chocolate with 24 blocks shared among friends in Mbombela, a school soccer team in Polokwane that won 12 of its 20 matches, and a hundred square used to write fractions as hundredths. The governing idea is that equivalent fractions name the same amount in different ways, that you make them by multiplying or dividing the numerator and the denominator by the same number, and that a fraction in its simplest form uses the smallest possible numbers. Making equivalent fractions, finding missing numbers, simplifying fractions and writing fractions as hundredths are examinable.

## Subtopic: Making Equivalent Fractions by Multiplying

Equivalent fractions are fractions that have the same value. 2/3 and 4/6 are equivalent: if you shade 2/3 of a strip and then cut every third into 2 equal pieces, the same shaded part now shows 4/6.

To make an equivalent fraction, multiply the numerator and the denominator by the same number. 2/3: multiply the top and bottom by 2 to get 4/6. By 3 to get 6/9. By 4 to get 8/12. By 5 to get 10/15. All of these are equivalent to 2/3. This list is called a family of equivalent fractions.

Another family: 3/4, 6/8, 9/12, 12/16, 15/20. Every fraction in the list is the same amount, three quarters. Notice that the numerators go up in 3s and the denominators go up in 4s, which is a quick way to build the family.

Finding a missing number uses the same idea. 5/8 equals box over 40. What was the denominator multiplied by? 8 times 5 is 40. So multiply the numerator by 5 too: 5 times 5 is 25. The box is 25, and 5/8 equals 25/40. Always do the same thing to the top and the bottom.

The chocolate slab shows why this is useful. The slab has 24 blocks. A third of the slab is 8 blocks, because 24 divided by 3 is 8, so 1/3 equals 8/24. A quarter is 6 blocks, so 1/4 equals 6/24. A sixth is 4 blocks, so 1/6 equals 4/24. If Sipho eats 1/3, Naledi eats 1/4 and Johan eats 1/6, they eat 8, 6 and 4 blocks, which is 18 blocks altogether. Writing every fraction in twenty-fourths lets you count blocks instead of juggling different fractions.

The questions for this section are with you now: building families of equivalent fractions and finding missing numbers.

## Subtopic: Simplifying Fractions by Dividing

Simplifying a fraction means writing it with smaller numbers, without changing its value. Divide the numerator and the denominator by the same number. The friends eat 18 of the 24 blocks of chocolate, which is 18/24 of the slab. Both 18 and 24 can be divided by 6: 18 divided by 6 is 3, and 24 divided by 6 is 4. So 18/24 is the same as 3/4.

A fraction is in its simplest form when no number bigger than 1 divides into both the numerator and the denominator. 3/4 is in simplest form, because only 1 divides into both 3 and 4.

If you cannot see the biggest number straight away, simplify in steps. 18/24: divide by 2 to get 9/12, then by 3 to get 3/4. You end in the same place. Dividing by the highest common factor, which you met in Term 1, gets you there in one step.

The soccer team won 12 of its 20 matches, which is 12/20. Divide the top and bottom by 4 to get 3/5. The team won 3 out of every 5 matches. More examples: 45/60 simplifies to 3/4, dividing by 15. 16/40 simplifies to 2/5, dividing by 8. 14/21 simplifies to 2/3, dividing by 7.

The questions for this section are with you now: simplifying fractions and finding the simplest form.

## Subtopic: Equivalent Fractions with Two-Digit Denominators

Fractions with two-digit denominators are common in real life, especially hundredths. A hundred square has 100 small squares. 3/4 of it is 75 squares, so 3/4 equals 75/100. Half of it is 50 squares, so 1/2 equals 50/100. 2/5 is 40/100, because 100 divided by 5 is 20 and 2 times 20 is 40. 7/10 is 70/100.

Writing fractions as hundredths is useful because it makes comparing easy, and it prepares you for percentages and decimals later this term. To change a fraction with a denominator of 2, 4, 5, 10, 20, 25 or 50 into hundredths, ask: what do I multiply the denominator by to get 100? Then multiply the numerator by the same number. 9/20: 20 times 5 is 100, so 9 times 5 is 45, and 9/20 is 45/100.

Two-digit denominators can also be simplified. 36/48: divide both by 12 to get 3/4. 24/36: divide both by 12 to get 2/3. 15/25: divide both by 5 to get 3/5.

Twelfths and twenty-fourths are two-digit denominators too. On a clock face, 15 minutes is a quarter of an hour, and an hour has 60 minutes, so 1/4 equals 15/60. Half an hour is 30/60, which is 1/2. In a dozen eggs, 9 eggs is 9/12, which simplifies to 3/4.

To check whether two fractions are equivalent, simplify both. 8/12 and 30/45: 8/12 simplifies to 2/3, and 30/45 simplifies to 2/3. They are equivalent.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: adding the same number to the top and bottom, so 2/3 becomes 4/5; you must multiply or divide. Two: multiplying only the denominator, so 5/8 becomes 5/40. Three: stopping too early, so 18/24 is simplified to 9/12 and left there; 3/4 is the simplest form. Four: thinking that bigger numbers mean a bigger fraction, so 75/100 is bigger than 3/4; they are equal.

The questions for this section are with you now: hundredths, two-digit denominators and checking equivalence.
