### subtopic_1 — Adding Fractions with Related Denominators
**Assistant:** Why can't I just add the tops and add the bottoms?

**Tutor:** Because the bottom number is the size of the pieces, and adding sizes makes no sense. Think of 1/2 a cup of sugar plus 1/4 of a cup. Adding the tops and bottoms gives 2/6, which is a third of a cup. But you started with half a cup, and then you added more sugar. You cannot end up with less than you started with! So 2/6 must be wrong. The trouble is that halves and quarters are different-sized pieces. It is like adding 1 R5 coin and 1 R2 coin and saying you have 2 coins of R7: the coins are not the same, so you cannot just count them together. Change the halves into quarters first, because quarters fit exactly into halves: 1/2 is 2/4. Now everything is in quarters, and 2 quarters plus 1 quarter is 3 quarters. The denominator stays 4, because the pieces are still quarters. A quick check after any fraction sum is to ask: does the answer make sense? Adding should give more than either fraction alone, and 3/4 is more than both 1/2 and 1/4.

### subtopic_2 — Subtracting Fractions with Related Denominators
**Assistant:** How do I know which fraction to change?

**Tutor:** Change the fraction with the smaller denominator into the bigger denominator. In Grade 6 the questions have one denominator that is a multiple of the other, so the bigger denominator is always a good choice. For 2/3 plus 1/6, the denominators are 3 and 6. Six is a multiple of 3, so change thirds into sixths. To do that, ask what 3 is multiplied by to make 6. The answer is 2, so multiply the top and bottom of 2/3 by 2 to get 4/6. The 1/6 does not change. For 3/5 plus 3/10, change fifths into tenths by multiplying by 2: 3/5 is 6/10. For 1/4 plus 5/12, change quarters into twelfths by multiplying by 3: 1/4 is 3/12. If you are not sure which number is the multiple, divide the bigger denominator by the smaller one. If the answer is a whole number, you can change the smaller one. Later in high school you will add fractions like 1/3 plus 1/4, where neither denominator is a multiple of the other. Then you need a new denominator in both times tables, like 12. But the idea is the same: make the pieces match.

### subtopic_3 — Adding and Subtracting Mixed Numbers
**Assistant:** What does breaking a whole mean when I subtract mixed numbers?

**Tutor:** It means changing one whole into fraction pieces so that you have enough pieces to take away. In 3 and 2/4 minus 1 and 3/4, the fraction parts are 2/4 and 3/4. You cannot take 3 quarters from 2 quarters. So you borrow one of the 3 wholes and cut it into 4 quarters. Now you have 2 wholes, and 2/4 plus 4/4, which is 6/4. The number is written 2 and 6/4. It looks strange, but it is the same amount as 3 and 2/4. Now you can subtract: 6/4 minus 3/4 is 3/4, and 2 minus 1 is 1. The answer is 1 and 3/4. This is just like borrowing in column subtraction, where you break a ten into 10 units. Here you break a whole into as many pieces as the denominator says: 4 quarters, 8 eighths, 12 twelfths. Another way is to change both mixed numbers into improper fractions: 3 and 1/2 is 14/4 and 1 and 3/4 is 7/4. 14/4 minus 7/4 is 7/4, which is 1 and 3/4. Both ways work, so choose the one you find easier, and use the other to check.
