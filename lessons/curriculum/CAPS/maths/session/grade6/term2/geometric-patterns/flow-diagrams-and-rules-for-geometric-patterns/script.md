# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers determining input values, output values and rules for geometric patterns using flow diagrams and tables, and the equivalence of different descriptions of the same pattern. It builds on extending geometric patterns in the last lesson and on flow diagrams for numeric patterns. The anchor is a school hall in Kimberley where square tables are pushed together in a long row for a prize-giving dinner, long tables that seat more guests, and rows of hexagons made from matchsticks. The governing idea is that the picture of a pattern shows you the rule, that the rule can be written as a flow diagram, and that the flow diagram can be run forwards to find outputs or backwards to find inputs. Finding the rule from pictures, completing tables, working backwards and matching the four descriptions are examinable.

## Subtopic: From Picture to Flow Diagram

One square table seats 4 people, one on each side. Push two tables together and the seats where they touch disappear: 2 tables seat 6. Three tables seat 8. Four tables seat 10. The pattern is 4, 6, 8, 10, going up by 2 for each table added.

Look at the picture to find the rule. Every table has one person on its top side and one on its bottom side, which is 2 people per table. Then there is one person at each end of the row, which is 2 more. So the number of seats is 2 times the number of tables, plus 2. As a flow diagram: number of tables, then times 2, then plus 2, then number of seats.

Run the flow diagram forwards. 5 tables: 10 plus 2 is 12 seats. 25 tables: 50 plus 2 is 52 seats. The flow diagram lets you jump straight to any number of tables without drawing them all.

Now the hexagons. One matchstick hexagon uses 6 matches. Two hexagons side by side share a side, so they need 11. Three need 16. Four need 21. Each new hexagon adds 5 matches. The picture shows 1 match on the far left, and then 5 matches for every hexagon. The flow diagram is: hexagons, times 5, plus 1, matches. 10 hexagons need 51 matches.

The questions for this section are with you now: finding the rule from a picture and running the flow diagram forwards.

## Subtopic: Working Backwards to the Input

Sometimes you know the output and want the input. The school is expecting 62 guests at square tables in one row. How many tables are needed? The rule is times 2, plus 2. Undo the last step first: 62 minus 2 is 60. Then undo times 2 by dividing: 60 divided by 2 is 30. The school needs 30 tables. Check: 30 times 2 plus 2 is 62.

How many hexagons can you make in a row with 101 matches? Undo plus 1: 100. Undo times 5: 100 divided by 5 is 20. You can make 20 hexagons. Check: 20 times 5 plus 1 is 101.

Sometimes the answer is not a whole number. With 60 matches, 60 minus 1 is 59, and 59 divided by 5 is 11 remainder 4. You can make 11 complete hexagons, using 56 matches, with 4 matches left over. A pattern always needs whole pictures, so the remainder tells you the leftover pieces.

The school also has long tables, each seating 4 people along the sides plus 1 at each end. One long table seats 6, two seat 10, three seat 14. Each new table adds 4 seats. The rule is times 4, plus 2. For 62 guests: 62 minus 2 is 60, and 60 divided by 4 is 15 long tables.

The questions for this section are with you now: working backwards from the output to find the input.

## Subtopic: Words, Tables, Diagrams and Number Sentences

The square tables pattern can be described in four equivalent ways. In words: each table seats 2 people along its sides, plus 1 person at each end of the row. As a flow diagram: tables, times 2, plus 2, seats. As a table: 1 table 4 seats, 2 tables 6 seats, 3 tables 8 seats, 4 tables 10 seats. As a number sentence: number of tables times 2 plus 2 equals number of seats, so 5 times 2 plus 2 equals 12.

To check whether two descriptions match, test the same inputs in both. A learner says the rule is add 2 to the number of tables, then double. For 1 table: 1 plus 2 is 3, doubled is 6. But the picture shows 4 seats for 1 table, so this rule does not match. Another learner says double the tables and add 2. For 1 table: 4. For 2: 6. For 3: 8. That matches every picture, so it is equivalent.

Different-looking rules can sometimes be equivalent. Add 1 to the number of tables and then double gives 1 plus 1 is 2, doubled is 4; 2 plus 1 is 3, doubled is 6. That also matches! Both rules give the same seats for every number of tables. The second learner simply saw the picture differently: as one extra table's worth of end seats, with 2 people for each.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: saying 2 square tables seat 8 because each table seats 4; the seats where tables touch disappear. Two: working backwards with the wrong order, so 62 guests becomes 62 divided by 2, minus 2, which is 29 tables. Three: saying each hexagon needs 6 matches, so 10 hexagons need 60; shared sides mean the rule is times 5, plus 1. Four: deciding two rules match after testing only one input.

The questions for this section are with you now: matching words, flow diagrams, tables and number sentences.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the tables and the hexagons — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Picture to Machine

Look at the picture to find the rule.

Square tables: 2 seats per table, plus 2 at the ends. Tables, times 2, plus 2.

Hexagons: times 5, plus 1.

Keep the picture in mind and try the questions for this part: rules from pictures.

## Subtopic: How Many Shapes

Know the output? Work backwards. Undo the last step first.

62 guests: minus 2, then divide by 2. 30 tables.

101 matches: minus 1, then divide by 5. 20 hexagons.

Hold the undo steps in mind and try this part's questions: finding inputs.

## Subtopic: Four Ways, One Pattern

Words, flow diagram, table, number sentence: one pattern, four ways.

Test the same inputs. Same answers? Equivalent.

Double and add 2 matches the square tables.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Look at the pictures to see what each new shape adds and what stays the same. Write the rule as a flow diagram and run it forwards to find outputs. Work backwards by undoing the last step first to find inputs. Words, flow diagrams, tables and number sentences are equivalent when they give the same outputs for every input.

Hold the four ways in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: equivalent descriptions.
