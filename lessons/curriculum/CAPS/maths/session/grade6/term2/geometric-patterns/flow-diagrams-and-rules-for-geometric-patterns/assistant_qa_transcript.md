### subtopic_1 — From Picture to Flow Diagram
**Assistant:** How do I find the plus part of a rule from the picture?

**Tutor:** First find the times part, which is how much each new shape adds. For square tables in a row, each new table adds 2 seats, so the rule starts with times 2. Then look at the picture for anything that is not part of the repeating bit. In a row of square tables, each table has a person on its top and bottom sides, which is the 2 per table. But there is also one person at the far left end and one at the far right end. Those 2 end seats do not repeat; they are always there, however many tables there are. So the rule is times 2, plus 2. For the hexagons, each hexagon adds 5 matches, so the rule starts with times 5. Then there is one extra match, the very first side on the left, which is not shared. So the rule is times 5, plus 1. If you cannot see it in the picture, use the table: for 1 shape, times the number gives a value, and the difference between that value and the real output is the plus part. For 1 square table, 1 times 2 is 2, but there are 4 seats, so add 2.

### subtopic_2 — Working Backwards to the Input
**Assistant:** Why can two rules that look different give the same pattern?

**Tutor:** Because there can be more than one way to see the same picture, and each way of seeing it gives a different-looking rule. For the square tables, one learner sees 2 people per table plus 2 at the ends, and writes double the tables and add 2. Another learner sees the row of tables and imagines one extra table, then counts 2 people for each: add 1 to the tables and then double. Both are correct descriptions of the same seats, and you can check: for 3 tables, the first rule gives 6 plus 2, which is 8, and the second gives 4 doubled, which is also 8. In fact, adding 1 and then doubling is the same as doubling and then adding 2, because doubling the extra 1 gives 2. This is the distributive property you met in Term 1, in disguise. Seeing a picture in different ways is a real strength in mathematics. It helps you check your work and it prepares you for algebra in high school, where you will learn to show that two expressions are equivalent. The test is always the same: put in the same inputs and compare the outputs.

### subtopic_3 — Words, Tables, Diagrams and Number Sentences
**Assistant:** What should I do if working backwards gives a remainder?

**Tutor:** It means the output you were given cannot be made exactly with whole shapes, so you need to think about what the question is asking. For hexagons with the rule times 5, plus 1, suppose you have 60 matches. Work backwards: 60 minus 1 is 59, and 59 divided by 5 is 11 remainder 4. That tells you that 11 whole hexagons use 11 times 5 plus 1, which is 56 matches, and 4 matches are left over. You cannot make half a hexagon in a pattern, so the answer is 11 hexagons with 4 matches left. In a seating problem, the remainder might mean something different. If 63 guests come and square tables seat 2 times the tables plus 2, then 63 minus 2 is 61, and 61 divided by 2 is 30 remainder 1. 30 tables seat only 62 people, so one guest would have no seat. Now you must round up and use 31 tables, with one seat spare. So, just like in division problems, the story decides whether the remainder is left over or whether you need one more shape.
