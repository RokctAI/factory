# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers investigating and extending geometric patterns, represented in physical or diagram form, that are not limited to a constant difference or ratio, including patterns of your own creation, and describing the relationships in your own words. The anchor is a row of squares made from matchsticks, a beadwork artist near Eshowe who makes square panels of beads, a pool surrounded by paving tiles, and a sheet of paper folded in half again and again. The governing idea is that a geometric pattern is a number pattern you can see, and that by counting what is added from one picture to the next you can find the rule, continue the pattern and predict pictures you have not drawn. Counting the parts of each picture, writing them in a table, finding the rule and extending the pattern are examinable.

## Subtopic: Matchstick Patterns

Make one square with matchsticks: it takes 4 matches. Add a second square next to it, sharing a side: you only need 3 more matches, so 2 squares take 7. A third square adds 3 more: 10 matches. A fourth: 13 matches. The numbers 4, 7, 10, 13 have a constant difference of 3.

Why 3 and not 4? Because each new square shares one side with the square before it. You only add the top, the bottom and the far side. Seeing why the pattern grows the way it does is just as important as finding the numbers.

Put the pattern in a table. Number of squares: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5. Number of matches: 4, 7, 10, 13, 16. To find the rule, look at the pictures. There is 1 match on the left end, and then 3 matches for every square. So the number of matches is 3 times the number of squares, plus 1. Test it: for 4 squares, 3 times 4 plus 1 is 13. Correct.

Now you can predict without building. For 10 squares, 3 times 10 plus 1 is 31 matches. For 50 squares, 151 matches. A row of triangles works the same way: 1 triangle takes 3 matches, 2 triangles take 5, 3 take 7. Each new triangle adds 2 matches, and the rule is 2 times the number of triangles, plus 1.

The questions for this section are with you now: counting matches, finding the constant difference and predicting later pictures.

## Subtopic: Growing Shapes and Square Numbers

The beadwork artist makes square panels. The first panel is 1 bead. The second is 2 rows of 2 beads, which is 4 beads. The third is 3 rows of 3, which is 9 beads. The fourth is 4 rows of 4, which is 16 beads. These are the square numbers: 1, 4, 9, 16, 25.

The gaps are 3, 5, 7 and 9. They are not constant, so this is not a constant difference pattern. But look at the pictures to see why. To grow a 3 by 3 square into a 4 by 4 square, you add a row of 4 along the top and a column of 4 down the side, but the corner bead is counted twice, so you add 4 plus 4 take away 1, which is 7 beads. Each new panel adds one more odd number of beads.

The rule is easy once you see it: panel number times panel number. Panel 10 needs 10 times 10, which is 100 beads.

Not every growing shape is a square. A pool is surrounded by a border of paving tiles. A pool 1 tile long needs 8 border tiles. A pool 2 tiles long needs 10. A pool 3 tiles long needs 12. Each extra tile of pool adds 2 border tiles, one on each side, and the rule is 2 times the length, plus 6. A pool 10 tiles long needs 26 border tiles.

The questions for this section are with you now: square numbers in shapes, and growing borders.

## Subtopic: Describing and Creating Geometric Patterns

Fold a sheet of paper in half. Open it, and there are 2 sections. Fold it in half twice, and there are 4 sections. Three folds give 8, four folds give 16. Each fold doubles the number of sections: 2, 4, 8, 16, 32. This geometric pattern has a constant ratio. After 6 folds there would be 64 sections, though the paper gets very hard to fold.

To describe any geometric pattern, say what the first picture looks like and what is added or changed each time. For the matchsticks: start with one square of 4 matches and add 3 matches for each new square. For the beads: each panel is a square, one row and one column bigger than the last. For the paper: each fold doubles the sections.

You can create your own geometric pattern. Draw a starting shape, choose a rule for how it grows, and draw three pictures. Then make a table and give it to a friend to continue. For example, start with a cross of 5 dots and add 4 dots each time, one at the end of each arm: 5, 9, 13, 17.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: saying each new matchstick square needs 4 matches; the shared side means only 3 are added. Two: extending 1, 4, 9, 16 by adding 7 again to get 23; the next square is 25. Three: finding 10 squares of matchsticks as 10 times 4, which is 40; the rule is 3 times 10 plus 1, which is 31. Four: describing a pattern only as it gets bigger; say what is added each time.

The questions for this section are with you now: constant ratio shapes, describing rules and making your own pattern.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the matchsticks, the beads and the paper — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Count What Is Added

Look at what each new picture adds.

Each new square adds 3 matches, because it shares a side. 4, 7, 10, 13.

Matches: 3 times the squares, plus 1.

Keep the shared side in mind and try the questions for this part: matchsticks.

## Subtopic: Shapes That Grow Faster

Square panels: 1, 4, 9, 16, 25. The gaps are 3, 5, 7, 9.

Panel 10 is 10 times 10: 100 beads.

Pool borders: 8, 10, 12. Add 2 each time.

Hold the squares in mind and try this part's questions: growing shapes.

## Subtopic: Make Your Own

Folding paper doubles the sections: 2, 4, 8, 16.

Say what the first picture is and what is added each time.

Draw three pictures, make a table, and let a friend continue.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A geometric pattern is a number pattern you can see. Count the parts in each picture and put them in a table. Look at the pictures to see what is added each time: a constant amount, a growing amount, or a doubling. Use the rule to predict later pictures without drawing them, and describe the pattern in your own words.

Hold the pictures in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: describing and creating.
