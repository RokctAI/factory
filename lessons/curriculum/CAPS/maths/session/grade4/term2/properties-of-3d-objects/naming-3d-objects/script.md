# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers recognising, visualising and naming 3D objects in the environment and in geometric settings: rectangular prisms, spheres, cylinders and pyramids. The anchor is a kitchen cupboard in Soweto holding a cereal box, a tin of beans and an orange, a soccer ball on the field, and a photograph of the pyramids of Giza. The governing idea is that a 3D object takes up space and is named by its overall form, that a rectangular prism has six rectangular faces, a cylinder has two circular faces and a curved surface, a sphere is curved all over, and a pyramid has a polygon base with triangular faces meeting at a point. Naming these objects, matching them to everyday things and telling a 3D object from a 2D shape are examinable.

## Subtopic: From Flat Shapes to Solid Objects

A 2D shape is flat and can be drawn on paper: a square, a circle, a triangle. A 3D object is solid and takes up space: you can hold it, stack it or put something inside it. A square drawn on paper is 2D; a dice is 3D. A circle is 2D; a ball is 3D. The D stands for dimensions, the directions a thing stretches in: 2D shapes have length and width, 3D objects have length, width and height.

The flat surfaces of a 3D object are called faces, and each face is a 2D shape. A cereal box has six faces, all rectangles. The edges are where two faces meet, and the corners where edges meet are vertices. A curved surface, like the side of a tin, is not called a face in Grade 4; we say the object has a curved surface.

The four objects in this topic are the rectangular prism, the sphere, the cylinder and the pyramid. You will meet cones and other prisms later. Learn these four by their everyday examples first: a box, a ball, a tin and a pyramid. Then learn what makes each one what it is.

Picture an object from different sides to be sure of its name. A tin standing up shows a circle from above and a rectangle from the side. A cereal box shows a rectangle from every side. A ball shows a circle from every side. Seeing the object in your mind from above and from the side is called visualising, and it is a skill this topic builds.

The questions for this section are with you now: the difference between 2D shapes and 3D objects, faces, edges and vertices, and visualising an object from different sides.

## Subtopic: Rectangular Prisms and Cylinders

A rectangular prism is a box shape. It has 6 faces, all rectangles, 12 edges and 8 vertices. Opposite faces are the same size. A cereal box, a brick, a shoebox, a classroom and a matchbox are all rectangular prisms. If all six faces are squares the prism is a cube, like a dice, and a cube is a special rectangular prism just as a square is a special rectangle.

The prism gets its name from having the same cross-section all the way through. Slice a cereal box anywhere parallel to its base and the cut is the same rectangle every time. That is what prism means: a shape pushed straight through space. A rectangular prism is a rectangle pushed up into a box.

A cylinder is a tin shape. It has 2 flat faces, both circles, one at each end, and 1 curved surface wrapping round. A tin of beans, a drinking glass with straight sides, a toilet roll, a pipe and a candle are cylinders. A cylinder is a circle pushed straight up, which is why its cross-section is always the same circle.

Prisms and cylinders can roll or not depending on the surface. A cylinder rolls on its curved surface but stands still on a flat face. A rectangular prism cannot roll at all; it slides or tips. This difference in behaviour is a clue to the object's name when you cannot see it clearly.

The questions for this section are with you now: the faces, edges and vertices of a rectangular prism, the parts of a cylinder, and why both are called the same all the way through.

## Subtopic: Spheres and Pyramids

A sphere is a ball shape. It has no flat faces, no edges and no vertices, only one curved surface all over. Every point on the surface is the same distance from the centre, which is why a ball rolls in any direction. A soccer ball, an orange, a marble, a globe and the planet Earth are roughly spheres.

A pyramid has a flat polygon base and triangular faces that lean in to meet at one point, the apex. The pyramids of Giza have square bases, so each has 1 square face and 4 triangular faces, 5 faces in all, with 8 edges and 5 vertices. A pyramid with a triangular base has 4 triangular faces. Pyramids are named by their base: square pyramid, triangular pyramid.

The pyramid is the odd one out of the four because it is not the same all the way through. Slice it parallel to the base and each cut is a smaller square than the last, until the top is a point. A rooftop that comes to a point, a tent with a square floor and a pointed top, and a sharpened pencil tip are pyramid-like shapes in daily life.

To name an object, ask three questions. Is it curved all over? Sphere. Does it have two circle ends and a curved side? Cylinder. Does it have six rectangle faces? Rectangular prism. Does it have a flat base and triangles meeting at a point? Pyramid. These four questions sort every object in this topic.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: calling a ball a circle; a circle is a flat 2D shape and a ball is a 3D sphere. Two: counting a cylinder's curved surface as a face; in Grade 4 a cylinder has 2 faces and 1 curved surface. Three: saying a square pyramid has 4 faces; the 4 triangles plus the square base make 5. Four: thinking a cube is not a rectangular prism; a cube is a rectangular prism whose faces are all squares.

The questions for this section are with you now: the properties of a sphere and a square pyramid, why the pyramid is not a prism, and the four questions that name any object.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the cereal box, the tin, the soccer ball and the pyramids — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Flat or Solid

Flat shapes you can draw: 2D. Solid objects you can hold: 3D. A circle is flat; a ball is solid. A square is flat; a dice is solid.

The flat sides of a solid are its faces, and each face is a 2D shape. A cereal box has 6 rectangle faces.

Four objects to know: the box, the ball, the tin and the pyramid. Picture each from the top and the side.

Keep the kitchen cupboard in mind and try the questions for this part: flat shapes against solid objects.

## Subtopic: Boxes and Tins

A rectangular prism is a box. Six rectangle faces, 12 edges, 8 corners. A cereal box, a brick, a shoebox. If every face is a square, it is a cube, like a dice.

A cylinder is a tin. Two circle faces at the ends and one curved surface round the middle. A tin of beans, a toilet roll, a candle.

Both are the same shape all the way through. Slice them and every slice matches.

Hold the box and the tin in mind and try this part's questions: rectangular prisms and cylinders.

## Subtopic: Balls and Pyramids

A sphere is a ball. No flat faces, no edges, no corners. One curved surface everywhere. An orange, a marble, the Earth.

A pyramid has a flat base and triangle faces that meet at a point on top. The Giza pyramids have a square base: 1 square and 4 triangles, 5 faces.

Name any object with four questions. Curved all over? Sphere. Two circles and a curved side? Cylinder. Six rectangles? Rectangular prism. Base and triangles to a point? Pyramid.

Pocket summary of the lesson. 3D objects take up space and their flat surfaces are 2D faces. A rectangular prism has 6 rectangular faces, 12 edges and 8 vertices, and a cube is a special one. A cylinder has 2 circular faces and 1 curved surface. A sphere has one curved surface and no faces, edges or vertices. A pyramid has a polygon base with triangular faces meeting at an apex; a square pyramid has 5 faces. Four questions sort any of these objects.

Hold the soccer ball and the pyramid in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: spheres, pyramids and naming.
