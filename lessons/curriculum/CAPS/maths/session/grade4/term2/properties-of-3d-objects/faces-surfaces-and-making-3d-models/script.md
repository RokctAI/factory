# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers describing, sorting and comparing 3D objects in terms of the shapes of their faces and flat and curved surfaces, and making 3D models using cut-out polygons. The anchor is a shoe box and a tin in a classroom in George, a soccer ball, and a set of cut-out squares and triangles that will be taped into a cube and a square pyramid. The governing idea is that every 3D object can be described by listing the 2D shapes of its faces and counting its flat and curved surfaces, and that a model is built by joining the correct polygons along their edges, which shows exactly which faces an object has. Describing and sorting objects by faces and surfaces, and constructing models from cut-outs, are examinable.

## Subtopic: Describing Objects by Their Faces

To describe a 3D object, list its faces by shape and count them. A shoe box has 6 faces, all rectangles, in three matching pairs: top and bottom, front and back, left and right. A cube has 6 faces, all squares. A square pyramid has 1 square face and 4 triangular faces. A triangular pyramid has 4 triangular faces. The list of faces is a fingerprint for the object.

Then count the flat and curved surfaces. A shoe box has 6 flat surfaces and 0 curved. A tin has 2 flat surfaces, the circular ends, and 1 curved surface. A ball has 0 flat surfaces and 1 curved. A pyramid has only flat surfaces. Writing these counts next to the face list gives a complete description.

Comparing two objects means stating what is the same and what differs. A shoe box and a cube both have 6 flat faces, 12 edges and 8 vertices, but the cube's faces are all squares while the box's are rectangles of two or three sizes. A tin and a ball both have a curved surface, but the tin also has two flat faces and the ball has none. A box and a pyramid both have only flat faces, but the box has rectangles only and the pyramid has triangles meeting at a point.

The number of vertices and edges follows from the faces. A square pyramid has 5 vertices, 4 at the base and 1 at the apex, and 8 edges, 4 around the base and 4 running up to the apex. A rectangular prism has 8 vertices and 12 edges. A cylinder and a sphere have no vertices, because vertices are where straight edges meet, and they have no straight edges.

The questions for this section are with you now: listing the faces of an object by shape, counting flat and curved surfaces, and comparing two objects.

## Subtopic: Sorting Objects by Surfaces

Objects sort naturally by their surfaces. Flat surfaces only: rectangular prisms, cubes and pyramids. Curved surfaces only: spheres. Both flat and curved: cylinders, and also cones, which you will meet later. This three-way sort is the 3D version of the straight-and-curved sort you did with 2D shapes.

A second sort uses the shapes of the faces. Objects with only rectangular faces: rectangular prisms. Objects with triangular faces: pyramids. Objects with circular faces: cylinders. Objects with no faces: spheres. A two-way table can combine the two sorts, with flat, curved and both across the top and the face shapes down the side.

Sorting also uses behaviour. Objects that roll in every direction: spheres. Objects that roll in one direction: cylinders on their side. Objects that do not roll: prisms and pyramids. Objects that stack: prisms and cylinders on their ends. Objects that do not stack well: spheres and pyramids. Shop shelves are full of prisms and cylinders for exactly this reason.

When you sort, say the property. The tin is in the both group because it has two flat faces and a curved surface. The ball is in the curved group because it has no flat faces. Naming the property is what turns a pile into a sort.

The questions for this section are with you now: sorting objects by flat and curved surfaces, by face shapes, and by whether they roll or stack.

## Subtopic: Making Models from Cut-Out Polygons

A model of a 3D object can be built from its faces. To make a cube, cut out 6 equal squares and tape them along their edges so that each square meets four others. To make a square pyramid, cut out 1 square and 4 equal triangles whose bases match the square's sides, tape each triangle to a side of the square, then fold the triangles up and tape their slanted edges together at the apex.

The flat pattern of faces before folding is called a net. The net of a cube is often drawn as a cross of six squares: four in a column and one on each side of the second square. Fold the column round and the side squares close the box. Not every arrangement of six squares folds into a cube; a straight row of six does not, because the end squares would overlap. Trying different arrangements is a good investigation.

Making the model proves the face list. If you try to build a square pyramid with 3 triangles, there is a gap; with 5, one triangle has nowhere to go. Exactly 4 triangles and 1 square close the shape. Building is how you check that a description is complete. Count the faces, edges and vertices of your finished model and compare with the lists from earlier.

Cylinders and spheres cannot be built from cut-out polygons, because their surfaces are curved. A cylinder can be made from a rectangle rolled into a tube with two circles taped on the ends, which shows that its curved surface is really a bent rectangle. A sphere cannot be made from flat pieces at all without gaps, which is why a soccer ball is sewn from many small pentagons and hexagons that only approximate a sphere.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: counting a tin's curved surface as a third face; a cylinder has 2 flat faces and 1 curved surface. Two: describing a cube only as having 6 faces; a shoe box also has 6 faces, so say that the cube's faces are all squares. Three: trying to fold a straight row of six squares into a cube; the end squares overlap, so the net must be arranged differently. Four: building a square pyramid with 3 or 5 triangles; exactly 4 triangles and 1 square close the shape.

The questions for this section are with you now: building a cube and a square pyramid from cut-out polygons, understanding a net, and why curved objects need different methods.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the shoe box, the tin, the ball and the cut-out polygons — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: List the Faces

Describe an object by its faces. Shoe box: 6 rectangles, in matching pairs. Cube: 6 squares. Square pyramid: 1 square and 4 triangles.

Then count flat and curved surfaces. Box: 6 flat, 0 curved. Tin: 2 flat, 1 curved. Ball: 0 flat, 1 curved.

Compare: the box and the cube both have 6 faces, but the cube's are all squares.

Keep the shoe box in mind and try the questions for this part: describing objects by faces and surfaces.

## Subtopic: Sort by Surface

Three groups: flat surfaces only, curved only, both. Boxes and pyramids: flat only. Balls: curved only. Tins: both.

Or sort by what they do. Balls roll anywhere. Tins roll one way on their side. Boxes do not roll but stack beautifully.

Always say why. The tin is in the both group because of its two flat ends and curved side.

Hold the three groups in mind and try this part's questions: sorting 3D objects.

## Subtopic: Build It

Cut out 6 equal squares and tape them edge to edge. Fold them up and you have a cube. The flat pattern before folding is called a net.

Cut out 1 square and 4 matching triangles. Tape a triangle to each side of the square, fold them up to a point, tape the slanted edges. A square pyramid.

Try the wrong number of pieces and you get a gap or a spare. Building proves the face list. Curved objects need a rolled rectangle for a tin, and a ball cannot be made from flat pieces at all.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Describe a 3D object by listing its faces by shape and counting its flat and curved surfaces: a box has 6 rectangles, a cube 6 squares, a square pyramid 1 square and 4 triangles, a tin 2 circles and a curved surface, a ball one curved surface. Sort objects by flat, curved or both, by face shape, or by whether they roll or stack, and say why. Build models by taping the correct polygons edge to edge; the flat pattern is a net, and building proves the face list.

Hold the cut-outs in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: making models and nets.
