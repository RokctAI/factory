### subtopic_1 — From Flat Shapes to Solid Objects
**Assistant:** Why can't I just call a ball a circle? It looks like one.

**Tutor:** It looks like one because a drawing or a photograph of a ball is a circle, and your eye sees the outline. But the ball itself is not flat. You can hold it, it has an inside, and it looks like a circle from every direction, which no flat circle can do; a coin seen edge-on is a thin line. The word for the solid is sphere, and the circle is what you see when you look at a sphere or when you cut one in half. This distinction matters more than it seems. In Term 4 you will measure the area of flat shapes and the volume of solid objects, and they are completely different kinds of measurement: a circle has an area but no volume, while a sphere has a volume and a surface. If you call a ball a circle you will reach for the wrong measurement. It also matters in everyday language. An architect who asks for a circular window and a spherical lamp wants two different things. A good habit is to ask whether you could pick the thing up and turn it over. If you could, it is 3D and needs a solid name: sphere, cylinder, prism, pyramid. If it only exists on a surface, it is 2D: circle, rectangle, triangle. The faces of a 3D object are where the two worlds meet, because each face is a flat 2D shape sitting on a solid.

### subtopic_2 — Rectangular Prisms and Cylinders
**Assistant:** Is a cube a rectangular prism or its own thing? The names seem to overlap.

**Tutor:** A cube is both, in the same way that a square is both a square and a rectangle. A rectangular prism is any box whose six faces are rectangles, and a square is a rectangle, so a box with six square faces passes the test and is a rectangular prism. It also passes a stricter test, all faces equal squares, which earns it the special name cube. Mathematicians like these nested names because they save work: anything true about rectangular prisms, such as having 12 edges and 8 vertices, is automatically true about cubes, and you never need to prove it separately. The overlap feels odd because in everyday speech we tend to use the most specific name we have and never the general one, calling a dice a cube and a cereal box a box, so the two names sound like they describe two unrelated families. In CAPS Grade 4 the official list is rectangular prisms, spheres, cylinders and pyramids, and a cube is included as a special rectangular prism. In Grade 5 and 6 you will meet other prisms, with triangular or hexagonal ends, and you will see that rectangular prism was itself a special member of a bigger prism family. Keep the simple rule: a cube is a rectangular prism with all square faces, and when a question asks you to name a dice, cube is the best answer, but rectangular prism is not wrong.

### subtopic_3 — Spheres and Pyramids
**Assistant:** Why is a pyramid in the same list as prisms when it is so different?

**Tutor:** It is on the list precisely because it is different, and seeing the difference teaches you what a prism is. A prism is the same all the way through: slice a cereal box or a tin anywhere parallel to its base and you get the same rectangle or the same circle every time. A pyramid is the opposite. Slice a square pyramid parallel to its base and each slice is a smaller square than the one below it, until at the top the square has shrunk to a single point, the apex. That narrowing is what makes a pyramid a pyramid, and its sloping triangular faces are the visible sign of it. Having both kinds of object side by side lets you compare how they behave. Prisms stack neatly, which is why boxes and bricks are prisms; pyramids do not stack, which is why you rarely see them in a warehouse but often see them as monuments, roofs and tents, where a pointed top sheds rain and looks grand. The sphere is different again, curved everywhere, and the cylinder is a prism with a circle for its base. So the four objects give you three kinds of behaviour: same all through, narrowing to a point, and curved all over. Learning them together is like learning three animals from different families: each one makes the others clearer.
