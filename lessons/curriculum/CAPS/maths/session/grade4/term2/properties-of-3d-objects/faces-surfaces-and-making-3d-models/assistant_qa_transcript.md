### subtopic_1 — Describing Objects by Their Faces
**Assistant:** Why do we say a tin has 2 faces? The curved part is a surface too.

**Tutor:** The curved part is certainly a surface, and in this topic we count it as a curved surface, but we keep the word face for flat surfaces, and there are good reasons for the distinction. A flat face is a 2D shape you can name, draw, cut out and measure: the circles at the ends of the tin are circles. The curved side is not a 2D shape in the same way; you cannot lay it flat without unrolling it, and when you do unroll it, it turns into a rectangle, which shows that it was really a bent rectangle all along. So saying 2 faces and 1 curved surface records exactly what the tin is made of: two circles and one rectangle wrapped round. It also keeps the counting consistent with the other objects. A box has 6 faces and no curved surfaces, a ball has no faces and 1 curved surface, and the tin sits in between with 2 and 1, which is what makes it the object that belongs in the both group when you sort. Some older books do call the curved side a face, and in high school the word surface is used for everything, so you will meet both usages. In CAPS Grade 4, the rule is simple: faces are flat, surfaces can be flat or curved, and you report both counts so that nothing about the object is left out.

### subtopic_2 — Sorting Objects by Surfaces
**Assistant:** How do I know which arrangements of six squares fold into a cube?

**Tutor:** By trying them, which is why this is set as an investigation rather than a fact to memorise. There are eleven different nets of a cube, and finding them is a classic puzzle. The cross shape, four squares in a column with one square on each side of the second square, is the most familiar. A T shape, four in a column with one on each side of the first square, also works. A row of four with one square on top at one end and one below at the other end works too. What never works is any arrangement with five or six squares in a straight line, because when you fold the row round, the fifth square lands on top of the first, and the shape can never close with a face missing somewhere else. Also, four squares around one central square, like a plus sign, with the sixth attached anywhere, fails because two squares end up fighting for the same position. A practical test is to pick one square as the bottom, fold the squares touching it up to be walls, and see whether the remaining squares can become the other walls and the lid without overlapping. Cut out six squares, tape them in an arrangement, and fold; the squares will tell you. Keep a record of the arrangements that work and you will see patterns in them. Puzzles like this are how mathematicians learn to visualise, and visualising is the real skill of this topic.

### subtopic_3 — Making Models from Cut-Out Polygons
**Assistant:** Why can't I make a ball out of flat pieces? Soccer balls are made of patches.

**Tutor:** You are right that a soccer ball is sewn from flat patches, 12 pentagons and 20 hexagons in the classic design, and your eye tells you the result is a sphere. But look closely at a new ball and you will see that it is not perfectly round; each patch is still a little flat, with slight ridges along the seams. The patches only approximate a sphere, and the ball becomes rounder when it is pumped up, because the air pressure stretches the leather and bends the flat patches into slight curves. A true sphere is curved at every point, so no matter how small you cut your flat pieces, each piece is still flat and the sphere is not, and there will always be a tiny gap or ridge. Map-makers have the same problem in reverse: they cannot lay the curved surface of the Earth flat on a page without stretching or tearing it, which is why every world map distorts the sizes of countries somewhere. A cylinder is different, as the lesson showed, because its curved surface is only curved in one direction; a rectangle bends round into a tube with no stretching at all, which is why a tin label peels off flat. Mathematicians say a cylinder is developable and a sphere is not. For Grade 4, the lesson is that a model of a sphere from flat pieces is always an approximation, and the more pieces you use, the closer it gets, but it never quite arrives.
