# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers determining input values, output values and rules for geometric patterns using flow diagrams, and determining the equivalence of descriptions of the same rule presented verbally, in a flow diagram and by a number sentence. The anchor is a row of hexagonal paving tiles outside a school in Phuthaditjhaba, matchstick squares in a row, and a fence of posts and rails. The governing idea is that a flow diagram is a machine: the input is the picture number, the rule is the operation or operations applied, and the output is the counted feature, so that the same rule can be written in words, as a diagram and as a number sentence. Completing flow diagrams, finding the rule from inputs and outputs, and matching equivalent descriptions are examinable.

## Subtopic: Inputs, Rules and Outputs

A flow diagram has three parts: an input on the left, a rule in the middle, and an output on the right. Put the picture number in, apply the rule, and the output is the number of things in the picture. For the fence, each section has 1 post and 2 rails, plus 1 post at the far end. For the matchstick squares the rule is times 3, then plus 1: input 1 gives 3 plus 1, which is 4; input 2 gives 6 plus 1, which is 7; input 3 gives 9 plus 1, which is 10.

A flow diagram can have one operation or two. A pattern of hexagonal tiles where each picture adds one tile with 6 sides each, not sharing sides, has the single rule times 6: input 4 gives 24 sides. The matchstick squares need two boxes in a row, times 3 and then plus 1, because the shared sides make the count smaller than 4 per square and the first left side adds one.

To find an output, feed the input through every box in order. Input 7 into times 3 then plus 1: 7 times 3 is 21, plus 1 is 22. Picture 7 of the matchstick squares has 22 sticks. To find a missing input, run the machine backwards: the output 31 came from plus 1, so take 1 off to get 30, and 30 came from times 3, so divide by 3 to get 10. Picture 10 has 31 sticks.

Order matters when there are two operations. Times 3 then plus 1 on input 2 gives 7. Plus 1 then times 3 on input 2 gives 9. The flow diagram shows the order by the arrangement of the boxes, and you must follow it left to right.

The questions for this section are with you now: feeding an input through a flow diagram, running it backwards to find an input, and respecting the order of the boxes.

## Subtopic: Finding the Rule

Sometimes the inputs and outputs are given and the rule is missing. Inputs 1, 2, 3, 4 give outputs 5, 9, 13, 17 for a cross of dots. First find the difference between consecutive outputs: 4 each time. That tells you the rule has times 4 in it. Then check: 1 times 4 is 4, but the output is 5, so the rule also adds 1. Rule: times 4 then plus 1. Check all of them: 2 times 4 plus 1 is 9, 3 times 4 plus 1 is 13, 4 times 4 plus 1 is 17.

The difference between outputs is always the number you multiply by when the inputs go up by 1. Outputs 3, 6, 9, 12 have a difference of 3 and start at 3, so the rule is simply times 3. Outputs 4, 7, 10, 13 have a difference of 3 and start at 4, so the rule is times 3 then plus 1. Outputs 2, 5, 8, 11 have a difference of 3 and start at 2, so the rule is times 3 then minus 1.

Always test a rule on every pair you have been given, not just the first. A rule that fits one pair might fail the second. Inputs 1, 2, 3 with outputs 2, 4, 8 have a first difference of 2, so you might guess times 2, but 3 times 2 is 6, not 8. This pattern doubles each time instead, and it is not a constant-difference pattern. Testing catches it.

Describe the rule in words as well as in the diagram: multiply the picture number by 4 and add 1. A learner who can say the rule aloud, draw the flow diagram and explain where the 4 and the 1 come from in the picture has understood the pattern completely.

The questions for this section are with you now: finding the multiplier from the differences, finding the added or subtracted number from the first output, and testing the rule on every pair.

## Subtopic: Same Rule, Three Ways

One rule can be written in three ways. In words: multiply the picture number by 3 and add 1. As a flow diagram: input, then a box saying times 3, then a box saying plus 1, then output. As a number sentence: 3 times box plus 1 equals the number of matchsticks, where the box holds the picture number. All three say exactly the same thing.

Checking equivalence means checking that two descriptions give the same output for every input. Times 3 then plus 1 and the number sentence 3 times box plus 1 clearly match. But is plus 1 then times 3 the same? Input 2: the first gives 7, the second gives 9. Not the same. Equivalent descriptions must agree on every input, and one disagreement is enough to show they are different.

Sometimes two descriptions look different and are the same. Double the picture number and add the picture number again, then add 1, is the same as times 3 then plus 1, because doubling and adding once more is multiplying by 3. Test with input 4: double is 8, plus 4 is 12, plus 1 is 13; and 3 times 4 plus 1 is 13. They agree, and they will agree for every input.

Moving between the three forms is a skill you will use for years. Words are how a problem is given to you; the flow diagram is how you think about it; the number sentence is how you calculate with it, and in later grades the number sentence becomes a formula with a letter in place of the box.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: doing plus 1 before times 3 when the diagram says times 3 then plus 1; follow the boxes in order. Two: running the diagram forwards to find a missing input; use the inverse operations backwards. Three: deciding the rule from the first pair only; test it on every pair given. Four: calling two rules equivalent because they agree on one input; they must agree on every input.

The questions for this section are with you now: writing a rule in words, as a flow diagram and as a number sentence, and testing whether two descriptions are equivalent.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the paving tiles, the matchstick squares and the fence — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: The Number Machine

A flow diagram is a machine. Number goes in, rule happens, number comes out. Picture number in, matchsticks out.

Matchstick squares: times 3, then plus 1. Put in 3: 9, then 10. Put in 7: 21, then 22.

Run it backwards to find an input. Output 31: take off 1 to get 30, divide by 3 to get 10. Picture 10.

Keep the machine in mind and try the questions for this part: inputs, rules and outputs.

## Subtopic: Find the Rule

Inputs 1, 2, 3, 4, outputs 5, 9, 13, 17. Jumps of 4, so times 4. Check input 1: 4, but we need 5. So plus 1. Rule: times 4 then plus 1.

Outputs 3, 6, 9, 12: jumps of 3, starts at 3, so just times 3. Outputs 4, 7, 10, 13: jumps of 3, starts at 4, so times 3 then plus 1.

Test the rule on every pair. One that does not fit means the rule is wrong.

Hold the jump trick in mind and try this part's questions: finding the rule.

## Subtopic: Three Ways to Say It

Words: times the picture number by 3 and add 1. Diagram: in, times 3, plus 1, out. Number sentence: 3 times box plus 1 equals the matchsticks.

Are two rules the same? Try a number. Times 3 then plus 1 on 2 gives 7. Plus 1 then times 3 on 2 gives 9. Different.

Words, diagram, sentence. Same rule, three outfits.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A flow diagram takes an input, applies one or more rules in order, and gives an output. Find outputs by running it forwards and inputs by running it backwards with inverse operations. Find a missing rule from the differences between outputs, then adjust with a plus or minus, and test on every pair. The same rule can be written in words, as a flow diagram and as a number sentence, and two descriptions are equivalent only if they agree on every input.

Hold the three outfits in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: equivalent descriptions.
