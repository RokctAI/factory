### subtopic_1 — Seeing What Changes
**Assistant:** Why do we bother with the pictures? The number pattern is all that matters in the end.

**Tutor:** The pictures are where the numbers come from, and they are also the thing that makes a rule trustworthy. If you only see 4, 7, 10 you can guess that the next is 13, but a guess is all it is; many different rules could start with those three numbers. When you look at the matchstick squares you can see why the pattern adds 3: each new square needs a top, a bottom and a right side, and shares its left side with the square before. Now you are not guessing; you know, and you can explain it to someone who doubts you. The pictures also reveal the position rule. Seeing that every square gives 3 sticks and the first square needs one extra left side tells you that picture 20 has 3 times 20 plus 1, which is 61, without drawing anything. Numbers alone would not have shown you that. There is a deeper reason too. In Grade 7 and beyond you will learn algebra, and a formula like 3n plus 1 is exactly the matchstick rule written in symbols. Learners who have seen the picture behind such a formula understand what the letter stands for; learners who only ever juggled numbers find the symbols mysterious. So the pictures are not a warm-up before the real maths. They are the real maths, and the numbers are the summary.

### subtopic_2 — Describing the Rule in Words
**Assistant:** How can a staircase and a triangle of dots give the same numbers? They look different.

**Tutor:** Because underneath the different drawings they are the same arrangement. Picture 4 of the staircase has columns of 1, 2, 3 and 4 blocks standing side by side, and 1 plus 2 plus 3 plus 4 is 10. Picture 4 of the triangle of dots has rows of 1, 2, 3 and 4 dots stacked on top of each other, and 1 plus 2 plus 3 plus 4 is 10. Turn the staircase so the columns become rows and you have the triangle; the two pictures are the same structure drawn in different orientations, with blocks in one and dots in the other. The numbers 1, 3, 6, 10, 15, 21 are so common that they have a name, the triangular numbers, because each one is the number of dots in a triangle with that many rows. They turn up in bowling pins, snooker balls, stacked cans in a shop display and the number of handshakes in a room. Finding the same sequence in two different-looking patterns is one of the most important experiences in mathematics, because it shows that the rule is about structure, not about appearance. When you meet a new pattern, ask not only what it looks like but how it is built; two patterns built the same way will always share their numbers, however differently they are drawn.

### subtopic_3 — Patterns of Your Own
**Assistant:** My own pattern seemed clear to me but my friend could not continue it. What went wrong?

**Tutor:** Nothing went wrong; you have just discovered the difference between a rule you know and a rule you have shown, and that discovery is the whole point of the exercise. When you make a pattern, the rule is in your head, and every picture you draw looks obvious because you know what you were doing. Your friend only has the pictures, and from four pictures several different rules might fit. Perhaps your pattern added 2 matchsticks to the top bar each time, but your friend saw the shape getting wider and thought the bar grew by one on each side, which is also 2 in total but makes a different picture 5. Or perhaps one of your drawings was a little untidy and the count came out wrong. The fix is to make the regular change as visible as possible. Draw the pictures neatly in a row with equal spacing. Make the new part of each picture stand out, perhaps with a different colour or a different shade. Write the count under each picture so the number sequence confirms the drawing. And if there is still doubt, write the rule in one sentence beside the pictures. A pattern that a friend can continue is a pattern that is truly regular; a pattern that only its maker can continue is still an idea in one person's head.
