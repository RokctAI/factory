### subtopic_1 — Inputs, Rules and Outputs
**Assistant:** Why do we draw a flow diagram at all? I can just write the rule.

**Tutor:** You can, and for a one-step rule like times 3 the diagram adds little. The diagram earns its keep when there are two or more steps, because it makes the order impossible to miss and it shows you exactly how to run the rule backwards. Times 3 then plus 1 and plus 1 then times 3 are different rules, and in a sentence the difference is easy to skim past, while in a diagram the boxes sit in a row and you cannot help reading them in order. To find a missing input you walk the diagram from right to left, undoing each box with its inverse: plus 1 becomes minus 1, times 3 becomes divided by 3. Learners who try to do that from a sentence often undo the operations in the wrong order and get an input that does not work. The diagram is also a stepping stone. In Term 2 you will meet tables of inputs and outputs for numeric patterns, and in later grades the diagram becomes a function and then a formula. Each of those is the same machine drawn in a more compact way. So the diagram is not a substitute for knowing the rule; it is a way of holding a multi-step rule in your hands so that forwards and backwards both become mechanical. Once a rule is in your head so firmly that you never confuse the order, you may skip the drawing; until then, draw it.

### subtopic_2 — Finding the Rule
**Assistant:** How do the differences tell me the multiplier? That feels like a trick.

**Tutor:** It is not a trick; it follows from what multiplying does. Suppose the rule is times 4 then plus 1. Going from input 1 to input 2, the input increases by 1, so the part that is multiplied by 4 increases by 4, and the plus 1 is the same for both, so it adds nothing to the change. The output therefore goes up by exactly 4. The same happens from 2 to 3 and from 3 to 4. So when the inputs step up by 1, the outputs step up by the multiplier every time, and reading the step tells you the multiplier directly. The added or subtracted number does not show up in the differences at all, because it is the same for every input; it shows up only when you compare the first output to what the multiplier alone would give. For outputs 5, 9, 13, 17, the step is 4, so the multiplier is 4; 1 times 4 is 4 and the output is 5, so the rule adds 1. It is worth knowing when this reasoning fails. If the inputs do not step up by 1, say they are 2, 4, 6, 8, the output differences are twice the multiplier. And if the differences are not constant, as in 2, 4, 8, 16, the rule is not a times-and-add rule at all. The method is reliable precisely because you understand why it works, and that understanding tells you when not to use it.

### subtopic_3 — Same Rule, Three Ways
**Assistant:** What does equivalent mean? If two rules give 13 for input 4, aren't they the same?

**Tutor:** Equivalent means they give the same output for every possible input, not just for one. Two rules agreeing on a single number is a coincidence that happens surprisingly often. Take times 3 then plus 1, and plus 9 on its own. For input 4 the first gives 13 and the second gives 13 too. But for input 1 the first gives 4 and the second gives 10, so they are not the same rule. One disagreement is enough to prove two rules different; no number of agreements can prove them the same by testing alone, although if two rules agree on several inputs you have good reason to look for an explanation. The explanation is what settles it. Double the number, add the number again and add 1 is equivalent to times 3 then plus 1 because doubling a number and adding it once more is three of that number, whatever the number is; that argument covers every input at once. So the honest way to handle equivalence is in two steps: test a few inputs to catch rules that are obviously different, and then explain why the ones that keep agreeing must agree for every input. This idea of a statement being true for all numbers rather than for one is the heart of algebra, and you are meeting it here in a gentle form with flow diagrams.
