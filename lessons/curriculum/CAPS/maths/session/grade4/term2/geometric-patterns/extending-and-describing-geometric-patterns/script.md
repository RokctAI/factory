# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers investigating and extending geometric patterns, looking for relationships or rules in sequences that are not limited to a constant difference or ratio and in patterns of learners' own creation, and describing the observed rules in words. The anchor is a row of matchstick squares built by a learner in Bethlehem, a staircase of blocks that grows one step at a time, and dots arranged in triangles. The governing idea is that a geometric pattern is a sequence of pictures that changes in a regular way, that counting a feature of each picture gives a number sequence, and that the rule can be described either from one picture to the next or from the position of the picture. Drawing the next pictures, counting to make a sequence and describing the rule in words are examinable.

## Subtopic: Seeing What Changes

A geometric pattern is a row of pictures that change in a regular way. Picture 1 is one square made of 4 matchsticks. Picture 2 is two squares side by side, sharing a side, made of 7 matchsticks. Picture 3 is three squares in a row, made of 10 matchsticks. To extend the pattern, ask what was done to get from each picture to the next: one more square was added on the right, and it needed 3 new matchsticks because one side was shared.

Counting a feature of each picture turns the pattern into a number sequence. Matchsticks: 4, 7, 10. Squares: 1, 2, 3. Both sequences describe the same pictures. The matchstick sequence grows by 3 each time, so picture 4 needs 13 matchsticks and picture 5 needs 16. Drawing picture 4 and counting confirms 13.

A staircase of blocks: picture 1 is one block. Picture 2 is a step of 1 block beside a column of 2, which is 3 blocks. Picture 3 adds a column of 3, making 6 blocks. Picture 4 adds a column of 4, making 10. The sequence 1, 3, 6, 10 does not grow by the same amount each time: the differences are 2, 3, 4, growing by one each time. Not every pattern has a constant difference.

Describe what you see before counting. For the matchsticks: each picture adds one square to the right end. For the staircase: each picture adds a new column one block taller than the last. A good description lets someone who has not seen the pictures draw them.

The questions for this section are with you now: describing how one picture becomes the next, counting a feature to make a sequence, and recognising when the differences change.

## Subtopic: Describing the Rule in Words

There are two ways to describe a pattern's rule. The first says how to get from one picture to the next: add 3 matchsticks, or add a column one taller. The second says how to get the number straight from the picture's position: for the matchsticks, multiply the picture number by 3 and add 1, because 3 times 1 plus 1 is 4, 3 times 2 plus 1 is 7, 3 times 3 plus 1 is 10.

The position rule is more powerful. To find the matchsticks in picture 20 with the first rule you must add 3 nineteen times. With the position rule you work out 3 times 20 plus 1, which is 61, in one step. In Grade 4 you are mostly asked for the next few pictures and the rule from one to the next, but seeing the position rule when it is easy is good practice.

Why 3 times the position plus 1? Look at the picture. Every square contributes 3 matchsticks, a top, a bottom and a right side, and the very first square also needs a left side, which is the extra 1. The rule is in the picture, not just in the numbers. A rule that can be explained from the picture is a rule you can trust.

Triangle dots: picture 1 is 1 dot, picture 2 is a triangle of 3 dots, picture 3 is a triangle of 6 dots, picture 4 is 10 dots. The one-to-next rule is add a new bottom row one dot longer than the last. The sequence 1, 3, 6, 10 is the same as the staircase, because a staircase is a triangle of blocks. Different pictures can hide the same numbers.

The questions for this section are with you now: describing a rule from one picture to the next, describing a rule from the position, and explaining a rule from the picture itself.

## Subtopic: Patterns of Your Own

Making your own pattern is the best test of understanding. Choose a starting picture and a rule for changing it, then draw four pictures and write down the sequence you get by counting. For example, start with a T shape of 5 matchsticks and add 2 matchsticks to the top bar each time: 5, 7, 9, 11. Or start with a cross of 5 dots and add a dot to each of the four arms: 5, 9, 13, 17.

Your pattern must be regular: the same change every time, or a change that itself changes in a regular way. A pattern that adds 2, then 5, then 1 is not a pattern anyone can continue. When you swap patterns with a friend, each of you should be able to draw the other's picture 5 from the first four pictures alone. If you cannot, the rule is not clear enough.

Describe your rule in both ways if you can. For the cross: add 4 dots each time, or 4 times the position plus 1. For the T shape: add 2 each time, or 2 times the position plus 3. Checking the position rule on every picture you drew is the way to be sure it is right.

Geometric patterns appear in real things. Tiles on a floor, bricks in a wall, beads on a necklace, the panels of a fence all grow by a regular rule. In a brick wall with staggered rows, each extra row adds the same number of bricks. Spotting the rule tells a builder how many bricks a longer wall will need, which is the same reasoning as picture 20.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: counting 4 new matchsticks for each new square; the shared side means only 3 are added. Two: assuming every pattern grows by the same amount; the staircase 1, 3, 6, 10 grows by 2, 3, 4. Three: describing the rule as just the numbers without the picture; a rule you can see in the picture is one you can trust. Four: making a pattern whose change is different every time; a pattern must be regular enough for a friend to continue.

The questions for this section are with you now: creating a regular pattern, checking that a friend can continue it, and describing its rule both ways.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the matchsticks, the block staircase and the triangle dots — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: What Changed

Look at the pictures in a row. Ask: what was added to get the next one? Matchstick squares: one more square each time, using 3 new matchsticks. 4, 7, 10, 13.

Count something in each picture and write the numbers down. That is your sequence.

Some patterns grow by more each time. The block staircase: 1, 3, 6, 10. The jumps are 2, 3, 4. Still a pattern, just a growing one.

Keep the matchsticks in mind and try the questions for this part: seeing what changes and writing the sequence.

## Subtopic: Say the Rule

Say the rule two ways. From one picture to the next: add 3 matchsticks. From the picture's number: 3 times the number plus 1.

Check it: picture 3 is 3 times 3 plus 1, which is 10. Yes. Picture 10 would be 31.

The rule is in the picture. Each square gives 3 sticks, and the first square needs one extra on the left. That is the plus 1.

Hold the two kinds of rule in mind and try this part's questions: describing a rule in words.

## Subtopic: Make Your Own

Pick a starting shape and a rule. T shape of 5 sticks, add 2 to the top each time: 5, 7, 9, 11.

Draw four pictures, count, write the sequence. Give it to a friend. If they can draw picture 5, your rule is clear.

Patterns are everywhere: bricks in a wall, tiles on a floor, beads on a string. Spot the rule and you can predict the big picture.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A geometric pattern is a sequence of pictures that change by a regular rule. Count a feature of each picture to get a number sequence, and find the differences to see whether the growth is constant. Describe the rule from one picture to the next, and where possible from the picture's position, explaining it from the picture itself. A pattern of your own must be regular enough for someone else to continue.

Hold your own pattern in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: making and describing patterns.
