# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers recognising, drawing and describing lines of symmetry in 2D shapes. The anchor is a butterfly photographed in a garden in Pietermaritzburg, capital letters cut from card, and the shapes on flags and road signs. The governing idea is that a line of symmetry divides a shape into two halves that match exactly when the shape is folded along it, that a shape may have no lines, one line or several, and that the number of lines of symmetry is a property that helps describe and compare shapes. Identifying whether a line is a line of symmetry, drawing all the lines of symmetry of a shape and counting them are examinable.

## Subtopic: What a Line of Symmetry Is

A shape has a line of symmetry if it can be folded along that line so that the two halves fit exactly on top of each other. The fold line is the line of symmetry, also called a mirror line, because each half is the mirror image of the other. A butterfly has a line of symmetry down the middle of its body: fold it there and the left wing lands on the right wing.

Two tests find a line of symmetry. The folding test: cut the shape out, fold along the line, and see whether the halves match with no overhang. The mirror test: stand a small mirror on the line and look; if the reflection completes the shape exactly as it really is, the line is a line of symmetry. Both tests are fair, and the folding test is the one you can use on paper in a test by imagining the fold.

A line that cuts a shape into two equal halves is not always a line of symmetry. A diagonal of a rectangle cuts it into two equal triangles, but fold along it and the triangles do not land on each other; they stick out. Equal halves is not enough; the halves must be mirror images that fit exactly when folded.

Lines of symmetry can be vertical, horizontal or slanted. The capital letter A has a vertical line. The letter B has a horizontal line. A square has slanted lines along its diagonals as well as vertical and horizontal ones. Always check all directions rather than only the up-and-down line.

The questions for this section are with you now: the folding and mirror tests, why equal halves are not always symmetric, and lines in all directions.

## Subtopic: Lines of Symmetry of Common Shapes

A square has 4 lines of symmetry: one vertical, one horizontal and two along the diagonals. Fold a paper square each way and the halves match every time. A rectangle that is not a square has 2 lines of symmetry, the vertical and the horizontal through its centre; its diagonals fail the folding test.

An equilateral triangle, with three equal sides, has 3 lines of symmetry, each running from a corner to the middle of the opposite side. A triangle with two equal sides, called isosceles, has 1 line. A triangle with no equal sides, called scalene, has no lines of symmetry at all. A regular pentagon has 5 lines and a regular hexagon has 6: a regular polygon has as many lines of symmetry as it has sides.

A circle has infinitely many lines of symmetry, because any line through its centre folds it onto itself. Everyday shapes vary: a heart has 1 vertical line, a plain T-shirt has 1, a kite has 1 along its long diagonal, and most leaves have 1 down the middle vein. Many shapes have none; an L shape and a scalene triangle are examples.

To draw all the lines of symmetry, draw the shape, then test every candidate line in turn with an imagined fold, drawing in those that pass as dotted lines. For a square, that means drawing four dotted lines. Count them and write the number. A common slip is to stop after the obvious vertical line; a square has three more.

The questions for this section are with you now: the number of lines of symmetry of squares, rectangles, triangles, regular polygons and circles, and drawing them all.

## Subtopic: Symmetry in Letters, Flags and Everyday Life

Capital letters are a good test ground. A, M, T, U, V, W and Y have one vertical line of symmetry. B, C, D, E and K have one horizontal line. H, I, O and X have both a vertical and a horizontal line. F, G, J, L, N, P, Q, R, S and Z have no lines of symmetry. Cut the letters out and fold to check; N, S and Z look balanced but fail the fold test, because they have a different kind of symmetry, turning symmetry, which you will meet later.

Flags and signs use symmetry deliberately. A stop sign, a regular octagon, has 8 lines of symmetry. A yield sign, an equilateral triangle, has 3. The South African flag has no line of symmetry because of its colours and the Y shape running from the left, even though the rectangle it is printed on has 2. When you test a picture for symmetry, the colours and patterns count, not just the outline.

Symmetry appears all through nature and design. Faces, leaves, butterflies and starfish are approximately symmetric. Buildings often have a vertical line of symmetry through the front door. Pottery and beadwork patterns from many South African cultures use lines of symmetry to build their designs, as you will see in the transformations topic in Term 3.

Describing symmetry in words means saying how many lines a shape has and where they run. The square has 4 lines of symmetry: vertical, horizontal and both diagonals. The letter H has 2 lines of symmetry, one vertical and one horizontal. The letter F has no lines of symmetry. A full description gives the count and the directions.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: treating a rectangle's diagonal as a line of symmetry because it makes equal halves; the halves do not fit when folded. Two: giving a square only 2 lines of symmetry; the two diagonals bring the total to 4. Three: saying the letter S has a line of symmetry because it looks balanced; no fold makes its halves match. Four: ignoring colours when testing a flag; the pattern must match, not only the outline.

The questions for this section are with you now: the symmetry of capital letters, flags and signs where colour matters, and describing symmetry in words.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the butterfly, the letters and the flag — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Fold It

A line of symmetry is a fold line. Fold the shape along it and the two halves sit exactly on top of each other. The butterfly folds down the middle of its body.

Test with a fold or a mirror. If the halves do not match, it is not a line of symmetry, even if the two pieces are the same size. A rectangle's diagonal makes equal pieces but is not a fold line.

Lines can go up and down, side to side, or slanted. Check them all.

Keep the butterfly in mind and try the questions for this part: what a line of symmetry is.

## Subtopic: Count the Lines

Square: 4 lines, up, across and both diagonals. Rectangle: only 2, up and across. Its diagonals fail.

Triangles: three equal sides give 3 lines, two equal sides give 1, no equal sides give none. Regular shapes have as many lines as sides: a regular hexagon has 6.

A circle has endless lines, any line through the centre. Draw all the lines you find as dotted lines and count them.

Hold the square and the rectangle in mind and try this part's questions: counting lines of symmetry.

## Subtopic: Letters and Flags

A, M, T, U, V, W, Y: fold down the middle. B, C, D, E, K: fold across. H, I, O, X: both ways. F, G, J, L, N, P, Q, R, S, Z: no fold works.

Colours matter. The South African flag's rectangle has 2 lines, but the flag's pattern has none.

Describe symmetry by saying how many lines and where: H has 2 lines, one vertical and one horizontal.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A line of symmetry is a fold line along which the two halves of a shape fit exactly; test with a fold or a mirror, and check vertical, horizontal and slanted lines. Equal halves are not enough. A square has 4 lines, a rectangle 2, an equilateral triangle 3, a regular polygon as many as its sides, a circle infinitely many, and many shapes none. Letters, flags and pictures are tested with their colours and patterns included, and symmetry is described by the number of lines and their directions.
