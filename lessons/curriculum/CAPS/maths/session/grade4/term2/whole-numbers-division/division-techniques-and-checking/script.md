# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the techniques used to perform and check division: estimation, building up and breaking down numbers, and multiplication and division as inverse operations. The anchor is 144 athletes at a sports day in Mthatha spread across 8 lanes, R675 of bus money shared among 5 teams, and 252 exercise books packed into stacks of 7. The governing idea is that a division can be reshaped into easier steps without changing the quotient, and that multiplying the quotient by the divisor, then adding any remainder, must give back the dividend. Choosing and showing two techniques, and checking a division by multiplication, are examinable.

## Subtopic: Estimating a Quotient

Before dividing, estimate. 144 divided by 8: 8 times 10 is 80 and 8 times 20 is 160, so the answer is between 10 and 20, nearer 20. R675 divided by 5: 5 times 100 is 500 and 5 times 200 is 1 000, so between 100 and 200, nearer 100. The estimate tells you how many digits the answer has and roughly what the first digit is.

A quick way to estimate is to round the dividend to a number the divisor fits neatly. 144 is close to 160, and 160 divided by 8 is 20, so 144 divided by 8 is a little under 20. 252 is close to 280, and 280 divided by 7 is 40, so 252 divided by 7 is a little under 40. 675 is close to 700, and 700 divided by 5 is 140, so 675 divided by 5 is a little under 140.

The estimate catches the two commonest division errors: a missing digit and a misplaced chunk. If 675 divided by 5 comes out as 15, the estimate of about 140 shouts that a digit is missing. If 252 divided by 7 comes out as 46, the estimate of under 40 shows a chunk has been counted twice.

Estimation also tells you whether a remainder is sensible. 144 divided by 8 cannot leave a remainder of 8 or more; if your working shows one, you have stopped chunking too early. Estimate, divide, compare, and only then write the answer.

The questions for this section are with you now: estimating a quotient between two multiples, rounding the dividend to a friendly number, and using the estimate to catch errors.

## Subtopic: Breaking Down and Building Up

Breaking down splits the dividend into pieces the divisor fits. 144 divided by 8: 144 is 80 plus 64. 80 divided by 8 is 10 and 64 divided by 8 is 8, so 18. The sports day has 18 athletes per lane. Choose the first piece as the biggest multiple of 10 times the divisor that fits, then look at what is left.

R675 divided by 5: 675 is 500 plus 150 plus 25. 500 divided by 5 is 100, 150 divided by 5 is 30, 25 divided by 5 is 5. Add: 135. Each team gets R135. Three pieces, each a known fact, and the answer is just their sum. Breaking down is chunking written as pieces instead of take-aways; they are the same idea.

Building up starts from a multiplication you know and climbs. 252 divided by 7: 7 times 30 is 210, which is less than 252. The gap is 42, and 7 times 6 is 42. So 7 times 36 is 252, and 252 divided by 7 is 36. There are 36 stacks of books. Building up is multiplication backwards made visible.

Halving helps when the divisor is even. 144 divided by 8: halve 144 to 72, halve to 36, halve to 18. Three halvings divide by 8. 675 divided by 5 can also be found by dividing by 10 and doubling: 675 divided by 10 is 67 and a half, doubled is 135. That last route uses a half, which is fine if you are comfortable with it; the breaking-down route avoids it.

The questions for this section are with you now: splitting the dividend into friendly pieces, building up from a known product, and halving to divide by 2, 4 and 8.

## Subtopic: Checking with Multiplication

Division and multiplication undo each other, so the check for a division is to multiply back. If 144 divided by 8 is 18, then 8 times 18 must be 144. 8 times 10 is 80 and 8 times 8 is 64, total 144. The division is confirmed. If 675 divided by 5 is 135, then 5 times 135 should be 675: 5 times 100 is 500, 5 times 30 is 150, 5 times 5 is 25, total 675. Confirmed.

When there is a remainder, multiply back and then add the remainder. 255 books in stacks of 7: 252 divided by 7 is 36, so 255 divided by 7 is 36 remainder 3. Check: 7 times 36 is 252, plus 3 is 255. The rule is divisor times quotient plus remainder equals dividend.

The check must be a different operation from the one you used. If you divided by chunking, multiply back. If you built up from a multiplication, that building up already contains a multiplication, so check by chunking or by estimation instead. Two different roads to the same answer is the standard.

When the check fails, the answer is wrong, not the check. Suppose 144 divided by 8 came out as 17; 8 times 17 is 136, not 144, so 17 is wrong and 8 is missing, which is one more 8, so the answer is 18. The check does not just say wrong; it often shows you by how much.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: skipping the estimate and accepting 15 for 675 divided by 5; the estimate of about 140 shows a digit is missing. Two: checking a division with a remainder by multiplying only; the remainder must be added back to reach the dividend. Three: breaking 144 into 100 and 44 to divide by 8; choose pieces the divisor fits, like 80 and 64. Four: treating a failed check as a mistake in the check; the answer is wrong, and the gap often shows by how much.

The questions for this section are with you now: multiplying back to check a division, adding the remainder in the check, and using a failed check to fix the answer.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the lanes, the bus money and the exercise books — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Guess How Big

Guess first. 144 divided by 8: 8 times 10 is 80, 8 times 20 is 160. So between 10 and 20, nearer 20.

Round to a friendly number. 675 is near 700, and 700 divided by 5 is 140. So a bit under 140.

If your answer is 15 for 675 divided by 5, the guess says no. A digit is missing.

Keep the lanes in mind and try the questions for this part: estimating before dividing.

## Subtopic: Friendly Pieces

Break the number into pieces the divisor likes. 144 divided by 8: 80 and 64. 10 and 8. Answer 18.

675 divided by 5: 500, 150 and 25. 100, 30 and 5. Answer 135.

Or build up. 252 divided by 7: 7 times 30 is 210, 42 to go, 7 times 6 is 42. So 36.

Hold the bus money in mind and try this part's questions: breaking down and building up.

## Subtopic: Multiply Back to Check

Divide, then multiply back. 144 divided by 8 is 18? 8 times 18 is 144. Yes.

Remainder? Multiply back and add it. 255 divided by 7 is 36 remainder 3. 7 times 36 is 252, plus 3 is 255. Yes.

If the check fails, the answer is wrong. 8 times 17 is 136, not 144, so 17 is one 8 short. The answer is 18.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Estimate a quotient between two multiples or by rounding the dividend to a friendly number. Break the dividend into pieces the divisor fits, build up from a known product, or halve repeatedly for even divisors. Check by multiplying the quotient by the divisor and adding any remainder to get back the dividend. Use two different techniques every time.

Hold the multiply-back check in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: proving a division.
