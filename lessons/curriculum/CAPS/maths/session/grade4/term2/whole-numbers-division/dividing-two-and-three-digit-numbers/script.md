# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers division of at least a two-digit number by a one-digit number, and of at least a whole three-digit number by a one-digit number. The anchor is 84 sweets shared among 4 friends at a school in Kuruman, 96 learners seated in rows of 8, and 372 loaves of bread packed into boxes of 6. The governing idea is that division is the inverse of multiplication, so a division is answered by asking how many times the divisor fits into the number, and that a three-digit number can be divided by breaking it into multiples of the divisor. Division by sharing and by grouping, division with and without remainders, and dividing by chunking are examinable.

## Subtopic: Two Faces of Division

Division answers two kinds of question. Sharing: 84 sweets are shared equally among 4 friends; how many does each get? Grouping: 96 learners sit in rows of 8; how many rows are there? Both are divisions, 84 divided by 4 and 96 divided by 8, and both are solved by the same thinking: how many times does the divisor fit?

Division undoes multiplication. 84 divided by 4 asks which number times 4 is 84. Since 4 times 20 is 80 and 4 times 1 is 4, 4 times 21 is 84, so each friend gets 21 sweets. 96 divided by 8 asks which number times 8 is 96; 8 times 12 is 96, so there are 12 rows. Every division fact is a multiplication fact read backwards, which is why the tables matter so much here.

The parts of a division have names. In 84 divided by 4 equals 21, the 84 is the dividend, the number being divided; the 4 is the divisor, the number you divide by; and 21 is the quotient, the answer. You do not need to use the words every time, but you will see them in textbooks and tests.

Order matters in division. 84 divided by 4 is 21, but 4 divided by 84 is a small fraction, not 21. The dividend is the whole amount and comes first; the divisor is the size of the share or the group. Read the story to see which number is the whole and which is the size.

The questions for this section are with you now: sharing and grouping as division, division as multiplication backwards, and keeping the dividend first.

## Subtopic: Dividing a Two-Digit Number

To divide a two-digit number, break it into pieces that the divisor fits into easily. 84 divided by 4: break 84 into 80 plus 4. 80 divided by 4 is 20, and 4 divided by 4 is 1, so 84 divided by 4 is 21. Breaking into tens and units works when the tens are a multiple of the divisor.

When the tens are not a multiple, choose friendlier pieces. 96 divided by 8: 90 is not a multiple of 8, so break 96 into 80 plus 16 instead. 80 divided by 8 is 10 and 16 divided by 8 is 2, so 12. Look for the biggest multiple of 10 that the divisor fits into, then deal with what is left.

Sometimes there is a remainder. 85 sweets among 4 friends: 84 divided by 4 is 21, and 1 sweet is left over. We write 85 divided by 4 is 21 remainder 1. Check: 4 times 21 is 84, plus 1 is 85. The remainder is always smaller than the divisor; if it were 4 or more, another sweet could go to each friend.

Estimate before dividing. 84 divided by 4 is a bit more than 80 divided by 4, so a bit more than 20. 96 divided by 8 is a bit less than 100 divided by 8, which is about 12. If an answer comes out as 210 or 2, the estimate rejects it.

The questions for this section are with you now: breaking a two-digit number into pieces the divisor fits, handling a remainder, and estimating.

## Subtopic: Dividing a Three-Digit Number by Chunking

372 loaves are packed in boxes of 6. How many boxes? 372 divided by 6. Chunking takes away big multiples of 6 until nothing is left. 6 times 50 is 300, so take 300 away: 72 left. 6 times 10 is 60, take it away: 12 left. 6 times 2 is 12, take it away: 0 left. Add the chunks: 50 plus 10 plus 2 is 62 boxes.

Chunking works with any multiples you are sure of. Someone else might take 6 times 60, which is 360, leaving 12, then 6 times 2, giving 60 plus 2, which is 62. Bigger chunks mean fewer steps, but any correct chunks reach the same answer. Write each chunk and what is left so the steps can be checked.

Chunking with a remainder: 375 loaves in boxes of 6. Take 360, 15 left. Take 12, 3 left. 3 is less than 6, so stop. 375 divided by 6 is 62 remainder 3: 62 full boxes and 3 loaves over. Check: 6 times 62 is 372, plus 3 is 375.

Estimate first: 372 is close to 360, which is 6 times 60, so the answer is a little more than 60. A quotient of 620 or 6 would fail. Estimation is the quickest guard against misplacing a chunk, and you should do it before every three-digit division.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: dividing the small number by the big one, 4 divided by 84; the whole amount is the dividend and comes first. Two: leaving a remainder of 5 when dividing by 4; the remainder must be smaller than the divisor, so share out one more. Three: splitting 96 into 90 and 6 to divide by 8; choose pieces the divisor fits into, like 80 and 16. Four: forgetting to add the chunks at the end; 50, 10 and 2 must be totalled to give 62.

The questions for this section are with you now: dividing a three-digit number by taking away chunks, choosing chunk sizes, and handling the remainder.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the sweets, the rows of learners and the bread boxes — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Share It or Group It

Dividing answers two questions. Sharing: 84 sweets among 4 friends, how many each? Grouping: 96 learners in rows of 8, how many rows? Both are divides.

Division is multiplication backwards. 84 divided by 4: which number times 4 makes 84? 4 times 21 is 84. So 21.

The whole amount comes first. 84 divided by 4, not 4 divided by 84.

Keep the sweets in mind and try the questions for this part: sharing, grouping and multiplication backwards.

## Subtopic: Break It into Pieces

84 divided by 4: break 84 into 80 and 4. 80 divided by 4 is 20. 4 divided by 4 is 1. Together, 21.

96 divided by 8: 90 does not work for 8, so use 80 and 16. 80 divided by 8 is 10, 16 divided by 8 is 2. Together, 12.

85 sweets among 4: 84 goes in, 1 is left. 21 remainder 1. The remainder is always smaller than what you divide by.

Hold the pieces in mind and try this part's questions: dividing two-digit numbers, with and without remainders.

## Subtopic: Big Numbers in Chunks

372 loaves, boxes of 6. Take away big chunks of 6. 6 times 50 is 300, so 72 left. 6 times 10 is 60, so 12 left. 6 times 2 is 12, so 0 left. Chunks: 50, 10, 2. Answer 62 boxes.

375 loaves? Take 360, 15 left. Take 12, 3 left. 3 is less than 6, so stop. 62 remainder 3.

Guess first. 372 is near 360, and 6 times 60 is 360. So a bit more than 60.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Division shares an amount equally or groups it into equal sets, and it undoes multiplication: 84 divided by 4 asks which number times 4 is 84. Break a two-digit number into pieces the divisor fits into. Divide a three-digit number by chunking, taking away large multiples of the divisor and adding the chunks. A remainder is what is left and is always smaller than the divisor. Estimate first.

Hold the bread boxes in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: chunking three-digit numbers.
