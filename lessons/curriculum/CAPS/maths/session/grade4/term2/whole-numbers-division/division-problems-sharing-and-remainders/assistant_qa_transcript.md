### subtopic_1 — Sharing Money and Measurement
**Assistant:** How can the same division give 8 for one question and 9 for another? That seems unfair.

**Tutor:** It seems unfair until you notice that the two questions are asking about different things, and the division is only the first step for both. 125 divided by 15 is 8 remainder 5 whatever the story, and that part never changes. What changes is what the story does with the 5. When the 5 are learners who need to get to the outing, nobody is going to leave them at the gate, so a ninth minibus is called even though it will be mostly empty, and the answer to how many minibuses is 9. When the 5 are sweets that do not fill a bag, the shop cannot sell a part bag, so they stay in the jar, and the answer to how many full bags is 8. Same numbers, different question, different answer. This is why word problems ask you to read to the end and why a bare 8 remainder 5 often earns only part of the marks. The division gives you the raw material; the sentence tells you how to shape it. A useful habit is to say your answer back into the story and see whether it would satisfy the person asking. Eight minibuses for 125 learners would make a teacher ask about the other five. Nine bags of sweets when the ninth has five sweets in it would make a customer complain. If the answer would not satisfy the person in the story, it is not the answer to their question.

### subtopic_2 — What the Remainder Means
**Assistant:** Why do I divide to find a rate? Last term we multiplied with rates.

**Tutor:** Both are true, and they are the two directions of the same relationship. A rate is an amount for one unit, like 80 kilometres for one hour. If you know the rate and want the amount for several units, you multiply: 80 kilometres per hour for 6 hours is 480 kilometres. If you know the total and the number of units and want the rate, you divide: 480 kilometres in 6 hours is 480 divided by 6, which is 80 kilometres for one hour. Finding the rate means finding the amount for one, and dividing by the number of units is exactly how you get down to one. The same two directions appear with prices. R35 per box times 8 boxes gives R280; R280 for 8 boxes divided by 8 gives R35 per box. In the shop, dividing is usually what you need, because the shelf shows the total price for a pack and you want to know whether the big pack or the small pack is better value per item. Supermarkets print the price per kilogram on the label for exactly this reason, and it is found by dividing the price by the mass. So multiply when you are scaling a rate up to many units, divide when you are bringing a total down to one unit, and remember that each operation checks the other: 480 divided by 6 is 80 because 80 times 6 is 480.

### subtopic_3 — Rate and Ratio by Division
**Assistant:** In sharing R100 in the ratio 3 to 2, why do I add 3 and 2 first?

**Tutor:** Because the ratio tells you how many parts each person gets, and before you can know the size of one part you have to know how many parts the whole has been cut into. A ratio of 3 to 2 means that for every 3 parts the first learner gets, the second gets 2. Lay the parts out as blocks: three blocks for the first learner and two for the second, five blocks in a row. The R100 covers all five blocks, so each block is worth 100 divided by 5, which is R20. Now the first learner's three blocks are worth 3 times 20, which is R60, and the second learner's two blocks are worth 2 times 20, which is R40. The check that 60 plus 40 is 100 confirms that nothing has been lost. The common mistake is to treat the 3 and the 2 as if they were the shares themselves, giving R30 and R20 and leaving R50 unshared, or to divide R100 by 3 and by 2 separately, which gives amounts that add to far more than R100. The ratio is a recipe for cutting, not a pair of amounts. Adding the parts tells you how many slices the cake is in, dividing tells you the size of a slice, and multiplying hands out the right number of slices to each person. Those three steps work for any ratio, including ones with three people, like 2 to 3 to 5, where there would be 10 parts.
