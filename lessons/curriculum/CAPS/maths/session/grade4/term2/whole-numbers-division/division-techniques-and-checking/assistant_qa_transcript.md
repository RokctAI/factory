### subtopic_1 — Estimating a Quotient
**Assistant:** Why estimate a division? The answer is usually small anyway.

**Tutor:** The answer is often small, but the mistakes are not. The commonest division error is not getting a digit slightly wrong; it is losing a whole digit or a whole chunk, which makes the answer ten times too small or fifty too big. When a learner writes 15 for 675 divided by 5, every step looked reasonable to them, and only an estimate of about 140 reveals that the hundreds have vanished. Estimating also tells you where to start chunking. If you know 144 divided by 8 is between 10 and 20, you take 8 times 10 as your first chunk without hesitating, and you know you are finished after one more small chunk. Without the estimate learners sometimes take tiny chunks of 8 at a time and run out of patience. There is a quieter reason too. Estimating builds number sense, the feel for how big things are, and that feel is what you will rely on when calculators do the arithmetic later in life. A calculator will cheerfully tell you that 675 divided by 5 is 1 350 if you press a wrong key, and the only thing standing between you and that error is your sense that the answer should be about 140. Five seconds of estimating is the cheapest insurance in mathematics.

### subtopic_2 — Breaking Down and Building Up
**Assistant:** Breaking down and chunking look the same to me. Are they?

**Tutor:** They are the same idea wearing two outfits, and it is good that you noticed. Chunking starts with the whole dividend and takes away big multiples of the divisor until little or nothing is left, recording each chunk. Breaking down starts by splitting the dividend into pieces that are each a multiple of the divisor, divides each piece, and adds the results. For 675 divided by 5, chunking says: take 500, which is 5 times 100, leaving 175; take 150, which is 5 times 30, leaving 25; take 25, which is 5 times 5, leaving 0; total 135. Breaking down says: 675 is 500 plus 150 plus 25; 100 plus 30 plus 5 is 135. The same three pieces appear in both, and the only difference is whether you write them as take-aways or as a split. Use whichever layout feels clearer. Chunking is safer when you cannot see the split in advance, because you can take any multiple you are sure of and the leftovers tell you what to do next. Breaking down is faster when the pieces are obvious, as they are for 675 and 5. Both are the distributive property in reverse, the idea from Term 1 that a number can be split, each part dealt with, and the results recombined. Recognising that one idea underneath two methods is exactly the kind of understanding that makes maths feel smaller and friendlier.

### subtopic_3 — Checking with Multiplication
**Assistant:** If the check fails, how do I find the mistake?

**Tutor:** Use the size of the gap. A failed check does not just say wrong; it tells you by how much, and that number is a clue. If you think 144 divided by 8 is 17, the check gives 8 times 17 equals 136, which is 8 short of 144, and 8 is exactly one divisor. So you missed one group of 8, and the answer is 18. If the check comes out 16 short, you missed two groups. If the check comes out too big, you counted a chunk twice, and the excess tells you how many times. When the gap is not a neat multiple of the divisor, the mistake is probably in the multiplication you used for the check itself or in the remainder, so redo the check slowly in pieces: 8 times 10 is 80, 8 times 7 is 56, total 136. Then look back at your chunks. Add up the amounts you took away and see whether they total the dividend; if they do not, the subtraction between chunks has slipped, and you can find the line where it happened. Mistakes in division are almost always one of three things: a chunk added wrongly at the end, a subtraction slip between chunks, or a wrong table fact. The gap in the check points towards which one, and the written chunks let you find the exact line.
