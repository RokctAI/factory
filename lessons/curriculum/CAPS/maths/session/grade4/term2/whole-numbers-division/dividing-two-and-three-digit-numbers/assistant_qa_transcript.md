### subtopic_1 — Two Faces of Division
**Assistant:** Why do we need both sharing and grouping? Isn't division just division?

**Tutor:** The arithmetic is the same, which is why we call both of them division, but the stories feel completely different and learners who only know one get stuck when the other appears. In sharing you know how many groups there are and want the size of each group: 84 sweets, 4 friends, how many each. In grouping you know the size of each group and want how many groups: 96 learners, rows of 8, how many rows. Picture how you would act them out. Sharing is dealing out, one sweet to each friend in turn until the bag is empty, then counting a pile. Grouping is counting out 8 at a time and seeing how many rows you make. Both end at the same kind of number sentence, but the remainder means different things. In sharing, a remainder of 1 is a sweet nobody gets. In grouping, a remainder of 3 is a part-row of 3 learners. Knowing both faces also helps with the tricky question of which number comes first. Whether sharing or grouping, the whole amount is the dividend, and the number of groups or the size of a group is the divisor. Chunking, the method in this lesson, is really the grouping picture: how many sixes can I take out of 372? And the tables, read backwards, serve both. So division is just division, but you need both stories to recognise it when it comes dressed in words.

### subtopic_2 — Dividing a Two-Digit Number
**Assistant:** How do I know which chunks to take? There seem to be so many choices.

**Tutor:** There are many choices, and that is a strength, not a problem, because any correct set of chunks gives the same answer. The only rule is that each chunk must be a multiple of the divisor that you are sure of, and that you keep taking chunks until what is left is smaller than the divisor. The quickest route uses the biggest chunks you can see, which usually means multiples of 10 and of 100. For 372 divided by 6, ask what 6 times 10 is, 60, and what 6 times 100 is, 600. 600 is too big, so try 6 times 50, 300. Take it. Now 72 is left, and 6 times 10 is 60, so take that. Now 12, which is 6 times 2. Three chunks: 50, 10, 2. A learner who knows the 6 times table up to 12 might see at once that 6 times 60 is 360 and need only two chunks. A learner who is less sure might take 6 times 10 six times and then 6 times 2, which is seven chunks and still perfectly correct, just slower. As your tables get stronger your chunks will get bigger without any extra effort. Write each chunk with the amount left beside it, because that record is what lets you, or a marker, find a slip. Chunking is a method where the working really is the answer.

### subtopic_3 — Dividing a Three-Digit Number by Chunking
**Assistant:** What happens to the remainder? Do I just leave it?

**Tutor:** It depends on the story, and reading the story is part of the question. If 375 loaves go into boxes of 6, the remainder 3 is three loaves that do not fill a box, and a baker would either sell them loose or wait for three more. The answer 62 remainder 3 is complete and correct. If the question asks how many boxes are needed to pack every loaf, then the three loose loaves need a box of their own, and the answer is 63 boxes, one of them not full. If the question asks how many full boxes there are, the answer is 62. If 85 sweets are shared among 4 friends, the remainder 1 is a sweet that cannot be split fairly, and most learners would say each gets 21 and one is left over, or perhaps give it to the teacher. Later in the year, in the fractions topic, you will learn that a remainder can be shared too: 1 sweet among 4 friends is a quarter each, so 85 divided by 4 can also be written as 21 and a quarter. For now the rule is to write the remainder clearly, check that it is smaller than the divisor, and then ask what the story wants done with it. The arithmetic ends at 62 remainder 3; the answer to the question might be 62, 63, or 62 with 3 left over, and only the words tell you which.
