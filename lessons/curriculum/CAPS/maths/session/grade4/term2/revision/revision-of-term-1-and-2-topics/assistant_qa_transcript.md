### subtopic_1 — Numbers and the Four Operations
**Assistant:** There are two terms of work in this test. How do I revise without panicking?

**Tutor:** By remembering that two terms of work rest on a small number of ideas, and by revising the ideas rather than the pages. Almost everything in the number topics comes back to place value, the tables, and the two checking habits of estimating and using the inverse. If those are solid, then addition, subtraction, multiplication, division, rounding, number sentences and most of patterns follow. Spend your first revision session on exactly those: say what each digit in a four-digit number is worth, run through the tables that still hesitate, and practise estimating a few sums before calculating. The shape topics rest on naming by properties: count the sides, list the faces, fold for symmetry. One good afternoon with paper cut-outs covers all three. Then do a mixed set of problems, two or three from each topic, writing the number sentence, estimating, calculating, checking and reading the question again for every one, so that the routine becomes automatic. Keep a list of what went wrong and look at it the night before. Do not reread notes passively; doing problems is revision, reading is not. And sleep. A rested learner who knows the ideas will outscore a tired learner who has reread every page. Panic comes from the pile looking big; sort it into its few ideas and the pile shrinks.

### subtopic_2 — Sentences, Properties, Multiples, Factors and Patterns
**Assistant:** Why does the test mix topics in one question? I know each one on its own.

**Tutor:** Because that is how the topics live in the world, and the test is checking whether you can recognise a skill when it is not labelled. At the sports day nobody announces that this is now a division question; there are 192 runners and heats of 8, and you have to see that the structure is sharing into equal groups. A question that gives you a table of fares and asks for a rule is patterns; a question that asks for the fare for 25 passengers using that rule is multiplication; a question asking how many passengers a fare of 65 means is the flow diagram run backwards. Mixing them tests understanding rather than memory of a chapter. The way to prepare is to practise asking, for every problem, what is really being asked. Is something being combined, separated, repeated or shared? Is a rule being found or used? Is a shape being named, sorted or tested for symmetry? Once you can answer that, the right tool from the right topic is obvious. Mixed questions also reward the routine you have practised all year: write the number sentence, estimate, calculate, check, read the question again. That routine works for every topic, which is why it is worth more than any single fact. If a mixed question still stumps you, do the part you can, write it down, and move on; partial marks for a correct first step are real marks.

### subtopic_3 — Shapes, Objects and Symmetry
**Assistant:** What are the mistakes most learners make in this test, so I can avoid them?

**Tutor:** They are mostly reading and checking mistakes rather than arithmetic ones, which is good news, because those are the easiest to fix. First, answering a different question from the one asked: giving the total when the question wanted the change, or 8 remainder 5 when the question wanted the number of minibuses. Reread the question after you have an answer. Second, skipping the estimate and so missing a slipped digit, such as 5 218 for a sum that should be 1 645. Five seconds of rounding catches it. Third, applying a rule where it does not belong: swapping the numbers in a subtraction, or dividing only one side of a ratio. Ask whether the operation is addition or multiplication before you swap. Fourth, in shapes, trusting the eye instead of the properties: calling a tilted square a diamond, calling a ball a circle, counting a rectangle's diagonal as a line of symmetry. Count the sides, list the faces, imagine the fold. Fifth, leaving units off: 385 is not an answer, 385 metres is. And sixth, running out of time by sitting on one hard question. Read the whole paper first, do the quick ones, and come back. None of these needs more knowledge than you already have. They need the habits you have practised all year, used calmly on the day.
