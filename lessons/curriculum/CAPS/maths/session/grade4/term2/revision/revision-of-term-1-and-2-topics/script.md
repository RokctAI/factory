# Part 1 — Expert

This session revises the Term 1 and Term 2 topics: whole numbers to 10 000 with place value and rounding; number sentences and properties of operations; addition, subtraction, multiplication and division with techniques and checks; multiples and factors; geometric and numeric patterns with flow diagrams and tables; properties of 2D shapes and 3D objects; and symmetry. The anchor is a school sports day in Polokwane with 1 248 learners, 8 lanes, medal ribbons in patterns, and equipment in boxes, tins and balls. The governing idea is that each topic supplies a tool, that estimation and inverse operations check every calculation, and that shapes and patterns are described by stated properties and rules. All Term 1 and Term 2 topics are examinable in the mid-year test.

## Subtopic: Numbers and the Four Operations

Place value and rounding: 1 248 learners is 1 000 plus 200 plus 40 plus 8; the 4 is worth 40. To the nearest 100 it is 1 200, to the nearest 1 000 it is 1 000. Comparing from the left, 1 248 is less than 1 300. Counting in 25s from 1 200 gives 1 225, 1 250, 1 275.

Addition and subtraction: 1 248 learners plus 375 visitors is 1 623 people; estimate 1 250 plus 400 is 1 650, close. If 1 623 people are there and 486 leave early, 1 623 minus 486 is 1 137; check by adding 1 137 plus 486, which is 1 623. Round and compensate for 1 623 minus 486: take 500 to get 1 123, add back 14 to get 1 137.

Multiplication: 8 lanes with 24 runners each over the day is 8 times 24, which is 160 plus 32, which is 192 runners. 16 boxes of 25 medals is 16 times 25, halve and double to 8 times 50, which is 400 medals. Check 400 divided by 16 is 25. The grid for 23 times 14 gives 200, 30, 80, 12, total 322.

Division: 192 runners in heats of 8 is 192 divided by 8; 160 divided by 8 is 20 and 32 divided by 8 is 4, so 24 heats. 400 medals shared among 6 schools: 6 times 66 is 396, remainder 4, so 66 each with 4 left. Check: 6 times 66 plus 4 is 400. Division with a remainder must be read against the story: 125 learners in minibuses of 15 need 9 minibuses, not 8.

The questions for this section are with you now: place value and rounding, the four operations with checks, and remainders in context.

## Subtopic: Sentences, Properties, Multiples, Factors and Patterns

Number sentences: box plus 375 equals 1 623 is solved by the inverse, 1 623 minus 375, which is 1 248, and proved by substitution. Properties: swap and regroup for addition and multiplication only; distribute to multiply, 5 times 23 is 100 plus 15, which is 115; adding zero changes nothing.

Multiples and factors: the multiples of 8 are 8, 16, 24, 32, 40 and onward; the factors of 24 are 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 24. 24 runners can line up in 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12 or 24 equal rows. The highest common factor of 24 and 36 is 12; the lowest common multiple of 6 and 8 is 24.

Geometric patterns: medal ribbons laid as matchstick squares use 4, 7, 10, 13 sticks, adding 3 each time; the position rule is 3 times the picture number plus 1, so picture 10 uses 31. Numeric patterns: a constant difference pattern like 20, 35, 50 adds 15; a constant ratio pattern like 3, 6, 12, 24 multiplies by 2; subtract neighbours to test for a difference, divide to test for a ratio.

Flow diagrams and tables: the rule times 12 then plus 5 gives outputs 17, 29, 41, 53 for inputs 1 to 4; run it backwards from 65 by subtracting 5 and dividing by 12 to get 5. Find a rule from a table by reading the output step for the multiplier and fixing the start with a plus or minus, then test every row. Words, diagram, table and number sentence are equivalent only if they agree on every input.

The questions for this section are with you now: solving number sentences, properties, multiples and factors, geometric and numeric patterns, and flow diagrams.

## Subtopic: Shapes, Objects and Symmetry

2D shapes: a polygon is closed with straight sides only, named by side count: triangle, quadrilateral, pentagon, hexagon, heptagon, octagon. Regular means all sides and corners equal; a square is a regular quadrilateral and a rectangle is irregular. A circle has one curved side and is not a polygon. Sort by straight or curved and by side count, and say why. Draw on grid paper by counting squares, with a ruler for slanted sides.

3D objects: the equipment store holds rectangular prisms, the boxes with 6 rectangular faces, 12 edges and 8 vertices; cylinders, the tins with 2 circular faces and 1 curved surface; spheres, the balls with one curved surface; and a square pyramid trophy with 1 square and 4 triangular faces. Sort into flat only, curved only and both. Models are built by taping polygons edge to edge, and the flat pattern is a net.

Symmetry: a line of symmetry is a fold line along which the halves fit exactly. A square has 4, a rectangle 2, an equilateral triangle 3, a regular polygon as many as its sides, a circle infinitely many. A rectangle's diagonal is not a line of symmetry even though it makes equal halves. Letters A and T fold vertically, B and C horizontally, H and O both, S not at all.

Describing a shape or object fully means stating its properties: its name, whether it is regular, the number of sides or faces, flat or curved surfaces, and its lines of symmetry. The stop sign at the gate is a regular octagon with 8 sides, 8 vertices and 8 lines of symmetry. A medal is a circle with no vertices and endless lines of symmetry.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: answering 8 minibuses for 125 learners in 15-seaters; the remainder means a ninth is needed. Two: calling a rectangle's diagonal a line of symmetry; equal halves are not enough, the fold must match. Three: testing a constant ratio pattern by subtracting; divide neighbours to find the ratio. Four: skipping the check; estimate first and prove with the inverse on every calculation.

The questions for this section are with you now: naming and sorting 2D shapes and 3D objects, and counting lines of symmetry.
