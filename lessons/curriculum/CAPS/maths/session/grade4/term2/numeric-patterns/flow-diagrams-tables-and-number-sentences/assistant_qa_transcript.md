### subtopic_1 — Tables from Rules
**Assistant:** Why do we need tables if flow diagrams already show the rule?

**Tutor:** A flow diagram shows the rule but it shows only one input and one output at a time, while a table shows many at once, and that changes what you can see. Lay out the taxi fares for 1 to 6 passengers in a table and the pattern in the outputs jumps out: 17, 29, 41, 53, 65, 77, going up by 12 every row. That constant step is how you discover the multiplier when nobody has told you the rule, which a flow diagram with empty boxes cannot do. A table is also what you actually use in real life. A tuck shop pins up a price list, which is a table of how many vetkoek against how much to pay, and a customer reads the answer off without calculating. A taxi driver keeps a fare table in his head for 1 to 15 passengers. Bus timetables, exchange rates and sports league tables are all tables of inputs and outputs. The flow diagram is the better tool when you want to run the rule backwards or explain the order of steps, and the number sentence is better for a single big calculation. The skill CAPS is building is to move between all of them freely: find a rule from a table, draw it as a diagram, write it as a sentence, and use the sentence to fill a table with inputs the original did not have. Each form shows the rule from a different angle, and together they show it completely.

### subtopic_2 — Rules from Tables and Missing Values
**Assistant:** When I run a two-step rule backwards, why must I undo the last step first?

**Tutor:** Because that is how undoing works for anything, not only for numbers. If you put on your socks and then your shoes, taking them off means shoes first, then socks; you cannot get the socks off while the shoes are still on. A two-step rule is the same. The taxi rule takes the passengers, multiplies by 12, then adds 5, and the fare you are holding is the result of both. The plus 5 was the last thing done to it, so it is the first thing to take away: 65 minus 5 gives 60, which is the number that was there just after the multiplying. Now undo the multiplying: 60 divided by 12 gives 5 passengers. If you divide first, 65 divided by 12 is not even a whole number, and subtracting 5 afterwards gives nonsense. The flow diagram makes this automatic because you simply walk from the output back to the input, undoing each box as you pass it, and the boxes are in order on the page. The same logic will carry you through solving equations in later grades, where you undo whatever was done to the unknown in reverse order until it stands alone. Socks and shoes is the rule to remember: last on, first off.

### subtopic_3 — Four Descriptions, One Rule
**Assistant:** The rule times 17 works for the first row. Why isn't that good enough?

**Tutor:** Because a rule is a promise about every row, not a lucky hit on one. The taxi table says input 1 gives 17, and times 17 does give 17 for input 1, so it looks fine. But the table also says input 2 gives 29, and times 17 gives 34, so the promise is already broken on the second row. A rule that is wrong for even one row is simply wrong, because someone using it would be charged the wrong fare. This is why the method for finding a rule has two parts and a test. First read the step between outputs, which gives the multiplier, 12. Then compare the first output with what the multiplier alone would give, 17 against 12, which gives the plus 5. Then, and this is the part learners skip, test the rule on every remaining row: 2 times 12 plus 5 is 29, 3 times 12 plus 5 is 41, 4 times 12 plus 5 is 53. Only after the whole table agrees is the rule found. Testing matters even more when you have made the rule up yourself from a story, because it is easy to misread a story. If the taxi story had said R12 per passenger including bags, the rule would be times 12 alone, and a table would show it at once because the first output would be 12, not 17. Tables keep rules honest, and testing every row is how they do it.
