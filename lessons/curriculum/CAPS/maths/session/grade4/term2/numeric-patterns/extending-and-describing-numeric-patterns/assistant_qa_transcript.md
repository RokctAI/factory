### subtopic_1 — Constant Difference Patterns
**Assistant:** Why does the multiplying pattern grow so much faster? They both just go up.

**Tutor:** They both go up, but what they add each step is very different. In the adding pattern the step is fixed: 3 more, then 3 more, then 3 more, no matter how big the numbers have become. In the multiplying pattern the step is a copy of the whole number so far: going from 3 to 9 adds 6, going from 9 to 27 adds 18, going from 27 to 81 adds 54. Each step is bigger than the last because the number it is based on is bigger. So the multiplying pattern feeds on itself, and the bigger it gets the faster it grows. This is why doubling is so dramatic. If you fold a sheet of paper in half 10 times it is over a thousand layers thick, and after 20 folds it would be over a million. A rumour that each person tells two others reaches a whole school in a dozen rounds. Money saved in a bank grows this way too, slowly at first and then faster, which is why starting to save early matters more than saving a lot. The adding pattern is steady and predictable and describes things like a fixed weekly pocket money. The multiplying pattern describes anything that grows in proportion to its size, and it always wins the race in the end, however small it starts.

### subtopic_2 — Constant Ratio Patterns
**Assistant:** What if a pattern has a difference that changes by the same amount each time? Is that a constant difference?

**Tutor:** Not quite, and you have spotted something that mathematicians care about. In 1, 3, 6, 10, 15 the differences are 2, 3, 4, 5, so the differences themselves form a constant-difference pattern, going up by 1. The first differences are not constant but the second differences are. We say the pattern has a constant second difference, and it is neither a constant-difference nor a constant-ratio pattern in the Grade 4 sense. Such patterns come from geometric pictures like the staircase and the triangle of dots, and from square numbers 1, 4, 9, 16, 25, whose differences 3, 5, 7, 9 go up by 2. You can still extend them: find the next difference by continuing the pattern of differences, then add it. For 1, 3, 6, 10, 15 the next difference is 6, so the next number is 21. For the squares the next difference is 11, so the next number is 36. In Grade 4 you are expected to recognise that these are patterns and to continue them, and to know that they are not the two simple kinds. In high school you will learn that constant first differences give straight-line graphs and constant second differences give curves called parabolas. For now, keep the habit of writing the differences in a row above the sequence, because that row tells you which kind of pattern you have.

### subtopic_3 — Which Kind Is It, and Make Your Own
**Assistant:** Why do I have to say the start number? Surely add 15 is the rule.

**Tutor:** Add 15 is the rule for how the numbers change, but it does not tell anyone which numbers they are. Start at 20 and add 15 gives 20, 35, 50, 65. Start at 7 and add 15 gives 7, 22, 37, 52. Same rule, completely different sequences, and if a friend only hears add 15 they cannot write down a single number of your pattern. A pattern is pinned down by two things: where it starts and how it moves. Think of giving directions. Walk two blocks north tells someone how to move, but unless you also say from the school gate, they could end up anywhere. The same two pieces appear in the flow diagrams you met with geometric patterns, where the rule was times 3 then plus 1 and the inputs started at picture 1; change the first input and the outputs change. Later, in high school, the formula for a constant-difference pattern is written with both pieces in it, the first term and the common difference, and the formula for a constant-ratio pattern has the first term and the common ratio. You are learning the same idea now in words. So when you describe a pattern, always say two things: the first number, and what you do to get each next one. Then your description is complete, and anyone can rebuild your pattern from scratch.
