# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers recognising, visualising and naming 2D shapes in the environment and in geometric settings: regular and irregular polygons, including triangles, squares, rectangles, other quadrilaterals, pentagons, hexagons and heptagons, and circles. The anchor is a set of South African road signs, a stop sign being an octagon, a yield sign a triangle and a speed sign a circle, together with floor tiles in a school in Kimberley and a R5 coin. The governing idea is that a polygon is a closed flat shape made only of straight sides, that polygons are named by the number of sides, that a regular polygon has all sides equal and all angles equal, and that a circle has one curved side and so is not a polygon. Naming polygons by side count, distinguishing regular from irregular, and recognising circles are examinable.

## Subtopic: What Makes a Polygon

A 2D shape is flat; it has length and width but no thickness, like a drawing on paper. A polygon is a 2D shape that is closed, meaning it has no gaps, and that is made only of straight sides joined at corners. The corners are called vertices, and one corner is a vertex. A shape with a gap is not a polygon, and a shape with any curved part is not a polygon.

Polygons are named by how many sides they have. Three sides: triangle. Four sides: quadrilateral. Five: pentagon. Six: hexagon. Seven: heptagon. Eight: octagon. The number of sides always equals the number of vertices. A stop sign has eight sides and eight corners, so it is an octagon.

To name a polygon, count the sides carefully, touching each one. Long thin shapes and tilted shapes can trick the eye. A triangle balanced on one corner is still a triangle. A very flat hexagon is still a hexagon. The name depends only on the count of straight sides, never on how the shape is turned or how stretched it looks.

Quadrilaterals are a big family with their own names. A square has four equal sides and four square corners. A rectangle has four square corners but only opposite sides equal. Other quadrilaterals have four sides without those special properties, such as a kite or a shape with no equal sides at all. Every square is a quadrilateral, and so is every rectangle.

The questions for this section are with you now: what makes a shape a polygon, naming polygons by side count, and the quadrilateral family.

## Subtopic: Regular and Irregular Polygons

A regular polygon has all its sides the same length and all its corners the same size. A square is a regular quadrilateral. A stop sign is a regular octagon. The yield sign on the road is a regular triangle, also called an equilateral triangle. The yellow floor tiles in the hall are regular hexagons, which is why they fit together with no gaps.

An irregular polygon has sides or corners that are not all the same. A rectangle that is not a square is an irregular quadrilateral, because two sides are longer than the other two, even though all its corners are square. A triangle with sides of 3, 4 and 5 centimetres is irregular. A pentagon drawn freehand is almost always irregular.

Regular and irregular shapes with the same number of sides share the same name. A regular pentagon and a squashed pentagon are both pentagons. The word regular or irregular is added to say more about the shape, not to change what it is called. When a question asks you to name a shape, give the side-count name; when it asks you to describe it, add regular or irregular.

Regular shapes are common in things people make, such as coins, signs and tiles, because equal sides and equal corners are easy to manufacture and look balanced. Irregular shapes are common in nature and in land, such as fields, leaves and lakes. Both are polygons as long as the sides are straight and the shape is closed.

The questions for this section are with you now: telling regular from irregular polygons, and knowing that both share the side-count name.

## Subtopic: Circles and Shapes in the World

A circle is a 2D shape with one curved side and no corners. Every point on the edge is the same distance from the centre. Because its side is curved, a circle is not a polygon. A speed limit sign is a circle, a R5 coin is a circle, and the face of a clock is a circle.

Shapes in the environment often approximate the geometric ones. A window is usually a rectangle; a slice of pizza is roughly a triangle; a honeycomb cell is a hexagon; a soccer ball's dark patches are pentagons and its light patches are hexagons; a 50 cent coin is a circle. Looking for shapes in the world helps you remember their names and properties.

Shapes can also be inside other shapes. A square can be cut along a diagonal into two triangles. Two squares side by side make a rectangle. Six equilateral triangles around a point make a regular hexagon, which is why the hexagon tiles can be seen as triangles too. Noticing how shapes combine is the start of the work you will do with composite shapes and tessellations later in the year.

When you describe a shape in the environment, name it, say whether it is regular or irregular, and count its sides and corners. The stop sign is a regular octagon with 8 sides and 8 vertices. The classroom door is an irregular quadrilateral, a rectangle, with 4 sides and 4 vertices. The clock face is a circle with 1 curved side and no vertices.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: calling a circle a polygon; a polygon has only straight sides, and a circle's side is curved. Two: renaming a tilted or stretched shape; a triangle on its corner is still a triangle. Three: saying a rectangle is regular because its corners are equal; its sides are not all equal, so it is irregular. Four: thinking regular and irregular shapes have different names; both five-sided shapes are pentagons.

The questions for this section are with you now: why a circle is not a polygon, finding shapes in the environment, and describing a shape fully.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the road signs, the tiles and the coin — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Straight Sides, No Gaps

A flat shape with straight sides and no gaps is a polygon. Curves or gaps mean it is not.

Count the sides to name it. 3 is a triangle, 4 a quadrilateral, 5 a pentagon, 6 a hexagon, 7 a heptagon, 8 an octagon. Sides and corners always match.

Turning or stretching a shape does not change its name. A tilted triangle is still a triangle.

Keep the stop sign in mind and try the questions for this part: polygons and their names.

## Subtopic: All Equal or Not

Regular means every side is the same length and every corner is the same size. A square is regular. A stop sign is regular.

Irregular means some sides or corners are different. A rectangle that is not a square is irregular. A freehand pentagon is irregular.

Regular or irregular, the name stays. Both are pentagons if they have five sides.

Hold the square and the rectangle in mind and try this part's questions: regular and irregular.

## Subtopic: Round Shapes and Real Shapes

A circle has one curved side and no corners. It is not a polygon. Coins, clock faces and speed signs are circles.

Shapes are everywhere. Windows are rectangles. Honeycomb cells are hexagons. A slice of pizza is roughly a triangle.

Describe a shape fully: its name, regular or irregular, how many sides, how many corners. The stop sign: regular octagon, 8 sides, 8 corners.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A polygon is a closed flat shape with only straight sides, named by its number of sides: triangle, quadrilateral, pentagon, hexagon, heptagon, octagon. Sides and corners match in number, and turning or stretching does not change the name. A regular polygon has all sides and all corners equal; otherwise it is irregular, but the side-count name stays. A circle has one curved side and no corners, so it is not a polygon.

Hold the coin in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: circles and shapes around you.
