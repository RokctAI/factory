# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers describing, sorting and comparing 2D shapes in terms of straight and curved sides and the number of sides, and drawing 2D shapes on grid paper. The anchor is a bag of cut-out shapes at a school in Welkom, a tiled floor made of squares and triangles, and a quilt whose patches are rectangles and hexagons. The governing idea is that shapes can be classified by stating their properties, straight or curved sides and the number of sides, and that a grid is a tool for drawing straight sides of exact lengths and square corners. Sorting by two properties, comparing shapes, and drawing squares, rectangles, triangles and other polygons accurately on grid paper are examinable.

## Subtopic: Sorting by Straight and Curved Sides

The first sorting question is whether a shape's sides are straight or curved. Tip out the bag of shapes. Triangles, squares, rectangles, pentagons and hexagons all have straight sides only, so they go in the straight pile. Circles and ovals have curved sides only, so they go in the curved pile. A semicircle has one straight side and one curved side, so it needs a third pile: both.

Sorting into a table makes the groups clear. Draw three columns headed straight sides only, curved sides only, and both. Write or stick each shape under its heading. Every shape goes in exactly one column, and the headings together cover every possible shape. A good sort leaves nothing out and puts nothing in two places.

Sorting circles, two overlapping rings, are another tool. One ring is has straight sides and the other is has four sides. A square goes in the overlap, because it has straight sides and four sides. A triangle goes in the straight ring only. A circle goes outside both rings. Shapes in the overlap satisfy both descriptions.

When you sort, say the reason. The square is in this group because all its sides are straight. The circle is outside because its side is curved. Being able to state the property is more important than getting the pile right by luck, and it is what a test asks for.

The questions for this section are with you now: sorting shapes by straight and curved sides into tables and sorting circles, and stating the reason.

## Subtopic: Sorting and Comparing by Number of Sides

The second sorting question is how many sides. Among the straight-sided shapes, group all the three-sided shapes together, then the four-sided, the five-sided and so on. Each group shares a name: triangles, quadrilaterals, pentagons, hexagons. Within a group the shapes may look very different, a tall thin triangle beside a wide flat one, but the count holds them together.

Comparing two shapes means saying what is the same and what is different. A square and a rectangle both have four straight sides and four square corners; they differ because the square has all four sides equal and the rectangle does not. A square and a rhombus both have four equal sides; they differ in their corners. A pentagon and a hexagon both have straight sides; they differ in the count, five against six.

The quilt shows comparison in cloth. Its rectangle patches have 4 sides and its hexagon patches have 6. Two hexagons placed together share a side, so a row of hexagons uses fewer edges than the count of sides suggests. Comparing the patches by counting sides and corners is exactly what the sorting tables do, and it tells the quilter how many edges must be sewn.

Sorting can use both questions at once. Across the top of a table write straight and curved; down the side write 3, 4, 5, 6 and no sides. A hexagon goes in the straight and 6 cell. A circle goes in the curved and no sides cell, because a circle has one curved edge and no straight sides to count. Two-way tables like this are the most powerful sorting tool you will use this year.

The questions for this section are with you now: grouping by number of sides, comparing two shapes by what is the same and different, and using a two-way table.

## Subtopic: Drawing Shapes on Grid Paper

Grid paper is squared paper, and the squares make accurate drawing easy. To draw a square with sides of 3 units, start at a corner of a grid square, count 3 squares along a line and mark, count 3 squares up and mark, and join the marks along the grid lines. The grid lines keep the sides straight and the corners square without a set square.

A rectangle is drawn the same way with two different lengths: 5 squares along and 2 squares up gives a 5 by 2 rectangle. Count carefully from the starting corner, because off-by-one errors are the commonest mistake: counting the squares you pass, not the lines you touch. A 5 by 2 rectangle has 5 squares along the bottom, not 5 grid lines.

Triangles need one slanted side. Draw the base along a grid line, say 4 squares, go up 3 squares at one end along a vertical grid line, and join the top to the far end of the base with a ruler. The slanted side will not follow the grid, which is why the ruler matters. A triangle with a square corner at the base end is called a right-angled triangle.

Other polygons are drawn by plotting their corners and joining them in order. For a hexagon, mark six points, then join each to the next and the last back to the first, using a ruler for every side. Count the sides after drawing to check that you have the shape you intended. A drawing that comes out with the wrong count is not wrong forever; add or remove a corner and join again.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: putting a semicircle in the curved-only pile; it has one straight side and one curved side, so it belongs in both. Two: sorting by how a shape looks, tall or wide, instead of by its properties; count the sides. Three: counting grid lines instead of grid squares when drawing a 5 by 2 rectangle; count the squares along the side. Four: drawing a slanted side freehand; use a ruler so the triangle has straight sides.

The questions for this section are with you now: drawing squares and rectangles by counting grid squares, drawing a triangle with a slanted side, and checking the side count of a drawn polygon.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the bag of shapes, the tiled floor and the quilt — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Straight or Curved

Tip out the shapes. Straight sides only? Triangles, squares, rectangles, pentagons, hexagons. Curved only? Circles and ovals. Both? A semicircle.

Put them in a table with three headings. Every shape goes in exactly one place.

Or use two overlapping rings. A square with straight sides and four sides goes in the middle, where the rings overlap. Always say why a shape is where it is.

Keep the bag of shapes in mind and try the questions for this part: sorting by straight and curved.

## Subtopic: How Many Sides, and What Is Different

Group the straight shapes by their number of sides. All the triangles together, all the quadrilaterals together, and so on. Tall or wide does not matter.

Compare two shapes: what is the same, what is different? Square and rectangle: both have four straight sides and square corners. The square's sides are all equal. The rectangle's are not.

A two-way table sorts by both at once: straight or curved across the top, number of sides down the side.

Hold the quilt in mind and try this part's questions: sorting by side count and comparing.

## Subtopic: Draw It on the Grid

Grid paper keeps your sides straight and your corners square. A 3 by 3 square: start at a corner, count 3 squares along, 3 squares up, join along the lines.

A 5 by 2 rectangle: 5 squares along, 2 up. Count the squares, not the lines.

A triangle needs one slanted side, so use a ruler for that one. Then count the sides to check.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Sort 2D shapes by two properties: straight or curved sides, and the number of sides, using tables, sorting rings or a two-way table, and state the reason for each placement. Compare shapes by saying what is the same and what is different. Draw shapes on grid paper by counting squares along the grid lines for straight sides and square corners, using a ruler for slanted sides, and check the side count afterwards.

Hold the grid in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: drawing shapes accurately.
