# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers describing patterns in nature, modern everyday life and our cultural heritage, referring to lines, 2D shapes, 3D objects and lines of symmetry. The anchor is a walk through a village and town in Mpumalanga: a garden with leaves, flowers and a beehive, a street with road signs, paving and buildings, and Ndebele painted houses, alongside Zulu beadwork and Basotho litema patterns. The governing idea is that the shape vocabulary of this year, straight and curved lines, polygons, circles, prisms, cylinders, spheres, pyramids and lines of symmetry, can describe the patterns we see everywhere, and that describing a pattern means naming its shapes and saying how they repeat and whether it is symmetric. Identifying shapes and symmetry in pictures of natural, everyday and cultural patterns and describing them in words are examinable.

## Subtopic: Patterns in Nature

Nature is full of shapes and symmetry. Many leaves have one line of symmetry down the middle vein, with the left half a mirror image of the right. A butterfly has one line of symmetry down its body. Many flowers have several lines of symmetry: a flower with 5 equal petals spaced evenly has 5 lines of symmetry, one through each petal.

Bees build honeycomb from hexagons. The hexagons fit together with no gaps, so honeycomb is a natural tessellation. Spider webs are made of straight lines running out from the centre crossed by threads going round and round. Zebra stripes are curved lines that repeat but are never exactly the same.

Nature has 3D objects too. An orange is close to a sphere. A tree trunk is close to a cylinder. A termite mound can look a little like a pyramid. Natural objects are rarely perfect shapes, so we say close to or roughly when we describe them.

Not everything in nature is symmetric. Look carefully: a leaf might be slightly bigger on one side, or a stone might have no line of symmetry at all. Describing nature honestly means saying what you see, not what you expect.

The questions for this section are with you now: symmetry in leaves, butterflies and flowers, hexagons in honeycomb, 3D shapes in nature, and describing honestly.

## Subtopic: Patterns in Everyday Life

Towns and cities are full of shapes. Buildings are often rectangular prisms with rectangular windows and doors. Roofs can be triangular prisms. Water tanks are cylinders. Paving bricks and floor tiles form tessellations of rectangles and squares.

Road signs use shapes on purpose so that drivers recognise them quickly. A stop sign is a regular octagon with 8 lines of symmetry. A yield sign is a triangle with 3 equal sides and 3 lines of symmetry. Many other signs are circles or rectangles. A robot, the traffic light, is a rectangle with 3 circles in a straight line.

Lines are everywhere too. Fences have upright posts that are parallel, the same distance apart all the way along. Road markings are straight lines and dashes. Pedestrian crossings are rows of rectangles. Railway tracks are two lines that never meet.

Logos, packaging and clothing use shapes and symmetry to look balanced and easy to remember. A soccer ball is made of pentagons and hexagons stitched into a shape close to a sphere. A cereal box is a rectangular prism. Looking at everyday objects this way turns any walk into a maths lesson.

The questions for this section are with you now: shapes and 3D objects in buildings, road signs, lines in the street, and shapes in objects we use.

## Subtopic: Patterns in Our Cultural Heritage

South Africa's cultures have rich traditions of patterns. Ndebele house painting, from Mpumalanga and Gauteng, uses bold black outlines, bright colours and shapes like rectangles, triangles and steps. Many Ndebele designs are symmetric: a vertical line of symmetry often runs through the doorway, with the pattern on the left matching the pattern on the right.

Zulu beadwork uses tiny beads to build shapes, especially triangles and diamond shapes, in strong colours. Patterns are repeated in rows, and many pieces have a line of symmetry down the middle. The beads themselves are arranged in a grid, a little like drawing shapes on grid paper.

Basotho litema is a tradition of decorating the walls and floors of homes with patterns pressed or drawn into the mud plaster. Litema patterns often repeat a shape in a grid, with lines, curves and blocks arranged so the pattern has lines of symmetry. Woven baskets and mats from many communities use circles that spiral outwards and repeating triangles.

To describe a cultural pattern, use maths words carefully and respectfully: name the shapes, say whether the lines are straight or curved, say how the shapes repeat, and say whether the pattern has lines of symmetry and where they are. These patterns are part of our heritage, and seeing the maths in them is one way of appreciating the skill of the artists who make them.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: saying every natural object is perfectly symmetric; look closely and say roughly or close to when it is not perfect. Two: calling a ball a circle in a pattern description; a ball is a 3D sphere, while a circle is a flat 2D shape. Three: describing a pattern only by its colours; name the shapes, lines, repeats and lines of symmetry too. Four: saying a stop sign is a circle; it is a regular octagon with 8 sides and 8 lines of symmetry.

The questions for this section are with you now: shapes, lines and symmetry in Ndebele painting, Zulu beadwork, Basotho litema and woven crafts, and describing a pattern in maths words.
