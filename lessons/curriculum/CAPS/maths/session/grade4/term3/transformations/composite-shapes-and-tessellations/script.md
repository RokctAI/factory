# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers putting 2D shapes together to make composite shapes, including some with line symmetry, and packing out 2D shapes to make tessellated patterns, including some with line symmetry. The anchor is a box of pattern blocks in a classroom in East London, a kitchen floor being tiled with square tiles, and a driveway paved with rectangular bricks. The governing idea is that 2D shapes can be joined edge to edge to build new shapes that can be described by their parts, and that some shapes can be repeated to cover a surface with no gaps and no overlaps while others cannot. Building and describing composite shapes, testing them for line symmetry, and making and recognising tessellations are examinable.

## Subtopic: Making Composite Shapes

A composite shape is made by joining two or more shapes. Put a triangle on top of a square and you get a house shape. That house shape has 5 sides, so it is a pentagon made from a square and a triangle. Join two squares side by side and you get a rectangle.

Shapes are joined edge to edge: a whole side of one shape fits exactly against a whole side of another. Two triangles cut from a square along a diagonal can be joined again to make the square, or joined a different way to make a bigger triangle. The pieces are the same, but the composite shape changes.

Pattern blocks show how many shapes can be built from small parts. Six triangles meeting at a point make a hexagon. Two trapeziums make a hexagon too. Three triangles make a trapezium. Describing a composite shape means naming its parts and saying how they are joined: a hexagon made of 6 triangles.

Count the sides of the new shape, not of the parts. A square joined to a triangle has 5 sides on the outside, even though the parts had 4 sides and 3 sides. The side where they join is inside the new shape and no longer counts.

The questions for this section are with you now: joining shapes edge to edge, naming composite shapes, and counting the outside sides.

## Subtopic: Composite Shapes with Line Symmetry

Some composite shapes have lines of symmetry. The house shape, a triangle on a square with the triangle's point in the middle, has one vertical line of symmetry: fold it down the middle and the two halves match. If the triangle is pushed to one side, the house is lopsided and the line of symmetry is gone.

You can build symmetric shapes on purpose. Start with a line down the middle of your page. Every shape you place on the left, copy as a mirror image on the right. A shape built this way will always have that line of symmetry. A rectangle with a triangle on each end, like an arrow pointing both ways, has 2 lines of symmetry: one vertical and one horizontal.

Pattern block hexagons made from 6 triangles have 6 lines of symmetry, just like a regular hexagon. A cross made from 5 squares, one in the middle and one on each side, has 4 lines of symmetry: vertical, horizontal and two diagonals. An L shape made from 3 squares has one line of symmetry, the diagonal through the corner square.

Always test with an imagined fold. Ask whether every part on one side has a matching part in the mirror position on the other side. If even one piece has no partner, the line is not a line of symmetry.

The questions for this section are with you now: testing composite shapes for lines of symmetry and building shapes that are symmetric on purpose.

## Subtopic: Tessellations

A tessellation is a pattern of shapes that covers a flat surface with no gaps and no overlaps. The square tiles on a kitchen floor tessellate: each tile fits snugly against its neighbours, and the floor is covered completely. Rectangular paving bricks on a driveway tessellate too, often with each row shifted half a brick, like a brick wall.

Some shapes tessellate on their own and some do not. Squares, rectangles, triangles and regular hexagons all tessellate. Bees build honeycomb from hexagons because they fit together with no wasted space. Circles do not tessellate: however you pack them, there are gaps between them. Regular pentagons do not tessellate either; they leave gaps.

Tessellations can mix shapes. Octagons and small squares together cover a floor, a pattern you often see in bathroom tiles. Hexagons and triangles together make another pattern. Making a tessellation means repeating your shapes so that every gap is filled and nothing overlaps.

Many tessellations have lines of symmetry. A floor of square tiles in two colours, like a chessboard, can be folded along lines between the tiles or through the middle of a row and match. When you make your own tessellation, colour it so that it has a line of symmetry and test it with a fold.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: counting the joining side when counting the sides of a composite shape; only the outside sides count. Two: assuming every composite shape is symmetric; test with a fold, because one lopsided piece breaks the symmetry. Three: calling a pattern of circles a tessellation; circles leave gaps, so they do not tessellate. Four: letting shapes overlap to fill gaps; a tessellation has no gaps and no overlaps.

The questions for this section are with you now: what a tessellation is, which shapes tessellate and which do not, and tessellations with lines of symmetry.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the pattern blocks, the floor tiles and the paved driveway — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Build a Shape

Join shapes edge to edge to make a new shape. A triangle on a square makes a house shape.

Six triangles meeting in the middle make a hexagon.

Count only the sides on the outside. The house shape has 5.

Keep the pattern blocks in mind and try the questions for this part: composite shapes.

## Subtopic: Fold the Whole Shape

Fold a composite shape down the middle. If the halves match, that is a line of symmetry.

House shape: 1 line down the middle. Cross of 5 squares: 4 lines.

Build symmetric shapes by mirroring every piece across a line.

Hold the house shape in mind and try this part's questions: symmetry in composite shapes.

## Subtopic: No Gaps, No Overlaps

A tessellation covers a surface with shapes. No gaps. No overlaps. Like floor tiles.

Squares, rectangles, triangles and hexagons fit. Circles leave gaps.

Colour your tessellation so it has a line of symmetry, like a chessboard floor.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A composite shape is made by joining 2D shapes edge to edge, described by its parts, with only the outside sides counted. Some composite shapes have lines of symmetry, tested by folding, and you can build symmetric shapes by mirroring every piece. A tessellation covers a surface with no gaps and no overlaps: squares, rectangles, triangles and regular hexagons tessellate, circles and regular pentagons do not, and tessellations can be coloured to have lines of symmetry.
