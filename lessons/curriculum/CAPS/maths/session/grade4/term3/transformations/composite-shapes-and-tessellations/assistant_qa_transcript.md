### subtopic_1 — Making Composite Shapes
**Assistant:** Why don't circles tessellate? They are such a nice shape.

**Tutor:** Circles are lovely, but their curved sides cannot fit flat against each other. When two circles touch, they touch at only one point, and there is space all around that point. Pack a whole tray of round biscuits as tightly as you can and you will still see little gaps shaped like curved triangles between them. Those gaps mean the circles do not cover the surface completely, so they do not make a tessellation. Shapes with straight sides can fit edge to edge, side touching side all the way along. That is why squares, rectangles and triangles make such good tiles. Even then, the corners matter: where tiles meet at a point, their corners must fill the space around that point exactly. Four square corners fill the space perfectly, six triangle corners do too, and so do three hexagon corners. Regular pentagons are an interesting case: they have straight sides, but three pentagon corners are not quite enough to fill the space around a point, and four are too much, so they leave gaps or overlap. Next time you walk on a tiled floor or past a brick wall, look at the corners where the shapes meet and you will see this idea at work.

### subtopic_2 — Composite Shapes with Line Symmetry
**Assistant:** What is the point of composite shapes? Real things aren't made of pattern blocks.

**Tutor:** Actually, a lot of real things are made of simple shapes joined together, and seeing that helps you understand and describe them. Look at a house from the front: often a rectangle or square with a triangle roof on top, a composite shape. A door is a rectangle, sometimes with a half circle above it. Road signs, logos, flags and the shapes in fabric patterns are often built from triangles, rectangles and circles. Engineers and builders think this way too. When a builder works out how many bricks or tiles a wall needs, he splits the wall into simple rectangles and triangles. When you study area next term, you will find the area of an L-shaped room by splitting it into two rectangles, which is exactly the composite-shape idea. Composite shapes also train your eye. If you can look at a hexagon and see six triangles inside it, you start to notice how shapes are related, which helps with fractions, symmetry and patterns. And building with pattern blocks is a good way to discover things for yourself, like the fact that a hexagon can be made from six triangles, from three rhombuses or from two trapeziums.

### subtopic_3 — Tessellations
**Assistant:** Do tessellations have to use only one shape?

**Tutor:** No, they can use one shape or several, as long as the whole surface is covered with no gaps and no overlaps. A tessellation using one shape is the simplest kind: square floor tiles, rectangular paving bricks, or the hexagons of a honeycomb. Many beautiful tessellations use two or more shapes. A common bathroom floor uses octagons with small squares filling the gaps between them. Hexagons and triangles can be mixed in a star-like pattern. Some patterns mix squares and triangles. Artists from many cultures have made tessellations with wonderful combinations of shapes, and you can find them in tiled walls, woven mats and beadwork. When you design your own, start with one shape and see whether it fits on its own. If it leaves gaps, ask which other shape would fill the gaps exactly, the way small squares fill the gaps between octagons. Then repeat the arrangement across the page. Finally, add colour. A good tessellation can also have lines of symmetry if the colours are arranged carefully, so test your finished pattern with a fold. Making tessellations is a great way to practise thinking about shapes, corners and symmetry all at once.
