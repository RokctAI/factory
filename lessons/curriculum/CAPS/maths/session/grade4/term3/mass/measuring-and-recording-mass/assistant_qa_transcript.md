### subtopic_1 — Grams and Kilograms
**Assistant:** Why is it called mass and not weight? Everyone at home says weight.

**Tutor:** At home and in shops, people say weight, and that is perfectly fine for everyday talk. In maths and science we say mass, because scientists use the word weight for something slightly different. Mass is the amount of stuff in an object, and it stays the same wherever the object is. Weight is how hard the Earth pulls on that stuff. On Earth the two go together so closely that a scale can use the pull to tell you the mass, which is why everyone mixes up the words. The difference only really shows up in space. An astronaut on the Moon has the same mass as on Earth, because she is made of the same amount of stuff, but the Moon pulls much more weakly, so she would feel much lighter and could jump much higher. In Grade 4 you do not need to worry about this; just know that when your book says mass, it means how heavy something is, measured in grams and kilograms. If someone at home asks how much the potatoes weigh, they are asking for the mass in kilograms, and you can answer them confidently: 2 kilograms. Using the word mass in maths class shows you know the proper term.

### subtopic_2 — Using Balances and Scales
**Assistant:** How do I read a scale when the marks don't have numbers?

**Tutor:** Every scale has some numbered marks and some unnumbered ones in between, and the trick is to work out what the unnumbered ones are worth before you read anything. Find two numbered marks next to each other, say 0 and 500 g. Count the spaces between them, not the marks. Say there are 5 spaces. Then each space is worth 500 divided by 5, which is 100 g. Now count spaces from the last number before the pointer. If the pointer is 3 spaces past 500 g, the mass is 500 plus 3 times 100, which is 800 g. Different scales have different spacing, so do this check every time. A bathroom scale might go 0, 10, 20 kg with 10 spaces in each gap, so each space is 1 kg. Another kitchen scale might have 4 spaces between 0 and 200 g, so each is 50 g. If the pointer is halfway between two marks, the mass is halfway between their values. And look straight at the dial, not from the side, because looking from an angle can make the pointer seem to be on a different mark. With this habit you can read any scale, even one you have never seen before.

### subtopic_3 — Recording, Comparing and Ordering Mass
**Assistant:** Why do I have to change kilograms to grams to compare? I can see 1 kg is a bag of sugar.

**Tutor:** Because numbers only compare fairly when they count the same thing. 750 g counts grams and 1 kg counts kilograms, and a kilogram is a thousand times bigger than a gram. If you just compare the numbers, 750 looks much bigger than 1, and you would wrongly say the rice is heavier. Changing 1 kg into 1 000 g puts both masses into grams, and now 1 000 is clearly bigger than 750, so the sugar is heavier. It is the same as comparing money: R5 and 50c. The number 50 is bigger than 5, but R5 is more money, because rands and cents are different units. Change R5 into 500c and the comparison is easy. You are right that you can picture a 1 kg bag of sugar, and that is a great benchmark for checking. But when the numbers get trickier, like 2 kg and 1 950 g, or 3 kg and 3 100 g, pictures are not enough, and changing units is the reliable way. In this lesson we usually change kilograms into grams, because that gives whole numbers. 3 kg is 3 000 g, and that is less than 3 100 g. Same unit first, then compare.
