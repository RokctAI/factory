# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers estimating and practically measuring the mass of 3D objects using bathroom scales, kitchen scales and balances, and recording, comparing and ordering mass in grams and kilograms. The anchor is a gogo's kitchen in Ladysmith with a packet of yeast, a tub of margarine, a bag of sugar, a bag of potatoes and a bag of mielie meal, and a balance and a bathroom scale at school. The governing idea is that mass is measured against a standard unit, grams for light things and kilograms for heavy things, with 1 000 grams in 1 kilogram, and that every scale is read by first working out what each small mark stands for. Choosing a sensible unit, estimating, reading scales with different intervals and ordering masses after changing them to the same unit are examinable.

## Subtopic: Grams and Kilograms

Mass tells us how heavy something is. We measure mass in grams and kilograms. The short way to write grams is g, and kilograms is kg. A paper clip has a mass of about 1 gram. A bag of sugar from the shop has a mass of 1 kilogram. 1 kilogram is the same as 1 000 grams.

Use grams for light things and kilograms for heavy things. Gogo's packet of yeast is 10 g. Her tub of margarine is 500 g. The bag of sugar is 1 kg, the bag of potatoes is 2 kg, and the bag of mielie meal is 5 kg. A Grade 4 learner might have a mass of about 30 kg. Measuring a learner in grams would give a huge number, and measuring yeast in kilograms would give a tiny part of one.

Benchmarks help you estimate. A benchmark is something whose mass you already know, like the 1 kg bag of sugar. Hold the sugar in one hand and an unknown object in the other. If the object feels about the same, estimate 1 kg. If it feels about half as heavy, estimate 500 g.

Big does not always mean heavy. A large bag of chips is light, and a small tin of fish is heavier than it looks. That is why we estimate by feeling the mass, not by looking at the size, and then measure to check.

The questions for this section are with you now: what mass is, grams and kilograms, choosing the unit, and estimating with benchmarks.

## Subtopic: Using Balances and Scales

A balance compares two masses. Put an object on each side. The heavier side goes down and the lighter side goes up. If the two sides are level, the masses are equal. To measure with a balance, put the object on one side and add known masses, like 100 g and 500 g pieces, to the other side until it is level. Then add up the masses you used.

A kitchen scale has a dial or a screen. On a dial, the pointer moves round as you put food on the tray. Not every mark has a number, so first work out what each small mark stands for. If the numbers go 0, 500 g, 1 kg and there are 5 equal spaces between 0 and 500 g, each space is 500 divided by 5, which is 100 g.

Read the pointer carefully. If it points 3 marks after 500 g on that scale, the mass is 500 plus 300, which is 800 g. If it points halfway between two marks, the mass is halfway between them. Always look straight at the dial, not from the side, so that you read the right mark.

A bathroom scale measures heavier things, usually in kilograms. The numbers might go 0, 10, 20, 30 with 10 small marks in each gap, so each mark is 1 kg. A learner standing on it whose pointer is at 4 marks past 30 has a mass of 34 kg.

The questions for this section are with you now: using a balance, working out what each mark stands for, and reading kitchen and bathroom scales.

## Subtopic: Recording, Comparing and Ordering Mass

Record a mass with its number and its unit. 500 g and 500 kg are very different! Writing only 500 is not a measurement, because nobody knows whether it means a tub of margarine or a small car. Write 500 g.

To compare masses, make sure they are in the same unit. Which is heavier, 750 g of rice or 1 kg of sugar? Change 1 kg into grams: 1 000 g. 1 000 g is more than 750 g, so the sugar is heavier. Comparing 750 with 1 without changing units would give the wrong answer.

To order masses, change them all to the same unit first. Order gogo's groceries from lightest to heaviest: potatoes 2 kg, margarine 500 g, mielie meal 5 kg, a pumpkin 1 500 g. In grams: 2 000 g, 500 g, 5 000 g, 1 500 g. From lightest: margarine 500 g, pumpkin 1 500 g, potatoes 2 kg, mielie meal 5 kg.

A table helps you record many masses. Write the object, your estimate and the measured mass in three columns. Compare your estimates with the measurements. Getting closer each time shows your sense of mass is growing.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: measuring a learner in grams or yeast in kilograms; use grams for light things and kilograms for heavy things. Two: reading every mark on a dial as 1; first work out what each space is worth, such as 500 divided by 5, which is 100 g. Three: writing a mass without its unit; 500 could mean 500 g or 500 kg, so always write the unit. Four: deciding 750 g is heavier than 1 kg because 750 is bigger than 1; change 1 kg to 1 000 g before comparing.

The questions for this section are with you now: recording mass with units, changing to the same unit to compare, and ordering masses.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with gogo's groceries and the scales — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Heavy and Light

Mass is how heavy something is. Light things in grams, heavy things in kilograms.

1 kilogram is 1 000 grams. A bag of sugar is 1 kg. A paper clip is about 1 g.

Feel the mass to estimate. Big is not always heavy.

## Subtopic: Read the Scale

On a balance, the heavier side goes down. Level means equal.

On a dial, find what each little mark stands for. 5 spaces from 0 to 500 g: each is 100 g.

Look straight at the pointer. 3 marks after 500 g is 800 g.

Hold the kitchen scale in mind and try this part's questions: reading scales.

## Subtopic: Compare and Order

Always write the unit: 500 g, not just 500.

Same unit before you compare. 1 kg is 1 000 g, so it is more than 750 g.

Order: 500 g, 1 500 g, 2 kg, 5 kg. Lightest first.

Estimate with benchmarks like a 1 kg bag of sugar, then measure with a balance, a kitchen scale or a bathroom scale, first working out what each mark stands for.
