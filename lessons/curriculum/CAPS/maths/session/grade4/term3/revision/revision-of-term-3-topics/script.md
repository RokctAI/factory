# Part 1 — Expert

This session revises the Term 3 topics: common fractions, including comparing, ordering, equivalent fractions, adding with the same denominator and fractions of groups; data handling, from tallies and tables to pictographs, bar graphs, pie charts, summaries and predictions; probability with coins and dice; mass in grams and kilograms; and transformations, including composite shapes, tessellations and patterns. The anchor is a school market day in Graaff-Reinet with a cake stall, a survey table, a coin-toss game, a sweet stall with a scale, and a tiled courtyard. The governing idea is that each topic supplies a tool, that diagrams, units and totals check every answer, and that every shape and pattern is described by its properties. All Term 3 topics are examinable in the Term 3 test.

## Subtopic: Fractions

Naming and comparing: a cake cut into 8 equal slices has slices of one eighth. 3 slices sold is three eighths, and five eighths remain. With the same bottom number, more parts win: five eighths is more than three eighths. With the same top number, the smaller bottom wins: one third is more than one fifth. Compare with one half: three eighths is less than a half because half of 8 is 4.

Equivalent fractions: half a cake is two quarters and four eighths. Multiply top and bottom by the same number: three quarters is six eighths. Use this to compare: three quarters or five eighths? Six eighths beats five eighths, so three quarters is bigger.

Adding fractions with the same bottom number: two eighths of a cake sold in the morning and three eighths in the afternoon is five eighths. Add the tops, keep the bottom. When the top equals the bottom, it is one whole: four quarters is one cake.

Fractions as sharing: 3 cakes shared among 4 families gives each three quarters of a cake. Fractions of a group: three quarters of 24 cupcakes is 24 divided by 4, which is 6, times 3, which is 18 cupcakes. 7 pies between 2 tables is 3 and a half each.

The questions for this section are with you now: naming, comparing and ordering fractions, equivalent fractions, adding with the same bottom number, and fractions of groups and sharing.

## Subtopic: Data and Probability

The data cycle: pose a question, collect, organise, represent, analyse and report. At the survey table, 40 visitors chose their favourite stall: cakes 15, sweets 10, games 9, crafts 6. The tallies are bundled in fives, and the total, 15 plus 10 plus 9 plus 6, is 40, matching the visitors asked.

Graphs: a bar graph has the stalls along the bottom and a scale from 0 in equal steps up the side, here counting in 2s or 5s. A one-to-one pictograph uses one same-size picture per visitor with a key. A pie chart shows fractions of the whole: if half of 40 visitors came by car, that slice stands for 20; a quarter is 10.

Analysing and summarising: cakes were most popular with 15, crafts least with 6, and 15 minus 6 is 9 more for cakes. A summary: 40 visitors were asked; cakes were the favourite with 15, and crafts the least popular with 6. A prediction: next year's cake stall will probably be busy again, so bake plenty.

Probability: the coin-toss game has two outcomes, heads and tails, equally likely. A dice has six outcomes, 1 to 6. In 20 tosses expect about 10 heads, not exactly 10. The coin has no memory, so tails is never due.

The questions for this section are with you now: the data cycle, tallies and tables, reading graphs and pie charts, summaries and predictions, and outcomes of coins and dice.

## Subtopic: Mass and Transformations

Mass: grams for light things, kilograms for heavy ones, with 1 kg equal to 1 000 g. At the sweet stall, bags are weighed on a kitchen scale with 5 spaces between 0 and 500 g, so each space is 100 g. Half a kilogram is 500 g, a quarter is 250 g, three quarters is 750 g.

Converting and problems: 2 kg 300 g is 2 300 g, and 2 kg 30 g is 2 030 g. Order masses after changing to the same unit: 750 g is less than 1 kg. 6 bags of 250 g is 1 500 g, one and a half kilograms. 2 kg of sweets shared into 8 bags is 250 g each.

Transformations: composite shapes are made by joining shapes edge to edge; a triangle on a square makes a house shape with 5 outside sides and one line of symmetry. The courtyard is tiled in a tessellation of squares with no gaps and no overlaps. Squares, triangles and hexagons tessellate; circles do not.

Patterns: honeycomb hexagons, leaves with a line of symmetry, a stop sign with 8 lines, and Ndebele paintings with triangles and a line of symmetry through the door. Describe patterns by their shapes, lines, repeats and symmetry.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: adding the bottom numbers, 2/8 + 3/8 = 5/16; add the tops and keep the bottom, 5/8. Two: starting a bar graph scale at the smallest value; start at 0 and use equal steps. Three: writing 2 kg 30 g as 2 300 g; the 30 g goes in the tens and units, so it is 2 030 g. Four: calling a pattern of circles a tessellation; circles leave gaps, so they do not tessellate.

The questions for this section are with you now: mass units, reading scales, converting and mass problems, composite shapes, tessellations and patterns.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again, walking through the market from the cake stall to the courtyard — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: At the Cake Stall: Fractions

8 slices, 3 sold: three eighths sold, five eighths left. Five eighths beats three eighths.

Three quarters is six eighths, so it beats five eighths. Two eighths plus three eighths is five eighths: add the tops, keep the bottom.

Three quarters of 24 cupcakes: divide by 4, times 3. 18 cupcakes.

Keep the cake stall in mind and try the questions for this part: fractions.

## Subtopic: At the Survey Table: Data and Chance

Tally in fives and check the total: 15, 10, 9 and 6 make 40 visitors.

Bar graphs start at 0 in equal steps. Pie chart: half of 40 is 20, a quarter is 10.

Coin: heads or tails, about half each. Dice: 1 to 6. No memory.

Hold the survey table in mind and try this part's questions: data and chance.

## Subtopic: At the Scale and the Tiles: Mass and Shapes

1 kg is 1 000 g. Half a kilogram is 500 g, a quarter is 250 g. 2 kg 30 g is 2 030 g.

6 bags of 250 g is 1 500 g. Change to grams, then calculate.

Tiles: no gaps, no overlaps. Squares fit, circles do not. House shape: 5 sides, 1 line of symmetry.
