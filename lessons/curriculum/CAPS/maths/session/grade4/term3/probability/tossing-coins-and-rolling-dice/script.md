# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers performing simple repeated events, such as tossing a coin and rolling a dice, and listing the possible outcomes. The anchor is a school fun day in Kokstad with a coin-toss game, a dice game and a spinner with four equal colours. The governing idea is that a single toss or roll cannot be predicted, but its possible outcomes can be listed, and that when an event is repeated many times the results can be recorded with tallies and compared with what we expect from the outcomes. Listing the outcomes of a coin, a dice and a spinner, recording repeated trials with tally marks, and describing what the results show are examinable.

## Subtopic: Possible Outcomes

An outcome is a result of something that happens by chance. When you toss a coin, it lands showing either heads or tails. Those are the two possible outcomes. When you roll an ordinary dice, it shows 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 or 6. Those are the six possible outcomes.

Listing the possible outcomes means writing down everything that could happen, with nothing missing and nothing extra. For the coin: heads, tails. For the dice: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6. A 7 is not a possible outcome on an ordinary dice, and neither is 0. A spinner with four equal parts coloured red, blue, green and yellow has four possible outcomes: red, blue, green and yellow.

For a fair coin, heads and tails are equally likely. For a fair dice, each of the six numbers is equally likely. Equally likely means each outcome has the same chance of happening. A 6 is not harder to roll than a 1, even if it sometimes feels that way when you are waiting for one in a game.

You cannot know the outcome of one toss in advance. Even if the last three tosses were heads, the next toss is still just as likely to be heads as tails. The coin does not remember what it did before.

The questions for this section are with you now: what an outcome is, listing all the outcomes of a coin, a dice and a spinner, and what equally likely means.

## Subtopic: Doing a Repeated Experiment

An experiment in chance means doing the same thing many times and recording what happens. Each go is called a trial. At the fun day, Ayanda tosses a coin 20 times and records each outcome with a tally mark next to heads or tails.

Ayanda's results: heads, 11 tallies; tails, 9 tallies. Check the total: 11 plus 9 is 20, the number of trials. Every toss has been recorded once. Tally marks bundled in fives make the counting easy, just as in a survey.

Then Ayanda rolls a dice 30 times. Results: 1 came up 4 times, 2 came up 6 times, 3 came up 5 times, 4 came up 3 times, 5 came up 7 times and 6 came up 5 times. Check: 4 plus 6 plus 5 plus 3 plus 7 plus 5 is 30. A frequency table with a total row keeps this tidy.

Results change each time you repeat an experiment. If Ayanda's friend tosses a coin 20 times, she might get 8 heads and 12 tails, or exactly 10 and 10. That is normal for chance events. What stays the same is the list of possible outcomes.

The questions for this section are with you now: doing trials, recording them with tally marks in a frequency table, and checking that the total matches the number of trials.

## Subtopic: What the Results Show

Compare the results with the possible outcomes. A coin has two equally likely outcomes, so in 20 tosses we expect about half heads and half tails, about 10 each. Ayanda's 11 heads and 9 tails is close to that. It is not exactly 10 and 10, and it does not need to be.

A dice has six equally likely outcomes, so in 30 rolls we expect each number about 5 times, because 30 divided by 6 is 5. Ayanda's results, from 3 times to 7 times, are spread around 5. Small differences like these are what chance looks like.

The more trials, the closer the results usually come to equal. In 20 tosses, 11 heads and 9 tails is common. In 200 tosses, the results would usually be much closer to half and half, as a fraction of all the tosses. That is why one or two trials prove nothing, but many trials show the pattern.

Results that are very far from expected can be a clue. If a dice showed 6 in 25 out of 30 rolls, something might be wrong with the dice: it could be weighted or unfair. Comparing results with expectations is how people check whether games of chance are fair.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: listing 7 as a possible outcome of an ordinary dice; the outcomes are 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6 only. Two: thinking tails is due after three heads; the coin has no memory, so each toss is still equally likely to be heads or tails. Three: expecting exactly 10 heads in 20 tosses; results are usually close to the expected number, not exactly on it. Four: deciding a coin is unfair after only a few tosses; many trials are needed before the pattern can be trusted.

The questions for this section are with you now: what we expect from equally likely outcomes, why results are only close to the expected numbers, and why more trials show the pattern better.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the coin, the dice and the spinner at the fun day — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: What Can Happen

A coin can land heads or tails. That is 2 possible outcomes.

A dice can show 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 or 6. That is 6 possible outcomes. No 7 and no 0.

Each one is equally likely. And the coin does not remember the last toss.

Keep the coin and the dice in mind and try the questions for this part: listing outcomes.

## Subtopic: Try It and Tally

Do the same thing many times. Each go is a trial.

Make a tally mark for each result. 20 tosses: heads 11, tails 9. Check they add up to 20.

Try again and your results will probably change. That is chance.

Hold the tally sheet in mind and try this part's questions: experiments.

## Subtopic: What the Results Show

A fair coin should give about half heads. 20 tosses: about 10. 11 is close enough.

A fair dice: 30 rolls, about 5 of each number, because 30 divided by 6 is 5.

More trials show the pattern better. Very strange results might mean an unfair dice.

Pocket summary of the lesson. An outcome is a result of a chance event. A coin has 2 possible outcomes, heads and tails; an ordinary dice has 6, the numbers 1 to 6; a spinner has one outcome for each part. Fair coins and dice have equally likely outcomes, and they have no memory. Repeat the event many times, record each trial with tally marks, check the total, and compare the results with what you expect: about half heads for a coin, about one sixth of the rolls for each number on a dice. More trials show the pattern more clearly.
