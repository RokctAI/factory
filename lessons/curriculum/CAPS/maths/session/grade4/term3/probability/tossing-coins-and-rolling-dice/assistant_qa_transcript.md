### subtopic_1 — Possible Outcomes
**Assistant:** If I get three heads in a row, isn't tails more likely next time?

**Tutor:** It feels that way, but no. A coin has no memory. It does not know what it landed on before, and it is not trying to even things out. Each toss is a brand new event with the same two possible outcomes, heads and tails, and for a fair coin they are equally likely every single time. Three heads in a row happens quite often in a long run of tosses; it is not a sign that tails is owed. People who play games of chance sometimes lose a lot of money by believing that a result is due, and this mistake is so common that it has a name, the gambler's fallacy, where fallacy means a wrong idea. So where does the idea that things even out come from? It is true that in a very large number of tosses, about half will be heads and half will be tails. But that happens because the big number of tosses swamps a few unusual runs, not because the coin corrects itself. If you toss 3 heads and then 1 000 more times, the extra 3 heads hardly matter in the total. So the honest answer for the next toss is always the same: heads or tails, equally likely.

### subtopic_2 — Doing a Repeated Experiment
**Assistant:** Why didn't I get exactly 10 heads in 20 tosses if heads is half?

**Tutor:** Because half is what we expect on average, not what must happen every time. Think of each toss as a separate surprise. In 20 tosses, getting exactly 10 heads is actually less likely than getting some other number close to 10, like 9, 11, 8 or 12, because there are so many different ways for the tosses to fall. If everyone in your class tossed a coin 20 times, you would see results like 8, 9, 10, 11, 12 and maybe even 6 or 14 heads, spread around 10. That spread is what chance looks like. If you put the whole class's results together, the total would usually be very close to half heads, because the ups and downs of different learners cancel each other out. That is why scientists and game designers do lots of trials before they decide anything. So when your result is 11 heads and 9 tails, do not worry that you did it wrong. It is a perfectly normal result. Only if your results were very far off, like 19 heads out of 20, would it be worth wondering whether the coin is fair or whether something went wrong in how it was tossed.

### subtopic_3 — What the Results Show
**Assistant:** What is the point of listing outcomes if I can't predict the result anyway?

**Tutor:** Listing outcomes is what lets you think clearly about chance, even though you cannot predict a single result. First, it tells you what is possible and what is not. Nobody should bet on rolling a 7 with one ordinary dice, because 7 is not on the list. Second, it tells you how many equally likely outcomes there are, which helps you know what to expect over many trials. A coin has 2 outcomes, so heads should come up about half the time; a dice has 6, so each number should come up about one sixth of the time, about 5 times in 30 rolls. Third, it helps you spot unfair games. If a game at the fun day says you win on a 6 and lose on anything else, the list shows you have 1 winning outcome and 5 losing ones, so you will usually lose. If a dice keeps showing far more 6s than the list suggests, it may be unfair. In later grades you will use the list of outcomes to work out probabilities as fractions, like one sixth for rolling a 6. So even though one roll is a surprise, the list of outcomes is the map that tells you what kind of surprise to expect.
