# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers drawing pictographs with one-to-one correspondence and drawing bar graphs. The anchor is the Mbombela class's fruit survey, apple 9, banana 7, orange 5, mango 8 and grapes 3, and a count of library books read by five reading groups. The governing idea is that a graph shows data so that it can be compared at a glance, and that this only works if the graph is drawn fairly: pictures the same size and evenly spaced, bars the same width with equal gaps, and a scale that counts up in equal steps from zero. Drawing pictographs with a key, drawing bar graphs with a title, labelled axes and a scale, and checking a graph against its table are examinable.

## Subtopic: Drawing Pictographs

A pictograph shows data with small pictures. In a one-to-one pictograph, one picture stands for one learner. To show the fruit survey, draw one small fruit for each learner: 9 apples in the apple row, 7 bananas in the banana row, 5 oranges, 8 mangoes and 3 bunches of grapes.

Every picture must be the same size, and the pictures must be lined up in neat columns. If the banana pictures are drawn bigger than the apple pictures, the banana row looks longer even though it has fewer pictures. Lining the pictures up makes it easy to see which row is longer at a glance.

A pictograph needs a title, labels and a key. The title says what the graph shows: Favourite fruit of Grade 4B. Each row is labelled with its category. The key shows what one picture means: one picture stands for one learner. Even when the key seems obvious, always draw it, because later you will meet pictographs where one picture stands for more than one.

Simple pictures work best. A circle with a stalk for an apple, a curved shape for a banana. The pictures only need to be clear and the same size; they do not need to be works of art.

The questions for this section are with you now: drawing a one-to-one pictograph with same-size pictures, neat rows, a title, labels and a key.

## Subtopic: Drawing Bar Graphs

A bar graph shows data with bars. Each category gets one bar, and the height of the bar shows the number. Draw two lines at right angles: the horizontal axis along the bottom and the vertical axis up the side. The categories go along the horizontal axis and the numbers go up the vertical axis.

The numbers up the vertical axis are the scale. The scale starts at 0 at the bottom and goes up in equal steps. For the fruit survey, counting in 1s from 0 to 10 works well, because the biggest number is 9. If the biggest number were 40, counting in 5s or 10s would fit better. The steps must always be equal: 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10 is fine, but 0, 1, 5, 6, 10 is not.

Draw the bars. The apple bar goes up to 9, the banana bar to 7, the orange bar to 5, the mango bar to 8 and the grapes bar to 3. All the bars are the same width, and the gaps between them are equal. Use a ruler for the bars and read across from the top of each bar to the scale to check its height.

Label everything. Write a title at the top: Favourite fruit of Grade 4B. Label the horizontal axis Fruit and the vertical axis Number of learners. Write each category under its bar. A bar graph without labels is just a row of rectangles.

The questions for this section are with you now: drawing the axes, choosing a scale with equal steps from 0, drawing bars of equal width, and labelling the graph.

## Subtopic: Choosing and Checking a Graph

The reading groups in the class counted the library books they read in a term. Lions read 18, Eagles 24, Kudus 12, Zebras 20 and Impalas 16. The biggest number is 24, so a scale counting in 2s from 0 to 26, or in 4s from 0 to 28, fits the page. Counting in 1s would make the graph very tall. Choose a scale that makes the biggest bar fill most of the page.

With a scale in 2s, some bars end between marks only if the number is odd. All these numbers are even, so every bar ends exactly on a mark. If a number were 15, the bar would end halfway between 14 and 16. Read the scale carefully before drawing each bar.

Pictographs and bar graphs each have their strengths. A one-to-one pictograph is quick to understand, and you can count the pictures. A bar graph is neater for bigger numbers, because drawing 24 little books would take a long time. For the reading groups, the bar graph is the better choice.

Always check your graph against your table. Read each bar's height off the scale and compare it with the table. Check that there is a bar for every category and that the title and labels are there. A graph that does not match its table tells the wrong story.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: drawing some pictures bigger than others; every picture must be the same size so longer rows mean more. Two: starting the scale at the smallest number instead of 0; the scale starts at 0 so the bar heights are fair. Three: using unequal steps on the scale, like 0, 1, 5, 6, 10; the steps must be equal. Four: leaving off the title and axis labels; a graph without labels does not tell anyone what it shows.

The questions for this section are with you now: choosing a scale for bigger numbers, reading between marks, choosing the graph type, and checking a graph against its table.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the fruit survey and the reading groups — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: One Picture, One Learner

In a pictograph, one picture stands for one learner. 9 learners chose apple, so draw 9 apples.

Make every picture the same size and line them up. Then the longest row really is the biggest.

Add a title, label every row, and draw the key: one picture stands for one learner.

Keep the fruit survey in mind and try the questions for this part: pictographs.

## Subtopic: Bars Up the Side

Draw the bottom line and the side line. Fruits along the bottom, numbers up the side.

Start the numbers at 0 and count up in equal steps.

Draw each bar up to its number, all the same width, with equal gaps. Label the axes and add a title.

Hold the bar graph in mind and try this part's questions: bar graphs.

## Subtopic: Graph Checklist

Big numbers? Count in 2s, 4s or 5s up the side so the graph fits the page. Lions 18, Eagles 24, Kudus 12.

For big numbers, a bar graph is quicker than drawing lots of pictures.

Check: title, labels, a bar for every group, and every bar the right height.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A one-to-one pictograph uses one same-size picture for each learner, in neat rows, with a title, labels and a key. A bar graph has categories along the horizontal axis and a scale up the vertical axis that starts at 0 and goes up in equal steps; the bars are the same width with equal gaps, and the graph has a title and axis labels. Choose a scale that fits the biggest number, use bar graphs for bigger numbers, and check every graph against its table.

Hold the reading groups in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: choosing and checking graphs.
