### subtopic_1 — Reading Pictographs and Bar Graphs
**Assistant:** Why can't I just look at the bars? The tallest one is obviously the biggest.

**Tutor:** The tallest bar is the biggest, and for some questions a quick look is enough. But most questions ask for more than which is biggest. They ask how many, how many more, or how many altogether, and for those you need the exact values, which come from the scale. If you skip the scale, you might assume it counts in 1s when it actually counts in 2s, and every answer you give will be half of the right one. If you skip the title, you might think the graph shows learners when it shows vetkoek, or this week when it shows last year. And if you skip the labels, you might mix up which bar is which day. Reading from the outside in takes about ten seconds and saves you from all those mistakes. It is a bit like reading the name of a bus before you get on. The bus might look like yours, but checking the sign on the front is what gets you home. Some graphs are even drawn to trick a quick looker, with a scale that does not start at 0, so a careful reader who checks the scale first is much harder to fool.

### subtopic_2 — Reading Pie Charts
**Assistant:** How do pie charts work if there are no numbers on them?

**Tutor:** A pie chart shows fractions of a whole, so you can read it with what you know about fractions. The whole circle is the whole group. If you know how many are in the group, you can work out each slice. Suppose the circle stands for 40 learners. A slice that is half the circle is half of 40, which is 20. A slice that is a quarter, like a slice of pizza cut into four, is 40 divided by 4, which is 10. A slice that is an eighth is 40 divided by 8, which is 5. You recognise these slices by their shape: half the circle has a straight edge right across the middle, a quarter has a square corner at the centre, and an eighth is half of a quarter. Even without knowing the total, you can compare: a slice twice as big as another stands for twice as many people. If the slices are close in size, it is hard to tell them apart, and that is why good pie charts usually write the numbers or the fractions on the slices. When a pie chart has numbers on it, add them up to check that they make the whole group. That checks your reading and the chart.

### subtopic_3 — Comparing, Combining and Reading Critically
**Assistant:** What does it mean to read a graph critically?

**Tutor:** It means not believing a graph just because it looks official. You ask a few questions before you trust it. Does it have a title, so you know exactly what was counted? Does the scale start at 0 and go up in equal steps? Are the pictures all the same size? Are the bars the same width? Is there a key? If any of these is missing or wrong, the graph might be giving a false picture, by accident or on purpose. For example, an advert might show a bar graph where their product's bar is twice as tall as another brand's, but the scale starts at 90, so the real difference is tiny. Reading critically also means asking where the data came from. A graph of the class's favourite fruit tells you about one class, not about all children in South Africa. And it means checking that words and graphs agree: if a report says most learners walk to school but the walking bar is the shortest, something is wrong. Being a critical reader of data is a real-life skill. You will see graphs in newspapers, on television and on phones for the rest of your life, and the habit of asking these questions will help you make good decisions.
