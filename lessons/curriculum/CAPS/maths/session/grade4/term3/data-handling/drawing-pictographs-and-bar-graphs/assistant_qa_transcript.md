### subtopic_1 — Drawing Pictographs
**Assistant:** Why does the scale have to start at 0? My graph looks better starting at 10.

**Tutor:** Starting at 10 might look tidier, but it makes the graph tell a false story. The height of a bar is supposed to show the whole amount. Suppose two groups read 12 and 24 books. On a scale starting at 0, the 24 bar is exactly twice as tall as the 12 bar, which is true: one group read twice as many. On a scale starting at 10, the 12 bar is only 2 steps tall and the 24 bar is 14 steps tall, so the second bar looks seven times as tall. Anyone glancing at the graph would think the difference was huge. Starting at 0 keeps the bars honest, so that comparing heights is the same as comparing numbers. Newspapers and adverts sometimes break this rule on purpose to make a small difference look big, so knowing the rule also helps you read their graphs carefully. If your biggest number is large and the graph is getting too tall, the fix is to count in bigger steps, like 2s, 5s or 10s, not to start higher up. That keeps the graph a good size and still fair. So start at 0, choose equal steps that fit the page, and your bars will always tell the truth.

### subtopic_2 — Drawing Bar Graphs
**Assistant:** Why does the key matter in a pictograph if one picture is just one learner?

**Tutor:** Because a reader cannot know what a picture means unless you tell them. You know that each apple stands for one learner, because you drew it. But someone else looking at your graph might wonder whether each apple means one learner, two learners or ten. Writing the key removes the guesswork. It is also a habit worth building now, because in later grades you will meet pictographs where one picture stands for 2, 5 or even 100 things. A pictograph of the number of cars passing a school might use one car picture for every 10 cars, and without a key you would be off by a lot. The key is like the legend on a map: it tells you how to read everything else. A good key shows one picture and says what it stands for, for example one apple picture equals one learner. Put it near the graph, usually at the bottom. When you read other people's pictographs, look for the key first before you count anything, because the same rows of pictures can mean very different numbers depending on the key.

### subtopic_3 — Choosing and Checking a Graph
**Assistant:** How do I choose between a pictograph and a bar graph?

**Tutor:** Think about the numbers and about who will read the graph. A one-to-one pictograph is great when the numbers are small and the readers are young or the topic is fun, because people can count the pictures and the graph is friendly. The fruit survey, with numbers from 3 to 9, works well as a pictograph. A bar graph is better when the numbers are bigger, because drawing 24 little books for one group and 20 for another takes a long time and the rows get very long. A bar graph only needs one bar per group, no matter how big the number is, as long as you choose a sensible scale. A bar graph also makes it easier to read exact values off the scale and to compare bars side by side. So ask yourself: are the numbers small enough to draw one picture each? Do I want it to look friendly or to be quick and neat? Either way, both graphs need a title and labels, and both must be drawn fairly, with same-size pictures or same-width bars. In tests, read the question carefully, because it often tells you which kind of graph to draw.
