# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers analysing data by answering questions related to data categories, and summarising data verbally and in short written paragraphs, including making predictions based on the data. The anchor is a tuck shop in Bethlehem that sold 24, 18, 20, 16 and 34 vetkoek from Monday to Friday, and a class of 36 learners in Bethlehem whose favourite subjects were Maths 11, English 7, Natural Sciences 9, Life Skills 6 and Social Sciences 3. The governing idea is that data is analysed by asking questions about the categories, that a summary states the main facts with numbers in a few sentences, and that predictions are sensible guesses based on patterns in the data, not certainties. Answering questions about categories, writing a short summary paragraph and making and justifying predictions are examinable.

## Subtopic: Asking Questions of the Data

Analysing data means asking questions about it and answering them. Start with the categories. In the subject survey of 36 learners, the categories are the subjects. Which category has the most? Maths, with 11. Which has the fewest? Social Sciences, with 3. Which two are closest? Life Skills with 6 and English with 7.

Then ask comparing questions. How many more learners chose Maths than English? 11 minus 7 is 4. How many chose either Maths or Natural Sciences? 11 plus 9 is 20. Is that more than half the class? Half of 36 is 18, and 20 is more than 18, so yes.

Ordering the categories helps you see the whole picture. From most to fewest: Maths 11, Natural Sciences 9, English 7, Life Skills 6, Social Sciences 3. Check the total: 11 plus 9 plus 7 plus 6 plus 3 is 36. Every learner is counted.

Some questions cannot be answered from the data. The survey does not tell you why learners like Maths, or which subject the Grade 5s prefer. A careful analyst says when a question needs more data instead of guessing.

The questions for this section are with you now: most and fewest, how many more, how many altogether, ordering categories and spotting questions the data cannot answer.

## Subtopic: Summarising Data in Words

A summary tells the main story of the data in a few sentences. A good summary says what the data is about, names the biggest and smallest categories with their numbers, and points out anything interesting. It does not list every number; the table already does that.

Here is a summary of the tuck shop data. The tuck shop sold 112 vetkoek from Monday to Friday. Friday was the busiest day, with 34 vetkoek, and Thursday was the quietest, with 16. Sales on the other days were between 18 and 24. Friday's sales were more than double Thursday's.

Check the summary against the data. 112 is the total of 24, 18, 20, 16 and 34. Friday's 34 is the highest and Thursday's 16 the lowest. Double 16 is 32, and 34 is more than 32, so more than double is true. Every sentence in a summary must be backed by the numbers.

Summaries can be spoken too. Telling the class, Most of us like Maths best, 11 out of 36, and only 3 chose Social Sciences, is a verbal summary. Use the numbers, keep it short, and speak clearly.

The questions for this section are with you now: what a good summary includes, checking each sentence against the data, and giving a short spoken summary.

## Subtopic: Making Predictions

A prediction is a sensible guess about what will happen, based on the data. The tuck shop sold the most vetkoek on Friday. If next week is an ordinary week, we can predict that Friday will again be the busiest day, and the cook should make more vetkoek on Friday, about 30 to 35, than on Thursday.

A prediction is not a promise. Next Friday might be rainy, or there might be a school trip, and fewer vetkoek might be sold. Use words like probably, likely and about when you predict. The tuck shop will probably sell about 30 to 35 vetkoek next Friday is a good prediction. The tuck shop will sell exactly 34 is too certain.

More data makes better predictions. One week of sales is a small sample. If Friday was the busiest day for four weeks in a row, the prediction is much stronger than after one week. Before acting on a prediction, ask whether there is enough data behind it.

Predictions should follow the pattern in the data. If 11 out of 36 learners in one class like Maths best, we might predict that Maths will also be popular in a similar class next door, but we cannot say exactly 11 learners will choose it there. Give a range or a likely order, not an exact number.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: writing a summary that lists every number again; a summary gives the main story with a few key numbers. Two: saying something the data does not show, like why learners like Maths; say that more data is needed. Three: predicting an exact number, like exactly 34 next Friday; use probably and about, and give a range. Four: making a strong prediction from very little data; more weeks of data give a better prediction.

The questions for this section are with you now: making predictions from data, using words like probably and about, and why more data gives better predictions.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the tuck shop and the favourite subjects — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Ask the Data

Ask: which is most, which is fewest? Maths 11 is most, Social Sciences 3 is fewest.

Ask: how many more? Subtract. Maths 11, English 7: 4 more. How many altogether? Add.

Some questions the data cannot answer, like why. Say so.

Keep the subject survey in mind and try the questions for this part: asking questions of data.

## Subtopic: Say It in Sentences

A summary is a few sentences with the main facts. It says what the data is about, the biggest and the smallest.

The tuck shop sold 112 vetkoek. Friday was busiest with 34, Thursday quietest with 16.

Check each sentence against the numbers before you say it.

Hold the tuck shop in mind and try this part's questions: summaries.

## Subtopic: What Will Happen Next?

A prediction is a sensible guess from the data. Friday was busiest, so next Friday will probably be busy too.

Say probably and about, not exactly. Things can change, like rain or a school trip.

More weeks of data make a stronger prediction than one week.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Analyse data by asking questions about the categories: most and fewest, how many more, how many altogether, and the order from most to fewest, and notice which questions the data cannot answer. A summary is a few sentences with the main facts and key numbers, each checked against the data. A prediction is a sensible guess based on the pattern in the data, said with words like probably and about, and more data makes it stronger.

Hold the tuck shop in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: predictions.
