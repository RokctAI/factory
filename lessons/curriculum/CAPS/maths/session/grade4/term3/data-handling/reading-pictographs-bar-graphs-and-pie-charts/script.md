# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers critically reading and interpreting data presented in words, pictographs, bar graphs and pie charts. The anchor is a tuck shop in Bethlehem whose vetkoek sales are shown in a pictograph and a bar graph, and a pie chart showing the sports chosen by 40 learners: soccer half, netball a quarter, cricket an eighth and athletics an eighth. The governing idea is that a graph is read from the outside in, title, key, labels and scale first and the bars or pictures second, and that a pie chart shows each category as a fraction of the whole group. Reading values from graphs, comparing and combining them, reading pie charts as fractions of a total, and spotting graphs that are hard to trust are examinable.

## Subtopic: Reading Pictographs and Bar Graphs

Read a graph from the outside in. First read the title: Vetkoek sold at the tuck shop in one week. Then read the labels: the days of the week along the bottom and the number of vetkoek up the side. Then read the scale or the key. Only then look at the bars or pictures. Skipping these steps is the most common reason for wrong answers.

In a one-to-one pictograph, count the pictures. If the Monday row has 24 vetkoek pictures, 24 vetkoek were sold on Monday. Bundling the pictures in your head helps: 4 rows of 5 and 4 more is 24. Always check the key to be sure that one picture stands for one vetkoek.

On a bar graph, read across from the top of each bar to the scale. The tuck shop bar graph has a scale in 2s. The Monday bar reaches 24, Tuesday 18, Wednesday 20, Thursday 16 and Friday 34. If a bar stops halfway between two marks, the value is halfway between them: halfway between 16 and 18 is 17.

Use a ruler or the edge of a paper to read across in a straight line. Reading at a slant can make you read the mark above or below by mistake. Write the value at the top of each bar in pencil if it helps you answer several questions.

The questions for this section are with you now: reading the title, labels, scale and key first, counting pictures, and reading bar heights from the scale.

## Subtopic: Reading Pie Charts

A pie chart is a circle cut into slices. The whole circle stands for the whole group, and each slice is a fraction of the group. 40 learners each chose one sport. The pie chart shows soccer as half the circle, netball as a quarter, cricket as an eighth and athletics as an eighth.

To find how many chose each sport, find that fraction of 40. Half of 40 is 20, so 20 learners chose soccer. A quarter of 40 is 40 divided by 4, which is 10, so 10 chose netball. An eighth of 40 is 40 divided by 8, which is 5, so 5 chose cricket and 5 chose athletics. Check: 20 plus 10 plus 5 plus 5 is 40.

Even without numbers, a pie chart shows comparisons at a glance. The biggest slice is the most popular choice. Two slices that are the same size are equally popular. Soccer's slice is twice the size of netball's, so twice as many learners chose soccer.

Pie charts are good at showing parts of a whole. They are less good for reading exact values, because slices that are close in size are hard to tell apart. That is why pie charts often have the numbers or fractions written on the slices.

The questions for this section are with you now: reading a pie chart as fractions of the whole group and finding how many are in each slice.

## Subtopic: Comparing, Combining and Reading Critically

Graphs answer three kinds of questions. How many: read one value. How many more or fewer: subtract two values. How many altogether: add values. On the tuck shop graph, Friday sold 34 and Thursday 16, so Friday sold 34 minus 16, which is 18, more. Over the whole week the shop sold 24 plus 18 plus 20 plus 16 plus 34, which is 112 vetkoek.

Ordering the categories is also a question graphs answer well. From most to least, the days are Friday 34, Monday 24, Wednesday 20, Tuesday 18 and Thursday 16. The most popular value is the highest bar or longest row, and the least popular is the lowest or shortest.

Read critically: ask whether the graph can be trusted. Does the scale start at 0 and go up in equal steps? Are the pictures the same size? Does the graph have a title, so you know what is being counted? A graph with a scale starting at 10 can make a small difference look huge, and a graph with no title might be about something quite different from what you think.

Data can also be given in words. The tuck shop sold 24 vetkoek on Monday, 18 on Tuesday and 20 on Wednesday is the same information as three bars. Being able to move between words, tables and graphs, and to check that they agree, is part of reading data well.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: reading the bars before the title, scale and key; read the graph from the outside in. Two: reading a bar against the wrong mark by looking at a slant; read straight across with a ruler. Three: thinking a quarter slice of a pie chart for 40 learners means 4 learners; a quarter of 40 is 40 divided by 4, which is 10. Four: answering how many more by adding; how many more means subtract the smaller value from the bigger one.

The questions for this section are with you now: how many, how many more and how many altogether, ordering categories, and checking whether a graph can be trusted.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the tuck shop and the sports pie chart — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Read the Pictures and Bars

Read the title, labels and key or scale first. Then the bars or pictures.

Pictograph: count the pictures. One picture is one vetkoek.

Bar graph: read straight across from the top of each bar to the scale. Halfway between 16 and 18 is 17.

Keep the tuck shop in mind and try the questions for this part: reading pictographs and bar graphs.

## Subtopic: Slices of a Circle

A pie chart is a circle cut into slices. The whole circle is the whole group of 40 learners.

Half the circle is soccer: half of 40 is 20. A quarter is netball: 10. An eighth each for cricket and athletics: 5 and 5.

Bigger slice, more learners. Same size slices, same number.

Hold the sports pie chart in mind and try this part's questions: pie charts.

## Subtopic: More, Less and Altogether

How many more? Subtract. Friday 34, Thursday 16: 18 more on Friday.

How many altogether? Add. The whole week: 112 vetkoek.

Can you trust the graph? Check for a title, a scale from 0 in equal steps, and same-size pictures.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Read every graph from the outside in: title, labels, scale or key, then the bars or pictures. Count pictures in a pictograph and read straight across from each bar to the scale in a bar graph. A pie chart is a whole group cut into fraction slices, so half of 40 is 20, a quarter is 10 and an eighth is 5. Subtract for how many more, add for how many altogether, and check that a graph has a title and a fair scale before trusting it.

Hold the tuck shop graph in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: comparing and combining.
