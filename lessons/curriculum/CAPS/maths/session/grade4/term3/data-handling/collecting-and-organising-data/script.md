# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers collecting data using tally marks and tables for recording, within the data cycle. The anchor is a Grade 4 class of 32 learners in Mbombela asking which fruit each learner likes best and how each learner gets to school. The governing idea is that data is collected to answer a question, that tally marks grouped in fives make counting fast and accurate, and that a table organises the counts so that every learner is counted exactly once and the totals can be checked. Naming the steps of the data cycle, recording with tally marks, reading tally marks as numbers and completing frequency tables are examinable.

## Subtopic: The Data Cycle

Data is information collected to answer a question. A Grade 4 class in Mbombela wants to know which fruit the class likes best, so that the tuck shop can stock the right fruit. The answers the learners give are the data. Data can be words, like mango or banana, or numbers, like how many minutes it takes to walk to school.

Collecting and using data follows a cycle of steps. First, pose a question. Second, collect the data, by asking people, counting or measuring. Third, organise the data, with tally marks and a table. Fourth, represent it, with a pictograph or a bar graph. Fifth, analyse it, by reading and comparing. Sixth, report what you found and what it means. Often the report leads to a new question, and the cycle starts again.

A good question is clear and has answers that can be counted. Which fruit do you like best: apple, banana, orange, mango or grapes? is a good survey question, because each learner gives one answer from a list. Do you like fruit? gives answers that are too simple to help the tuck shop. Giving a short list of choices makes the answers easy to count.

Collect data from the right people and count each person once. To learn about the class, ask every learner in the class, and make sure nobody answers twice. If a learner is absent, write that down too, so your total makes sense later.

The questions for this section are with you now: what data is, the six steps of the data cycle, and asking a good survey question.

## Subtopic: Counting with Tally Marks

A tally mark is a short line drawn for each answer as it is collected. When a learner says mango, draw one line next to mango. Tally marks are quick, because you do not have to stop and count while people are answering.

Tally marks are grouped in fives. Draw four lines standing up, and for the fifth line draw a line across the four. That bundle shows 5. Then start a new bundle. Grouping in fives means you can count tally marks by counting in fives and then adding the single lines: two bundles and three lines is 10 plus 3, which is 13.

In the fruit survey the tallies came out like this. Apple: one bundle and four lines, which is 9. Banana: one bundle and two lines, which is 7. Orange: one bundle, which is 5. Mango: one bundle and three lines, which is 8. Grapes: three lines, which is 3.

Check your tally. Add up all the counts: 9 plus 7 plus 5 plus 8 plus 3 is 32. There are 32 learners in the class, so every learner has been counted exactly once. If the total does not match the number of people asked, someone was missed or counted twice, and the count must be done again.

The questions for this section are with you now: drawing tally marks, bundling them in fives, reading them as numbers, and checking the total.

## Subtopic: Organising Data in Tables

A table organises data in rows and columns. A frequency table has a column for the categories, a column for the tally marks and a column for the totals. Frequency means how often something happens: the frequency of mango is 8, because 8 learners chose mango.

The class also asked how each learner gets to school. The table showed: walk, 14; taxi, 8; bus, 6; car, 4. The total of the frequency column is 14 plus 8 plus 6 plus 4, which is 32, matching the class. A total row at the bottom of the table is a quick way to check.

Tables make data easy to read and compare. From the transport table, more learners walk than use any other way, and walking is more than three times as common as coming by car, because 14 is more than 3 times 4. Questions like these are easy to answer from a table and hard to answer from a page of names.

A table needs a title and clear headings. The title says what the data is about: How learners in Grade 4B get to school. The headings name each column: Way of travel, Tally, Number of learners. Without them, a reader cannot tell what the numbers mean.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: drawing the fifth tally mark standing up like the others; the fifth line goes across the four to close the bundle. Two: letting a learner answer twice; each person is counted once, so the total must match the number asked. Three: asking a question with answers that are hard to count, like do you like fruit; give a short list of clear choices. Four: leaving out the title and headings; without them nobody knows what the numbers mean.

The questions for this section are with you now: frequency tables with tally and total columns, checking the total, and adding a title and headings.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the fruit survey and the trip to school — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Ask, Count, Show, Tell

Data is information that answers a question. Which fruit does our class like best?

The data cycle: ask a question, collect the answers, organise them, show them in a graph, read the graph, tell people what you found.

Ask a clear question with a short list of answers. Ask everyone once.

Keep the fruit survey in mind and try the questions for this part: the data cycle.

## Subtopic: Bundles of Five

One line for each answer. The fifth line goes across the other four to make a bundle of 5.

Count bundles in fives, then add the single lines. One bundle and four lines: 9.

Add all the counts. If they make 32 and there are 32 learners, everyone was counted once.

Hold the tally sheet in mind and try this part's questions: tally marks.

## Subtopic: Tidy Tables

A frequency table has a category, the tally marks and the total. Walk 14, taxi 8, bus 6, car 4.

Add the totals to check: 32, the whole class.

Give the table a title and headings so everyone knows what it shows.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Data is information collected to answer a question, and the data cycle goes: pose a question, collect, organise, represent, analyse and report. Tally marks record each answer with a line, bundled in fives with the fifth line across, so they can be counted in fives. A frequency table has categories, tallies and totals, a title and headings, and its totals must add up to the number of people asked.

Hold the transport table in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: organising data in tables.
