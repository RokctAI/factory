# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers addition of common fractions with the same denominators, for halves, thirds, quarters, fifths, sixths, sevenths and eighths, and solving problems in context. The anchor is a family in Mthatha sharing a pizza cut into 6 slices, a chocolate slab of 8 blocks, and a jug of juice filled in quarters. The governing idea is that fractions with the same denominator are pieces of the same size, so adding them means adding the numerators while the denominator stays the same, and that when the numerator reaches the denominator the fractions make one whole. Adding fractions with the same denominator, recognising totals of one whole and more, and solving addition problems with fractions are examinable.

## Subtopic: Adding Pieces of the Same Size

Lindiwe eats 2 slices of a pizza cut into 6 equal slices, and her brother eats 1 slice. Each slice is one sixth. Together they ate 3 slices, which is three sixths of the pizza. We write two sixths plus one sixth equals three sixths: 2/6 + 1/6 = 3/6.

When the bottom numbers are the same, the pieces are the same size. So adding fractions with the same bottom number is just counting pieces. Add the top numbers. Keep the bottom number. The bottom number is the name of the pieces, like sixths or eighths, and adding pieces does not change their size.

Two eighths plus three eighths: add the tops, 2 plus 3 is 5, keep the eighths. Five eighths. One fifth plus two fifths plus one fifth: 1 plus 2 plus 1 is 4, so four fifths. Adding more than two fractions works the same way.

A picture proves it. Draw a slab of 8 blocks. Colour 2 blocks red and 3 blocks blue. 5 of the 8 blocks are coloured, which is five eighths. The picture shows clearly that the answer is not five sixteenths, because the slab still has only 8 blocks.

The questions for this section are with you now: adding fractions with the same bottom number by adding the top numbers.

## Subtopic: Making One Whole and More

Sometimes fractions add up to a whole. A jug is filled with one quarter of a litre of juice, then two quarters more, then one quarter more. 1 plus 2 plus 1 is 4, so the jug holds four quarters of a litre. Four quarters is one whole litre. When the top number equals the bottom number, the fractions make one whole.

Knowing what makes a whole helps with problems. If three fifths of the class walk to school, then the rest is two fifths, because three fifths plus two fifths is five fifths, the whole class. If five eighths of a slab is eaten, three eighths is left. Ask: how many more parts make the whole?

Fractions can add up to more than one whole. Three quarters of a litre plus three quarters of a litre is six quarters. Four quarters make one litre, and there are 2 quarters more, so the total is 1 whole litre and 2 quarters. Two quarters is one half, so the total is one and a half litres.

So when your top number is bigger than your bottom number, take out the wholes. Seven sixths is six sixths and one sixth more: one whole and one sixth. Nine eighths is one whole and one eighth.

The questions for this section are with you now: fractions that add up to one whole, finding the part that is left, and totals bigger than one whole.

## Subtopic: Fraction Problems

In a word problem, look for the parts being joined: altogether, in total, how much did they use. A family in Mthatha eats two sixths of a pizza at lunch and three sixths at supper. How much did they eat altogether? 2/6 + 3/6 = 5/6. How much is left? Six sixths make the whole, and 5 have been eaten, so one sixth is left.

Measurement problems use fractions of units. A builder uses three eighths of a bag of cement in the morning and four eighths in the afternoon. That is seven eighths of a bag. He needs one more eighth to have used one whole bag.

Time problems use fractions of an hour. Thabo spends one quarter of an hour on spelling and two quarters of an hour on maths. That is three quarters of an hour of homework. One quarter of an hour is 15 minutes, so three quarters is 45 minutes.

Always check your answer with a picture or with the whole. If the total of the parts is more than the whole when that is impossible, like eating more than a whole pizza that had only 6 slices, something has gone wrong.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: adding the bottom numbers as well, 2/8 + 3/8 = 5/16; the pieces stay eighths, so the answer is 5/8. Two: forgetting that 4/4 is one whole and leaving the answer as a fraction that seems to be less; when the top equals the bottom you have a whole. Three: writing 6/4 as 6 wholes; take out four quarters as one whole and keep 2 quarters, so it is 1 whole and 2 quarters. Four: answering how much is left with the amount eaten; subtract the eaten parts from the whole, 6/6, to find what is left.

The questions for this section are with you now: adding fractions in pizza, cement and time problems, and finding the part that is left.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the pizza, the chocolate and the juice jug — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Count the Pieces

Same bottom number means same size pieces. So just count the pieces.

Two eighths plus three eighths: 2 pieces and 3 pieces, 5 pieces. Five eighths. Add the tops, keep the bottom.

Not five sixteenths. The slab still has 8 blocks.

Keep the chocolate slab in mind and try the questions for this part: adding the top numbers.

## Subtopic: Filling a Whole

When the top number is the same as the bottom number, you have a whole. Four quarters is one litre.

To find what is left, ask how many parts finish the whole. Three fifths walk, so two fifths do not.

More than a whole? Take the whole out. Six quarters is one whole and two quarters.

Hold the juice jug in mind and try this part's questions: making one whole.

## Subtopic: Fraction Stories

Look for altogether. Two sixths of a pizza at lunch, three sixths at supper: five sixths eaten, one sixth left.

Three eighths of a bag of cement and four eighths more: seven eighths. One more eighth makes a whole bag.

One quarter of an hour and two quarters more: three quarters of an hour, which is 45 minutes.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Fractions with the same bottom number are pieces of the same size, so add the top numbers and keep the bottom number: two eighths plus three eighths is five eighths. When the top equals the bottom, you have one whole, and the parts left over are the parts that finish the whole. Totals bigger than one whole are written as wholes and parts, like six quarters as one whole and two quarters. In problems, look for altogether and check with a picture.

Hold the family supper in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: fraction stories.
