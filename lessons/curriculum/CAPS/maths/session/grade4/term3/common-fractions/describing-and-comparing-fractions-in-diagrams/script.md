# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers describing common fractions in diagram form and comparing them in diagrams, for halves, thirds, quarters, fifths, sixths, sevenths and eighths. The anchor is a melktert cut into equal slices at a bakery in Bloemfontein, a slab of chocolate with 8 equal blocks, and strips of paper folded into a fraction wall. The governing idea is that a fraction names equal parts of a whole: the bottom number says how many equal parts the whole is cut into, and the top number says how many of those parts we are talking about. Naming the shaded and unshaded parts of a diagram, recognising when parts are not equal, and comparing fractions drawn on wholes of the same size are examinable.

## Subtopic: What a Fraction Is

A fraction is a part of a whole. The whole can be a melktert, a slab of chocolate, a strip of paper or a field. To make a fraction, the whole is cut into equal parts. If a melktert is cut into 4 equal slices, each slice is one quarter of the melktert. If it is cut into 8 equal slices, each slice is one eighth.

The names come from the number of equal parts. 2 equal parts are halves, 3 are thirds, 4 are quarters, 5 are fifths, 6 are sixths, 7 are sevenths and 8 are eighths. When we write a fraction we write two numbers, one above the other with a line between them. One quarter is written as 1 over 4. Three eighths is written as 3 over 8.

The bottom number is the denominator. It tells you how many equal parts the whole has been cut into. The top number is the numerator. It tells you how many of those parts you are talking about. In three eighths, the 8 says the whole is cut into 8 equal parts and the 3 says we are talking about 3 of them.

The parts must be equal. If a melktert is cut into 4 pieces of different sizes, the pieces are not quarters, even though there are 4 of them. Before you name a fraction in a picture, check that the parts are the same size. Only then do the names halves, thirds and quarters make sense.

The questions for this section are with you now: what a fraction is, the names of the parts, and the jobs of the top and bottom numbers.

## Subtopic: Reading and Drawing Fractions in Diagrams

To read a fraction from a diagram, count two things. First count all the equal parts: that is the denominator. Then count the shaded parts: that is the numerator. A chocolate slab has 8 equal blocks and 3 of them have been eaten. 3 of the 8 blocks are gone, so three eighths of the slab is eaten.

The parts that are not shaded make a fraction too. If 3 of the 8 blocks are eaten, 5 of the 8 are left, so five eighths of the slab is left. Three eighths and five eighths together make eight eighths, and eight eighths is the whole slab. The shaded and unshaded fractions always add up to one whole.

Fractions can be drawn on many shapes. A circle can be cut like a melktert. A rectangle can be cut into strips or blocks. A strip of paper folded in half and then in half again shows quarters. When you draw a fraction, first cut the whole into the right number of equal parts, then shade the number of parts on top.

One whole can be written as a fraction too. Four quarters is one whole melktert. Seven sevenths is one whole. When the top number and the bottom number are the same, the fraction is one whole. When the top number is 0, nothing is shaded and the fraction is zero.

The questions for this section are with you now: reading shaded and unshaded fractions from a picture, drawing a fraction, and writing one whole as a fraction.

## Subtopic: Comparing Fractions in Diagrams

Pictures let us compare fractions. Fold one paper strip into halves and another the same length into quarters. One half covers more of its strip than one quarter does, so one half is bigger than one quarter. The more equal parts the whole is cut into, the smaller each part is.

A fraction wall puts strips of the same length one under another. The top strip is one whole. Under it are halves, then thirds, quarters, fifths, sixths, sevenths and eighths. Looking down the wall, the pieces get smaller as the denominator gets bigger. One eighth is the smallest piece on the wall; one half is the biggest.

To compare two fractions on a fraction wall, find each one and see which reaches further along. Three quarters reaches further than two thirds, so three quarters is bigger. Two fifths does not reach as far as one half, so two fifths is smaller than one half. The wall also shows fractions that reach exactly the same point, like one half and two quarters.

Comparing only works when the wholes are the same size. Half of a big melktert is more tart than half of a small one, but that is because the wholes are different, not because the fractions are. Fraction diagrams that are being compared must be drawn on wholes of the same size.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: naming 4 unequal pieces as quarters; the parts must be equal before they get a fraction name. Two: thinking one eighth is bigger than one half because 8 is bigger than 2; more parts means each part is smaller. Three: writing the shaded parts over the unshaded parts, 3 over 5 for 3 of 8 blocks; the bottom number counts all the parts, so it is 3 over 8. Four: comparing half of a big melktert with half of a small one; fractions can only be compared on wholes of the same size.

The questions for this section are with you now: comparing fractions with strips and the fraction wall, and why the wholes must be the same size.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the melktert, the chocolate slab and the paper strips — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Equal Parts

A fraction is a part of a whole cut into equal parts. 4 equal slices of melktert: each one is one quarter. 8 equal slices: each one is one eighth.

The bottom number says how many equal parts the whole has. The top number says how many parts we take. Three eighths: 8 equal parts, 3 of them.

No equal parts, no fraction. 4 pieces of different sizes are not quarters.

Keep the melktert in mind and try the questions for this part: what a fraction is.

## Subtopic: Shade and Name

Count all the parts: that is the bottom number. Count the shaded parts: that is the top number. 3 blocks of 8 eaten: three eighths.

The rest is a fraction too. 5 blocks of 8 are left: five eighths. Three eighths and five eighths make eight eighths, the whole slab.

Top and bottom the same means one whole. Four quarters is one melktert.

Hold the chocolate slab in mind and try this part's questions: reading and drawing fractions.

## Subtopic: Look and Compare

Same size strips, different cuts. Halves are bigger than quarters. More parts means smaller parts.

The fraction wall shows it all. Halves at the top, eighths at the bottom. Three quarters reaches further than two thirds, so it is bigger.

Only compare wholes of the same size. Half a big tart is not the same as half a small one.

Read a diagram by counting all the parts and then the shaded parts; the shaded and unshaded fractions make one whole. More parts means smaller parts, and the fraction wall compares fractions on wholes of the same size.
