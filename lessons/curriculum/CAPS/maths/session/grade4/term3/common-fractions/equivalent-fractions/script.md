# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers recognising and using equivalent forms of common fractions where one denominator is a multiple of the other, such as halves, quarters and eighths, or thirds and sixths. The anchor is a pizza at a family supper in Kimberley cut first into halves, then quarters, then eighths, a fraction wall, and paper strips folded again and again. The governing idea is that cutting every part into the same number of smaller parts changes the name of a fraction but not its size, so multiplying the top and bottom numbers by the same number gives an equivalent fraction. Finding equivalent fractions from diagrams and by multiplying, and using them to compare fractions, are examinable.

## Subtopic: Seeing Equivalent Fractions

A pizza is cut in half. One half is for Mom. Now cut each half into 2 equal slices. The pizza has 4 slices, and Mom's half is 2 of them: two quarters. Cut every slice in two again. Now there are 8 slices and Mom's half is 4 of them: four eighths. Mom has the same amount of pizza all along. One half, two quarters and four eighths are equivalent fractions.

Equivalent means having the same value. Equivalent fractions name the same amount of the same whole in different ways. On the fraction wall, one half, two quarters and four eighths all end at exactly the same point. So do one third and two sixths, and three quarters and six eighths.

Folding paper shows it too. Fold a strip in half and shade one half. Fold it in half again: the shaded part is now two quarters. Fold again: four eighths. The shading has not changed; only the number of parts has. Each fold doubles the number of parts in the whole and doubles the number of shaded parts.

We write equivalent fractions with an equals sign. 1/2 = 2/4 = 4/8. 1/3 = 2/6. 3/4 = 6/8. The equals sign says the amounts are the same, even though the numbers look different.

The questions for this section are with you now: seeing equivalent fractions on the pizza, the fraction wall and folded paper.

## Subtopic: Making Equivalent Fractions

The pizza shows a rule. When every part is cut into 2, the number of parts doubles and the number of Mom's parts doubles. So 1/2 becomes 2/4: the bottom number is multiplied by 2 and the top number is multiplied by 2. To make an equivalent fraction, multiply the top and the bottom by the same number.

Use this when one bottom number is a multiple of the other. To write three quarters in eighths, ask: what times 4 gives 8? The answer is 2, so multiply the top by 2 as well: 3 times 2 is 6. Three quarters equals six eighths. To write two thirds in sixths: 3 times 2 is 6, so 2 times 2 is 4. Two thirds equals four sixths.

You can multiply by any number, as long as top and bottom get the same number. One half in sixths: 2 times 3 is 6, so 1 times 3 is 3, and one half equals three sixths. One half in eighths: 2 times 4 is 8, so one half equals four eighths. The bigger the multiplier, the smaller the new parts, and the more of them you need.

Never add the same number to top and bottom. Adding 1 to the top and bottom of 1/2 gives 2/3, and two thirds is more than one half, as the fraction wall shows. Adding changes the amount; multiplying top and bottom by the same number keeps it.

The questions for this section are with you now: multiplying top and bottom by the same number to make equivalent fractions in quarters, sixths and eighths.

## Subtopic: Using Equivalent Fractions

Equivalent fractions make comparing easier. Which is bigger, three quarters or five eighths? Change three quarters into eighths: six eighths. Now both have the same bottom, and six eighths is more than five eighths. So three quarters is bigger. Changing to the same bottom number turns a hard comparison into an easy one.

They also help with fair sharing. One learner eats one half of a slab of chocolate with 8 blocks, and another eats three eighths. Who ate more? One half is four eighths, so the first learner ate 4 blocks and the second ate 3. The first ate more, by one eighth.

Equivalent fractions can be found backwards too. Four eighths can be written as two quarters or one half by dividing the top and bottom by the same number. Six eighths is three quarters, dividing top and bottom by 2. Saying a fraction with the smallest numbers possible is often the clearest way to say it.

Check with a picture whenever you are unsure. Draw two strips of the same length, cut one into quarters and one into eighths, and shade three quarters and six eighths. If the shading ends at the same point, the fractions are equivalent.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: adding the same number to top and bottom, writing 1/2 = 2/3; multiply top and bottom by the same number instead. Two: multiplying only the bottom, writing 3/4 = 3/8; the top must be multiplied by the same number, giving 6/8. Three: thinking 4/8 is bigger than 1/2 because the numbers are bigger; equivalent fractions name the same amount. Four: comparing 3/4 and 5/8 by their top numbers alone; change 3/4 to 6/8 first, then compare.

The questions for this section are with you now: using equivalent fractions to compare, to share fairly, and to write a fraction in smaller numbers.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the pizza, the fraction wall and the folded paper — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Same Size Pieces

Half a pizza. Cut every slice in two: two quarters. Cut again: four eighths. Same amount of pizza every time.

Fractions that name the same amount are equivalent. On the fraction wall they end at the same place.

1/2 = 2/4 = 4/8. 1/3 = 2/6. 3/4 = 6/8.

Keep the pizza in mind and try the questions for this part: seeing equivalent fractions.

## Subtopic: Times Top and Bottom

To make an equivalent fraction, multiply the top and the bottom by the same number.

Three quarters into eighths: 4 times 2 is 8, so 3 times 2 is 6. Six eighths.

Never add to the top and bottom. Adding 1 to both turns one half into two thirds, which is more.

Hold the folded paper in mind and try this part's questions: making equivalent fractions.

## Subtopic: Put Them to Work

Three quarters or five eighths? Change three quarters to six eighths. Six eighths beats five eighths.

One half or three eighths? One half is four eighths. Four eighths beats three eighths by one eighth.

Go backwards to smaller numbers: six eighths is three quarters, four eighths is one half.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Equivalent fractions name the same amount in different ways: one half is two quarters and four eighths, one third is two sixths, three quarters is six eighths. Cutting every part into the same number of smaller parts changes the name but not the amount, so multiply the top and bottom by the same number. Use equivalent fractions to give two fractions the same bottom number before comparing, and go backwards by dividing to write a fraction with smaller numbers.

Hold the fraction wall in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: using equivalent fractions.
