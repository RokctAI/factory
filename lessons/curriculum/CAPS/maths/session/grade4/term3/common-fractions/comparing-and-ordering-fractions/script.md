# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers comparing and ordering common fractions with different denominators: halves, thirds, quarters, fifths, sixths, sevenths and eighths. The anchor is a school fun run in Durban where learners have run different fractions of the route, and bottles of juice that are partly full. The governing idea is that fractions are compared by thinking about the size and the number of parts: with the same numerator, the smaller denominator gives bigger parts; with the same denominator, the bigger numerator gives more parts; and one half and the fraction wall help when neither is the same. Comparing two fractions with the symbols for is greater than and is less than, and ordering several fractions, are examinable.

## Subtopic: Comparing Unit Fractions

A unit fraction has 1 on top: one half, one third, one quarter, one fifth, and so on. Each one is a single part of a whole. To compare unit fractions, look only at the bottom number. The bigger the bottom number, the more parts the whole is cut into, and the smaller each part is.

At the fun run, the route is the same length for everyone. Sipho has run one third of the route and Lerato has run one fifth. One third is bigger than one fifth, because a route cut into 3 parts has longer parts than a route cut into 5. Sipho has run further.

So the order of the unit fractions from biggest to smallest is one half, one third, one quarter, one fifth, one sixth, one seventh, one eighth. This is the order of the rows in the fraction wall, from the top down.

We write the comparison with symbols. One third is greater than one fifth is written 1/3 > 1/5. One eighth is less than one half is written 1/8 < 1/2. The open side of the symbol faces the bigger fraction, just as it does for whole numbers.

The questions for this section are with you now: comparing unit fractions by their bottom numbers and writing the comparison with symbols.

## Subtopic: Same Bottom or Same Top

When two fractions have the same bottom number, the parts are the same size, so just compare the top numbers. Five eighths is more than three eighths, because 5 parts of the same size are more than 3. A juice bottle that is five eighths full has more juice than one that is three eighths full.

When two fractions have the same top number, they have the same number of parts, so compare the size of the parts. Two thirds is more than two fifths, because thirds are bigger than fifths. Two big slices beat two small slices. Here the smaller bottom number gives the bigger fraction.

Be careful which rule you use. Same bottom: bigger top wins. Same top: smaller bottom wins. Say to yourself which number is the same before you decide. 4/7 and 6/7 have the same bottom, so 6/7 is bigger. 3/4 and 3/8 have the same top, so 3/4 is bigger.

Fractions with the same top and bottom number are one whole: 7/7, 5/5, 8/8. A fraction with a smaller top than bottom is less than one whole. So 6/7 is just less than a whole, one seventh short, and 7/8 is one eighth short of a whole.

The questions for this section are with you now: comparing fractions with the same denominator and with the same numerator.

## Subtopic: Ordering with a Half and the Fraction Wall

When neither the tops nor the bottoms match, use one half as a measuring stick. A fraction is more than one half if its top is more than half of its bottom. Three eighths is less than one half, because half of 8 is 4 and 3 is less than 4. Four sevenths is more than one half, because half of 7 is three and a half and 4 is more. So three eighths is less than four sevenths.

The fraction wall settles harder cases. Two thirds and three quarters are both more than one half. On the wall, three quarters reaches further than two thirds, so three quarters is bigger. Each is one part short of a whole: two thirds is one third short and three quarters is one quarter short, and a quarter is a smaller gap than a third.

To order several fractions, sort them into groups first: less than a half, exactly a half, more than a half. Then compare within each group. Order 3/4, 1/5, 1/2 and 3/8 from smallest to biggest. 1/5 and 3/8 are less than a half, 1/2 is a half, 3/4 is more. 1/5 is less than 3/8 on the wall. So the order is 1/5, 3/8, 1/2, 3/4.

Ordering fractions can mean smallest first, called ascending order, or biggest first, called descending order. Read the question carefully. Then check your list once more, pair by pair, with the wall or the half rule.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: deciding 1/8 is bigger than 1/3 because 8 is bigger than 3; with 1 on top, the bigger bottom number means a smaller piece. Two: using the same-top rule on fractions with the same bottom, saying 3/8 is bigger than 5/8; with the same bottom, the bigger top wins. Three: thinking 3/8 is more than one half because 3 is close to 4; half of 8 is 4, so 3/8 is less than a half. Four: giving the order biggest first when the question asks for ascending order; ascending means smallest first.

The questions for this section are with you now: comparing with one half, using the fraction wall, and ordering several fractions.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the fun run and the juice bottles — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: One Piece Each

One on top means one piece. To compare, look at the bottom. Bigger bottom, smaller piece.

Same route, cut into 3 or 5. One third is longer than one fifth. Sipho, on one third, has run further than Lerato, on one fifth.

Biggest to smallest: one half, one third, one quarter, down to one eighth.

Keep the fun run in mind and try the questions for this part: comparing unit fractions.

## Subtopic: Same Bottom, Same Top

Same bottom: the pieces are the same size, so more pieces wins. Five eighths of juice beats three eighths.

Same top: the same number of pieces, so bigger pieces win. Two thirds beats two fifths.

First ask which number is the same. Then use the right rule.

Hold the juice bottles in mind and try this part's questions: same bottom and same top.

## Subtopic: Line Them Up

Use one half to help. Half of 8 is 4, so three eighths is less than a half. Half of 7 is three and a half, so four sevenths is more than a half.

Use the wall for close ones. Three quarters reaches past two thirds.

To order: group into below a half, a half, above a half. Then sort each group. 1/5, 3/8, 1/2, 3/4.

Pocket summary of the lesson. With 1 on top, the bigger the bottom number the smaller the fraction. With the same bottom number, the bigger top wins; with the same top number, the smaller bottom wins. When neither matches, compare each fraction with one half and use the fraction wall. To order fractions, group them below, at and above a half, then sort each group, and read whether the question wants smallest or biggest first.

Hold the fraction wall in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: ordering fractions.
