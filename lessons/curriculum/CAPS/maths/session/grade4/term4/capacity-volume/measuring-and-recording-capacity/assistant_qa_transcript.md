### subtopic_1 — Millilitres and Litres
**Assistant:** Why do we need millilitres at all? Couldn't we just use litres for everything?

**Tutor:** We could try, but the numbers would get awkward very quickly. A teaspoon holds 5 ml, which in litres is only a tiny part of a litre, five thousandths of it. Imagine a medicine bottle saying take five thousandths of a litre. Nobody would understand it, and it would be easy to give the wrong amount. Millilitres were made for small amounts, so the numbers stay whole and simple: 5 ml, 15 ml, 250 ml. Litres were made for large amounts, so we do not have to write 10 000 ml for a big bucket, just 10 l. This is the same idea you met with lengths and masses. We use millimetres and centimetres for small lengths and kilometres for long distances, and grams for light things and kilograms for heavy things. In each case, 1 000 of the small unit make one of the big unit, so changing between them is always multiplying or dividing by 1 000. Choosing the right unit is part of measuring well. A good test is to ask whether the number feels sensible. If you would need a very big number or a tiny fraction, try the other unit.

### subtopic_2 — Estimating and Measuring with Spoons, Cups and Jugs
**Assistant:** Why must I read the jug at eye level? I can see the water from the top.

**Tutor:** From the top you can see the water, but not exactly where it reaches on the scale. The marks are printed on the side of the jug, and the water surface sits a little way inside the glass or plastic. If your eyes are above the water, the surface lines up with a mark that is lower down the side, so you read too little. If your eyes are below, you read too much. Only when your eyes are level with the water does the surface line up with the right mark. Holding the jug up in your hand causes another problem: your hand tilts it a little, so the water is higher on one side than the other. That is why the rule is: put the jug on a flat table, bend down until your eyes are level with the water and then read the mark. Scientists and cooks do the same thing. It takes a few seconds and can change your reading by many millilitres, which matters when you are following a recipe or measuring medicine. Try it at home with a measuring jug and some water: read it from above, then from eye level, and see the difference for yourself.

### subtopic_3 — Recording, Comparing and Ordering Capacity
**Assistant:** How do I estimate capacity? I never know what to guess.

**Tutor:** Estimating gets easier when you have a few known amounts in your head to compare with. Learn these benchmarks: a teaspoon is 5 ml, a tablespoon is 15 ml, a measuring cup or a normal cup of tea is about 250 ml, a can of cold drink is 330 ml, a small water bottle is 500 ml, a milk carton is 1 litre, a big cold drink bottle is 2 litres and a bucket is about 10 litres. When you need to estimate, find the benchmark that is closest in size. A jug that looks about the size of four cups holds about 1 litre, because 4 times 250 ml is 1 000 ml. A pot that looks like it would take five milk cartons holds about 5 litres. Then measure to check, and notice how close you were. If you guessed 1 litre and the jug held 1 200 ml, your guess was good. Each time you check, your estimates get better. You do not need to be exact when estimating. The goal is a sensible amount, so that you notice straight away if a measurement says a cup holds 25 litres, which cannot be right.
