# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers estimating and practically measuring the capacity of 3D objects using measuring spoons, cups and jugs, and recording, comparing and ordering capacity and volume in millilitres and litres. The anchor is a family kitchen in Upington making ginger beer for a Heritage Day braai, with a 5 ml teaspoon, a 15 ml tablespoon, a 250 ml measuring cup, a 1 litre jug, cold drink cans and bottles, and a bucket. The governing idea is that capacity is measured against a standard unit, millilitres for small amounts and litres for large amounts, with 1 000 millilitres in 1 litre, and that every jug is read by first working out what each small mark stands for. Choosing a sensible unit, estimating, reading jugs with different intervals, and ordering capacities after changing them to the same unit are examinable.

## Subtopic: Millilitres and Litres

Capacity tells us how much a container can hold. We measure capacity in millilitres and litres. The short way to write millilitres is ml, and litres is l. A millilitre is a tiny amount, about 20 drops of water. A litre is the amount in a carton of milk. 1 litre is the same as 1 000 millilitres.

Small amounts are measured in millilitres. A teaspoon holds 5 ml, which is why medicine bottles say take 5 ml. A tablespoon holds 15 ml. A measuring cup holds 250 ml. A can of cold drink holds 330 ml, and a small bottle of water holds 500 ml.

Large amounts are measured in litres. A big bottle of cold drink holds 2 l. A kettle holds about 1 l to 2 l. A bucket holds about 10 l. A bath holds many litres. Using litres for big amounts keeps the numbers small and easy to understand.

To choose a unit, ask: is it a small amount or a large one? A spoon of vanilla essence for the ginger beer is millilitres. The water in the big pot is litres. Saying the bucket holds 10 ml would be silly, because 10 ml is just two teaspoons.

The questions for this section are with you now: what capacity is, millilitres and litres, the sizes of spoons and cups, and choosing a sensible unit.

## Subtopic: Estimating and Measuring with Spoons, Cups and Jugs

Before measuring, estimate. Look at the jug and the cup and guess how many cups will fill the jug. Then measure to check. An estimate is a sensible guess, and each time you check it, your next guess gets better.

Measuring spoons and cups must be filled exactly to the top or to the line, not heaped and not half full. 3 teaspoons of 5 ml fill a tablespoon, because 3 times 5 is 15. For the ginger beer, the recipe needs 2 tablespoons of ginger, which is 2 times 15, so 30 ml. A 1 litre jug is filled by 4 measuring cups, because 4 times 250 is 1 000.

A measuring jug has a scale up the side. First work out what each small mark is worth. On one jug the numbers are 200, 400, 600, 800 and 1 000 ml, with one small mark halfway between each pair. From 600 to 800 is 200 ml, split into 2 spaces, so each space is 100 ml. Water at the small mark between 600 and 800 is 700 ml.

Read a jug carefully. Put it on a flat table, bend down so your eyes are level with the water, and read the mark at the top of the water. If you look from above or hold the jug tilted, the water seems higher or lower than it really is.

The questions for this section are with you now: estimating before measuring, filling spoons and cups exactly, working out what each mark on a jug is worth, and reading a jug at eye level.

## Subtopic: Recording, Comparing and Ordering Capacity

Always record a capacity with its number and its unit: 330 ml, 2 l, 1 l 500 ml. A number on its own does not say enough. A table helps: one column for the container, one for the estimate and one for the measured capacity.

To compare capacities, use the same unit. The family has a jug of 750 ml of ginger beer and a 1 l bottle. 750 looks bigger than 1, but 1 l is 1 000 ml, and 1 000 ml is more than 750 ml. So the bottle holds more, by 1 000 minus 750, which is 250 ml.

To order capacities, change them all to millilitres first. The containers at the braai are a juice box of 200 ml, a can of 330 ml, a water bottle of 500 ml, a milk carton of 1 l and a cold drink bottle of 2 l. From smallest to biggest: 200 ml, 330 ml, 500 ml, 1 l, 2 l. The can holds 330 minus 200, which is 130 ml more than the juice box.

Mixed capacities, like 1 l 500 ml, are written in millilitres by changing the litres: 1 000 ml plus 500 ml is 1 500 ml. Then 1 l 500 ml can be compared with 2 l, which is 2 000 ml. The 2 l bottle holds 500 ml more.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: using litres for a teaspoon of medicine; small amounts are measured in millilitres, so it is 5 ml. Two: reading a jug from above or while holding it up; put it on a flat table and read the water level at eye level. Three: saying 750 ml is more than 1 l because 750 is bigger than 1; change to millilitres first, and 1 l is 1 000 ml. Four: counting every small mark on a jug as 1 ml; work out what each mark is worth first, which here is 100 ml.

The questions for this section are with you now: recording capacity with a unit, comparing with the same unit, ordering containers, and mixed capacities like 1 l 500 ml.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the ginger beer, the spoons and the jug — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Small and Big Amounts

Capacity is how much a container holds. Small amounts: millilitres, ml. Big amounts: litres, l.

A teaspoon is 5 ml, a tablespoon 15 ml, a cup 250 ml.

1 litre is 1 000 ml. A bucket holds about 10 l.

Keep the ginger beer pot in mind and try the questions for this part: millilitres and litres.

## Subtopic: Fill, Pour and Count

Guess first, then measure. Fill spoons and cups exactly.

3 teaspoons make a tablespoon. 4 cups of 250 ml make 1 litre.

Find what each mark on the jug is worth. Read at eye level on a flat table.

Hold the measuring jug in mind and try this part's questions: measuring capacity.

## Subtopic: Which Holds More

Write the unit every time: 330 ml, not just 330.

Change to millilitres before comparing. 1 l is 1 000 ml, so it beats 750 ml.

Order the braai bottles: 200 ml, 330 ml, 500 ml, 1 l, 2 l.
