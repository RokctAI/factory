# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers finding the areas of regular and irregular shapes by counting squares on grids to develop an understanding of square units. The anchor is a table being covered with square sticky notes, a house plan in Gqeberha drawn on grid paper, and leaf and puddle shapes traced onto a grid. The governing idea is that area is the amount of surface inside a shape, measured by counting how many equal squares cover it with no gaps and no overlaps, that rows of squares can be counted with multiplication, and that area and perimeter are different measurements. Counting squares to find area, including half squares for irregular shapes, and comparing area and perimeter are examinable.

## Subtopic: What Area Is

Area is the amount of flat surface a shape covers. The top of a table, the floor of a room and the page of a book all have area. Perimeter is the distance around the edge; area is the space inside. A garden's fence is its perimeter, and its grass is its area.

To measure area, cover the surface with equal squares, with no gaps and no overlaps, and count them. If a table top is covered exactly by 24 square sticky notes, its area is 24 sticky notes. Squares are used because they fit together perfectly, like the tessellations you made in Term 3.

The answer is in square units. If each square on a grid is 1 square centimetre, a shape covering 15 squares has an area of 15 square centimetres. If the squares are 1 square metre each, the area is 15 square metres. Square units tell you the size of the squares you counted.

The size of the squares matters. The same table covered with bigger squares needs fewer of them, and with smaller squares needs more. When you compare areas, use squares of the same size, just as you use the same unit to compare lengths.

The questions for this section are with you now: what area is, covering with squares with no gaps and no overlaps, square units, and using squares of the same size.

## Subtopic: Counting Squares in Rectangles

On a grid, count the squares inside a shape. A rectangle 5 squares long and 3 squares wide has 3 rows of 5 squares. Count them one by one and you get 15. Or count by rows: 5, 10, 15. So its area is 15 square units.

Counting rows is a quick way to count. A rectangle with 4 rows of 6 squares has 6, 12, 18, 24 squares, which is 4 times 6, which is 24 square units. You do not need to count every square, as long as every row has the same number.

On the house plan, each square stands for 1 square metre. The kitchen is 4 squares by 3 squares, so its area is 12 square metres. The bedroom is 4 squares by 4 squares, so its area is 16 square metres. The bedroom is bigger, by 4 square metres.

A shape made of rectangles can be split into parts. An L-shaped lounge on the plan is a rectangle 5 by 2 joined to a rectangle 2 by 2. 10 squares plus 4 squares is 14 square metres. Count each part, then add.

The questions for this section are with you now: counting squares, counting by rows, reading a plan in square metres, and splitting L shapes into rectangles.

## Subtopic: Irregular Shapes and Area versus Perimeter

Irregular shapes, like a leaf or a puddle traced onto a grid, do not fill squares exactly. Count the full squares first. Then look at the part squares: two half squares make one whole square. Squares that are more than half covered can be counted as 1, and squares less than half covered can be left out. This gives a good estimate of the area.

A leaf traced on a centimetre grid covers 12 full squares and 6 half squares. The 6 halves make 3 whole squares. So the area of the leaf is about 12 plus 3, which is 15 square centimetres.

Area and perimeter measure different things. A rectangle 6 by 2 has an area of 12 square units and a perimeter of 16 units. A rectangle 4 by 3 also has an area of 12 square units, but its perimeter is 14 units. Same area, different perimeters. So you cannot work out one from the other; measure each separately.

The units are different too. Perimeter is in units of length, like centimetres. Area is in square units, like square centimetres. Writing the right kind of unit shows which one you have measured.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: counting the edges round the outside for area; area counts the squares inside. Two: leaving gaps or overlapping squares when covering a shape; squares must fit with no gaps and no overlaps. Three: counting every half square as a whole square; two half squares make one whole square. Four: thinking shapes with the same area must have the same perimeter; a 6 by 2 and a 4 by 3 rectangle both cover 12 squares but have perimeters of 16 and 14.

The questions for this section are with you now: estimating the area of irregular shapes with full and half squares, and how area and perimeter differ.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the sticky notes, the house plan and the leaf on the grid — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: The Space Inside

Area is the space inside a shape. The fence is the perimeter. The grass is the area.

Cover the shape with equal squares, no gaps, no overlaps. Count them.

The answer is in square units.

## Subtopic: Rows of Squares

Count the squares inside. Or count in rows: 3 rows of 5 is 15.

Kitchen 4 by 3: 12 square metres. Bedroom 4 by 4: 16 square metres.

L shapes: split into two rectangles, count each, add.

Hold the house plan in mind and try this part's questions: counting squares.

## Subtopic: Leaves, Puddles and the Difference

Count the full squares. Two half squares make one more.

Leaf: 12 full and 6 halves. 12 plus 3 is about 15 squares.

Area and perimeter are different. 6 by 2 and 4 by 3 both cover 12 squares, but their fences are 16 and 14.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Area is the amount of flat surface inside a shape, measured by covering it with equal squares with no gaps and no overlaps and counting them; the answer is in square units. Count rectangles by rows, split L shapes into rectangles, and for irregular shapes count full squares and add one for every two half squares. Area and perimeter are different: shapes with the same area can have different perimeters, and area uses square units while perimeter uses units of length.
