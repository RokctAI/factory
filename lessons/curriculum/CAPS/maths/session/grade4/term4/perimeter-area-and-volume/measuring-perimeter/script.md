# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers measuring perimeter using rulers or measuring tapes, for rectangles, squares and other polygons, and solving problems involving perimeter. The anchor is a school vegetable garden in Thohoyandou that needs fencing, a photo frame that needs ribbon round its edge, and shapes drawn on grid paper. The governing idea is that perimeter is the total length of the boundary of a shape, found by measuring every side and adding the lengths, with all sides in the same unit, and that a rectangle's opposite sides are equal and a square's four sides are equal. Measuring and calculating the perimeter of polygons, finding perimeters on a grid, and solving fencing and framing problems are examinable.

## Subtopic: What Perimeter Is

The perimeter of a shape is the distance all the way around its outside edge. If you walk round the edge of the school vegetable garden, starting and ending at the gate, the distance you walk is the perimeter of the garden. The word comes from peri, meaning around, and meter, meaning measure.

To find a perimeter, measure every side and add the lengths together. A book is 30 cm long and 20 cm wide. Its edges are 30 cm, 20 cm, 30 cm and 20 cm. The perimeter is 30 plus 20 plus 30 plus 20, which is 100 cm.

Measure carefully with a ruler for small shapes and a tape measure for big ones. Start each side at the 0 mark and go corner to corner. Write each length down beside its side so that you do not miss one or count one twice.

Perimeter is a length, so it is measured in units of length: millimetres, centimetres, metres or kilometres. All the sides must be in the same unit before you add. A garden with sides of 4 m and 250 cm should be written as 400 cm and 250 cm, or 4 m and 2 m 50 cm, before adding.

The questions for this section are with you now: what perimeter is, measuring and adding every side, and using one unit of length.

## Subtopic: Perimeter of Rectangles and Squares

A rectangle has two pairs of equal sides: two lengths and two widths. The vegetable garden is a rectangle 6 m long and 4 m wide. Its perimeter is 6 plus 4 plus 6 plus 4, which is 20 m. A quicker way: one length and one width is 6 plus 4, which is 10 m, and doubling gives 20 m.

A square has four equal sides. A square sandpit with sides of 5 m has a perimeter of 5 plus 5 plus 5 plus 5, which is 20 m, or 4 times 5, which is 20 m. Notice that the square sandpit and the rectangular garden have the same perimeter, even though they are different shapes.

On grid paper, count the unit lengths along the edges. A rectangle 5 squares long and 3 squares wide has a perimeter of 5 plus 3 plus 5 plus 3, which is 16 units. Count the edges of the squares around the outside, not the squares themselves.

If you know the perimeter and some sides, you can find a missing side. A rectangle has a perimeter of 30 m and a length of 10 m. The two lengths make 20 m, so the two widths make 10 m, and each width is 5 m.

The questions for this section are with you now: perimeters of rectangles and squares, counting edges on a grid, and finding a missing side.

## Subtopic: Other Shapes and Perimeter Problems

Any polygon's perimeter is the sum of its sides. A triangle with sides 3 cm, 4 cm and 5 cm has a perimeter of 12 cm. A regular hexagon with sides of 6 cm has a perimeter of 6 times 6, which is 36 cm. An L-shaped flower bed has six sides; measure all six and add them.

Fencing problems use perimeter. The vegetable garden, 6 m by 4 m, needs a fence all round. The fence is 20 m long. If fencing costs R25 per metre, the fence costs 20 times 25, which is R500. If the garden has a gate 1 m wide, the fencing needed is 20 minus 1, which is 19 m, costing 19 times 25, which is R475.

Framing problems use perimeter too. A photo is 15 cm by 10 cm. Ribbon to go round its edge must be 15 plus 10 plus 15 plus 10, which is 50 cm. If the ribbon needs 2 cm extra to overlap at the end, buy 52 cm.

Read each problem to see whether it asks for the whole perimeter or part of it. A wall along one side of a garden does not need fencing. Draw a sketch, label every side, and mark which sides need fencing.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: adding only two sides of a rectangle, 6 plus 4 equals 10 m; the perimeter has four sides, so it is 20 m. Two: counting the squares inside a grid shape instead of the edges round the outside; perimeter counts edges. Three: adding sides in different units, like 4 m plus 250 cm as 254; change to the same unit first. Four: fencing the gate as well when it does not need fencing; read the problem and sketch which sides need it.

The questions for this section are with you now: perimeters of triangles, hexagons and L shapes, fencing with costs and gates, and framing problems.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the vegetable garden, the photo frame and the grid — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Walk Around the Edge

Perimeter is the distance all the way round the outside. Walk round the garden from the gate back to the gate.

Measure every side and add them up. A book 30 cm by 20 cm: 100 cm round.

All sides in the same unit before you add.

Keep the garden walk in mind and try the questions for this part: what perimeter is.

## Subtopic: Add the Sides

Rectangle: length plus width, then double. 6 m and 4 m: 10 m, doubled is 20 m.

Square: 4 times the side. Side 5 m: 20 m.

On a grid, count the little edges round the outside, not the squares inside.

Hold the garden and the sandpit in mind and try this part's questions: rectangles and squares.

## Subtopic: Fences and Frames

Any shape: add all its sides. Triangle 3, 4 and 5 cm: 12 cm.

Fence round a 6 m by 4 m garden: 20 m. At R25 a metre: R500. Leave out the gate if it does not need fencing.

Ribbon round a 15 cm by 10 cm photo: 50 cm.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Perimeter is the distance all the way around the outside of a shape, found by measuring every side and adding, with all sides in the same unit of length. A rectangle's perimeter is length plus width, doubled; a square's is 4 times the side. On a grid, count the unit edges around the outside. For other polygons add every side. In problems, sketch the shape, label the sides, and check which sides are needed, as with a gate or a wall, before working out lengths and costs.

Hold the fence and the photo frame in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: perimeter problems.
