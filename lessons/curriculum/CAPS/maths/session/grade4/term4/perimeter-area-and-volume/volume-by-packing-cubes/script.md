# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers finding the volume or capacity of objects by packing or filling them, to develop an understanding of cubic units. The anchor is a classroom in Mahikeng with building blocks, a lunch box, a shoe box and a set of containers filled with cups of water and beans. The governing idea is that volume is the amount of space inside a 3D object, measured by packing it with equal cubes with no gaps and counting them, so that the answer is in cubic units, and that packing in layers lets you count by multiplying. Counting cubes in layers, including hidden cubes, comparing volumes, and comparing how much containers hold by filling them are examinable.

## Subtopic: What Volume Is

Volume is the amount of space an object takes up, or the amount of space inside a container. A shoe box has more space inside than a lunch box, so it has a bigger volume. Length measures a line, area measures a flat surface, and volume measures the space of a 3D object.

To measure volume, pack the space with equal cubes, with no gaps, and count them. Cubes are used because they stack and pack together perfectly, with no spaces in between, just as squares cover a flat surface with no gaps. If 24 building blocks, all the same size, fill a box exactly, the volume of the box is 24 blocks.

The answer is in cubic units. If each cube is a 1 centimetre cube, a box holding 24 of them has a volume of 24 cubic centimetres. A cubic centimetre is about the size of a dice. Cubic units tell you the size of the cubes you counted.

How much a container can hold is called its capacity. Capacity and volume are closely linked: the volume of the space inside a container is its capacity. You can measure it by packing it with cubes or by filling it with water, beans or sand, and counting how many cups it takes.

The questions for this section are with you now: what volume is, packing with equal cubes and no gaps, cubic units, and capacity.

## Subtopic: Packing Boxes in Layers

Pack a box in layers. A box is 4 cubes long, 3 cubes wide and 2 cubes high. The bottom layer has 3 rows of 4 cubes, which is 12 cubes. There are 2 layers, so the box holds 12 plus 12, which is 24 cubes. Its volume is 24 cubic units.

Counting by layers is much quicker than counting every cube, and it stops you missing hidden cubes. When a stack of cubes is drawn on paper, you can see only some of them. The cubes in the middle and at the back are hidden, but they are still there. Count how many are in one layer, then multiply by the number of layers.

Try a bigger box. A shoe box holds 5 rows of 6 cubes in its bottom layer, which is 30 cubes, and it is 3 layers high. Its volume is 30 plus 30 plus 30, which is 90 cubes. Each layer is the same, so 3 times 30 also works.

If cubes do not fill the box exactly, count the cubes that fit and say the volume is a little more. A lunch box that holds 20 cubes with a small gap at the top has a volume of a bit more than 20 cubes.

The questions for this section are with you now: packing in layers, counting hidden cubes, and boxes that are not filled exactly.

## Subtopic: Comparing Volumes and Filling Containers

To compare volumes, count the cubes. A box of 24 cubes has a bigger volume than a box of 20 cubes. Different shapes can have the same volume: 24 cubes can be packed as 4 by 3 by 2, or 6 by 2 by 2, or 2 by 3 by 4. All three have a volume of 24 cubic units.

Containers that are not box-shaped, like jugs, bottles and bowls, are easier to compare by filling. Fill each one with cups of water or beans and count the cups. A jug that takes 8 cups holds more than a bowl that takes 5 cups. The cups must all be the same size and filled to the same level each time.

The cubes or cups must be the same size for a fair comparison. If one box is packed with small cubes and another with big blocks, the counts cannot be compared. In the same way, comparing 8 small cups with 5 big mugs does not tell you which container holds more.

Volume, area and length grow differently. A box twice as long holds twice as many cubes. A box twice as long and twice as wide holds four times as many. You will explore this more in later grades; for now, count carefully in layers.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: counting only the cubes you can see in a drawing; the hidden cubes inside and at the back still count. Two: counting one layer and forgetting the other layers; multiply the cubes in one layer by the number of layers. Three: comparing boxes packed with cubes of different sizes; use cubes of the same size. Four: giving volume in square units; volume is measured in cubic units.

The questions for this section are with you now: comparing volumes by counting cubes, different shapes with the same volume, comparing containers by filling, and using equal-sized units.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the building blocks, the boxes and the cups of beans — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Space Inside

Volume is how much space something takes up or holds.

Pack it with equal cubes, no gaps. Count them. The answer is in cubic units.

Capacity is how much a container can hold.

Keep the building blocks in mind and try the questions for this part: what volume is.

## Subtopic: Layer by Layer

Count one layer, then count the layers. 3 rows of 4 is 12 in a layer. 2 layers: 24.

Hidden cubes still count. Do not count only the ones you can see.

Shoe box: 30 in a layer, 3 layers, 90 cubes.

Hold the shoe box in mind and try this part's questions: packing in layers.

## Subtopic: Compare and Fill

More cubes, more volume. 24 cubes beats 20.

Jugs and bowls: fill with same-size cups and count. 8 cups beats 5 cups.

Use same-size cubes and cups, or the comparison is not fair.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Volume is the amount of space an object takes up or holds, and capacity is how much a container can hold. Measure volume by packing equal cubes with no gaps and counting them, in cubic units. Count in layers: cubes in one layer times the number of layers, remembering hidden cubes. Different shapes can have the same volume. Compare containers by filling them with same-size cups, and always use units of the same size.

Hold the cups of beans in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: comparing volumes.
