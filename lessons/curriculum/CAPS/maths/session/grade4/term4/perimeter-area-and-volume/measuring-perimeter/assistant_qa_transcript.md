### subtopic_1 — What Perimeter Is
**Assistant:** Why can't I just add the length and the width to get the perimeter of a rectangle?

**Tutor:** Because a rectangle has four sides, not two. Length plus width gives you only half of the way round: from one corner, along the top and down one side. To get back to where you started, you still have to go along the bottom, which is another length, and up the other side, which is another width. So the full perimeter is length plus width plus length plus width. That is why the quick method says add the length and width, then double. For a garden 6 m by 4 m, 6 plus 4 is 10, which is only half way round, and doubling gives the full 20 m. A good way to avoid the mistake is to draw the rectangle and write the length of every side next to it, all four of them, before you add. Then count your numbers: if you are adding only two, something is missing. Another check is to imagine walking round the shape. Each side is one stretch of the walk, and you have to walk every stretch to get back to the gate. With practice you will remember that a rectangle has two lengths and two widths, and the doubling will feel natural.

### subtopic_2 — Perimeter of Rectangles and Squares
**Assistant:** On a grid, why do I count the edges and not the squares?

**Tutor:** Because perimeter is a length, the distance round the outside, and the edges of the squares are little lengths. The squares themselves are not lengths; they are pieces of surface, which is what area measures. When you count edges round a shape on grid paper, each little edge is 1 unit of length, like 1 centimetre, so counting them gives the distance round the outside. If a rectangle is 5 squares long and 3 squares wide, there are 5 edges along the top, 3 down the right, 5 along the bottom and 3 up the left, which is 16 units of perimeter. If you counted the squares inside, you would get 5 times 3, which is 15, and that is the area, not the perimeter. The two numbers are different, and they measure different things. A helpful picture: the perimeter is the fence, and the area is the grass inside the fence. To make counting edges easier, run your pencil along the outside of the shape and make a tiny tick on each edge as you count it, so you do not count any edge twice or skip a corner. At the corner squares, remember that two of their edges are on the outside.

### subtopic_3 — Other Shapes and Perimeter Problems
**Assistant:** How do the cost problems work? They seem to mix perimeter with money.

**Tutor:** They do mix two ideas, and the trick is to do them one at a time. First, work out the length you need, which is usually the perimeter or part of it. Then use the price per metre, which is a rate, to find the cost. For a garden 6 m by 4 m, the perimeter is 20 m. If fencing costs R25 per metre, every metre of fence costs R25, so 20 metres cost 20 times 25, which is R500. You learned in Term 1 that per means for each one, so you multiply. Now add a twist: the garden has a 1 m gate that needs no fencing. Draw the garden and mark the gate. The fencing needed is 20 minus 1, which is 19 metres, so the cost is 19 times 25, which is R475. A quick check is to compare: without the gate it was R500, and leaving out 1 metre saves R25, so R475 makes sense. Some problems might have a wall along one side, or need two rows of wire, or charge for posts as well. Read carefully, sketch the shape, mark what is needed, and then calculate. Length first, cost second.
