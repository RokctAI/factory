### subtopic_1 — What Area Is
**Assistant:** What is a square unit? Isn't a unit just a centimetre?

**Tutor:** A centimetre is a unit of length: a short line, about the width of your little finger. A square centimetre is a unit of area: a small square whose sides are each 1 centimetre long. It is a little patch of surface, not a line. Area needs a unit that covers surface, because area measures how much surface there is, and a line cannot cover anything. That is why we count squares. Each square on a centimetre grid is 1 square centimetre, and if a shape covers 15 of them, its area is 15 square centimetres. For a room, we use bigger squares: a square metre is a square with sides of 1 metre, about the size of a large poster. A bedroom might be 16 square metres. Writing square in the unit is important because it tells everyone you measured area. If you write that a garden is 20 metres, people will think you mean its perimeter or its length. If you write 20 square metres, they know you mean the surface inside. In later grades you will see square units written with a small 2, like cm with a 2 at the top, but in Grade 4 writing the words square centimetres is perfect.

### subtopic_2 — Counting Squares in Rectangles
**Assistant:** Why do I count rows instead of counting every square?

**Tutor:** Counting every square works, but it is slow and easy to lose your place, especially when the shape is big. Counting rows uses the fact that a rectangle is made of equal rows. A rectangle 6 squares long and 4 squares wide has 4 rows, and each row has 6 squares. So instead of counting 24 squares one by one, you count 6, 12, 18, 24, or simply work out 4 times 6. This is the same idea as the arrays you used in multiplication: rows and columns of objects, counted by multiplying. It is a shortcut, but only for shapes where every row has the same number of squares. For an L shape, the rows are not all the same, so split it into two rectangles first, count each one by rows, and add. For irregular shapes, like a leaf, the rows are ragged, so you go back to counting full and half squares. In later grades, this row idea will become the rule that the area of a rectangle is length times width. For now, counting rows of squares is the bridge between counting and that rule, and it lets you see why the rule works.

### subtopic_3 — Irregular Shapes and Area versus Perimeter
**Assistant:** If two shapes have the same area, why don't they have the same perimeter?

**Tutor:** Because area and perimeter measure different things, and changing a shape can change one without changing the other. Take 12 square tiles. You can arrange them in a rectangle 6 long and 2 wide, or 4 long and 3 wide, or 12 long and 1 wide. Every arrangement uses all 12 tiles, so the area is always 12 square units. But walk round the outside of each one. The 6 by 2 has a perimeter of 6 plus 2 plus 6 plus 2, which is 16. The 4 by 3 has 4 plus 3 plus 4 plus 3, which is 14. The long thin 12 by 1 has 12 plus 1 plus 12 plus 1, which is 26. Same area, very different perimeters. Long, thin shapes have more edge for their area, and square-like shapes have less. This matters in real life. A farmer with a fixed number of metres of fence can enclose more grass by making the field closer to a square. A builder with a fixed floor area might choose a shape that needs fewer metres of wall. So always measure area and perimeter separately, and never assume one tells you the other.
