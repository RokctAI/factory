### subtopic_1 — Changing Units of Length
**Assistant:** How do I remember whether to multiply or divide when I change units?

**Tutor:** Ask yourself whether the new unit is smaller or bigger, and think about how many of them you will need. Smaller units fit into the length more times, so you need more of them, and the number gets bigger: multiply. Bigger units fit fewer times, so the number gets smaller: divide or group. Picture a 4 metre plank. If you measure it in centimetres, which are tiny, you will count a lot of them: 400. If you measure a 400 centimetre plank in metres, which are big, you will count only a few: 4. It is like changing money: one R100 note is the same as a hundred R1 coins. Going from rands to cents, which are smaller, the number grows; going from cents to rands, it shrinks. Then you only need to know the linking numbers: 10 millimetres in a centimetre, 100 centimetres in a metre, 1 000 metres in a kilometre. A quick check helps too: after converting, ask whether the answer makes sense. If you turn 4 metres into 4 centimetres, your plank has become shorter than your finger, which is clearly wrong. Sense-checking catches most conversion mistakes.

### subtopic_2 — Fractions of Units
**Assistant:** Why is a quarter of a metre 25 centimetres and not 4?

**Tutor:** Because a quarter of something means the whole thing split into 4 equal parts, and one of those parts. The whole metre is 100 centimetres. Split 100 centimetres into 4 equal parts and each part is 100 divided by 4, which is 25 centimetres. So a quarter of a metre is 25 centimetres. The 4 in a quarter tells you how many parts to split into, not how long each part is. You can check with a metre stick: put marks at 25, 50 and 75 centimetres and you have 4 equal parts, each 25 centimetres long. Half a metre is the mark at 50, and three quarters is the mark at 75. The same idea works for kilometres, but with 1 000 metres: a quarter of a kilometre is 1 000 divided by 4, which is 250 metres. And for centimetres, with 10 millimetres: half a centimetre is 5 millimetres. Fractions of a unit are just fractions of a group, which you practised with sweets and cupcakes: divide by the bottom number, multiply by the top. Three quarters of a metre is 100 divided by 4, times 3, which is 75 centimetres.

### subtopic_3 — Length Problems
**Assistant:** Why do we change to the smaller unit before solving a problem?

**Tutor:** Because it turns every calculation into ordinary whole-number maths, which you already do well. Suppose a plank is 2 metres long and the builder cuts off 45 centimetres. If you try to work in metres, you would need to take 45 centimetres from 2 metres, and there is no easy way to write that without changing something. In centimetres, it is simply 200 minus 45, which is 155 centimetres. Then, if the question wants metres, you change back: 155 centimetres is 1 metre 55 centimetres. The same goes for adding. 1 km 200 m plus 1 km 200 m could be done by adding kilometres and metres separately, and here that works, but if the metres add up to more than 1 000 you would have to carry a kilometre. In metres, 1 200 plus 1 200 is 2 400, and changing back gives 2 km 400 m with no fuss. Working in the smaller unit avoids fractions and carrying across units. It is the same trick you used for mass, with grams and kilograms, and you will use it again for capacity with millilitres and litres. Smaller unit, calculate, change back, write the unit.
