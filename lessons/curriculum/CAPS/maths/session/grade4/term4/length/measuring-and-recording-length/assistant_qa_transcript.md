### subtopic_1 — Units of Length
**Assistant:** Why can't I start measuring from the end of the ruler?

**Tutor:** Because on most rulers the 0 mark is not right at the end. There is usually a small gap, a few millimetres, between the edge of the ruler and the 0 line, to protect the markings from wearing off. If you line your pencil up with the edge, your pencil starts before 0, and every measurement will come out too short by the size of that gap. On an old, chipped ruler, the end might even be broken off, making it worse. Starting at the 0 mark makes sure you are measuring from exactly the start of the scale. Some people like to start at the 1 cm mark when the start of a ruler is damaged, and that is fine as long as you remember to subtract 1 cm at the end. If the pencil reaches the 8 cm mark when you started at 1, the pencil is 8 minus 1, which is 7 cm long. The same idea applies to every measuring tool: a tape measure, a trundle wheel or a measuring jug. Always know where the zero is, line up with it, and read carefully at the other end. Measuring is only accurate if you start in the right place.

### subtopic_2 — Measuring Tools
**Assistant:** How does a trundle wheel know when it has rolled a metre?

**Tutor:** The wheel is made exactly the right size so that the distance around its edge is 1 metre. The distance around a circle is called its circumference, and you will learn more about it in later grades. When the wheel rolls along the ground through one complete turn, the whole of its edge touches the ground once, so it moves forward by exactly the distance around its edge: 1 metre. A little clicker on the wheel makes a sound once every turn, so each click means 1 more metre. To measure the school field, put the wheel at one end, set it so the clicker is at the start, walk in a straight line to the other end and count the clicks. 85 clicks means the field is about 85 metres long. Walking in a straight line matters: if you wander from side to side, the wheel rolls further than the straight distance and gives too big an answer. Trundle wheels are useful because rulers and even tape measures are too short or awkward for long distances outdoors. Road builders and sports groundskeepers use bigger versions of the same idea to measure roads and running tracks.

### subtopic_3 — Recording, Comparing and Ordering Lengths
**Assistant:** Why do I have to change lengths to the same unit before comparing them?

**Tutor:** Because a number on its own does not tell you how long something is; the unit does half the work. 128 cm and 1 m 32 cm look like they have very different numbers, but a metre is a hundred times longer than a centimetre, so 1 m is already 100 cm. Comparing 128 with 1 and 32 would be like comparing R128 with R1 and 32c and thinking R128 must be more because it has a bigger number, when you have not checked whether the numbers mean rands or cents. Changing to the same unit puts both lengths on the same scale. 1 m 32 cm becomes 132 cm, and now it is easy to see that 132 cm is more than 128 cm. It is usually easiest to change into the smaller unit, here centimetres, because then you get whole numbers. The same rule helps with ordering: change everything to centimetres, write the list, then order the numbers. Finally, write your answer in a sensible unit: saying Thabo is taller by 4 cm is clearer than by 0 m 4 cm. Same unit first, then compare, then answer in a unit that makes sense.
