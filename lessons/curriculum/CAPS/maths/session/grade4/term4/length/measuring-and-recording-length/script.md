# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers estimating and practically measuring 2D shapes and 3D objects using rulers, metre sticks, tape measures and trundle wheels, and recording, comparing and ordering lengths in millimetres, centimetres, metres and kilometres. The anchor is a Grade 4 class in Kimberley measuring a pencil, their desks, the classroom, each other's heights and the school field. The governing idea is that length is measured against standard units chosen to suit the size of the thing, that every measuring tool must be read from its zero mark, and that lengths must be in the same unit before they are compared. Choosing units, reading rulers in centimetres and millimetres, using the right tool, and ordering lengths are examinable.

## Subtopic: Units of Length

Length tells us how long, wide, tall or far something is. We measure it in millimetres, centimetres, metres and kilometres, written mm, cm, m and km. A millimetre is tiny, about the thickness of a bank card. A centimetre is about the width of your little finger. A metre is about the length of a big step for an adult, or the height of a kitchen counter. A kilometre is 1 000 metres, about the distance you walk in 12 to 15 minutes.

Choose the unit to suit the size. The thickness of a coin is measured in millimetres. The length of a pencil or a book is measured in centimetres. The length of a classroom or the height of a door is measured in metres. The distance from Kimberley to Bloemfontein, about 175 km, is measured in kilometres.

Estimate before you measure. Use benchmarks: your finger is about 1 cm wide, your hand span is about 15 cm, and a door is about 2 m high. If a desk is about 4 hand spans long, it is about 60 cm. Estimating first helps you notice when a measurement has gone wrong.

The units are linked. 10 millimetres make 1 centimetre. 100 centimetres make 1 metre. 1 000 metres make 1 kilometre. You will practise changing between them in the next lesson.

The questions for this section are with you now: the four units of length, choosing a sensible unit, and estimating with benchmarks.

## Subtopic: Measuring Tools

A ruler measures short lengths in centimetres and millimetres. Line up the 0 mark, not the end of the ruler, with one end of the object. Read the mark at the other end. The numbered marks are centimetres, and the small marks between them are millimetres. A pencil that ends 4 small marks past the 7 is 7 cm 4 mm long.

A metre stick is 1 metre long and is good for measuring desks, doors and the height of a learner. A tape measure rolls up and can bend round curved objects, so it is used for measuring round a tree trunk or a person's waist, and for longer lengths like the width of the classroom.

A trundle wheel measures long distances, like the length of the school field. It is a wheel on a handle that clicks once every time it rolls 1 metre. Walk it in a straight line and count the clicks. 85 clicks means 85 m.

Choose the tool to suit the job. Ruler for small things, metre stick or tape measure for things up to a few metres, tape measure for curved things, and trundle wheel for long distances outside. For distances between towns, use a map or a car's distance meter, called an odometer.

The questions for this section are with you now: starting at the 0 mark, reading centimetres and millimetres, and choosing a ruler, metre stick, tape measure or trundle wheel.

## Subtopic: Recording, Comparing and Ordering Lengths

Record every length with its unit. 120 on its own could be 120 mm, 120 cm or 120 m, three very different lengths. Thabo is 1 m 32 cm tall, or 132 cm. Write the unit every time.

To compare lengths, change them to the same unit first. Who is taller, Thabo at 1 m 32 cm or Lindiwe at 128 cm? Change Thabo's height to centimetres: 100 cm plus 32 cm is 132 cm. 132 is more than 128, so Thabo is taller, by 4 cm.

To order lengths, change all of them to the same unit, then order. Order these from shortest: 2 m, 150 cm, 1 m 75 cm, 95 cm. In centimetres: 200, 150, 175, 95. From shortest: 95 cm, 150 cm, 1 m 75 cm, 2 m.

A table helps you record a class's measurements: the object, the estimate, the measurement and the difference. If your estimates get closer to the measurements as you go, your sense of length is growing.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: lining up the object with the end of the ruler instead of the 0 mark; start at 0. Two: measuring the distance between towns in metres or a pencil in kilometres; choose the unit to suit the size. Three: writing a length without its unit; 120 could be 120 mm, 120 cm or 120 m. Four: deciding 128 cm is longer than 1 m 32 cm because 128 is more than 1 or 32; change to the same unit first.

The questions for this section are with you now: recording lengths with units, changing to the same unit to compare, and ordering lengths.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the pencil, the classroom and the school field — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Small to Long

Tiny things in millimetres. Pencils and books in centimetres. Rooms and doors in metres. Towns far apart in kilometres.

Your finger is about 1 cm wide. A door is about 2 m high. Use these to estimate.

10 mm make 1 cm. 100 cm make 1 m. 1 000 m make 1 km.

Keep the classroom in mind and try the questions for this part: units of length.

## Subtopic: Rulers, Tapes and Wheels

On a ruler, start at the 0 mark. Big numbers are centimetres, small marks are millimetres.

Metre stick for desks and doors. Tape measure for curves and longer lengths.

Trundle wheel for the field: one click, one metre.

Hold the ruler and the trundle wheel in mind and try this part's questions: measuring tools.

## Subtopic: Line Them Up

Always write the unit: 132 cm, not just 132.

Same unit before you compare. 1 m 32 cm is 132 cm, more than 128 cm.

Order: change all to centimetres, then shortest to longest.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Length is measured in millimetres, centimetres, metres and kilometres: 10 mm make 1 cm, 100 cm make 1 m and 1 000 m make 1 km. Choose the unit to suit the size, and estimate first with benchmarks like your finger and a door. Measure with a ruler from the 0 mark, a metre stick, a tape measure for curves and long lengths, or a trundle wheel for long distances. Record every length with its unit, and change lengths to the same unit before comparing or ordering.
