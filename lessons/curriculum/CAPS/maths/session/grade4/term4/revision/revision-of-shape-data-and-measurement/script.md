# Part 1 — Expert

This session revises the space and shape, data handling and measurement work of Terms 1 to 4: naming and describing 2D shapes and 3D objects, lines of symmetry, composite shapes and tessellations; the data cycle, tallies, tables, pictographs, bar graphs and pie charts, summaries and simple probability with coins and dice; and measurement of time, length, mass and capacity, with conversions using 1 000 and 100, perimeter, area by counting squares and volume by packing cubes. The anchor is a class trip to a park near Knysna, with a bus timetable, a picnic area, a survey of favourite activities, a vegetable garden to fence and a box of building blocks. The governing idea is that shapes are described by their properties, data is checked by its totals, and measurements always need the right unit. All these topics are examinable in the end-of-year test.

## Subtopic: Shapes, Objects, Symmetry and Tessellations

2D shapes are flat. A polygon has straight sides and is named by the number of sides: a triangle has 3, a quadrilateral 4, a pentagon 5, a hexagon 6, a heptagon 7 and an octagon 8. The stop sign at the park gate is an octagon. A circle is a 2D shape with one curved side, so it is not a polygon.

3D objects are solid. The picnic cooler box is a rectangular prism with 6 rectangular faces. A dice is a cube with 6 square faces. A tin of beans is a cylinder with 2 flat circular faces and a curved surface. A soccer ball is a sphere, with one curved surface. A tent can be shaped like a pyramid.

Symmetry: a line of symmetry folds a shape onto itself exactly. A rectangle has 2 lines of symmetry, across the middle both ways. A square has 4, including the two diagonals. An equilateral triangle has 3. The diagonals of a rectangle are not lines of symmetry, because folding along them does not match the halves.

Tessellations: the picnic area is paved with tiles that fit together with no gaps and no overlaps. Squares, rectangles, equilateral triangles and regular hexagons tessellate on their own. Circles do not, because they leave gaps between them.

The questions for this section are with you now: naming polygons, describing 3D objects by their faces and surfaces, lines of symmetry, and which shapes tessellate.

## Subtopic: Data Handling and Probability

The data cycle: ask a question, collect the data, organise it, show it, analyse it and report. The class asked 40 learners for their favourite park activity and kept a tally: swings 12, slides 8, picnic 5 and soccer 15. Tallies are bundled in fives, so 3 bundles and 2 lines is 17.

Check the total: 12 plus 8 plus 5 plus 15 is 40, which matches the 40 learners asked. If the total does not match, someone was counted twice or missed.

Graphs: a bar graph has the activities along the bottom and a scale up the side that starts at 0 and goes up in equal steps. A pie chart shows fractions of the whole: half of 40 is 20 learners, and a quarter of 40 is 10. Analysing: soccer was most popular with 15, picnic least with 5, and 15 minus 5 is 10 more for soccer.

Probability: a coin has 2 possible outcomes, heads and tails, which are equally likely. A dice has 6 possible outcomes, 1 to 6. If you toss a coin 20 times, you expect about 10 heads, but not always exactly 10. The coin has no memory.

The questions for this section are with you now: tallies and totals, bar graphs and pie charts, analysing data, and outcomes of coins and dice.

## Subtopic: Measurement: Time, Units, Perimeter, Area and Volume

Time: the bus left school at 08:15 and arrived at the park at 10:40. From 08:15 to 10:15 is 2 hours, and from 10:15 to 10:40 is 25 minutes, so the trip took 2 hours 25 minutes. The bus home leaves at 14:30, which is 2:30 p.m., because 14 minus 12 is 2.

Units: for length, 1 cm is 10 mm, 1 m is 100 cm and 1 km is 1 000 m. For mass, 1 kg is 1 000 g. For capacity, 1 l is 1 000 ml. So the 3 km walking trail is 3 000 m, the 2 kg bag of apples is 2 000 g and the 2 l bottle of water is 2 000 ml.

Perimeter is the distance all the way around the edge of a shape. The vegetable garden is a rectangle 8 m long and 5 m wide. Its perimeter is 8 plus 5 plus 8 plus 5, which is 26 m, so the class needs 26 m of fencing.

Area is the amount of flat surface inside a shape, found by counting squares. A rectangle on a grid 4 squares long and 3 squares wide covers 12 squares. Volume is the space inside a 3D object, found by packing cubes. A box with 6 cubes in each layer and 2 layers holds 12 cubes.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: saying a rectangle has 4 lines of symmetry; folding along a diagonal does not match the halves, so it has only 2. Two: starting a bar graph scale at the smallest value; start the scale at 0 and use equal steps. Three: finding the perimeter of an 8 m by 5 m rectangle as 13 m; add all four sides, 8 plus 5 plus 8 plus 5, which is 26 m. Four: mixing up area and perimeter; perimeter is the distance around the edge, and area is the number of squares inside.

The questions for this section are with you now: time intervals and the 24-hour clock, converting units of length, mass and capacity, perimeter, area and volume.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again, walking through the park trip from the bus to the garden — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Shapes and Objects

Name polygons by their sides: hexagon 6, octagon 8.

A cube has 6 square faces. A tin is a cylinder. A ball is a sphere.

A square has 4 lines of symmetry, a rectangle 2. Tiles fit with no gaps and no overlaps.

Keep the park gate and the picnic tiles in mind and try the questions for this part: shapes and objects.

## Subtopic: Count, Graph and Guess

Tally in fives. Add up the totals: they must match the number of people asked.

Bar graphs start at 0 and go up in equal steps.

A coin has 2 outcomes, a dice has 6. The coin has no memory.

Hold the park survey in mind and try this part's questions: data and chance.

## Subtopic: Measure It

Time: count on in hours, then minutes. 08:15 to 10:40 is 2 hours 25 minutes.

A thousand small units make one big unit: 1 km is 1 000 m, 1 kg is 1 000 g, 1 l is 1 000 ml.

Perimeter goes around the edge. Area counts the squares inside. Volume counts the cubes.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Polygons are named by their number of sides, and 3D objects are described by their faces and surfaces; a line of symmetry folds a shape onto itself, and shapes tessellate when they fit with no gaps and no overlaps. Data is collected with tallies, checked by its total and shown on bar graphs that start at 0 or on pie charts as fractions of the whole; a coin has 2 outcomes and a dice 6. For time, count on in hours and then minutes. For length, mass and capacity, 1 000 small units make 1 big unit, except 100 cm in a metre and 10 mm in a centimetre. Perimeter goes around the edge, area counts squares and volume counts cubes.

Hold the vegetable garden in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: measurement.
