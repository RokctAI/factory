### subtopic_1 — Whole Numbers: Place Value, Ordering and Rounding
**Assistant:** There is so much number work from the whole year. Where do I start revising?

**Tutor:** Start with place value, because everything else is built on it. If you know that the 7 in 4 736 is worth 700, then ordering, rounding, adding with carrying and multiplying by breaking numbers into tens and units all make sense. Spend your first revision session saying the value of digits, writing numbers in expanded form, ordering a few numbers and rounding them to 10, 100 and 1 000. Next, make sure your times tables are quick, because multiplying and dividing both depend on them. Ten minutes a day on the tables you find hardest, like 7 times 8 and 6 times 9, makes a big difference. Then practise one calculation of each kind: an addition with carrying, a subtraction with borrowing, a two-digit multiplication and a division with a remainder. For each one, estimate first and check afterwards. Finally, do a few patterns and number sentences, because they use all the calculation skills together. If you only have a little time, do one example from each family rather than many of one kind. The test will mix them up, so your revision should too. And always do the checks, because they turn a likely right answer into a certain one.

### subtopic_2 — The Four Operations and Their Checks
**Assistant:** Why does the remainder sometimes mean one more, and sometimes get left out?

**Tutor:** Because the story decides what the leftover means, not the sum. The sum 50 divided by 8 always gives 6 remainder 2. But think about what the 2 stands for each time. If 50 learners sit 8 to a table, the 2 are learners who still need a seat. You cannot leave them standing, so you need one more table: 7 tables. If you are packing 50 eggs into boxes of 8 and only full boxes can be sold, the 2 extra eggs do not make a full box, so you can sell 6 boxes and the 2 eggs are left over. If 50 sweets are shared among 8 friends, each friend gets 6 and there are 2 sweets left, which you might keep or cut into pieces. The same calculation gives three different answers: 7, 6 and 6 with 2 to spare. So after every division with a remainder, stop and ask: what are the leftovers, and what happens to them in this story? Do they need another table, bus or box? Are they thrown away? Are they shared out? Writing your final answer as a sentence, like 7 tables are needed, helps you check that it makes sense.

### subtopic_3 — Number Sentences and Patterns
**Assistant:** How do I find the rule of a pattern quickly?

**Tutor:** Look at how the numbers change from one to the next, and write the change in the gaps. In 5, 9, 13, 17, the gaps are 4, 4 and 4. The change is always the same, so the rule is add 4, and the next number is 21. In 50, 45, 40, 35, the gaps are all minus 5, so the next number is 30. If the gaps are not the same, try multiplying. In 3, 6, 12, 24, the gaps are 3, 6 and 12, which keep growing, but each number is double the one before. So the rule is multiply by 2, and the next number is 48. For a picture pattern, like squares made of matchsticks, count the matchsticks in each picture and write them in a table: 1 square uses 4, 2 squares use 7, 3 squares use 10. The gaps are 3 each time, because each new square shares one side with the last one and needs only 3 new sticks. So the rule is number of squares times 3, plus 1 for the very first stick. Always test your rule on a picture you already know: 3 times 3 is 9, plus 1 is 10. It works, so you can trust it for picture 5.
