# Part 1 — Expert

This session revises the number, operations and patterns work of Terms 1 to 4: whole numbers to 10 000, including counting, ordering, place value and rounding to 10, 100 and 1 000; addition and subtraction of four-digit numbers; multiplication of two-digit numbers; division of three-digit by one-digit numbers with remainders; multiples and factors; number sentences and the properties of operations; and numeric and geometric patterns with flow diagrams. The anchor is an end-of-year fun day at a school in George, with ticket sales, a cake stall, tables for learners and a matchstick pattern competition. The governing idea is that estimating before calculating and using inverse operations after calculating catch almost every mistake, and that a pattern is described by its rule. All these topics are examinable in the end-of-year test.

## Subtopic: Whole Numbers: Place Value, Ordering and Rounding

Place value: the fun day raised R4 736. In expanded form that is 4 000 plus 700 plus 30 plus 6. The 4 is worth 4 thousands, the 7 is worth 7 hundreds, which is 700, the 3 is worth 3 tens, which is 30, and the 6 is worth 6 units.

Ordering: three classes raised R3 095, R3 509 and R3 950. All have 3 thousands, so compare the hundreds: 0, 5 and 9. From smallest to biggest: R3 095, R3 509, R3 950. Always compare from the biggest place value first.

Rounding: look at the digit one place to the right. R4 736 to the nearest 10: the units digit is 6, which is 5 or more, so round up to R4 740. To the nearest 100: the tens digit is 3, less than 5, so round down to R4 700. To the nearest 1 000: the hundreds digit is 7, so round up to R5 000.

Counting: tickets were numbered in 25s from 1 250: 1 250, 1 275, 1 300, 1 325. Counting backwards in 50s from 2 000: 1 950, 1 900, 1 850. Counting can start at any number, not only at zero.

The questions for this section are with you now: the value of each digit, ordering four-digit numbers, rounding to 10, 100 and 1 000, and counting forwards and backwards.

## Subtopic: The Four Operations and Their Checks

Adding and subtracting: ticket sales were R2 468 in the morning and R1 375 in the afternoon. Estimate first: about 2 500 plus 1 400, which is 3 900. In columns, carrying where needed, the total is R3 843, close to the estimate. Check with subtraction: 3 843 minus 1 375 is 2 468. Subtracting works the same way: the target was R5 000, and 5 000 minus 3 843 is R1 157 still to raise.

Multiplying: the hall has 24 rows of 13 chairs. Break 13 into 10 and 3: 24 times 10 is 240, and 24 times 3 is 72. 240 plus 72 is 312 chairs. Estimate: about 25 times 13, a bit over 300, so 312 is sensible.

Dividing: 156 cupcakes are packed in boxes of 6. 156 divided by 6 is 26 boxes. Check with multiplication: 26 times 6 is 156. Remainders need thinking: 50 learners sit 8 to a table. 50 divided by 8 is 6 remainder 2, so 6 tables seat 48, and the other 2 learners need one more table: 7 tables.

Multiples and factors: the multiples of 6 are 6, 12, 18, 24 and so on, the numbers you say counting in sixes. The factors of 24 are the numbers that divide into 24 exactly: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12 and 24. They come in pairs: 1 and 24, 2 and 12, 3 and 8, 4 and 6.

The questions for this section are with you now: adding and subtracting with an estimate and a check, multiplying two-digit numbers, dividing with remainders, and multiples and factors.

## Subtopic: Number Sentences and Patterns

Number sentences: the cake stall must make R1 000 and has made R375. The number sentence is a box plus 375 equals 1 000. Solve it by thinking 1 000 minus 375, which is 625, and check by putting it back: 625 plus 375 is 1 000. The missing amount is R625.

Properties of operations: you can swap numbers when adding or multiplying, so 7 times 8 is the same as 8 times 7, which is 56. You can split a number to multiply 6 times 23: split 23 into 20 and 3. 6 times 20 is 120, and 6 times 3 is 18. 120 plus 18 is 138.

Numeric patterns: 5, 9, 13, 17 adds 4 each time, so the next two numbers are 21 and 25. 3, 6, 12, 24 doubles each time, so the next number is 48. First ask: is the same number added each time, or is it multiplied by the same number?

Geometric patterns: in the matchstick competition, squares in a row use 4, 7, 10 and 13 matchsticks for 1, 2, 3 and 4 squares. Each new square adds 3 sticks. The rule is: number of squares times 3, plus 1. For 5 squares: 5 times 3 is 15, plus 1 is 16. A flow diagram shows the same rule: input, times 3, plus 1, output.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: rounding 4 736 to the nearest 100 as 4 800; look at the tens digit, 3, which is less than 5, so round down to 4 700. Two: adding 2 468 and 1 375 without carrying and getting 3 733; carry the extra tens and hundreds, so the answer is 3 843. Three: saying 6 tables for 50 learners at 8 a table; 6 tables seat only 48, so the last 2 learners need a seventh table. Four: continuing 3, 6, 12, 24 by adding 3 to get 27; the rule is doubling, so the next number is 48.

The questions for this section are with you now: solving number sentences with a box, the properties of operations, numeric patterns, and geometric patterns with flow diagrams.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again, walking through the fun day from the ticket table to the pattern competition — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Place Value and Rounding

R4 736 is 4 000 plus 700 plus 30 plus 6. The 7 is worth 700.

To order numbers, compare the biggest place first: 3 095, 3 509, 3 950.

To round, look one place to the right. 5 or more: round up. 4 736 to the nearest 100 is 4 700.

Keep the ticket money in mind and try the questions for this part: place value and rounding.

## Subtopic: Calculate and Check

Estimate first: round to 2 500 and 1 400, about 3 900. The exact answer, 3 843, is close.

Check adding with taking away, and dividing with multiplying. 26 times 6 is 156.

Think about the remainder. 50 learners, 8 a table: 6 tables and 2 left over, so 7 tables.

Hold the fun day hall in mind and try this part's questions: calculating and checking.

## Subtopic: Spot the Rule

Is the same number added each time? 5, 9, 13, 17: add 4.

Or multiplied by the same number? 3, 6, 12, 24: double, so 48 is next.

Matchstick squares: times 3, plus 1. A box sentence: undo it to find the missing number.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Every digit has a place value, so 4 736 is 4 000 plus 700 plus 30 plus 6; order numbers by comparing the biggest place first, and round by looking one place to the right. Estimate before adding, subtracting, multiplying and dividing, and check with the inverse operation; think about what a remainder means in the story. Multiples come from counting in a number, and factors divide into it exactly. Solve number sentences by undoing them, and describe patterns by their rule: add the same, multiply by the same, or times and plus for matchstick shapes.

Hold the matchstick competition in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: patterns and number sentences.
