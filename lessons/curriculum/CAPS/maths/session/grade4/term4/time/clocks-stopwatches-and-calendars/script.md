# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers using clocks, watches and stopwatches, and reading calendars. The anchor is an athletics day in Bloemfontein where races are timed with stopwatches, and a classroom wall calendar used to plan school events, public holidays and birthdays. The governing idea is that a stopwatch measures how long something takes in minutes, seconds and parts of a second, that 60 seconds make a minute, and that a calendar organises days into weeks and months, with months of 28, 29, 30 or 31 days and a year of 365 days, or 366 in a leap year. Reading stopwatches, comparing times, knowing the days in each month and reading a calendar to find days and dates are examinable.

## Subtopic: Seconds and Stopwatches

A second is a very short time, about as long as it takes to say one thousand and one. 60 seconds make 1 minute, and 60 minutes make 1 hour. Seconds are used to time short events: a sprint race, holding your breath, or how long a kettle takes to boil.

A stopwatch measures how long something takes. Press start when the event begins and stop when it ends. A digital stopwatch might show 00:15, which means 0 minutes and 15 seconds. Some show more digits, like 00:15.42, where the 42 means 42 hundredths of a second, which are tiny parts of a second used to separate very close finishes.

At the athletics day, Thabo runs the 100 m in 16 seconds, Lerato in 15 seconds and Sipho in 18 seconds. In a race, the shortest time wins, because the fastest runner takes the least time. So Lerato wins, Thabo is second and Sipho is third. Lerato was 1 second faster than Thabo and 3 seconds faster than Sipho.

Longer races need minutes and seconds. The 800 m winner finished in 2:45, which is 2 minutes and 45 seconds. In seconds alone that is 2 times 60, which is 120, plus 45, which is 165 seconds. A time of 1 minute 30 seconds is 90 seconds.

The questions for this section are with you now: seconds in a minute, reading a stopwatch, the shortest time winning a race, and changing minutes and seconds into seconds.

## Subtopic: Reading a Calendar

A calendar shows the days of a year arranged in months and weeks. Each month is a block, with the days of the week across the top, usually Sunday or Monday first, and the dates in rows. Each row is one week of 7 days. To find what day a date falls on, find the date and look up to the top of its column.

There are 12 months in a year. Not all months have the same number of days. January, March, May, July, August, October and December have 31 days. April, June, September and November have 30 days. February has 28 days, or 29 in a leap year. A rhyme helps: thirty days has September, April, June and November.

A year has 365 days, which is 52 weeks and 1 day. A leap year has 366 days, with the extra day on 29 February. Leap years come every 4 years: 2024 and 2028 are leap years, and so is any year that divides exactly by 4, with a few exceptions you will learn about later.

Calendars help plan. On the classroom calendar, important dates are marked: Human Rights Day on 21 March, Freedom Day on 27 April, Youth Day on 16 June, Women's Day on 9 August, Heritage Day on 24 September and the Day of Reconciliation on 16 December. Dates are written as day, month and year, like 24 September 2026, or in short as 24/09/2026.

The questions for this section are with you now: finding the day of a date, the days in each month, leap years, and writing dates.

## Subtopic: Using a Calendar to Count and Plan

Weeks on a calendar help you count forward. One week after Monday 7 September is Monday 14 September, because each row is 7 days. Two weeks after is Monday 21 September. Counting down a column adds 7 days each step.

To find the day of the week for a date not shown, count on in sevens. If 1 March is a Sunday, then 8, 15, 22 and 29 March are also Sundays. So 24 March is 2 days after Sunday 22 March, a Tuesday. Counting in sevens is faster than counting every day.

Months follow each other in order: January, February, March, April, May, June, July, August, September, October, November, December. After December comes January of the next year. If a month ends on a Thursday, the next month starts on a Friday.

Calendars and clocks together answer questions like: the school concert is on 16 October at 18:30. That gives the day and the time, with nothing left to guess. When writing an invitation, give both the date and the time, and use a.m. and p.m. or the 24-hour way so there is no doubt.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking the longest time wins a race; the fastest runner takes the shortest time. Two: changing 2 minutes 45 seconds into 245 seconds; each minute is 60 seconds, so it is 120 plus 45, which is 165. Three: giving every month 30 days; count with the rhyme, thirty days has September, April, June and November. Four: counting day by day for dates far away; count on in sevens to keep the same day of the week.

The questions for this section are with you now: counting forward in weeks, finding the day of a date by counting in sevens, the order of the months, and giving a date and a time together.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the athletics day and the wall calendar — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Ready, Set, Stop

60 seconds make a minute. A stopwatch counts how long something takes.

In a race, the shortest time wins. Lerato 15 seconds beats Thabo 16 seconds.

Change minutes to seconds by timesing by 60. 2 minutes 45 seconds is 165 seconds.

Keep the athletics day in mind and try the questions for this part: seconds and stopwatches.

## Subtopic: Months and Days

12 months in a year, 7 days in a week. Thirty days has September, April, June and November.

The other months have 31, except February: 28, or 29 in a leap year.

365 days in a year, 366 in a leap year. Leap years come every 4 years.

Hold the wall calendar in mind and try this part's questions: months and days.

## Subtopic: Count in Weeks

Each row of a calendar is a week. Go down one row: 7 days later.

Count on in sevens. If 1 March is a Sunday, so are 8, 15, 22 and 29 March.

Give both the date and the time: 16 October at 18:30.

Pocket summary of the lesson. 60 seconds make a minute and 60 minutes make an hour. A stopwatch measures how long something takes, and in a race the shortest time wins; change minutes and seconds to seconds by multiplying the minutes by 60. A calendar arranges a year of 12 months and 365 days, or 366 in a leap year, into weeks of 7 days; September, April, June and November have 30 days, February 28 or 29, and the rest 31. Count on in sevens to find the day of a date.

Hold the calendar in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: counting with a calendar.
