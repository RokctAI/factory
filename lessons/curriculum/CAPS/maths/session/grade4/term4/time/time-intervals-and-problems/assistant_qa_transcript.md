### subtopic_1 — Units of Time
**Assistant:** Why can't I just subtract the times? 10:35 minus 06:50 seems quicker.

**Tutor:** It looks quicker, but it gives the wrong answer, because clock times do not work like ordinary numbers. In ordinary numbers, 100 ones make a hundred. On a clock, only 60 minutes make an hour. If you subtract 650 from 1035 as ordinary numbers you get 385, which you might read as 3 hours 85 minutes, and that is not even a real time, since 85 minutes is more than an hour. The real answer is 3 hours 45 minutes, which is 40 minutes less than your subtraction suggested. Counting on in friendly steps avoids the problem completely. From 06:50, jump 10 minutes to 07:00, then 3 whole hours to 10:00, then 35 minutes to 10:35. Add the jumps: 3 hours and 45 minutes. Every jump is easy, and you never have to borrow across the 60. You can also do it in your head by thinking like a taxi driver: first get to the next o'clock, then count whole hours, then add the bit left over. Later on you will learn ways to subtract times in columns that borrow 60 minutes instead of 100, but counting on will always be a reliable way to check your answer.

### subtopic_2 — Finding Intervals Between Clock Times
**Assistant:** How do I remember all the units of time and how they fit together?

**Tutor:** Learn them as a chain, from the smallest to the biggest, with the number that links each pair: 60 seconds make a minute, 60 minutes make an hour, 24 hours make a day, 7 days make a week, 12 months make a year, 10 years make a decade, and 100 years make a century. Say the chain aloud a few times: 60, 60, 24, 7, 12, 10, 100. Many of these you already live with. You know a week has 7 days because of the school week and the weekend. You know a day has 24 hours from the 24-hour clock. You know there are 12 months from the calendar. The tricky one for many learners is that weeks do not fit neatly into months or years: a month has about 4 weeks and a few extra days, and a year has 52 weeks and 1 extra day. So do not try to change months into weeks exactly; use days instead. When you change units, ask whether you are going to a smaller unit or a bigger one. Smaller units need more of them, so multiply. Bigger units need fewer, so divide or group. That one question keeps you from turning 3 hours into 20 minutes by mistake.

### subtopic_3 — Longer Intervals and Time Problems
**Assistant:** What is a decade and why do we use it?

**Tutor:** A decade is 10 years, just as a dozen is 12 things and a century is 100 years. We use it to talk about longer stretches of time without big numbers. Saying South Africa has been a democracy for more than three decades is a neater way of describing the years since 1994 than counting every year, and people often talk about the music, fashion or events of a decade, like the 1990s, which means the years 1990 to 1999. Decades are also handy for estimating. If you were born in 2016, you turned one decade old in 2026. Your parents might be three or four decades old, and your grandparents six or seven. When you work with decades, you are multiplying or dividing by 10: 4 decades is 40 years, and 50 years is 5 decades. Time intervals in years are found by subtracting, because years count in ordinary ones. From 1994 to 2026 is 2026 minus 1994, which is 32 years, a bit more than 3 decades. History lessons in Social Sciences use these ideas all the time, so the maths you learn here helps you understand timelines and how long ago events happened.
