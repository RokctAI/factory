### subtopic_1 — Reading Analogue and Digital Clocks
**Assistant:** Why do we need two ways of writing time? It would be easier with just one.

**Tutor:** It would be simpler to learn just one, but each way has a job it does best. The 12-hour way matches the analogue clock, which shows only 12 hours on its face, and it matches how people talk: we say seven in the morning, not zero seven hundred. That makes it friendly for everyday life at home. But the 12-hour way has a weakness: the same numbers happen twice a day, so 7:00 could mean morning or evening. If you forget the a.m. or p.m., someone could miss a bus by twelve hours. The 24-hour way fixes that weakness. Every minute of the day has its own number, from 00:00 at midnight to 23:59 just before the next midnight, so there is no doubt. That is why buses, trains, aeroplanes, hospitals and the army use it: a mistake there could be serious. Phones and computers often let you choose either way. So in real life you will meet both, and changing between them is a useful skill. The rule to remember is short: for afternoon and evening times, add 12 to go to the 24-hour way and take away 12 to come back.

### subtopic_2 — The 12-Hour Way: a.m. and p.m.
**Assistant:** How do I know which number the hour hand is on when it is between two numbers?

**Tutor:** The hour hand moves slowly all the time. At exactly six o'clock it points straight at the 6. Over the next hour it creeps towards the 7, and it reaches the 7 only at exactly seven o'clock. So whenever the hour hand is between 6 and 7, the time is six o'clock and some minutes: it is still in the six o'clock hour. The rule is to read the number it has just passed, not the one it is closest to. This is where many people slip. At 6:50 the hour hand is very close to the 7, and it is tempting to say seven, but it has not reached the 7 yet, so the hour is still 6 and the time is 6:50, or ten to seven. A good check is to look at the minute hand. If the minute hand is past the 6, in the second half of the hour, the hour hand will be more than halfway to the next number. If the minute hand is before the 6, the hour hand will be less than halfway. When they agree, you have read the clock correctly. With practice, your eyes will read both hands together in a second.

### subtopic_3 — The 24-Hour Way
**Assistant:** Why do I add 12 for p.m. times in the 24-hour way?

**Tutor:** Because the 24-hour way keeps counting the hours after midday instead of starting again at 1. In the morning, both ways count the same: 1, 2, 3, up to 11, then 12 at midday. After midday, the 12-hour clock starts again at 1 p.m., but the 24-hour clock carries on: 13, 14, 15, and so on, up to 23. One o'clock in the afternoon is the 13th hour of the day, because 12 hours have already passed since midnight. That is why you add 12: 1 p.m. becomes 13:00, 3:45 p.m. becomes 15:45 and 8:30 p.m. becomes 20:30. Going back, you take away 12: 18:00 is 6 p.m. and 22:10 is 10:10 p.m. Only the hours change; the minutes always stay the same. There are two special cases to watch. Times from 12:00 to 12:59 in the middle of the day are already in the afternoon half, so 12:30 p.m. stays 12:30. And times just after midnight start at 00 in the 24-hour way, so 12:15 a.m. is 00:15. Remember, if the hour in a 24-hour time is 13 or more, it is p.m.
