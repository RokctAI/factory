# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers writing number sentences to describe problem situations. The anchor is a spaza shop in Soweto where a customer buys bread for R18 and milk for R24, a school bus carrying 46 learners of whom 19 get off, and 36 oranges shared among 9 players after practice. The governing idea is that a number sentence records the structure of a problem before any calculating is done, using a symbol such as a box or a letter for the unknown. Writing a number sentence for a given situation, choosing the correct operation from the words of a problem and placing the unknown correctly are examinable.

## Subtopic: What a Number Sentence Is

A number sentence is a complete mathematical statement with an equals sign. 18 plus 24 equals 42 is a number sentence. 18 plus 24 on its own is only an expression, because it does not say what the result is. A number sentence can be true, like 18 plus 24 equals 42, or false, like 18 plus 24 equals 40, or open, like 18 plus 24 equals box, where the box stands for a number still to be found.

At the spaza shop a customer buys bread for R18 and milk for R24. Before touching a calculator the shopkeeper can write the number sentence 18 plus 24 equals box. The sentence says what has to happen: two amounts are combined, and the box will hold the total. Solving the sentence comes later; writing it comes first.

The unknown can be shown by an empty box, a question mark, a line or a letter. 18 plus 24 equals box, 18 plus 24 equals question mark, and 18 plus 24 equals n all mean the same thing. In Grade 4 the box is the most common. In later grades letters take over, so it is good to see both.

The unknown does not have to sit at the end. If the customer pays R50 and gets R8 change, the sentence is 50 minus box equals 8, with the box in the middle. Where the box goes depends on which part of the story is missing, not on a fixed rule that unknowns go last.

The questions for this section are with you now: recognising a number sentence, true, false and open sentences, and showing the unknown with a box.

## Subtopic: Choosing the Operation from the Words

The words of a problem point to the operation. Joining, altogether, total, in all and more than usually mean addition. Taking away, left, fewer, how many more and difference usually mean subtraction. Groups of, each, times and rows of usually mean multiplication. Shared equally, each gets and how many groups usually mean division.

The school bus carries 46 learners. At the first stop 19 get off. How many are still on the bus? Learners are leaving, so the number drops: 46 minus 19 equals box. If instead 19 more learners climbed on, the sentence would be 46 plus 19 equals box. The same two numbers, but the story decides the sign.

After practice the coach shares 36 oranges equally among 9 players. Sharing equally is division: 36 divided by 9 equals box. If each player had brought 4 oranges and there were 9 players, the sentence would be 9 times 4 equals box, because we are counting 9 groups of 4.

Be careful with the word more. Sipho has 19 marbles and Lerato has 27. How many more does Lerato have? More sounds like adding, but the question asks for the difference between two amounts, so it is 27 minus 19 equals box. Read the whole question, not just one word, and picture what is actually happening.

The questions for this section are with you now: matching the words of a problem to plus, minus, times or divide, and the trap in the word more.

## Subtopic: Two-Step Stories and Checking the Sentence

Some stories need two operations. The customer buys bread for R18 and milk for R24 and pays with a R50 note. How much change? First the total: 18 plus 24. Then the change: 50 minus that total. As one sentence, 50 minus 18 plus 24 in brackets equals box. The brackets show that the adding happens first. Without brackets the sentence would mean something different.

A good habit is to read the sentence back as a story and see if it matches. 50 minus 18 plus 24 in brackets equals box reads as fifty rand, take away the cost of the bread and milk together, leaves the change. That is the story, so the sentence is right.

Another check is to estimate. The bread and milk cost about R40, so the change should be about R10. If the sentence you wrote would give an answer near R90 or R2, the sentence is wrong even before you solve it.

Number sentences can also describe a story with the unknown at the start. Some learners are on the bus. 19 get off and 27 remain. How many were there at first? Box minus 19 equals 27. Write the story in order and let the box fall where the missing number is. Solving these sentences is the work of the next lesson.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: writing 18 plus 24 without an equals sign and calling it a number sentence; a sentence needs an equals sign. Two: seeing the word more and adding; how many more asks for a difference, which is subtraction. Three: always putting the box at the end; the box goes where the missing number is in the story. Four: leaving out the brackets in a two-step sentence; 50 minus 18 plus 24 without brackets does not say add first.

The questions for this section are with you now: writing a two-step sentence with brackets, reading a sentence back as a story, and placing the unknown at the start.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the spaza shop, the bus and the oranges — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: A Sum Written Down

A number sentence is a sum with an equals sign. 18 plus 24 equals 42. It can be true, false or open. Open means a number is missing, and we draw a box for it: 18 plus 24 equals box.

At the spaza shop, bread is R18 and milk is R24. Write the sentence before you work it out: 18 plus 24 equals box. The box is waiting for the total.

The box can be anywhere. Pay R50, get R8 change: 50 minus box equals 8. The box sits where the missing number is in the story.

Keep the till in mind and try the questions for this part: what a number sentence is and where the box goes.

## Subtopic: Which Sign

The story tells you the sign. Altogether or total means plus. Left or how many more means minus. Groups of or each means times. Shared equally means divide.

46 learners on the bus, 19 get off. Fewer learners, so minus: 46 minus 19 equals box. 36 oranges shared among 9 players: divide. 36 divided by 9 equals box.

Careful with more. How many more does Lerato have than Sipho? That is a difference, so minus: 27 minus 19 equals box.

Hold the four signal words in mind and try this part's questions: picking the sign from the story.

## Subtopic: Two Steps and a Check

Some stories have two steps. Bread R18, milk R24, paid with R50. Add first, then take away: 50 minus 18 plus 24 in brackets equals box. The brackets say do this part first.

Read your sentence back as a story. Does it say what happened? If yes, it is right.

Estimate too. Bread and milk are about R40, so the change should be about R10. If your sentence would give R90, start again.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A number sentence is a statement with numbers, signs and an equals sign; it can be true, false or open. The box stands for the unknown and sits where the story has a gap. The words of the problem point to plus, minus, times or divide, but read the whole question. Two-step sentences use brackets to show what happens first, and reading the sentence back as a story checks it.

Hold the read-it-back check in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: two-step sentences and brackets.
