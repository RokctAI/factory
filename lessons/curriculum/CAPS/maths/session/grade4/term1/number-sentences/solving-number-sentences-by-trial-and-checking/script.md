# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers solving and completing number sentences by inspection, by trial and improvement, and by checking through substitution. The anchor is a bag of marbles at a school in Mbombela where box plus 17 equals 45, change from a R100 note where 100 minus box equals 63, and chairs in the hall stacked so that 8 times box equals 96. The governing idea is that the two sides of a number sentence must balance, so the missing number is whatever makes the sentence true, and the inverse operation finds it directly. Finding the unknown in an addition, subtraction, multiplication or division sentence and proving the answer by substitution are examinable.

## Subtopic: Solving by Inspection

Inspection means looking at the sentence and seeing the answer because you already know the fact. Box plus 7 equals 12: the missing number is 5, because 5 plus 7 is 12 is a fact you know. 9 times box equals 54: the missing number is 6, from the 9 times table. Inspection is the fastest method, and it works whenever the numbers are small and familiar.

Inspection relies on seeing the sentence as a balance. The equals sign says that the left side and the right side are worth the same. In box plus 17 equals 45, the right side is 45, so the left side must also be worth 45. Seventeen is already there; the box must supply the rest, which is 28. Checking: 28 plus 17 is 45. The marble bag held 28 marbles before 17 were added.

For subtraction sentences inspection often means asking how much is missing. 100 minus box equals 63: a R100 note, something is spent, R63 is left. What was spent is the gap between 100 and 63, which is 37. Checking: 100 minus 37 is 63.

For division, inspection uses the times table in reverse. 96 divided by box equals 12: twelve times what is 96? Twelve times 8 is 96, so the box is 8. Checking: 96 divided by 8 is 12.

The questions for this section are with you now: solving sentences by inspection, using the equals sign as a balance, and finding a missing number from a known fact.

## Subtopic: Trial and Improvement

When the numbers are too big to see at once, guess, check and improve. 8 times box equals 96. Try 10: 8 times 10 is 80, too small. Try 13: 8 times 13 is 104, too big. The answer lies between 10 and 13. Try 12: 8 times 12 is 96. Exactly right, so the box is 12. The hall chairs are in 8 stacks of 12.

Trial and improvement works because each wrong guess teaches you something. If the result is too small, your number must grow. If it is too big, your number must shrink. Write each trial down with its result and whether it was too big or too small, so that you squeeze the answer from both sides.

Trial and improvement works for any operation. Box minus 268 equals 457. Try 700: 700 minus 268 is 432, too small. Try 730: 730 minus 268 is 462, too big. Try 725: 725 minus 268 is 457. The box is 725. A clever first guess saves time: the answer must be bigger than 457 plus 200, so start near 700.

Trial and improvement is slower than inspection but it never fails, and it is honest work: every step can be checked. Later in the year you will see that the inverse operation gives the same answers in one step. Both methods are correct, and knowing both lets you check one against the other.

The questions for this section are with you now: guessing, checking and improving towards a missing number, and reading too big or too small to choose the next guess.

## Subtopic: Checking by Substitution and Using Inverses

Substitution means putting your answer into the box and seeing whether the sentence is true. If you think the box in 100 minus box equals 63 is 37, write 100 minus 37 equals 63 and work out the left side: 100 minus 37 is 63. The sentence is true, so 37 is right. If you had thought 47, then 100 minus 47 is 53, not 63, and the sentence is false, so 47 is wrong.

Substitution is the only proof that an answer is correct. A guess that has not been substituted is still a guess. Make it the last step of every number sentence, even when you are sure.

The quickest route to the box is the inverse operation, the operation that undoes the one in the sentence. Addition and subtraction undo each other. Multiplication and division undo each other. In box plus 17 equals 45, undo the plus 17 by subtracting 17 from 45: the box is 28. In 8 times box equals 96, undo the times 8 by dividing 96 by 8: the box is 12.

Inverses need care with order in subtraction and division. In 100 minus box equals 63, the box is 100 minus 63, which is 37. But in box minus 268 equals 457, the box is 457 plus 268, which is 725, because the box was the starting amount and something was taken from it. Picture the story: if money was taken away and 457 is left, add it back to find what you started with.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: treating the equals sign as the answer comes next; it means both sides are worth the same, so the box can be on either side. Two: solving box minus 268 equals 457 as 457 minus 268; the box was the starting amount, so add 268 back to get 725. Three: stopping after a guess without substituting; only substitution proves the answer. Four: guessing randomly in trial and improvement; each result tells you whether to go bigger or smaller.

The questions for this section are with you now: proving an answer by substitution, using inverse operations, and watching the order in subtraction sentences.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the marbles, the R100 note and the stack of chairs — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: See It at Once

Sometimes you just know. Box plus 7 equals 12. Five. 9 times box equals 54. Six. That is solving by inspection.

The equals sign is a balance. Both sides weigh the same. Box plus 17 equals 45: the right side is 45, so the left side must be 45 too. 17 is there already, so the box is 28.

R100 note, R63 left. What was spent? The gap from 63 to 100 is 37. Check: 100 minus 37 is 63.

Keep the balance in mind and try the questions for this part: solving by inspection.

## Subtopic: Guess, Check, Improve

Big numbers? Guess, check, improve. 8 times box equals 96. Try 10: 80, too small. Try 13: 104, too big. Try 12: 96. Got it.

Too small means guess bigger. Too big means guess smaller. Write each try down so you can see which way to go.

Box minus 268 equals 457. Start near 700. 700 gives 432, too small. 725 gives 457. The box is 725.

Hold the guess-and-improve loop in mind and try this part's questions: trial and improvement.

## Subtopic: Prove It and Undo It

Always put your answer back in. 100 minus 37 equals 63? Work it out: yes, 63. True. The answer is proved.

The quick way is to undo. Plus 17 is undone by minus 17, so box plus 17 equals 45 gives 45 minus 17, which is 28. Times 8 is undone by divide by 8, so 8 times box equals 96 gives 96 divided by 8, which is 12.

Careful with take-away stories. Box minus 268 equals 457: something was taken and 457 is left, so add it back. 457 plus 268 is 725.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A number sentence balances: both sides are worth the same. Solve by inspection when you know the fact, by trial and improvement when you do not, or by using the inverse operation. Subtraction undoes addition and division undoes multiplication; watch the order in take-away sentences. Prove every answer by substituting it back into the sentence.

Hold the undo-and-prove habit in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: substitution and inverses.
