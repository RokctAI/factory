### subtopic_1 — Solving by Inspection
**Assistant:** If inspection is fastest, why learn trial and improvement at all?

**Tutor:** Because inspection only works when you already know the fact, and there will always be sentences where you do not. Box plus 7 equals 12 is inspection, because 5 plus 7 equals 12 lives in your memory. 8 times box equals 96 might be inspection for a learner who knows the 8 times table up to 12, and trial and improvement for one who does not yet. Trial and improvement is the method that never runs out: as long as you can do the forward calculation and compare, you can close in on any missing number. It also teaches something inspection cannot. Each wrong guess gives you information, too big or too small, and learning to use that information instead of being embarrassed by it is one of the most valuable habits in mathematics. Scientists, engineers and programmers solve many problems exactly this way, by trying a value, measuring the error and adjusting. Later you will learn methods that find the answer in one step, like the inverse operations in this lesson, and in high school you will learn algebra that does it for harder sentences. But when those methods are not available, or when you want to check them, trial and improvement is still there. Think of inspection as a shortcut you earn by knowing facts, and trial and improvement as the road that always gets you there.

### subtopic_2 — Trial and Improvement
**Assistant:** Why does subtracting work backwards for box minus 268 but not for 100 minus box?

**Tutor:** Because the box is playing a different part in each story, and the inverse has to undo what actually happened to the box. In 100 minus box equals 63, the box is the amount taken away. You started with 100, removed the box, and 63 remained. To find what was removed, you measure the gap between 100 and 63, which is 100 minus 63, giving 37. In box minus 268 equals 457, the box is the starting amount. You started with the box, removed 268, and 457 remained. To get back to the start you put the 268 back, which is 457 plus 268, giving 725. The same sign, minus, is in both sentences, but in one the box is what was taken and in the other the box is what you began with. The safest way to get this right is not to memorise a rule about where the box sits, but to tell the story. Ask what happened to the box. If it was reduced by 268, undo that by adding 268. If it was the piece removed from 100, find it by measuring from 63 up to 100. Then substitute to be sure: 725 minus 268 is 457, and 100 minus 37 is 63. Both sentences come out true, which is the final word.

### subtopic_3 — Checking by Substitution and Using Inverses
**Assistant:** Substitution feels like doing the sum twice. Is it really necessary?

**Tutor:** It feels like double work because, in a way, it is, and that is exactly why it is worth doing. The first time through, you are searching for the answer and your attention is on finding it. The second time, with the number in the box, you are only checking a plain calculation, and plain calculations are where slips get caught. Think of it as the difference between writing a sentence and reading it back. Nobody hands in a letter without reading it, and nobody should hand in a number sentence without substituting. The cost is small: 100 minus 37 takes a few seconds. The benefit is large: every mistake you catch at your desk is a mark you keep in a test and a wrong order you avoid in a shop. Substitution also settles arguments. If two learners get different answers for 8 times box equals 96, one saying 12 and one saying 13, there is no need to argue; substitute both. 8 times 12 is 96, true. 8 times 13 is 104, false. The sentence itself decides. That is the deeper point. The box is defined as the number that makes the sentence true, so substituting is not an extra step added to the method; it is the very meaning of having solved it.
