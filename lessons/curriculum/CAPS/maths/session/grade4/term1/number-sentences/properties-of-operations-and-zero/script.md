# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the commutative, associative and distributive properties of operations with whole numbers, and zero in terms of its additive property. The anchor is a minibus with 4 rows of 3 seats at a rank in Pietermaritzburg, trays of eggs stacked 6 by 4 and then 4 by 6, and a packet of 23 sweets shared out 5 times. The governing idea is that addition and multiplication can be reordered and regrouped without changing the answer, that multiplication can be spread over an addition, and that adding zero leaves a number unchanged. Recognising each property, using it to simplify a calculation and knowing that subtraction and division are not commutative are examinable.

## Subtopic: The Commutative Property

Commutative means the order can be swapped. For addition, 18 plus 24 equals 24 plus 18; both are 42. For multiplication, 4 times 3 equals 3 times 4; both are 12. The minibus shows it: 4 rows of 3 seats is 12 seats, and looking from the side it is 3 columns of 4 seats, still 12. Turning the picture round does not change how many seats there are.

The commutative property is useful because one order is often easier than the other. 7 plus 58 is easier as 58 plus 7, counting on from the bigger number. 25 times 4 is easier for most learners than 4 times 25, because 25, 50, 75, 100 is a counting pattern they already know. Swap to whichever order you find easier; the answer does not change.

Subtraction and division are not commutative. 10 minus 4 is 6, but 4 minus 10 is not 6. 12 divided by 3 is 4, but 3 divided by 12 is not 4. The order matters because one number is acting on the other. The egg trays show it: 24 eggs shared among 6 trays gives 4 per tray, but 6 eggs shared among 24 trays does not give 4.

When a problem lets you choose, swap only when the operation is addition or multiplication. If you see a learner write 268 minus 457 for 457 minus 268, the commutative habit has been applied where it does not belong, and the answer will be wrong.

The questions for this section are with you now: swapping numbers in addition and multiplication, choosing the easier order, and knowing which operations cannot be swapped.

## Subtopic: The Associative Property

Associative means the grouping can be changed when three or more numbers are added or multiplied. 7 plus 8 in brackets plus 2 equals 7 plus 8 plus 2 in brackets. The left side is 15 plus 2, which is 17. The right side is 7 plus 10, which is 17. The brackets moved, but the answer stayed. The second grouping is easier because 8 plus 2 makes a ten.

For multiplication: 2 times 5 in brackets times 7 equals 2 times 5 times 7 in brackets. The left side is 10 times 7, which is 70. The right side is 2 times 35, which is 70. Grouping the 2 with the 5 first makes 10, which is the easiest number to multiply by.

The egg trays show associative multiplication. 2 stacks of 6 trays, each tray holding 4 eggs. Count the trays first: 2 times 6 is 12 trays, times 4 eggs is 48. Or count one stack first: 6 times 4 is 24 eggs per stack, times 2 stacks is 48. Same eggs, two groupings.

Together, the commutative and associative properties let you add a list of numbers in any order with any grouping. For 17 plus 25 plus 3 plus 5, pair 17 with 3 to get 20 and 25 with 5 to get 30, then 20 plus 30 is 50. Looking for pairs that make tens or hundreds is one of the most useful mental maths habits there is. Subtraction and division are not associative, so this freedom does not apply to them.

The questions for this section are with you now: regrouping three numbers in addition and multiplication, choosing groups that make tens, and the limits of the property.

## Subtopic: The Distributive Property and Zero

Distributive means that multiplication can be spread over addition. 5 times 23 can be done as 5 times 20 plus 5 times 3. That is 100 plus 15, which is 115. We split 23 into 20 plus 3, multiplied each part by 5 and added the results. The packet of 23 sweets, handed out 5 times, gives 115 sweets whichever way you count.

The property is written with brackets: 5 times 20 plus 3 in brackets equals 5 times 20 plus 5 times 3. The 5 is shared out, or distributed, to both numbers inside the bracket. This is the idea behind the multiplication methods you will use in the next topic, where a two-digit number is broken into tens and units before multiplying.

The distributive property also works with subtraction inside the bracket. 6 times 19 is 6 times 20 minus 1 in brackets, which is 120 minus 6, giving 114. Rounding 19 up to 20 and then taking away the extra 6 is quicker than multiplying 19 directly.

Zero has a special additive property: adding zero to any number, or subtracting zero from it, leaves the number unchanged. 348 plus 0 is 348, and 348 minus 0 is 348. Zero is called the identity for addition because the number keeps its identity. This is not true for multiplication: 348 times 0 is 0, not 348. If a learner receives no marbles, the bag holds exactly what it held before; adding nothing changes nothing.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: swapping the numbers in a subtraction, writing 268 minus 457 for 457 minus 268; only addition and multiplication are commutative. Two: thinking the associative property lets you change a calculation's answer; it changes only the grouping, never the value. Three: distributing 5 over 23 as 5 times 20 plus 3; the 5 must multiply both parts, giving 100 plus 15, which is 115. Four: saying 348 times 0 is 348 because zero changes nothing; zero changes nothing only in addition and subtraction.

The questions for this section are with you now: splitting a number to multiply it, using the distributive property with subtraction, and the effect of adding zero.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the minibus seats, the egg trays and the sweets — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Swap It Round

Swapping is allowed for plus and times. 18 plus 24 is the same as 24 plus 18. 4 times 3 is the same as 3 times 4. The minibus has 4 rows of 3 seats or 3 columns of 4. Still 12 seats.

Swap to make it easier. 7 plus 58? Start at 58 and count on 7. 4 times 25? Say 25 times 4: 25, 50, 75, 100.

Never swap minus or divide. 10 minus 4 is 6, but 4 minus 10 is not. Order matters there.

Keep the minibus in mind and try the questions for this part: when you may swap and when you may not.

## Subtopic: Group It Differently

With three numbers you can choose which two to do first. 7 plus 8 plus 2: do 8 plus 2 first to make 10, then 7 plus 10 is 17.

2 times 5 times 7: do 2 times 5 first to make 10, then 10 times 7 is 70.

Look for pairs that make tens. 17 plus 25 plus 3 plus 5: 17 and 3 make 20, 25 and 5 make 30. Total 50. Only for plus and times, never for minus or divide.

Hold the make-a-ten trick in mind and try this part's questions: grouping to make calculating easy.

## Subtopic: Split It and Add Nothing

Big times tables? Split the number. 5 times 23: split 23 into 20 and 3. 5 times 20 is 100, 5 times 3 is 15, together 115.

Or round up and take away. 6 times 19: 6 times 20 is 120, take away one 6, that is 114.

Zero is the do-nothing number for adding. 348 plus 0 is 348. 348 minus 0 is 348. But watch out: 348 times 0 is 0.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Commutative: the order of addition or multiplication can be swapped. Associative: the grouping of three or more numbers being added or multiplied can be changed. Both are false for subtraction and division. Distributive: a multiplier is shared over the parts of a split number, so 5 times 23 is 5 times 20 plus 5 times 3. Adding or subtracting zero leaves a number unchanged.

Hold the split-and-add trick in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: the distributive property and zero.
