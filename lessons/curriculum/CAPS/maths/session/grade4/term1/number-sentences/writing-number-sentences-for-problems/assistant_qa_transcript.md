### subtopic_1 — What a Number Sentence Is
**Assistant:** Why write a sentence at all? I can just work out the answer.

**Tutor:** For small sums you can, and for a while that will work. The reason to write the sentence is that it separates two different skills: understanding the problem and doing the arithmetic. Most mistakes in word problems are not arithmetic mistakes; they are reading mistakes, where the learner subtracted when the story needed division or added two numbers that should never have met. If you go straight to an answer, those mistakes are invisible, and the teacher marking your work cannot tell whether you misread the story or slipped in the calculation. The sentence makes your thinking visible. 46 minus 19 equals box tells anyone exactly how you understood the bus story, and if the understanding is wrong it can be fixed before any arithmetic is wasted. There is a second, longer-term reason. In Grade 7 and beyond you will meet algebra, where the unknown becomes a letter and the sentences are called equations. Everything you will do there, from solving for x to balancing both sides, is built on the habit you are forming now of writing a sentence first. Grade 4 problems are easy enough to do in your head, which is exactly why this is the right time to practise the habit, while the numbers are not fighting you.

### subtopic_2 — Choosing the Operation from the Words
**Assistant:** What if a problem has words from two groups, like total and left?

**Tutor:** Then you have a two-step problem, and the words are telling you both steps. Suppose a shopkeeper has 46 loaves, bakes 19 more, and then sells 27. How many are left? The word more signals adding, and left signals subtracting, and both are needed. Write the story in order: 46 plus 19, then minus 27, so 46 plus 19 in brackets minus 27 equals box. The brackets make it clear that the baking happens before the selling. When the signal words seem to clash, stop looking at single words and act out the story instead. Picture the loaves on the shelf. More loaves arrive: the pile grows. Loaves are sold: the pile shrinks. Each change in the picture is one operation in the sentence. The signal words are only a first guess; the picture is the real test. This is also why the word more can be a trap on its own. Lerato has 27 marbles and Sipho has 19; how many more does Lerato have? Nothing is being added in the picture. Two piles sit side by side and you are measuring the gap between them, which is subtraction. Words point you towards an operation, but the story decides.

### subtopic_3 — Two-Step Stories and Checking the Sentence
**Assistant:** Does the box have to be a box? My older sister uses letters.

**Tutor:** A box, a question mark, a blank line and a letter all do the same job: they hold a place for the number you do not know yet. Your sister uses letters because from about Grade 7 the box turns into a letter such as x or n, and the subject gets the name algebra. Nothing changes except the symbol. 18 plus 24 equals box and 18 plus 24 equals n are the same sentence, and whatever number makes one true makes the other true. We start with boxes in Grade 4 for two practical reasons. A box looks like a space waiting to be filled, which is exactly what it is, and that picture helps when you are learning. And a letter can be confused with a measurement unit or a word, especially letters like m, l and g that also mean metres, litres and grams. By the time letters arrive you will be comfortable with the idea of an unknown, and the switch will feel small. If you want to try letters now, go ahead, because your teacher will not mark a correct sentence wrong for using n instead of a box, as long as you say what the letter stands for. The one thing to avoid is using the same letter for two different unknowns in one problem, which is a mistake even in high school.
