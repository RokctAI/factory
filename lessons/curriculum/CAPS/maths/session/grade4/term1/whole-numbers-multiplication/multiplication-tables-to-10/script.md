# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the multiplication of one-digit numbers by numbers up to 10, meaning the tables from 1 times 1 to 10 times 10. The anchor is egg boxes of 6 at a market in Rustenburg, a netball team of 7 players across several teams, and marbles arranged in rows. The governing idea is that multiplication is repeated addition of equal groups, which can be pictured as a rectangle called an array, and that the commutative property halves the number of facts to learn. Knowing the tables to 10 times 10 fluently, using arrays, and using patterns such as doubling and the 9 times finger trick are examinable.

## Subtopic: Groups, Arrays and the Easy Tables

Multiplication counts equal groups. 4 egg boxes with 6 eggs in each is 4 times 6, which is 6 plus 6 plus 6 plus 6, which is 24. The first number says how many groups, the second says how big each group is. An array shows the same fact as a rectangle: 4 rows of 6 dots has 24 dots. Turn it on its side and you see 6 rows of 4, which is why 4 times 6 and 6 times 4 are both 24.

The 1, 2, 5 and 10 times tables are the easy ones. Anything times 1 is itself. Anything times 2 is doubled: 7 times 2 is 14. Anything times 10 gets a zero at the end: 7 times 10 is 70. Anything times 5 ends in 5 or 0, and it is half of the times-10 answer: 7 times 10 is 70, so 7 times 5 is 35.

The 4 and 8 times tables come from doubling. 4 times a number is double the double: 7 times 2 is 14, doubled is 28, so 7 times 4 is 28. 8 times a number is double again: 28 doubled is 56, so 7 times 8 is 56. The 3 and 6 tables are linked the same way: 7 times 3 is 21, so 7 times 6 is 42.

Zero in multiplication is different from zero in addition. Any number times 0 is 0, because zero groups of anything is nothing. 6 times 0 is 0. This is worth stating clearly because the additive property of zero, that adding zero changes nothing, can lead learners to expect 6 times 0 to be 6.

The questions for this section are with you now: multiplication as groups and arrays, the easy tables, and building the 4, 6 and 8 tables by doubling.

## Subtopic: The 9 Times Table and the Hard Facts

The 9 times table has a pattern. 9 times 1 is 9, 9 times 2 is 18, 9 times 3 is 27, 9 times 4 is 36, 9 times 5 is 45, 9 times 6 is 54, 9 times 7 is 63, 9 times 8 is 72, 9 times 9 is 81, 9 times 10 is 90. In every answer up to 9 times 10 the digits add to 9, and the tens digit is one less than the number you multiplied by. For 9 times 7, the tens digit is 6 and the units digit is 9 minus 6, which is 3, giving 63.

The finger trick does the same thing. Hold up ten fingers and fold down the finger for the number you are multiplying by 9. For 9 times 7, fold the seventh finger. Six fingers stand to the left, three to the right: 63.

Most learners find a small set of facts hardest: 6 times 7 is 42, 7 times 8 is 56, 6 times 8 is 48, 7 times 7 is 49, 8 times 8 is 64, 6 times 9 is 54 and 9 times 9 is 81. These are the facts worth drilling until they are instant, because they appear inside almost every long multiplication.

Because of the commutative property, every fact comes with its twin. Knowing 7 times 8 is 56 means knowing 8 times 7 is 56. That halves the work: of the 100 facts in the 10 by 10 table, only 55 are different, and once the easy tables are removed, fewer than 20 need real memorising.

The questions for this section are with you now: the 9 times pattern, the finger trick, and the handful of hard facts.

## Subtopic: Using Tables in Problems

Tables answer real questions. Netball teams have 7 players. How many players in 8 teams? 8 times 7, which is 56 players. If each player needs 2 bibs, that is 56 times 2, which is 112 bibs, a fact just outside the tables that doubling handles easily.

Tables also work backwards. 48 marbles are laid out in rows of 6. How many rows? Ask which number times 6 gives 48: 8 times 6 is 48, so 8 rows. This backwards use is division, and knowing the tables makes division immediate.

Problems often hide a multiplication in words like each, every, per and rows of. A ticket costs R9 and 6 learners buy one each: 6 times 9 is 54, so R54. A grid has 9 rows of 9 squares: 81 squares. A week has 7 days, so 4 weeks is 4 times 7, which is 28 days.

When a fact will not come, do not guess. Use a nearby fact and adjust. For 7 times 6, take 7 times 5, which is 35, and add one more 7 to get 42. For 9 times 8, take 10 times 8, which is 80, and subtract one 8 to get 72. These adjustments are the distributive property at work, and they keep you accurate while the facts settle into memory.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: saying 6 times 0 is 6; zero groups of six is nothing, so 6 times 0 is 0. Two: mixing up 7 times 8 and 7 times 7; 7 times 8 is 56 and 7 times 7 is 49. Three: learning 6 times 7 and 7 times 6 as two separate facts; they are twins and share the answer 42. Four: guessing when a fact will not come; use a nearby fact and adjust instead.

The questions for this section are with you now: finding the multiplication hidden in a story, using tables backwards for division, and adjusting from a nearby fact.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the egg boxes and the netball teams — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Groups and Rectangles

Times means groups of. 4 boxes of 6 eggs: 4 times 6. Count them: 6, 12, 18, 24.

Draw it as a rectangle of dots. 4 rows of 6 is 24 dots. Turn it round: 6 rows of 4. Still 24. So 4 times 6 and 6 times 4 are twins.

Easy tables: times 1 is itself, times 2 is double, times 10 adds a zero, times 5 is half of times 10. Times 4 is double the double.

Keep the egg boxes in mind and try the questions for this part: groups, arrays and the easy tables.

## Subtopic: Nines on Your Fingers

Hold up ten fingers. For 9 times 7, fold down finger number 7. Six fingers on the left, three on the right: 63.

Every 9 times answer up to 90 has digits that add to 9. 18, 27, 36, 45, 54, 63, 72, 81. Check: 6 plus 3 is 9.

The hard facts: 6 times 7 is 42, 7 times 8 is 56, 6 times 8 is 48, 7 times 7 is 49, 8 times 8 is 64. Say them until they are instant.

Hold the finger trick in mind and try this part's questions: the 9 times table and the hard facts.

## Subtopic: Tables in Real Life

8 netball teams, 7 players each: 8 times 7 is 56 players.

Backwards too. 48 marbles in rows of 6: which number times 6 is 48? 8. So 8 rows.

Forgot a fact? Use a neighbour. 7 times 6: start at 7 times 5, which is 35, add 7 more: 42. 9 times 8: 10 times 8 is 80, take one 8 off: 72.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Multiplication counts equal groups and can be drawn as an array, which shows why each fact has a twin. Times 2 doubles, times 10 adds a zero, times 5 is half of times 10, and times 4 and times 8 come from doubling. The 9 times answers have digits that add to 9. The hard facts, like 6 times 7 is 42 and 7 times 8 is 56, must be drilled, and a forgotten fact can be rebuilt from a neighbour.

Hold the neighbour trick in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: tables in problems.
