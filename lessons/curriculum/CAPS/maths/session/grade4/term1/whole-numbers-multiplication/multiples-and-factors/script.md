# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers multiples of one-digit numbers to at least 100, and factors of one-digit numbers to at least 10 and of two-digit numbers to at least 100. The anchor is a classroom in Upington with 24 desks arranged in equal rows, biscuits packed in 6s and 8s for a school fete, and skipping in 7s. The governing idea is that multiples and factors are two views of the same multiplication fact: because 4 times 6 is 24, 24 is a multiple of both 4 and 6, and 4 and 6 are both factors of 24. Listing multiples of a number, listing all the factors of a number in pairs, and recognising common multiples and common factors are examinable.

## Subtopic: Multiples

The multiples of a number are the answers in its times table, continuing as far as you like. The multiples of 7 are 7, 14, 21, 28, 35, 42, 49, 56, 63, 70, 77, 84, 91, 98 and so on. Every multiple of 7 is 7 times some whole number. Skipping in 7s lands only on multiples of 7, so a skipper counting 7, 14, 21 will reach 98 but never 100.

Multiples go on forever, so you can never list them all. Questions therefore ask for the first few multiples, or for the multiples below some limit. The multiples of 6 below 50 are 6, 12, 18, 24, 30, 36, 42 and 48. The multiples of 8 below 50 are 8, 16, 24, 32, 40 and 48.

To test whether a number is a multiple of another, divide. 84 is a multiple of 7 because 84 divided by 7 is exactly 12. 85 is not a multiple of 7 because 85 divided by 7 leaves a remainder of 1. Every number is a multiple of 1 and of itself.

Common multiples are numbers that appear in two lists. Looking at the multiples of 6 and 8 below 50, both lists contain 24 and 48. So 24 and 48 are common multiples of 6 and 8, and 24 is the lowest common multiple. If biscuits come in packets of 6 and also in packets of 8, the smallest number of biscuits that can be made exactly with either kind of packet is 24.

The questions for this section are with you now: listing multiples, testing a number by division, and finding common multiples of two numbers.

## Subtopic: Factors

The factors of a number are the whole numbers that divide into it with no remainder. The factors of 24 are 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12 and 24. Each one divides 24 exactly. 5 is not a factor of 24 because 24 divided by 5 leaves a remainder of 4.

Factors come in pairs that multiply to the number. For 24: 1 times 24, 2 times 12, 3 times 8 and 4 times 6. These pairs are the ways of arranging 24 desks in equal rows: 1 row of 24, 2 rows of 12, 3 rows of 8, 4 rows of 6, and the same arrangements turned round. Listing the pairs in order, starting from 1 and climbing, is how you make sure no factor is missed.

Stop when the pairs start to repeat. For 24, after 4 times 6 the next number to try is 5, which does not work, and then 6, which gives 6 times 4, a pair already found. For 36: 1 times 36, 2 times 18, 3 times 12, 4 times 9, 6 times 6. The pair 6 times 6 uses the same number twice, so 36 has an odd number of factors: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 9, 12, 18 and 36, which is nine factors.

Every number has 1 and itself as factors. A number with exactly two factors, like 7 with factors 1 and 7, is called a prime number, and you will study primes later. The factors of a one-digit number are quick: the factors of 8 are 1, 2, 4 and 8; the factors of 9 are 1, 3 and 9; the factors of 10 are 1, 2, 5 and 10.

The questions for this section are with you now: listing factors in pairs, knowing when to stop, and the factors of one-digit numbers.

## Subtopic: Linking Multiples, Factors and Common Factors

Multiples and factors are the same fact seen from two sides. Because 4 times 6 is 24, we can say 24 is a multiple of 4 and of 6, and we can say 4 and 6 are factors of 24. Multiples are bigger than or equal to the number; factors are smaller than or equal to it. 24 is a multiple of 6, and 6 is a factor of 24.

Common factors are factors shared by two numbers. The factors of 24 are 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 24. The factors of 36 are 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 9, 12, 18, 36. Both lists contain 1, 2, 3, 4, 6 and 12, so those are the common factors, and 12 is the highest common factor. If 24 biscuits and 36 sweets are to be shared into identical party bags with nothing left over, the largest number of bags is 12, each holding 2 biscuits and 3 sweets.

Factors are the key to the equal-rows problem. 24 desks can be arranged in 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12 or 24 equal rows, and no other number works. 5 rows would leave 4 desks over; 7 rows would leave 3 over. A teacher who wants rows of equal length must pick a factor.

Knowing factors speeds up multiplication and division. Seeing that 36 is 4 times 9 turned 25 times 36 into 100 times 9 in the last lesson. Seeing that 24 is 8 times 3 lets you divide 240 by 24 as dividing by 8 and then by 3. Factors are not a separate topic; they are the structure inside every number you calculate with.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: mixing up multiples and factors; multiples are the number times something and are at least as big, factors divide into it and are at most as big. Two: missing a factor by not working in pairs; list 1 times 24, 2 times 12, 3 times 8, 4 times 6 and nothing is lost. Three: listing 5 as a factor of 24 because it is in the 24 times table region; 24 divided by 5 leaves a remainder, so 5 is not a factor. Four: trying to list all the multiples of a number; they never end, so give the first few or those below a limit.

The questions for this section are with you now: stating the same fact as a multiple and as a factor, finding common factors, and using factors for equal rows.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the desks, the biscuits and the skipping — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Counting in a Number

Multiples are the times table that keeps going. Multiples of 7: 7, 14, 21, 28, 35, 42, 49, 56, 63, 70. Skip in 7s and you land on them.

Is 84 a multiple of 7? Divide: 84 divided by 7 is 12 exactly. Yes. Is 85? There is 1 left over. No.

Two numbers can share multiples. Multiples of 6 and multiples of 8 both include 24 and 48. The smallest shared one, 24, is the lowest common multiple.

Keep the skipping rope in mind and try the questions for this part: multiples and common multiples.

## Subtopic: Numbers That Fit Exactly

Factors are the numbers that divide in with nothing left. Factors of 24: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 24.

Find them in pairs. 1 times 24, 2 times 12, 3 times 8, 4 times 6. Those are the ways to put 24 desks in equal rows. Stop when the pairs start to repeat.

Small numbers are quick. Factors of 8: 1, 2, 4, 8. Factors of 9: 1, 3, 9. Factors of 10: 1, 2, 5, 10.

Hold the desks in mind and try this part's questions: listing factors in pairs.

## Subtopic: Two Sides of One Fact

4 times 6 is 24. So 24 is a multiple of 4 and of 6. And 4 and 6 are factors of 24. One fact, two ways of saying it.

Common factors are shared factors. 24 and 36 share 1, 2, 3, 4, 6 and 12. The biggest shared one, 12, is the highest common factor.

24 biscuits and 36 sweets into identical party bags with nothing left: 12 bags, each with 2 biscuits and 3 sweets.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Multiples of a number are its times table continuing forever; test a number by dividing and checking for no remainder. Factors of a number divide into it exactly and come in pairs that multiply to the number; list the pairs from 1 upwards and stop when they repeat. One fact gives both views: 4 times 6 is 24 makes 24 a multiple of 4 and 6 and makes 4 and 6 factors of 24. Common multiples and common factors are shared between two lists.

Hold the party bags in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: multiples, factors and common factors.
