### subtopic_1 — Multiples
**Assistant:** Multiples and factors sound the same to me. How do I keep them apart?

**Tutor:** They are close relatives, which is why they get confused, but they point in opposite directions. Multiples go up: you take the number and multiply it, so the multiples of 6 are 6, 12, 18, 24 and onward, each one at least as big as 6 and the list never ending. Factors go down: you ask what divides into the number, so the factors of 24 are 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12 and 24, each one at most as big as 24 and the list always finishing. A memory hook is that multiples multiply, so they grow, and factors fit, so they are the pieces inside. Another is to say the sentence both ways and hear which one sounds right. 24 is a multiple of 6 sounds right because 24 is bigger and comes from counting in 6s. 6 is a factor of 24 sounds right because 6 fits into 24 exactly four times. If you accidentally say 6 is a multiple of 24, ask yourself whether 6 appears when you count in 24s, 24, 48, 72, and clearly it does not. The two words are really describing the same multiplication fact, 4 times 6 is 24, from the point of view of the big number and from the point of view of the small ones. Once you see that, the only thing to remember is which end of the fact you are standing at.

### subtopic_2 — Factors
**Assistant:** Why do factors come in pairs? Is that always true?

**Tutor:** Factors come in pairs because a factor is one half of a multiplication that makes the number, and a multiplication always has two numbers in it. If 3 divides into 24, then 24 divided by 3 is 8, so 3 times 8 is 24 and 8 is a factor too. Every factor brings its partner, and the partner is just the number divided by the factor. That is why listing in pairs is so safe: you only have to find the smaller member of each pair, and the division gives you the larger one for free. There is one interesting case where a pair looks like a single factor. For 36, the pair 6 times 6 has the same number twice, so 6 appears once in the list of factors, and 36 ends up with an odd number of factors, nine. This happens only for square numbers, those that are a number times itself: 1, 4, 9, 16, 25, 36, 49, 64, 81, 100. Every other number has an even count of factors because every factor has a different partner. The desks show it well. 36 desks can be arranged as 6 rows of 6, a perfect square, and that one arrangement is the same whether you look at it from the front or the side. 24 desks have no such arrangement, which is why all eight factors of 24 pair off into four distinct rectangles.

### subtopic_3 — Linking Multiples, Factors and Common Factors
**Assistant:** When would I ever need the highest common factor? The party bag problem feels made up.

**Tutor:** Party bags are the Grade 4 version of a problem that turns up everywhere things have to be shared or cut into equal pieces with nothing wasted. A tiler laying a floor 24 tiles wide and 36 tiles long wants the biggest square pattern that fits both ways without cutting, and the answer is 12 by 12, the highest common factor. A teacher with 24 boys and 36 girls who wants mixed groups all the same size, using every learner, can make at most 12 groups, each with 2 boys and 3 girls. A baker with 24 rolls and 36 buns packing identical boxes for a market faces exactly the party bag problem. The highest common factor is also the tool for simplifying fractions, which you will meet in Term 3: the fraction 24 over 36 simplifies to 2 over 3 by dividing top and bottom by 12. And the lowest common multiple, its partner, answers questions about when two repeating things line up, such as two buses that leave every 6 and every 8 minutes and next leave together after 24 minutes. The problems in class use biscuits and sweets because they are easy to picture, but the structure behind them is the structure inside every number, and spotting it is what lets you simplify a fraction in one step or plan a floor without wasting a tile.
