# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers multiplication of at least a two-digit number by a one-digit number, and of two-digit by two-digit numbers. The anchor is 24 crates of cold drinks with 6 bottles in each at a shop in Welkom, a hall with 23 rows of 14 seats, and a wall of 36 rows of 25 bricks. The governing idea is the distributive property: a number is broken into tens and units, each part is multiplied, and the partial products are added. Multiplying two-digit numbers by one-digit and two-digit numbers using breaking down and the grid method, and checking by estimation, are examinable.

## Subtopic: Two-Digit by One-Digit

To multiply 24 by 6, break 24 into 20 plus 4. Multiply each part: 6 times 20 is 120, and 6 times 4 is 24. Add: 120 plus 24 is 144. So 24 crates of 6 bottles hold 144 bottles. The two partial products, 120 and 24, are the two parts of the answer, and neither can be left out.

Multiplying by the tens part is easy because of place value. 6 times 20 is 6 times 2 tens, which is 12 tens, which is 120. In general, multiply by the digit and then by 10. 6 times 2 is 12, times 10 is 120.

Estimate before and after. 24 is about 25 and 25 times 6 is 150, so the answer should be near 150. The exact answer 144 is close, so it is believable. If a learner forgets the 120 and writes 24, or adds wrongly and writes 1 224, the estimate rejects the answer at once.

The same method handles any two-digit by one-digit multiplication. 47 times 8: 8 times 40 is 320, 8 times 7 is 56, total 376. Estimate: 50 times 8 is 400, close. 68 times 5: 5 times 60 is 300, 5 times 8 is 40, total 340. Estimate: 70 times 5 is 350, close.

The questions for this section are with you now: breaking a two-digit number into tens and units, multiplying each part, adding the partial products, and estimating.

## Subtopic: The Grid Method for Two-Digit by Two-Digit

To multiply 23 by 14, break both numbers: 23 is 20 plus 3, and 14 is 10 plus 4. Now every part of one number must multiply every part of the other, which gives four partial products. The grid method draws a 2 by 2 table with 20 and 3 across the top and 10 and 4 down the side, and fills each cell with a product.

Fill the grid. 10 times 20 is 200. 10 times 3 is 30. 4 times 20 is 80. 4 times 3 is 12. Now add the four cells: 200 plus 30 is 230, plus 80 is 310, plus 12 is 322. The hall has 322 seats.

Four products are needed because each row of seats is 14 long, which is 10 and 4, and there are 23 rows, which is 20 and 3. The 20 rows of 10 seats make 200, the 20 rows of 4 extra seats make 80, the 3 rows of 10 make 30, and the 3 rows of 4 make 12. The grid is a map of the hall.

Estimate: 23 is about 20 and 14 is about 15, so about 300, or round 23 up to 25 and 14 down to 10 for 250, or use 20 times 15 which is 300. The exact answer 322 is close to 300. The most common grid mistake is to find only two products, 20 times 10 and 3 times 4, giving 212; the estimate catches it because 212 is too far from 300.

The questions for this section are with you now: setting up the grid, filling all four cells, adding them, and checking with an estimate.

## Subtopic: Bigger Grids and Choosing the Easier Way

36 times 25 in the grid: 30 and 6 across, 20 and 5 down. 20 times 30 is 600. 20 times 6 is 120. 5 times 30 is 150. 5 times 6 is 30. Add: 600 plus 120 is 720, plus 150 is 870, plus 30 is 900. The wall has 900 bricks. Estimate: 36 is about 40 and 40 times 25 is 1 000, a little high because we rounded up, so 900 is believable.

Sometimes a smarter route exists. 25 times 36 can be seen as 25 times 4 times 9, because 36 is 4 times 9. 25 times 4 is 100, and 100 times 9 is 900. Spotting that 25 times 4 makes 100 saves the whole grid. Any method that is correct and that you can show is acceptable; the grid is the method that always works.

The partial products can also be laid out in a column without the grid lines, listing the four products under each other and adding. This is the expanded column method, and it is a bridge to the compact column method some learners will meet in Grade 5. Whatever the layout, the four products are the same and the sum is the same.

Keep the digits in their columns when adding the partial products. 600, 120, 150 and 30 must be lined up by place value or the hundreds and tens get mixed. Write them under one another, units under units, and add in columns just as in the addition topic.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: multiplying only the units, writing 24 for 24 times 6; the tens part, 6 times 20, is 120 and must be added. Two: filling only two cells of the grid for 23 times 14, giving 212; every part must multiply every part, so there are four cells. Three: writing 6 times 20 as 12 by forgetting the place value; 6 times 2 tens is 12 tens, which is 120. Four: adding the partial products without lining them up; 600, 120, 150 and 30 must sit in their place-value columns.

The questions for this section are with you now: a grid with larger numbers, spotting a shortcut such as 25 times 4, and adding the partial products in columns.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the crates, the hall seats and the brick wall — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Split the Number, Then Add

24 crates of 6 bottles. Split 24 into 20 and 4. 6 times 20 is 120. 6 times 4 is 24. Add them: 144 bottles.

The tens part is easy: 6 times 2 is 12, so 6 times 20 is 120. Just add the zero.

Guess first. 24 is nearly 25, and 25 times 6 is 150. 144 is close, so it is right.

Keep the crates in mind and try the questions for this part: two-digit by one-digit.

## Subtopic: The Grid for Two Numbers

23 rows of 14 seats. Split both numbers: 23 into 20 and 3, and 14 into 10 and 4. Draw a grid with 20 and 3 across and 10 and 4 down.

Fill the four boxes. 10 times 20 is 200. 10 times 3 is 30. 4 times 20 is 80. 4 times 3 is 12.

Add the four boxes: 200 plus 30 plus 80 plus 12 is 322 seats. Guess: 20 times 15 is 300. Close.

Hold the four boxes in mind and try this part's questions: the grid method.

## Subtopic: Big Walls and Smart Shortcuts

36 rows of 25 bricks. Grid: 20 times 30 is 600, 20 times 6 is 120, 5 times 30 is 150, 5 times 6 is 30. Add: 900 bricks.

Shortcut: 36 is 4 times 9. 25 times 4 is 100, and 100 times 9 is 900. Same answer, much faster.

When you add the four products, line them up by place value, units under units, so the hundreds and tens do not mix.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Break each number into tens and units, multiply every part by every part, and add the partial products. A two-digit by one-digit multiplication has two products; a two-digit by two-digit has four, which the grid method lays out so none is forgotten. Line up the products by place value when adding, estimate to check, and take a shortcut such as 25 times 4 equals 100 when you see one.

Hold the brick wall in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: bigger grids and shortcuts.
