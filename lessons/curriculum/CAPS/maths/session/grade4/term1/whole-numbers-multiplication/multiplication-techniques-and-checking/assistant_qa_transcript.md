### subtopic_1 — Estimate and Break Down
**Assistant:** Why does doubling one number and halving the other keep the answer the same? It feels like cheating.

**Tutor:** It feels like cheating because you are changing both numbers, but look at what the two changes do to each other. Multiplication counts groups: 16 times 25 is 16 groups of 25. Halving the 16 means you have only 8 groups. Doubling the 25 means each group now holds 50. You have half as many groups, each twice as big, and the total is untouched. Picture the trays in the bakery: 16 trays of 25 rolls, pushed together in pairs, become 8 trays of 50 rolls with not a single roll added or lost. In symbols, halving multiplies by one half and doubling multiplies by 2, and one half times 2 is 1, so the overall effect is multiplying the product by 1, which changes nothing. The same logic works for any pair of opposite changes. Multiply one number by 3 and divide the other by 3, and the product stays the same, so 15 times 12 is 45 times 4, which is 180. The technique is useful only when it leads somewhere friendly, and it usually does when one number is 5, 25 or 50, because doubling those gives 10, 50 and 100. It is not cheating. It is the same quantity regrouped, and you can always check with division: 400 divided by 25 is 16, as it should be.

### subtopic_2 — Doubling and Halving
**Assistant:** Dividing to check seems harder than the multiplication. Why bother?

**Tutor:** Dividing can feel harder, which is why you should choose the easy direction. If you have found 48 times 5 equals 240, do not divide 240 by 48; divide it by 5, which is a one-digit divisor and takes a few seconds: 200 divided by 5 is 40, 40 divided by 5 is 8, giving 48. Dividing by the smaller or friendlier number nearly always makes the check quick. The reason to bother is that division is a genuinely different operation from multiplication, so it will not repeat the same slip. If you misremembered 8 times 5 as 45 and wrote 245, multiplying again the same way may well repeat the misremembering, but 245 divided by 5 gives 49, not 48, and the error is exposed. Division is also how answers get checked in the real world. A shopkeeper who has worked out the cost of 48 chairs at R5 each checks by asking how many chairs R240 buys at R5 each, and a builder checks the bricks in a wall by dividing the total by the rows. If division is still slow for you, use a second multiplication technique as your check instead, such as doubling and halving after breaking down. The rule is two different roads, and division is simply the most independent road there is.

### subtopic_3 — Checking with Division
**Assistant:** How do I choose between all these techniques without wasting time?

**Tutor:** Spend five seconds looking at the numbers before you do anything, and let their shape choose for you. If one number is 5, 25 or 50 and the other is even, doubling and halving will take you to a 10, 50 or 100 almost immediately: 16 times 25, 14 times 5, 12 times 50 are all this kind. If one number is just below a round number, like 48 or 19 or 99, round it up and compensate: 48 times 5 is 50 times 5 minus 10. If one number is a single digit and the other has no special shape, break the two-digit number into tens and units and use your tables: 47 times 8 is 320 plus 56. If both numbers are two digits and nothing stands out, draw the grid, which always works. Then, whichever technique you used, check with a different one, and make estimation the frame around the whole thing, first to predict and last to compare. You will not pick the fastest route every time at first, and it does not matter, because every correct route gets full marks. What matters is building the habit of looking before calculating. After a term of that, the friendly shapes will announce themselves and the choosing will take no time at all.
