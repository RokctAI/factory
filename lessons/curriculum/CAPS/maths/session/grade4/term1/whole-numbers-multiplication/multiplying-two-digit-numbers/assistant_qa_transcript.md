### subtopic_1 — Two-Digit by One-Digit
**Assistant:** Why do I need four products for 23 times 14? Two numbers, two products, surely.

**Tutor:** It feels like two should be enough, but picture the hall. There are 23 rows and each row has 14 seats. Split the rows into 20 rows and 3 rows, and split each row into 10 seats and 4 seats. Now the hall is in four blocks. The big block is 20 rows of 10 seats, 200. Next to it, still in the first 20 rows, are the 4 extra seats per row, 20 rows of 4, which is 80. Then the last 3 rows have 10 seats each, 30, and their 4 extra seats each, 12. Four blocks, four products, and the hall is only completely counted when all four are added. The two-product mistake, 20 times 10 plus 3 times 4, counts the big block and the tiny corner block and forgets the two long strips, 80 and 30 seats, which is why it gives 212 instead of 322. The number of products is always the number of parts in one number times the number of parts in the other: a two-digit by one-digit sum has 2 times 1, which is 2 products, and a two-digit by two-digit has 2 times 2, which is 4. When you reach three-digit numbers later, you will need 3 times 2, which is 6. The grid is popular precisely because it makes a cell for every product so that none can hide.

### subtopic_2 — The Grid Method for Two-Digit by Two-Digit
**Assistant:** Is the grid method a real method or a baby method? My cousin does it in columns.

**Tutor:** The grid is a real method, and in fact it is the same method as your cousin's columns, laid out so that every step is visible. When your cousin multiplies 23 by 14 in columns, she multiplies 23 by 4 to get 92, then 23 by 10 to get 230, and adds to get 322. Her 92 is the grid's 80 plus 12 added early, and her 230 is the grid's 200 plus 30 added early. The column method just combines two grid cells at a time and uses carrying to do it. That is quicker once you are fluent, but it also hides work, which is why learners who go to columns too soon often multiply the wrong digits or lose a carry and cannot find the mistake. The grid shows all four cells, so an error is easy to spot and fix. Many adults who struggled with columns at school find that the grid finally makes multiplication make sense. CAPS wants you to be able to multiply correctly and explain what you did, and the grid does both. Use it now, make it fast and reliable, and when you move to columns in Grade 5 or 6 you will know what every line means. A method you understand is never a baby method. The baby method is the one you follow without knowing why.

### subtopic_3 — Bigger Grids and Choosing the Easier Way
**Assistant:** How do I spot shortcuts like 25 times 4? I would never have seen that.

**Tutor:** You spot them by knowing a few friendly pairs and glancing for them before you start. The pairs that make 100 are the most valuable: 25 times 4, 50 times 2 and 20 times 5. The pairs that make 10 are next: 2 times 5. Whenever one of the numbers you are multiplying is 25, 50 or 5, ask whether the other number contains a 4, a 2 or another 5 as a factor. In 36 times 25, 36 is 4 times 9, so the 4 pairs with the 25 to make 100, leaving 100 times 9. In 15 times 12, 12 is 2 times 6, and 15 times 2 is 30, leaving 30 times 6, which is 180. Doubling and halving is another shortcut in the same family: 25 times 36 is the same as 50 times 18, which is the same as 100 times 9. Each time you double one number and halve the other, the answer stays the same, and you steer towards a 100. You will not see these shortcuts the first time, and that is fine, because the grid always works. But each time you finish a grid, spend ten seconds asking whether there was a quicker road. After a few weeks of that habit, the friendly pairs jump out at you before you have drawn a single line.
