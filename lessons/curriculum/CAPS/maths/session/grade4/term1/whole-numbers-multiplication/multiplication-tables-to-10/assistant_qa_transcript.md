### subtopic_1 — Groups, Arrays and the Easy Tables
**Assistant:** Why must I memorise tables if I can always work them out by adding?

**Tutor:** You can work every fact out by adding, and in the beginning you should, because that is how you learn what the fact means. But adding is slow, and slowness is a problem when the fact is only a small step inside a bigger calculation. When you multiply 47 by 8 next week, you will need 7 times 8 and 4 times 8 in passing, and if each one takes you fifteen seconds of adding, you will lose track of the carrying and the columns while you do it. A fact you know instantly costs no attention, so all your attention stays on the method. The same is true in division, fractions, area, ratio and almost every topic ahead: the tables are the alphabet of those subjects, and nobody reads fluently while still sounding out letters. The good news is that the amount to memorise is small. The commutative property halves the hundred facts to 55, the 1, 2, 5 and 10 tables are patterns rather than memory work, and the 4, 8, 3 and 6 tables can be built by doubling. What is left is a short list, 6 times 7, 7 times 8, 6 times 8, 7 times 7, 8 times 8, 6 times 9, 9 times 9 and a few others, that a few minutes a day will fix in a fortnight. Memorise those, keep the patterns for the rest, and work them out by adding only when you are checking.

### subtopic_2 — The 9 Times Table and the Hard Facts
**Assistant:** Why does the 9 times finger trick work? It feels like magic.

**Tutor:** It works because 9 is one less than 10, and the trick is really the distributive property dressed up. 9 times 7 is 10 times 7 minus 1 times 7, which is 70 minus 7. Now look at what folding the seventh finger does. The six fingers on the left stand for six tens, 60, which is 70 with one ten removed. The three fingers on the right are the units. Why three? Because taking 7 from 70 is the same as taking 10 and giving 3 back, and 3 is 10 minus 7, which is also the number of fingers remaining to the right of the folded one. So the fingers are keeping track of the subtraction 70 minus 7 for you: one ten comes off, and the leftover units fill in. The same reasoning explains why the digits of every 9 times answer add to 9. Each time you go up one in the table, you add 10 and take away 1, so the tens digit rises by one and the units digit falls by one, and their total never changes. Start at 9 times 1 equals 9, where the digits are 0 and 9 and add to 9, and the total stays 9 all the way to 81. Magic tricks stop being magic when you see the mechanism, and the mechanism here is just that 9 is 10 minus 1.

### subtopic_3 — Using Tables in Problems
**Assistant:** I know my tables but I freeze in a timed test. What should I do?

**Tutor:** Freezing in a timed test is very common and it is not a sign that you do not know the facts; it is a sign that nerves are getting between you and them. Three things help. First, practise under the same conditions you will be tested in. If you only ever practise slowly and comfortably, speed will feel foreign. Do a two-minute burst each day with a timer, and the timer stops being frightening when it is familiar. Second, have a fallback for every fact so that a blank never stops you. If 7 times 8 vanishes, you know that 7 times 10 is 70 and you take off two sevens, 70 minus 14, which is 56. The fallback takes three seconds and it keeps you moving, which matters more than any single answer. Third, when you freeze on a fact, skip it, finish the others and come back. The fact usually arrives while you are busy with something else, because the panic has passed. On the day, breathe out slowly before you start, and remember that the test is checking facts you have already shown you know. Over time, as the facts become instant, the fear fades, because there is nothing left to freeze about. Fluency is the cure for nerves, and the daily two-minute burst is how fluency is built.
