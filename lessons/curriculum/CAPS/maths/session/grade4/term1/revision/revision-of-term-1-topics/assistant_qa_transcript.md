### subtopic_1 — Numbers: Counting, Place Value and Rounding
**Assistant:** There is so much in Term 1. What should I revise first?

**Tutor:** Start with the skills that every other skill depends on, because fixing those fixes several topics at once. Place value comes first: if you can say what each digit in 2 468 is worth, then column addition, column subtraction, rounding and the grid method all make sense, because each of them is place value in action. Second, the multiplication tables to 10, because two-digit multiplication, factors, multiples and the division you will meet in Term 2 all lean on them, and a shaky table makes every one of those slow. Third, the checking habits, estimation and inverse operations, because they turn a shaky answer into a confident one and they cost almost no time. Once those three are solid, go through the topics in order and do two or three problems from each, not twenty from one. For each problem, write the number sentence, estimate, calculate and check, so that you are practising the routine as well as the content. Keep a page of the facts you got wrong and look at it the night before the test. And do not revise the things you already find easy for longer than a minute each; the marks you can still gain are in the topics that feel uncomfortable, which is exactly where most learners avoid spending time.

### subtopic_2 — Sentences, Properties, Addition and Subtraction
**Assistant:** Why does the test ask me to show working? The answer is what matters.

**Tutor:** The answer matters, but the working is where most of the marks live, and for a good reason. A test is trying to find out what you understand, and a bare answer tells the marker very little. If you write 4 263 for 2 468 plus 1 795, the marker knows you got it right, but not whether you understood columns and carrying or copied a neighbour or guessed luckily. If you write the columns with the carries shown, the marker can see your method and give you marks for it even if a single digit slipped. In fact, a learner who shows a correct method and makes one small slip usually scores more than a learner who writes only a wrong answer, because the method marks are earned and only the final mark is lost. Working also helps you. Writing the number sentence stops you doing the wrong operation. Writing the estimate catches a slipped digit. Writing the check proves the answer to you before the marker ever sees it. In life, working is how you convince other people: a shopkeeper, a builder or an accountant who says trust me, the answer is 840 gets far less trust than one who shows 24 times 35 broken into 600, 100, 120 and 20. Show your thinking, and the marks and the trust both follow.

### subtopic_3 — Multiplication, Multiples and Factors
**Assistant:** I always run out of time in tests. How do I manage it?

**Tutor:** Running out of time usually comes from spending too long on one hard question while easier marks sit unanswered further on, so the first fix is to read the whole paper before you start and notice which questions are quick. Do those first. They settle your nerves and bank marks early. Then come back to the longer problems with the time that is left. For any single question, give it a fair try, and if you are stuck after a minute or two, write what you can, such as the number sentence and an estimate, mark it to come back to, and move on. Partial working earns partial marks; a blank earns nothing. Second, use the techniques that are fast for you. If the grid is reliable but slow, and doubling and halving is quick for 16 times 25, use the quick one and check with the grid only if time allows. Third, do not redo work that an estimate has already confirmed. Estimation takes five seconds and tells you whether a column answer is in the right region; if it is, move on rather than re-adding every column. Finally, practise at home with a clock, doing a page of mixed problems in a fixed time, because speed under a clock is a skill that improves with rehearsal just like the tables did. Knowing that you have done it before makes the test clock far less frightening.
