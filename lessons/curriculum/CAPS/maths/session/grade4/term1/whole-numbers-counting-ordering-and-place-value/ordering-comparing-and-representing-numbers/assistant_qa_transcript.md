### subtopic_1 — Comparing from the Left
**Assistant:** Why do we compare from the left? Units are the first digits we learn.

**Tutor:** We compare from the left because the leftmost digit is worth the most, and a bigger worth in one place can never be cancelled by smaller places. Take 1 850 and 1 805. The 5 tens in 1 850 are worth 50. The 5 units in 1 805 are worth only 5. Even if 1 805 had 9 units it would still be only 1 809, which is less than 1 850, because nine units can never make up for five missing tens. That is the whole reason place value works: each place is worth ten of the place to its right, so one step up on the left outweighs anything you could pile into the places on the right. The same idea is why a four-digit number always beats a three-digit number. 1 000 has one thousand, and 999 has none, and 9 hundreds plus 9 tens plus 9 units is still one short of a thousand. It helps to think of the mountains. If one mountain is 3 thousand and something metres and another is 2 thousand and something, you do not need to know the hundreds to say which is higher. You only look at the hundreds when the thousands tie, and you only look at the tens when the hundreds tie too. Comparing from the left is really a way of asking the biggest question first.

### subtopic_2 — Ordering and Representing Numbers
**Assistant:** Do we really need both ascending and descending order? Smallest to biggest seems enough.

**Tutor:** Both orders are used all the time, and which one you want depends on what you are looking for. A shop that wants to show its cheapest bicycles first puts prices in ascending order, because the customer scanning the list wants small numbers at the top. A sports results board puts the highest score first, in descending order, because the winner is what people want to see. Weather reports list the hottest towns at the top on a hot day, which is descending, and the coldest at the top on a cold day, which is ascending for the same list of temperatures. The two words are worth learning because teachers and test papers use them without explaining, and ascending and descending can sound alike. A memory hook is that ascending shares its start with the word ascend, which means to climb, so the numbers climb as you read; descending shares its start with descend, which means to go down, so the numbers fall as you read. Either way the method is the same: compare from the left, decide which is bigger, and then place the numbers in the direction the question asks for. If you can produce one order, you can produce the other by reading your list backwards.

### subtopic_3 — Odd and Even to 1 000
**Assistant:** Why does the last digit decide odd or even? Why not the first digit?

**Tutor:** Because every place except the units is already an even amount. Ten is even, so any number of tens is even: 10, 20, 30 and so on can all be split into two equal groups. A hundred is ten tens, so hundreds are even, and a thousand is ten hundreds, so thousands are even too. That means when you write a number in expanded form, such as 1 085 equals 1 000 plus 80 plus 5, every part except the last one is even. Even amounts added together stay even. So whether the whole number is odd or even is decided entirely by the units digit, the only part that might be odd. The 5 at the end of 1 085 cannot be shared into two equal groups, so 1 085 is odd. The first digit tells you nothing about this. 1 000, 3 000 and 9 000 are all even although they start with 1, 3 and 9, because they are all whole thousands. Children sometimes test odd and even by adding all the digits, perhaps because that trick works for the 3 times table. It does not work here. 1 085 has digits adding to 14, which is even, yet 1 085 is odd. Trust only the last digit, and you will never be wrong.
