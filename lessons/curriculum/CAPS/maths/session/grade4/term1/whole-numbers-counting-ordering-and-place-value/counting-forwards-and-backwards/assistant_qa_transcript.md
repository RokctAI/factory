### subtopic_1 — Counting in Steps from Any Start
**Assistant:** Why do we bother counting in 25s? Nobody counts sweets in 25s.

**Tutor:** Nobody counts sweets in 25s, but almost everyone counts money in 25s and 50s, because our coins and notes are built that way. A taxi fare of R25, a bus ticket of R50, a R100 note: when a conductor takes money from twelve passengers he is not adding one rand at a time, he is jumping 25, 50, 75, 100 and so on, and the pattern in the endings lets him do it without stopping to think. The same skill appears on a clock, where a quarter of an hour is 15 minutes and the minute hand sweeps 15, 30, 45, 60, and in sport, where a running track is marked every 25 or 50 metres. Counting in steps is really practice for multiplication: when you count 25, 50, 75, 100, 125, 150 you are finding six times 25 without knowing it. Later, when you meet 6 times 25 in a sum, your hand will already know the answer is 150. So the step counting we do now is building the tables you will use for the rest of your life, and 25s and 50s are the money steps that South Africans use every day.

### subtopic_2 — Counting Backwards
**Assistant:** Counting backwards past a thousand always trips me up. Is there a trick?

**Tutor:** There is a trick, and it is to listen for the hundreds digit reaching zero. When you count back in 100s and the hundreds digit is 0, as in 3 050, the next jump has to take a hundred out of the thousands. Think of 3 050 as 30 hundreds and 50. Take away one hundred and you have 29 hundreds and 50, which is 2 950. Said another way, the thousands digit goes down by one and the hundreds digit becomes 9, the biggest it can be, because you have just broken a thousand into ten hundreds and spent one of them. The same thing happens when you count back in 10s and the tens digit is 0: 4 307 back 10 is 4 297, because the hundreds digit drops to 2 and the tens digit becomes 9. A quick way to check any backwards count is to turn round and count forwards from where you landed. If 2 950 plus 100 gives 3 050, your backwards step was right. If you had written 3 950, counting forwards would give 4 050, which is nowhere near where you started, so the mistake shows itself immediately.

### subtopic_3 — Finding the Rule and the Missing Numbers
**Assistant:** What if two neighbours in a sequence give different steps? Is the question wrong?

**Tutor:** Not necessarily. It means the sequence is not a simple counting-in-steps sequence, and that is a signal to look more carefully rather than to give up. Some sequences grow by a changing amount, such as 1, 2, 4, 7, 11, where the gaps are 1, 2, 3, 4. We will meet those later in the year under numeric patterns. Other times a different step shows up because one of the numbers has been copied wrongly, and the good news is that checking two pairs is exactly how you catch that. Suppose you read 840, 843, 847, 849. The first gap is 3, the second is 4, the third is 2. Three different gaps in a row is a strong sign that 847 is a slip for 846. For the counting sequences in this lesson, the rule is that every gap must be the same, so the honest working is to write the gaps down above the sequence, look along the row of gaps, and only then fill in the blanks. That habit of checking on more than one pair is not only for Grade 4: scientists and accountants check the same way, by looking for a difference that does not fit.
