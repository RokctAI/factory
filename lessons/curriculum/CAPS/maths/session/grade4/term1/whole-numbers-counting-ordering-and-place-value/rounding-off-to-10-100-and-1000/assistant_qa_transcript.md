### subtopic_1 — Rounding to the Nearest 10
**Assistant:** Why does 5 round up? It is exactly in the middle.

**Tutor:** You are right that 5 is exactly in the middle, and that is why there has to be a rule rather than a measurement. 1 285 is 5 away from 1 280 and 5 away from 1 290, so neither ten is closer. Someone had to decide, and the agreed rule in school mathematics and in most everyday work is that a 5 rounds up. There is a sensible reason behind the choice. The digits 0, 1, 2, 3, 4 are five digits that round down, and 5, 6, 7, 8, 9 are five digits that round up, so the rule splits the ten possible digits evenly instead of giving six to one side and four to the other. The rule also keeps things simple: you look at one digit and act, with no second look at anything else. You should know that engineers and scientists sometimes use a different tie-breaking rule, rounding a 5 to whichever neighbour is even, so that over thousands of measurements the ups and downs balance out. That is not used in CAPS and will not appear in your tests. For Grade 4, and for shops, sports reports and newspapers, 5 goes up: 1 285 rounds to 1 290, R125 rounds to R130, and a crowd of 2 500 reported to the nearest thousand is 3 000.

### subtopic_2 — Rounding to the Nearest 100 and 1 000
**Assistant:** Rounding changes the number. Isn't that cheating?

**Tutor:** It would be cheating if you pretended the rounded number was the exact one, but that is not how rounding is used. Rounding is a tool for answering a different question: not what is the number, but about how big is the number. A stadium manager who hears that 3 468 people attended does not need the 68; the question that matters is whether the ground was about half full, and 3 500 answers that instantly. The honest way to use rounding has two parts. First, say that you have rounded, with a word like about or approximately, so that nobody mistakes 3 500 for the true count. Second, keep the exact number for anything that must be exact, such as the money in the till or the marks on a test. Rounding is also how you protect yourself from mistakes in exact work. If you calculate 4 times R1 285 and write R51 400, the rounded estimate of R5 200 shouts that you have slipped a digit. Rounding is not changing the truth; it is choosing how much detail you need, and then checking the detail against the big picture. Mathematicians, accountants and builders all round every day, and the skill is knowing when the approximation is good enough and when it is not.

### subtopic_3 — Rounding to Estimate
**Assistant:** How do I know which place to round to if the question doesn't say?

**Tutor:** When the question does say, obey it exactly, because a question asking for the nearest 100 marks 3 470 wrong even though it is a correct rounding to the nearest 10. When the question does not say, you choose, and the choice depends on how exact you need to be. Ask yourself what the number is for. If you are checking that an answer to an addition sum is roughly right, rounding each number to the nearest 100 is usually enough, because the estimate only has to show that the answer is in the right region. If you are deciding how much fuel to buy for a 1 753 kilometre drive, rounding to the nearest 100 kilometres gives 1 800, which is close enough to plan on; rounding to the nearest 1 000 gives 2 000, which is 247 kilometres too many, a difference that could pay for a night's accommodation. If you are describing a crowd to a friend, the nearest 500 or 1 000 is fine. A useful habit is to round to the place that leaves two digits that are not zero: 3 468 becomes 3 500, 1 753 becomes 1 800, 285 becomes 290. That keeps the estimate simple to work with while staying close to the real number. And always say about, so your listener knows you have rounded.
