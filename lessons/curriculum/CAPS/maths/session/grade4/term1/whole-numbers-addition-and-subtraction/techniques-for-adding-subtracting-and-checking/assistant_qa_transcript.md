### subtopic_1 — Estimate First, Then Break Down
**Assistant:** If column addition always works, why learn all these other techniques?

**Tutor:** Because column addition is a written method, and most of the adding and subtracting you will do in life happens without paper. Standing at a till, you want to know whether R1 248 plus R397 is going to be more than the R1 600 in your pocket, and you need the answer before the cashier does. Rounding 397 to 400 and compensating gives 1 645 in your head in a few seconds. The mental techniques are also what make column work safe, because they give you an independent check. A learner who only knows columns and makes a slip has no way of noticing. A learner who also estimates and compensates will see that the column answer does not match and look again. There is a third reason. The techniques are how numbers actually behave. Breaking down shows place value in action. Building up shows that subtraction is the distance between two numbers. Compensation shows that a sum stays the same when you add to one part and take the same from the other. These are the ideas you will need for fractions, decimals and algebra, and learning them now on friendly whole numbers is far easier than meeting them for the first time on harder ones. Columns give you an answer. The techniques give you understanding and a way to check.

### subtopic_2 — Number Lines and Rounding with Compensation
**Assistant:** How do I know which technique to pick? There are so many.

**Tutor:** Look at the numbers and let them choose. If one number is just below a round number, like 397 or 1 875, rounding and compensating is almost always the quickest, because the correction is tiny. If both numbers are messy, like 1 248 and 397, breaking down into parts is reliable, because every part is a single-digit fact. If you are subtracting and the two numbers are close together, like 2 000 and 1 875, building up from the smaller one is fastest, because the gap is small and you can see it. If you cannot see any pattern, columns always work. A practical habit is to spend five seconds looking for a round number nearby before you start; that one glance picks the method most of the time. You do not need to be loyal to a favourite technique. Strong calculators switch between them without noticing, the way a good cook reaches for a different knife for different jobs. And remember that the second technique, the check, should be a different one from the first. If you added by breaking down, check by compensating. Over the term, try every technique on every sum until each one feels natural; after that, picking becomes automatic.

### subtopic_3 — Inverse Operations as a Check
**Assistant:** When I check and get a different answer, how do I know which one is wrong?

**Tutor:** Start by being glad: the check has just saved you from handing in a wrong answer, and that is exactly what it is for. Then use the estimate as the judge. Suppose you got 1 645 by breaking down and 1 655 by compensating for 1 248 plus 397. The estimate is about 1 600, which does not separate them, so look for the kind of mistake each method is prone to. Compensation errors are almost always in the correction: adding 3 instead of subtracting it gives 1 651, and forgetting it altogether gives 1 648, so 1 655 does not fit a compensation slip and probably came from somewhere else. Breaking-down errors are usually in combining the parts, so recount: 1 000 plus 500 is 1 500, plus 130 is 1 630, plus 15 is 1 645. That looks solid. Now redo the compensation slowly: 1 248 plus 400 is 1 648, minus 3 is 1 645. The second answer was the slip. If neither method will confess, use a third, such as columns or a number line, and trust the answer that two methods agree on. What you must never do is pick the answer you like the look of. Disagreement is information, and the way to use it is to slow down and let the methods argue until one of them wins.
