# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the techniques used to perform and check written and mental addition and subtraction: estimation, building up and breaking down numbers, the number line, rounding off and compensating, and addition and subtraction as inverse operations. The anchor is a supermarket bill of R1 248 plus R397 in East London, a bus trip of 2 650 kilometres of which 1 875 have been driven, and a savings jar that grows from R1 395 to R2 000. The governing idea is that any addition or subtraction can be reshaped into easier steps without changing the answer, and that at least two techniques should be used on every calculation, one to work it out and one to check. Choosing a technique, showing the steps and checking by a second method are examinable.

## Subtopic: Estimate First, Then Break Down

Before any calculation, estimate. R1 248 plus R397: round to R1 200 plus R400, about R1 600. Now the exact answer must land near R1 600, and an answer of R1 245 or R5 218 would be caught at once. Estimation takes five seconds and protects every sum you do.

Breaking down means splitting numbers into their place-value parts and adding the parts. 1 248 is 1 000 plus 200 plus 40 plus 8, and 397 is 300 plus 90 plus 7. Add the like parts: 1 000 plus 500 plus 130 plus 15. Then combine: 1 000 plus 500 is 1 500, plus 130 is 1 630, plus 15 is 1 645. The bill is R1 645, which agrees with the estimate.

Building up is the same idea used for subtraction. For 2 650 minus 1 875, build up from 1 875 to 2 650 in easy jumps: 1 875 plus 25 is 1 900, plus 100 is 2 000, plus 650 is 2 650. The jumps were 25, 100 and 650, which total 775. The bus has 775 kilometres to go.

Breaking down also works for subtraction by taking away in parts. 2 650 minus 1 875: take away 1 000 to get 1 650, take away 800 to get 850, take away 70 to get 780, take away 5 to get 775. Each part is simple, and the answer matches the building-up method.

The questions for this section are with you now: estimating before calculating, breaking numbers into place-value parts, and building up from the smaller number.

## Subtopic: Number Lines and Rounding with Compensation

A number line turns a sum into jumps you can see. For 1 248 plus 397, start at 1 248 and jump 300 to 1 548, then 90 to 1 638, then 7 to 1 645. For subtraction jump backwards. The empty number line, with only the numbers you need marked, is the most useful tool for showing your thinking.

Rounding and compensating means changing one number to a round number, calculating, and then correcting. 397 is 3 less than 400. So 1 248 plus 397 is 1 248 plus 400 minus 3. That is 1 648 minus 3, which is 1 645. The compensation undoes the rounding.

Compensation in subtraction needs care with direction. 2 650 minus 1 875: round 1 875 up to 1 900, which is 25 more than 1 875. 2 650 minus 1 900 is 750. But you took away 25 too much, so give it back: 750 plus 25 is 775. Taking away more than you should means the answer must be increased.

The rule for compensating: in addition, if you added too much, subtract the extra; in subtraction, if you took away too much, add the extra back. Saying the correction out loud, I took away 25 too many, so add 25, prevents the most common mistake, which is correcting in the wrong direction.

The questions for this section are with you now: jumping along a number line, rounding one number and compensating, and getting the direction of the compensation right.

## Subtopic: Inverse Operations as a Check

Addition and subtraction undo each other. If 1 248 plus 397 is 1 645, then 1 645 minus 397 must be 1 248, and 1 645 minus 1 248 must be 397. Any one of these can check the others. The savings jar held R1 395 and now holds R2 000. The amount added is 2 000 minus 1 395, which is 605. Check by adding: 1 395 plus 605 is 2 000.

The strongest check uses a different method from the one that produced the answer. If you added in columns, check by rounding and compensating. If you subtracted by building up, check by adding the answer back in columns. Two different roads reaching the same place is strong evidence that the place is right. Doing the same method twice can repeat the same mistake twice.

The CAPS rule is to use any two techniques for every calculation. One is for working it out, the other for checking. For 2 650 minus 1 875, building up gave 775 and compensation gave 775. For 1 248 plus 397, breaking down gave 1 645 and compensation gave 1 645. Both calculations are now trusted.

When two techniques disagree, do not guess which is right. Estimate, and the estimate will usually side with one. If both are far from the estimate, both are wrong. Then redo the calculation slowly in columns. Disagreement is not a failure; it is the check doing its job.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: skipping the estimate; without it a slipped digit like 5 218 for 1 645 goes unnoticed. Two: compensating in the wrong direction, giving 750 minus 25 for 2 650 minus 1 875; you took away 25 too many, so add 25 to get 775. Three: checking with the same method you used to calculate; a repeated method can repeat the mistake. Four: treating a disagreement between two methods as a reason to guess; estimate, then redo the calculation in columns.

The questions for this section are with you now: using subtraction to check addition and addition to check subtraction, choosing two different techniques, and what to do when they disagree.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the supermarket bill, the bus trip and the savings jar — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Guess, Then Break It Up

Always guess first. R1 248 plus R397 is about R1 200 plus R400, about R1 600. Your answer must land near there.

Break the numbers into parts. 1 248 is 1 000, 200, 40 and 8. 397 is 300, 90 and 7. Add the parts: 1 000, then 500, then 130, then 15. Together 1 645.

For take away, build up. From 1 875 to 2 650: jump 25 to 1 900, jump 100 to 2 000, jump 650 to 2 650. Add the jumps: 775.

Keep the shopping bill in mind and try the questions for this part: estimating, breaking down and building up.

## Subtopic: Round It, Then Fix It

397 is nearly 400. So work out 1 248 plus 400, which is 1 648, then take off the extra 3: 1 645.

1 875 is nearly 1 900. 2 650 minus 1 900 is 750. But you took away 25 too many, so give 25 back: 775.

Say the fix out loud. Added too much? Take it off. Took away too much? Add it back.

Hold the round-and-fix idea in mind and try this part's questions: rounding and compensating.

## Subtopic: Check It the Other Way

Adding and taking away undo each other. 1 248 plus 397 is 1 645, so 1 645 minus 397 must be 1 248. If it is, the answer is right.

The jar grew from R1 395 to R2 000. Added: 2 000 minus 1 395 is 605. Check: 1 395 plus 605 is 2 000. Good.

Always use two techniques, one to find and one to check. If they disagree, estimate to see which one is sensible.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Estimate before every calculation. Break numbers into place-value parts, or build up from the smaller number in easy jumps, or jump along a number line. Round one number to make the sum easy and then compensate, adding back what you took away too much or removing what you added too much. Addition and subtraction undo each other, so use one to check the other, and always use two different techniques.

Hold the two-technique rule in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: checking with inverse operations.
