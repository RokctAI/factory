### subtopic_1 — Column Addition with Carrying
**Assistant:** Why do we start from the units? Reading goes left to right.

**Tutor:** Reading does go left to right, and in fact there are people who add from the left, but the units-first order is the one that makes carrying painless. Think about what a carry is. When the units add to 13, the 1 ten must go into the tens column before that column is totalled, otherwise you would have to go back and change a digit you had already written. Starting on the right means every carry arrives before you need it. If you start on the left, you write the thousands digit, then discover a carry from the hundreds and have to rub it out. Try it with 2 468 plus 1 795. Left first: 2 plus 1 is 3, write 3. Then 4 plus 7 is 11, so the thousands should have been 4, go back and fix it. Then 6 plus 9 is 15, so the hundreds should have been 2, fix again. Then 8 plus 5 is 13, so the tens should have been 6. Every column needed a correction. Units first, you write 3, 6, 2, 4 and never look back. Mental mathematicians often do start from the left because it gives a good estimate quickly, and that is a fine habit for checking. But for written column work, units first is the order that lets you write each digit once and be done.

### subtopic_2 — Column Subtraction with Exchanging
**Assistant:** What is actually happening when I borrow? It feels like the numbers are being changed.

**Tutor:** The numbers are being rewritten, not changed, and that difference is the whole point. 7 162 means 7 thousands, 1 hundred, 6 tens and 2 units. When you exchange a ten for units, you are saying the same amount as 7 thousands, 1 hundred, 5 tens and 12 units. Count it: 5 tens are 50, plus 12 is 62, exactly what 6 tens and 2 units were worth. Nothing has been added or taken away; one bundle of ten has been untied so that there are enough loose units to subtract 4 from. Money makes this concrete. Imagine R7 162 in notes and coins and you must pay R5 324. You have two R1 coins and need to hand over four. You cannot, so you change one R10 note into ten R1 coins. Now you have twelve coins and one fewer R10 note, and the pile is still worth R7 162. The word borrow is a little misleading because nothing is ever paid back; the better word, which CAPS uses, is exchange. Carrying in addition is the same exchange in the other direction: thirteen R1 coins are swapped for one R10 note and three coins. Once you see every carry and exchange as changing money without changing its value, the method stops being a set of rules and becomes something you could have invented.

### subtopic_3 — Subtracting Across Zeros
**Assistant:** Is the subtract-one trick for zeros allowed in a test, or must I show the exchanges?

**Tutor:** Any correct method that you can show is allowed, and the subtract-one trick is a correct method with a clear explanation: 4 000 minus 2 637 and 3 999 minus 2 637 differ by exactly 1, so you can do the easy one and adjust. What a marker needs to see is enough working to follow your thinking. Write 3 999 minus 2 637 equals 1 362 in columns, then write 1 362 plus 1 equals 1 363, and your reasoning is complete. If a question says use the column method, show the exchanges through the zeros as well, because that is the skill being tested; in that case the trick becomes your check rather than your method, and having two routes to the same answer is the strongest check there is. The trick belongs to a family of methods called compensation, which you will meet again in this term under rounding and compensating. The idea is always the same: change a number to something easier, do the calculation, then undo the change. Taking 1 from 4 000 is the smallest possible change and it removes every exchange. Learn both methods well. The column method works for every subtraction, and the trick is a gift for the special case where the top number is full of zeros.
