### subtopic_1 — Money Problems
**Assistant:** Why does the tuck shop problem count as maths? It is just reading.

**Tutor:** It is reading and maths together, and that combination is what maths is for. Nobody will ever hand you a page of bare sums in a shop, a bank or a workshop; the numbers always arrive wrapped in a situation, and the skill of unwrapping them is as important as the arithmetic. In the tuck shop problem the arithmetic is 2 375 plus 1 860, which most of the class can do. The maths that is really being tested is deciding that the operation is addition, because the question asks for both days together, and noticing that a second question, how much more, asks for a difference between the same two numbers. Those are mathematical decisions about structure, and they are exactly what number sentences are for. The reading matters in another way too. A learner who calculates R4 235 perfectly but writes it as the answer to how much more has read the question wrongly, and the mark is lost even though every digit was right. So treat the words as part of the problem, not as decoration around it. Underline the amounts, circle the question, and write the number sentence before any calculating. When the story and the sentence match, the arithmetic is the easy part.

### subtopic_2 — Distance and Length Problems
**Assistant:** Why do I have to write metres or litres after the number? The number is the answer.

**Tutor:** The number without its unit is not an answer; it is only part of one. If a farmer asks how much more wire she needs and you say 385, she still has to ask you 385 what. Metres makes sense, centimetres would be a tiny piece of wire, kilometres would fence the whole province. The unit tells the reader what kind of thing has been counted, and the same number means wildly different amounts in different units. Writing the unit also protects you while you work. If you keep writing m after every number in the fence problem, you will notice the moment a kilometre figure sneaks in, because km next to m looks wrong, and that is your cue to convert before adding. Money is the same: R515 is an amount, 515 is just a number, and in a shop the difference between R515 and 515 cents is real. There is a longer-term reason as well. In science from Grade 7 onwards, every measurement carries a unit and answers without units are marked wrong, so the habit you build now will be worth marks for years. Think of the unit as the last word of a sentence. Without it, the sentence trails off, and the reader cannot tell what you meant.

### subtopic_3 — Capacity, Mass and the Four-Step Routine
**Assistant:** The story check seems obvious. When would an answer ever be impossible?

**Tutor:** More often than you would think, and almost always because of a wrong operation rather than a wrong digit. Suppose a learner reads the tank problem and adds instead of subtracting, getting 8 245 litres. Every digit of that addition is correct, and a learner who does not check against the story will write it down and move on. But a 5 000 litre tank cannot need 8 245 litres to fill it, so the story rejects the answer at once and the learner looks again. The same happens with change. Adding R50 and R32 gives R82 of change for a R32 purchase, which no shopkeeper would ever hand over. Or with the marathon: adding 42 and 27 gives a water point 69 kilometres from the finish of a 42 kilometre race. In each case the arithmetic is fine and the sentence was wrong, and only the story reveals it. Even when an operation is right, the story can catch a slipped digit. If the tuck shop difference came out as R5 150 instead of R515, you would notice that Monday cannot have taken R5 150 more than Tuesday when Monday only took R2 375 in total. The story check takes two seconds: ask whether a sensible shopkeeper, runner or farmer would believe your number. If not, it is wrong, however neat the working.
