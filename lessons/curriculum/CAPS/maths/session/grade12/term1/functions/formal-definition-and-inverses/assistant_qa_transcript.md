**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First one, and it comes up a lot: what is the actual difference between the vertical line test and the horizontal line test? Half of us mix them up in the exam.

**Tutor:** They ask two different questions about two different directions of the pairing. The vertical line test asks whether the graph in front of you is a function at all: a vertical line is one input, so if any vertical line cuts the graph twice, one input has two outputs and the relation is not a function. The horizontal line test asks about the future — whether the INVERSE will be a function: a horizontal line is one output, so if any horizontal line cuts the original graph twice, two inputs share that output, and when you reverse the pairings that shared output becomes an input with two answers. The memory hook is that each test interrogates the graph you are looking at, but about different directions of traffic. Vertical: is this a function? Horizontal: will its reverse be a function? The parabola y equals x squared passes vertical and fails horizontal — it is a genuine function whose inverse relation is not, which is exactly why the restriction question exists. If you keep the two tests attached to those two questions, the mix-up disappears.

***

**Mandy:** Next: when we invert y equals 2 x squared and get y equals plus or minus the square root of x over 2, how do we know which sign to keep? Some of us just pick plus because it feels safer.

**Tutor:** The sign is never a guess — it is dictated by the domain you were given, because the domain of the original becomes the range of the inverse. Suppose the original came with domain x less than or equal to 0, the left arm of the parabola. Every input the original ever accepted was negative or zero. When the machine reverses, those old inputs become the new outputs, so the inverse may only ever produce numbers that are negative or zero — and that forces the minus sign: y equals negative square root of x over 2. Choose plus and your inverse would output positive numbers the original never used as inputs, and the pairings would not reverse. There is a concrete check that settles it in seconds: take one real point of the original, say minus 2 going to 8. The inverse must send 8 back to minus 2. Negative square root of eight over two is negative square root of four, which is minus 2 — correct. The plus version would have sent 8 to positive 2, a code the machine never had. Old domain, new range, and one test point: that is the whole decision.

**Mandy:** Third one, from the homework: why does the inverse of a straight line with gradient 3 have gradient one third? A few of us expected minus 3.

**Tutor:** Minus 3 is the trap answer, because it belongs to a different topic — perpendicular lines have gradients that multiply to minus 1, but an inverse is not a perpendicular, it is a reflection in the diagonal y equals x. Think about what gradient means: rise over run. The line y equals 3 x minus 6 climbs 3 units up for every 1 unit across. Reflecting in y equals x swaps the axes, so what used to be rise becomes run and what used to be run becomes rise. Three up for one across becomes one up for three across — gradient one third, the reciprocal, with the SAME sign. You can confirm it from the algebra with no picture at all: swapping and solving y equals 3 x minus 6 gives y equals one third x plus 2, and the one third is sitting right there in front of the x. A falling line keeps falling after inversion: gradient minus 2 inverts to gradient minus a half. So the rule to store is reciprocal, not negative — and if an inverse of a rising line ever comes out falling, a sign slipped somewhere in the solving.

**Mandy:** Last one: the question said, restrict the domain of y equals x squared so that the inverse is a function. Two people restricted to x greater than or equal to 0 and two restricted to x less than or equal to 0, and both got the marks. How can both be right?

**Tutor:** Because the task was never to find THE restriction — it was to make the function one-to-one, and the parabola offers two equally good ways to do that. The problem with y equals x squared is that its two arms duplicate outputs: 3 and minus 3 both produce 9. Any restriction that keeps only one arm removes every duplicate. Keep the right arm, x greater than or equal to 0, and the inverse is y equals the positive square root of x. Keep the left arm, x less than or equal to 0, and the inverse is y equals the negative square root of x. Both restricted originals pass the horizontal line test, both inverses are honest functions, and both earn full marks — they are simply different functions, each reversing its own half of the parabola. What loses marks is a restriction that still spans the turning point, like minus 1 less than or equal to x less than or equal to 1, because duplicates survive on both sides of zero. And when a question specifies the restriction for you, obey it exactly, because it silently fixes the sign of the root in your answer. The turning point is the hinge: cut there, keep one side, and the inverse behaves.
