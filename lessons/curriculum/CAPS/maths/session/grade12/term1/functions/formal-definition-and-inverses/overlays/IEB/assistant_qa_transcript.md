**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First up: why does swapping x and y build the inverse? It feels like a trick with no reason behind it.

**Tutor:** The reason is what an inverse IS. A function is a list of pairings: input two goes to output one, input zero goes to output minus five, and so on. The inverse is the same list read in the other direction — every pairing reversed. Now look at what a graph plots: the input on the horizontal axis, the output on the vertical. Reversing every pairing means every point trades its coordinates, and the algebraic way to trade coordinates in an equation is to trade their letters: write x where y stood, y where x stood. The equation now describes the reversed list — but untidily, so we solve for y to put it back in standard costume. That is the whole routine: swap to reverse the pairings, solve to tidy the result. And the picture agrees: trading a point's coordinates reflects it across the line y equals x, which is why a function and its inverse always hang as mirror twins about that diagonal. If the swap ever feels mechanical, anchor it with one concrete pairing: the machine sent 2 to 1, so the reversed machine must send 1 to 2 — and testing that single snack certifies your algebra every time.

***

**Mandy:** Next one: the minus one in f inverse keeps tricking people into writing one over f of x. How do we keep them apart?

**Tutor:** By remembering that the minus one sits on the NAME, not on a number. When a minus one exponent sits on a quantity — like three to the minus one — it commands a reciprocal: one third. But in f inverse the symbol attaches to the function's name, and on a name it is a label meaning the reversed machine, nothing arithmetic at all. The two objects give completely different answers, which is the quickest proof they are different things. Take f of x equals 3 x minus 5 at x equals 2: f of 2 is 1. The reciprocal, one over f of 2, is one over one — fine, 1 here, so try x equals 4 instead: f of 4 is 7 and its reciprocal is one seventh. But f inverse of 7 asks a different question entirely: which code produced the snack 7? Answer: 4, since the inverse is x plus 5 over 3. One seventh versus four — nowhere near each other. So when you meet the notation, say the words the reversed function in your head, never the power minus one. And if an exam expression genuinely wants a reciprocal, it will wrap the function in brackets with the exponent outside, or write one over f of x explicitly — the naked f inverse always names the machine running backwards.

***

**Mandy:** Third: in the restricted parabola question, how does the old domain decide whether the root gets a plus or a minus sign?

**Tutor:** Through the swap of roles that every inversion performs. Whatever a function accepted as inputs, its inverse must produce as outputs — the old domain becomes the new range, word for word. Now run the worked case: y equals 3 x squared with domain x less than or equal to 0. Swap and solve: y equals plus or minus the square root of x over 3, and the algebra alone cannot pick a sign, because it has forgotten which arm of the parabola survived. You remember for it. The surviving inputs were all zero or negative, so the inverse's OUTPUTS must all be zero or negative, and only the negative root delivers that: f inverse of x equals negative square root of x over 3. Then certify with a pairing that crosses the machinery: the original sends minus 2 to 12, and negative root of twelve over three is negative root four, which is minus 2. Reversed perfectly. Had we restricted to x greater than or equal to 0 instead, the positive root would have been the honest choice by the identical argument. So the sign is never a guess and never a habit — it is the old domain, speaking through the new range.

***

**Mandy:** Last one: why test the ORIGINAL graph with a horizontal line, when the failure only happens later, on the inverse?

**Tutor:** Because the horizontal line on the original and the vertical line on the inverse are the same line, seen before and after the mirror. Reflection across y equals x turns horizontal into vertical: a horizontal line at height 16 on the original becomes a vertical line at position 16 on the inverse. So if that horizontal line touches the original twice — at codes 4 and minus 4, say — its reflected vertical line must touch the inverse twice, and a vertical line touching twice is precisely the failure of the vertical line test. The horizontal test is therefore not a new rule to memorise; it is the old rule, applied through the mirror, and it lets you predict the stall before doing any algebra. Practical exam routine: given any function to invert, run your eye horizontally across its graph first. Every horizontal line touching at most once means the function is one-to-one and the inverse will be a clean function — proceed. Some horizontal line touching twice means the inverse will arrive with a plus or minus attached, and you should expect the question's next sentence to hand you a domain restriction. Reading that structure in advance turns a trap question into a checklist.
