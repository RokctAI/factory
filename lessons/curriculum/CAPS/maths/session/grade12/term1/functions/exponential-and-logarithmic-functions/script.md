# Part 1 — Expert

The previous work built inverses by swapping x and y. Now the swap meets the one graph it cannot untangle with ordinary algebra: the exponential. Inverting y equals b to the power x demands a brand-new word — the logarithm — and grade twelve is where that word is defined, graphed and put to work. Four pieces of equipment by the end: the exponential graph and its laws, the definition of a logarithm as a rewritten exponential, the log graph with its features, and the skill of solving for an exponent.

## Subtopic: The Exponential Function y Equals b to the x

The exponential function is y equals b to the power x, where the base b is positive and not equal to 1. Take b equal to 2 and read the table: 2 to the power 0 is 1, 2 to the power 3 is 8, and 2 to the power minus 2 is one over 4, which is 0,25. Three facts jump out. The graph always passes through the point 0 and 1, because any base to the power 0 is 1. The outputs are always positive — no exponent can drag b to the x to zero or below. And as x runs to the left, the outputs shrink towards zero without arriving: the x axis, the line y equals 0, is a horizontal asymptote.

The base decides the direction. When b is greater than 1, the graph rises — each step right multiplies by b. When b is between 0 and 1, the graph falls: take b equal to a half, and a half to the power 2 is 0,25, so each step right halves the output. The falling graph is the rising graph reflected in the y axis, because a half to the x is the same as 2 to the minus x.

Domain and range in one line, asked in nearly every paper: domain all real x, range y greater than 0. And the exponential laws travel with the function: same base multiplied means add the exponents, same base divided means subtract them, and a power of a power multiplies them.

Pause here — the questions for this section are with you now. Through 0 and 1, asymptote on the x axis, and the base decides rise or fall.

## Subtopic: The Logarithm Defined

Now the question the exponential cannot answer directly: 2 to the power WHAT gives 8? The answer is obviously 3, but there was, until now, no notation for the answer as an object. The logarithm is that notation. The statement y equals log base b of x means exactly x equals b to the power y. Same fact, two costumes: the log form isolates the exponent, the exponential form isolates the power. Converting between the two costumes is the single most examined skill in this topic.

Read some values straight from the definition. Log base 2 of 8 asks 2 to the what gives 8 — answer 3. Log base 10 of 1000 asks 10 to the what gives 1000 — answer 3. Log base 2 of 1 asks 2 to the what gives 1 — answer 0, and indeed the log of 1 is 0 in every base. Logs can be negative: log base 2 of 0,125 is minus 3, because 2 to the minus 3 is one eighth. And a base between 0 and 1 flips signs: log base a half of 8 is minus 3, because a half to the minus 3 is 8.

Two boundaries come straight from the definition. The base b must be positive and not 1, matching the exponential it came from. And the input x must be strictly positive: b to the power y is always positive, so no positive base can ever produce log of zero or log of a negative number. Asking log base 2 of minus 4 is asking 2 to the what gives minus 4, and no exponent answers.

Stop for this section's questions now — rewrite every log statement as an exponential statement, and the answer usually falls out.

## Subtopic: The Graph of y Equals log Base b of x

Since y equals log base b of x is the inverse of y equals b to the x, its graph is the exponential reflected in the line y equals x — and every feature reflects with it. The exponential passed through 0 and 1; the log passes through 1 and 0, so every log graph cuts the x axis at 1. The exponential hugged the horizontal asymptote y equals 0; the log hugs the vertical asymptote x equals 0, plunging alongside the y axis without touching it. The exponential had domain all real x and range y greater than 0; the log swaps them — domain x greater than 0, range all real y.

The base tells the same directional story as before. For b greater than 1, say log base 2, the graph rises: slowly, but without ceiling, reaching 3 at x equals 8 and 10 only at x equals 1024. For b between 0 and 1, say base a half, the graph falls, the mirror image in the x axis of the base 2 graph, because log base a half of x equals minus log base 2 of x.

To sketch, plot the anchor point 1 and 0, one more friendly point — for log base 2, the point 8 and 3 or the point 2 and 1 — draw the vertical asymptote as a dashed line on the y axis, and let the curve fall down the asymptote on the right side of it. A log graph drawn touching or crossing the y axis is the classic lost mark: the graph lives entirely in x greater than 0.

Quick pause — the questions on the log graph are with you now. Through 1 and 0, asymptote up the y axis, domain strictly positive.

## Subtopic: Solving for the Exponent

Everything converges on one practical power: when the unknown sits in the exponent, the logarithm digs it out. Cleanest case first: solve 5 times 2 to the power x equals 80. Isolate the power: 2 to the x equals 16. Recognise 16 as 2 to the 4, and x equals 4 — no calculator needed when both sides share a base.

When no shared base exists, the definition takes over. Solve 3 to the power x equals 20. In log costume, x equals log base 3 of 20. The calculator computes it as log 20 divided by log 3, which is 2,73 correct to two decimals. Sanity-check the size before accepting it: 3 squared is 9 and 3 cubed is 27, so the exponent had to land between 2 and 3 — and 2,73 does.

The inverse story completes the circle from the previous lesson. To invert y equals 3 to the x, swap: x equals 3 to the y. The unknown y is an exponent, so the definition rewrites it: y equals log base 3 of x. The logarithm IS the inverse of the exponential — that sentence is worth marks on its own, and the graphs confirm it, mirrored across y equals x.

One warning for the finance work ahead: solving for time in compound growth means solving an equation like 1,08 to the power n equals 2, and the answer n equals log 2 over log 1,08, about 9,01, is exactly this skill wearing a rand sign.

The final questions of this part are with you now — isolate the power, match bases if you can, and call the log when you cannot.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same exponentials and logs from a chain message and a folded page — same rules, same answers, built from things you can picture.

## Subtopic: Doubling on Repeat

A chain message starts with one person. Each hour, everyone who has it forwards it to one new person, so the count doubles: 1, then 2, then 4, then 8. After x hours the count is 2 to the power x. That is the exponential function — nothing more than doubling on repeat, written as a power.

Read the graph off the story. At hour 0 the count is 1, so the graph passes through 0 and 1. The count is always positive — a chain message cannot reach zero people. Run time backwards and the count halves each step: half a person, a quarter, an eighth — shrinking towards zero forever without touching it. That endless approach is the horizontal asymptote along the x axis.

Now change the story: a kettle of tea cooling, losing half its extra heat each hour. That is base a half — the same graph flipped, falling instead of rising, because multiplying by a half on repeat shrinks. Rising or falling, the shape rules stay: through 0 and 1, always positive, hugging the x axis at one end.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the doubling graph are coming to you right now. Start at one, double or halve each step, and never touch the floor.

## Subtopic: The Question the Log Asks

Here is the log, with no mystery attached. The chain message doubles every hour, and you ask: how many HOURS until 8 people have it? Not how many people — how many hours. The answer is 3, and the notation for that answer is log base 2 of 8. A logarithm is just the how-many-steps question, asked of a doubling process.

Every log value is readable this way. Log base 2 of 32: how many doublings from 1 to 32? Five. Log base 10 of 1000: how many times does 10 multiply to reach 1000? Three. Log base 2 of 1: how many doublings to stay at 1? Zero — you have not started. And log base 2 of 0,25: how many doublings from 1 down to a quarter? Minus 2 — two steps backwards, two halvings. Negative logs are simply steps in reverse.

Some questions have no answer, and the story shows why. How many doublings from 1 person to minus 4 people? No number of steps, forward or backward, makes a chain message reach a negative count — so log of a negative number does not exist, and neither does log of zero. The log only answers questions about positive amounts.

Your questions for this part are up now. Read every log as how many steps, count forwards for big numbers, backwards for fractions, and refuse the question when the target is zero or below.

## Subtopic: Mirror Twins Across the Diagonal

The exponential and the log are the same information read in opposite directions. The exponential table says: after 3 hours, 8 people. The log table says: 8 people means 3 hours have passed. Input and output have traded places — and trading input with output is exactly what an inverse does. So y equals log base 2 of x is the inverse of y equals 2 to the x, and the two graphs are mirror twins across the diagonal line y equals x.

Every feature crosses the mirror with its coordinates swapped. The exponential's anchor point 0 and 1 becomes the log's anchor 1 and 0 — every log graph cuts the x axis at 1. The exponential's floor, the horizontal asymptote y equals 0, stands up to become the log's wall, the vertical asymptote x equals 0. Domain and range trade: the exponential accepted every x and produced only positive y; the log accepts only positive x and produces every y.

Sketching a log graph is therefore a reflection exercise. Plot 1 and 0, plot 2 and 1, and 8 and 3 if there is room, dash the wall along the y axis, and keep every part of the curve strictly to the right of it. The single most common sketch error is letting the log curve lean back and touch the y axis — the wall is never touched, just as the exponential never lands on its floor.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: swap the coordinates, stand the floor up into a wall, and keep the log strictly right of the y axis.
