**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First one, and it comes up a lot: why can the base of an exponential or a log never be 1, or negative? The restriction feels arbitrary.

**Tutor:** Test the forbidden bases and watch them break. Base 1 first: 1 to any power is 1, so y equals 1 to the x is a flat horizontal line at height 1. It never doubles, never decays, and worse, it cannot be inverted — asking 1 to the what gives 8 has no answer, and asking 1 to the what gives 1 has infinitely many. A log with base 1 would either refuse to answer or give every answer at once, so base 1 is banned. Now a negative base, say minus 2: minus 2 squared is 4, minus 2 cubed is minus 8 — the outputs whiplash between positive and negative, and for fractional exponents like one half the value does not even exist among real numbers, since that would be the square root of a negative. No smooth curve survives that. So the rule b positive and not equal to 1 is not decoration; it is the exact condition under which b to the x is a smooth, always-positive, one-to-one function — which is precisely what lets its inverse, the log, exist. Store it as: the base must be able to grow or shrink honestly, and 1 and the negatives cannot.

**Mandy:** Next: some of us freeze when a question says, write 2 to the power 5 equals 32 in logarithmic form. Which number goes where?

**Tutor:** Anchor yourself to what a logarithm IS: a log is an exponent. That one sentence places every number. In 2 to the power 5 equals 32, the base is 2, the exponent is 5, and the answer of the powering is 32. The log form isolates the exponent, so the statement becomes: log base 2 of 32 equals 5. Base stays the base — it drops down into the subscript. The big number, the result of the powering, goes inside the log. And the exponent, the thing a log always equals, sits alone on the other side. Run the same placement in reverse when converting the other way: log base 3 of 81 equals 4 unpacks to 3 to the power 4 equals 81. When you are unsure, check your conversion by computing both sides — if 2 to the 5 really is 32, and your log statement says log base 2 of 32 is 5, the two agree and the placement is right. The freeze usually comes from treating the conversion as a formula to memorise; treat it instead as one question — which number is the exponent? — and the other two numbers place themselves.

**Mandy:** Third one, from the homework: solving 3 to the x equals 20, why are we allowed to write x equals log 20 over log 3 when the calculator has no log base 3 button?

**Tutor:** Two steps are hiding in that line, and both are legal. The first step is pure definition: 3 to the x equals 20 means, in log costume, x equals log base 3 of 20 — that IS the answer, exact and complete, and in some questions you stop right there. The second step is only about the calculator's limitations. Most calculators offer log base 10, so we use the change of base fact: log base 3 of 20 equals log base 10 of 20 divided by log base 10 of 3. You can see why it works from exponents: if x is log base 3 of 20, then 3 to the x is 20; take log base 10 of both sides, and the power rule drags the x down in front, giving x times log 3 equals log 20, so x equals log 20 over log 3. Notice that this is a quotient OF logs, not a log of a quotient — log 20 over log 3 is not log of twenty thirds; keep the two logs separate. Punch it in: 2,73 to two decimals. Then run the size check, because it costs nothing: 3 squared is 9, 3 cubed is 27, and 20 sits between them, so x had to land between 2 and 3. It did — the answer certifies itself.

**Mandy:** Last one: why does the log graph stop dead at the y axis? A few of us keep sketching it drifting across to the left.

**Tutor:** The graph does not stop so much as it was never allowed there, and the reason lives in the definition. A point on y equals log base 2 of x with a negative x would claim that 2 to some power equals a negative number. But 2 to any power is positive — doubling or halving a positive amount, any number of times, keeps it positive — so no such point exists. x equals 0 fails the same way: no exponent sends 2 to zero, only towards it. So the entire graph lives strictly in x greater than 0, and the y axis is a wall the curve approaches but never touches: at x equals a half the log reads minus 1, at a quarter minus 2, at one over 1024 minus 10 — diving deeper the closer you creep to the wall, without ever arriving. There is also a mirror argument, which is why the sketch matters in exams: the log is the exponential reflected in the line y equals x, and the exponential's floor — the horizontal asymptote y equals 0 that it never lands on — reflects into the log's wall, the vertical asymptote x equals 0 that it never touches. If your log sketch crosses the y axis, its mirror image would be an exponential dipping to zero and below, which you already know is impossible. Draw the dashed wall first, then keep every part of the curve to its right.
