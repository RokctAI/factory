**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First one, and it always comes up with the proofs: how much of the proportionality theorem proof must we actually memorise, and what gets the marks?

**Tutor:** Memorise the skeleton, not the sentences — five moves, each worth marks, in a fixed order. Move one: the construction, and it is the move most learners forget. Join B to E and join C to D; without those two lines nothing else exists, and the construction earns its own mark. Move two: compare triangle A D E with triangle B D E, stating the reason — same height from E onto line A B — so their areas are in the ratio A D to D B. Move three: the twin comparison on the other side, giving areas in the ratio A E to E C, same height from D this time. Move four, the hinge: triangles B D E and C E D have equal areas because they stand on the same base D E and lie between the same parallels, D E and B C. That phrase, same base, same parallels, is the quotable reason. Move five: since the two area ratios share the triangle A D E upstairs and have equal partners downstairs, the ratios themselves are equal: A D over D B equals A E over E C. Practise by drawing the figure and writing only the five reasons in the margin — construction, same height, same height, same base same parallels, conclusion. If you can reproduce the reasons, the sentences write themselves, and the proof is eight or so marks banked before the numbered questions even start.

**Mandy:** Next: in the numeric questions I keep mixing up which ratio to write — part over part or part over whole. Is there a rule that always works?

**Tutor:** There is, and it starts with your pencil before any fraction is written. Label every segment in the figure as either a part or a whole, out loud or in the margin. In the standard figure, A D and D B are the two parts of side A B; A E and E C are the two parts of side A C; A B and A C are wholes. The theorem then offers you three honest patterns, and you may use whichever suits the given numbers: part over its sibling part on one side equals part over sibling part on the other — A D over D B equals A E over E C; or upper part over whole equals upper part over whole — A D over A B equals A E over A C; or lower part over whole equals lower part over whole. The single forbidden move is crossing patterns: a part-to-part fraction equated with a part-to-whole fraction. That is where the six-over-four-equals-nine-over-fifteen disaster comes from — six to four compares siblings while nine to fifteen compares a child to the household. The always-works recipe: identify the unknown, find the pattern in which the unknown appears with three knowns, write that one line, cross-multiply. Then spend ten seconds on the free insurance: compute the whole sides and check the whole-to-whole ratio agrees. In the worked case, six tenths and nine fifteenths both reduce to three fifths — when that check passes, the answer is safe.

**Mandy:** Third: why does the similarity statement's letter order matter so much? My answer had the right numbers but the memo's triangle letters were in a different order.

**Tutor:** Because the statement is not naming two triangles — it is recording a PAIRING, and the pairing is the mathematics. Triangle A B C similar to triangle D E F is three claims in one line: A pairs with D, B pairs with E, C pairs with F, meaning the angle at A equals the angle at D and so on down the letters. Every ratio you are entitled to write reads directly off that ordering: first letters over first letters, A B over D E, must equal B C over E F and A C over D F. Scramble the order and the recorded pairing becomes false even if your private intention was right — triangle A B C similar to triangle E F D claims the angle at A equals the angle at E, which may simply be untrue, and any marker or any later line of your own working that trusts the statement inherits the error. The safe procedure has two steps. Step one: find the two pairs of equal angles first, from parallel lines, shared angles, or given information, and mark them on the figure. Step two: write the two triangles vertex by vertex in the order of those equal pairs — equal angle to equal angle, letter by letter. If the memo's order differs from yours, both can be correct as long as each records the same pairing; A B C to D E F and B C A to E F D are the same statement rotated. What can never be rescued is an order that pairs unequal angles. Order is the proof's grammar: get the grammar right and the ratios speak for you.

**Mandy:** Last one: in the Pythagoras proof, how do I keep the two relations straight — which piece of the hypotenuse goes with which leg squared?

**Tutor:** Use the shadow rule: each leg squares to the hypotenuse times the piece DIRECTLY UNDERNEATH that leg. Picture the right angle at the top, the altitude dropping to D on the hypotenuse B C. The foot D splits the hypotenuse into B D, lying under the leg A B, and D C, lying under the leg A C. Each leg owns its own floor: A B squared equals B C times B D, its own piece; A C squared equals B C times D C, its piece. If you catch yourself writing A B squared equals B C times D C, the picture itself objects — a leg does not stand on the far piece. The deeper check is the similarity that produced each relation: A B squared comes from triangle A B D similar to triangle C B A, and both of those triangles contain the angle B — the leg, its piece, and the shared angle all live in the same corner of the figure. Same-corner ingredients, honest equation. The altitude relation completes the family: A D squared equals B D times D C, the product of the two pieces, which is why pieces of 4 and 9 give an altitude of 6. And the beauty of the whole proof is that the two leg relations then simply add: B C times B D plus B C times D C factorises to B C times the whole hypotenuse — B C squared. Corner by corner, the oldest theorem in the book assembles itself.
