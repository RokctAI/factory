# Part 1 — Expert

Grade eleven geometry lived inside the circle. Grade twelve steps out of it and studies shape itself: when are two triangles the same shape at different sizes, and what does a parallel line do to the sides it crosses? Four pillars carry the topic: the proportionality theorem and its area-based proof, the skill of using it in numbered figures, the two similarity theorems, and the crowning application — proving the Theorem of Pythagoras with nothing but similar triangles.

## Subtopic: The Proportionality Theorem and Its Proof

The statement first. A line drawn parallel to one side of a triangle, cutting the other two sides, divides those two sides proportionally. Set up the standard figure in your mind: triangle A B C, with D a point on side A B and E a point on side A C, and the line D E parallel to B C. The theorem claims A D over D B equals A E over E C.

The proof is examinable and runs on one humble fact: the area of a triangle is half base times height, so two triangles with the SAME height have areas in the ratio of their bases. Join B to E and join C to D — these two lines are the whole construction. Compare triangle A D E with triangle B D E. Seen with A D and D B as bases, they share the identical height from E down to the line A B. So area A D E over area B D E equals A D over D B. Now compare triangle A D E with triangle C E D. With bases A E and E C, they share the height from D to line A C, so area A D E over area C E D equals A E over E C. The hinge of the proof: triangles B D E and C E D have the SAME area. They stand on the same base D E, and their apexes B and C lie on a line parallel to D E — parallel lines are everywhere the same distance apart, so both triangles have equal heights on the same base. Equal partners downstairs, the same triangle upstairs: therefore A D over D B equals A E over E C. Done.

The converse also holds and is used constantly: if a line divides two sides of a triangle proportionally, that line is parallel to the third side. And the midpoint theorem from grade ten is the special case where the ratio is one to one.

Pause here — the questions for this section are with you now. Same height means areas as bases, same base between parallels means equal areas, and the ratio statement falls out.

## Subtopic: Working with Ratios in Figures

Knowing the theorem and wielding it under exam pressure are different skills, so this section is pure technique. First technique: the theorem hands out ratios in several equivalent costumes. From A D over D B equals A E over E C you may also write A D over A B equals A E over A C, because if the parts are proportional, each part is proportional to its whole. Choose the costume that contains three known lengths and one unknown.

Worked case. In triangle A B C with D E parallel to B C: A D is 6, D B is 4, A E is 9, find E C. Write A D over D B equals A E over E C: six over four equals nine over E C. Cross-multiply: six E C equals thirty-six, so E C is 6. Always sanity-check with the whole sides: A B is 10 and A C is 15, and six tenths equals nine fifteenths — both are three fifths. Consistent.

Second technique: watch for the trap of mixing a part with a whole. Six over four is a part-to-part ratio; nine over fifteen is part-to-whole. Equating them is the most common error in the topic. Before writing any equation, say what each segment is — part or whole — and keep the same pattern on both sides.

Third technique: parallel lines can appear twice. If a figure has two transversals cut by the same parallel line, apply the theorem separately in each triangle and link the results through the shared segment. And when a question says, prove that D E is parallel to B C, run the converse: compute both ratios, show them equal, and conclude parallel — with the reason stated as, line divides two sides of a triangle in proportion.

Stop for this section's questions now — match part with part, check with the wholes, and let the converse work when parallelism is the thing to be proved.

## Subtopic: Equiangular Triangles Are Similar

Two triangles are similar when they have the same shape: all matching angles equal, all matching sides in the same ratio. The wonder of triangles — not shared by any other polygon — is that for them, each condition forces the other. A rectangle and a long thin rectangle have equal angles without equal ratios, but triangles allow no such escape.

Theorem one: if two triangles are equiangular, their matching sides are in proportion, and the triangles are similar. The proof strategy is a transplant. Given triangle A B C and triangle D E F with angle A equal to angle D, angle B equal to angle E, and angle C equal to angle F, mark H on A B so that A H equals D E, and K on A C so that A K equals D F. Triangles A H K and D E F are congruent — side angle side, using the equal angles at A and D. So angle A H K equals angle E, which equals angle B. But those are corresponding angles for the lines H K and B C, so H K is parallel to B C. Now the proportionality theorem speaks: A H over A B equals A K over A C. Replace the transplanted lengths: D E over A B equals D F over A C. Repeat the argument from another vertex and all three ratios agree. Equal angles have forced proportional sides.

Theorem two is the converse: if the three pairs of matching sides of two triangles are in proportion, the triangles are equiangular, hence similar. Together the two theorems mean one test suffices — angles alone, or sides alone.

Notation carries marks here. Writing triangle A B C similar to triangle D E F asserts A pairs with D, B with E, C with F — the ORDER is the pairing. Ratios then read straight off the ordered letters: A B over D E equals B C over E F equals A C over D F. Scrambled letters produce scrambled ratios and lost marks, so match the equal angles first, then write the vertices in matching order.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — two angles equal is enough for equiangular, order the letters by the equal angles, and read every ratio from the ordering.

## Subtopic: Pythagoras by Similar Triangles

The oldest theorem in school mathematics, proved this year with the newest tool. Take triangle A B C, right-angled at A, and drop the altitude from A to the hypotenuse, meeting B C at D. That one altitude splits the big triangle into two smaller ones, and here is the crucial claim: each small triangle is similar to the whole.

Look at triangle A B D against triangle C B A. Both contain a right angle — at D in the small one, at A in the big one. They share the angle at B. Two angles equal, so the third pair matches automatically: equiangular, therefore similar. Read the ratio from the ordered letters, taking hypotenuse over the side opposite the shared angle: A B over C B equals B D over B A. Cross-multiply: A B squared equals B D times B C. The same argument on the other side, with triangle A C D against triangle B C A sharing the angle at C, gives A C squared equals C D times C B.

Now add the two conclusions. A B squared plus A C squared equals B D times B C, plus C D times B C — factor out B C — equals B C times the quantity B D plus D C. But B D plus D C is exactly B C, the whole hypotenuse. So A B squared plus A C squared equals B C squared. The Theorem of Pythagoras, delivered by pure similarity.

The two stepping stones have their own name and their own exam questions: A B squared equals B D times B C says each leg squared equals the projection of that leg on the hypotenuse, times the hypotenuse. A third relation, provable the same way, gives the altitude: A D squared equals B D times D C. Numeric versions appear constantly: if B D is 4 and D C is 9, then A D squared is 36 and the altitude is 6.

The final questions of this part are with you now — right angle plus shared angle proves each small triangle similar to the whole, squares come from cross-multiplied ratios, and the two projections add up to the hypotenuse.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same theorems from shadows, ladders and photographs — same statements, same proofs, built from things you can picture.

## Subtopic: The Ladder Against the Wall

Lean a ladder against a wall, and let the sun throw its shadow. Halfway up the ladder there is a rung; the sun's rays pass every rung in parallel lines. Where the halfway rung's ray lands on the ground is exactly halfway along the ladder's shadow. A rung a third of the way up lands a third of the way along. Parallel rays never favour one part of the ladder over another — whatever fraction of the ladder you climb, the same fraction of the shadow is used up. That is the proportionality theorem: a line parallel to one side of a triangle divides the other two sides in the same ratio.

Why must it be true? The proof in the lesson uses areas, and its heart fits in a sentence: triangles standing on the same base, with their tips on the same parallel line, hold the same area — same floor, same ceiling, same space inside. Once those two helper triangles are equal, the two ratios upstairs are forced to agree.

Using the theorem is a matter of matching parts honestly. Cut the ladder at the rung: upper part to lower part must equal upper shadow to lower shadow. Part with part, whole with whole — never six over four on one side and nine over fifteen on the other, because one is part-to-part and the other part-to-whole. Say out loud what each length is before you build the fraction, and the classic mark-loser dies on the spot.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the parallel cut are coming to you right now. Same fraction up the ladder as along the shadow, and match your parts like with like.

## Subtopic: The Photograph and Its Enlargement

Take a photograph of a triangle and enlarge it. Every angle survives untouched — an enlargement turns nothing — and every side stretches by the same factor. That pair of facts IS similarity: same shape, same angles, sides in one shared ratio. The theorem of this lesson says something stronger, and only triangles can promise it: if the angles of two triangles match, the sides are ALREADY in proportion. You need not check both; for triangles, equal angles guarantee the enlargement factor exists.

The proof is a photocopier trick. Given two equiangular triangles, shrink-copy the big one onto the small one: mark off the small triangle's sides along two edges of the big one and join the marks. The joining line sits parallel to the third side — equal corresponding angles force that — and the proportionality theorem from the ladder instantly delivers the matching ratios. The new theorem stands on the shoulders of the previous one, which is how this whole topic is built.

Everyday similarity is everywhere. A streetlight and a person: the lamp post and its shadow-triangle, your body and yours, same angles at the ground because light rays run straight. If a two-metre person casts a three-metre shadow while the pole casts nine metres, the pole is six metres — the ratio two over three, applied to nine. Every map scale, every scale model, every zoomed screen is the same theorem wearing work clothes.

One discipline from the expert part matters even here: write the matching corners in matching ORDER. Person-feet-shadowtip listed against pole-base-shadowtip, and every ratio reads straight off the letters. Scramble the order and the ratios lie to you.

Your questions for this part are up now. Equal angles mean an enlargement factor exists, find it from one known pair, and keep the corners in matching order.

## Subtopic: The Folded Corner — Pythagoras Rebuilt

Here is the most famous theorem in mathematics, rebuilt with the photograph idea. Take a right-angled triangle and drop a line from the right angle straight down to the longest side, like folding the corner flat onto the hypotenuse. The fold cuts the big triangle into two smaller triangles — and each small one is a photograph of the whole. Check the little one on the left: it has a right angle where the fold lands, and it shares a corner angle with the big triangle. Two angles equal — that is the enlargement guarantee from the last section — so small and big are the same shape.

Same shape means matching ratios, and one cross-multiplication turns a ratio into a square: the left leg squared equals the whole hypotenuse times the piece of hypotenuse under that leg. The right-hand small triangle tells the mirror-image story: right leg squared equals hypotenuse times the other piece. Now add the two stories. Leg squared plus leg squared equals hypotenuse times the first piece plus hypotenuse times the second — and the two pieces together are simply the whole hypotenuse. So legs squared, added, equal hypotenuse squared. Pythagoras, falling out of two photographs and one addition.

There is a bonus hiding at the fold. The fold line itself obeys: fold squared equals the product of the two hypotenuse pieces. Pieces of 4 and 9 give a fold of 6 — the number that multiplies with itself to make 36. Builders once used exactly this to raise a true vertical over a marked baseline.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: each small triangle is a photograph of the whole, each leg squared is hypotenuse times its own piece, and the two pieces reassemble into the hypotenuse to finish the proof.
