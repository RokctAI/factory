**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First one, and it comes up a lot: in the exam, how do we decide FAST between the sine rule and the cosine rule without second-guessing for five minutes?

**Tutor:** Run a stocktake, not a debate — count what the question dealt you, and the count decides. Ask one question: do I hold a complete pair, meaning a side TOGETHER with the angle directly opposite it? If yes, the sine rule is open for business, and it will fetch any other side or angle for the price of one more given piece. If no complete pair exists, the sine rule physically cannot start — every one of its fractions needs a filled pair to anchor the chain — so the cosine rule takes over, and it accepts exactly two hands: two sides with the included angle, or all three sides. Notice the two rules barely overlap: side-angle-side and side-side-side belong to cosine; anything with a filled pair belongs to sine. There is one more shortcut worth its weight: before hunting exotic data, harvest the third angle by subtracting from 180 — it often completes a pair you did not realise you had, converting a cosine-looking question into a two-line sine rule question. Stocktake, pair check, subtract from 180. Ten seconds, decision made.

***

**Mandy:** Next: when the sine rule finds an angle, the teacher keeps warning about a second possible answer, but when the cosine rule finds an angle, nobody warns about anything. Why the difference?

**Tutor:** The difference is a fingerprint of the two functions between 0 and 180 degrees, the only interval a triangle's angle can live in. Sine is positive across that whole interval and it repeats: sine of 40 equals sine of 140, because sine climbs to its peak at 90 and comes back down symmetrically. So when your calculator answers 40 degrees from inverse sine, an obtuse twin at 140 gave exactly the same sine and might also be legal. That is the ambiguous case — you must test the twin against the 180 degree ceiling: if the other known angle plus 140 still fits under 180, two genuine triangles exist and both answers must be reported. Cosine has no twin problem: from 0 to 180 it strictly falls, from 1 through zero at 90 down to minus 1, never repeating a value. Every cosine value corresponds to exactly ONE angle in the interval — positive cosine means acute, negative cosine means obtuse, and the calculator's single answer is the whole truth. That is also why the rearranged cosine rule is the SAFE way to find an angle when all three sides are known: the sign of the answer even diagnoses obtuseness for free.

***

**Mandy:** Third one, from the homework: in the cosine rule, what is the minus 2 b c cos A part actually DOING? We can use the formula but nobody can say what it means.

**Tutor:** It is a hinge correction on Pythagoras. Start with the case you have known since grade nine: a right angle between sides b and c. Then the far side obeys a squared equals b squared plus c squared, full stop — and notice the cosine rule agrees, because cos of 90 is zero and the correction term dies. Now put the two sides on a hinge. Squeeze the hinge narrower than 90 degrees: the endpoints of the two sides swing closer together, so the far side must SHRINK below the Pythagoras value. Cosine of an acute angle is positive, so minus 2 b c cos A subtracts — the formula records the squeeze. In our field question, 130 minus 63 gave 67, well under the Pythagorean 130. Open the hinge wider than 90: the endpoints swing apart, the far side stretches beyond Pythagoras, and cosine obliges by turning negative, so subtracting a negative ADDS length. One formula, three regimes: narrow hinge shortens, square hinge is Pythagoras, wide hinge lengthens. Keep that picture and you will also stop misreading a negative cosine as an error — it is the formula telling you the hinge is open wide.

**Mandy:** Last one: the 3D tower question defeated half of us. Where do people actually go wrong, and what is the reliable way through?

**Tutor:** People go wrong in the same place almost every time: they try to solve the triangle that holds the ANSWER before solving the triangle that holds the DATA. The height lives in the vertical triangle, so eyes lock onto it — but that triangle starts the problem with only one usable fact, the 25 degree elevation, and no side lengths at all. Nothing can be computed there yet. The reliable route has four fixed stops. Stop one: identify the two flat triangles — the horizontal one on the ground, with both observers and the tower's base, and the vertical one containing the tower. Stop two: name the bridge, the one side the two triangles share; in our question it is A B, the flat distance from observer B to the base. Stop three: solve the GROUND triangle first, because it is data-rich — angles 70 and 50 given, third angle 60 by subtraction, and B C equal to 100 metres completes a pair, so the sine rule delivers A B as 100 sine 50 over sine 60, about 88,46 metres. Stop four: carry the bridge into the vertical triangle, which is right-angled because the tower meets flat ground at 90 degrees, and finish with tangent: height is 88,46 times tan 25, about 41,25 metres. If the 3D sketch confuses the eye, redraw the two triangles separately and flat — nothing is lost, and the bridge becomes obvious. Data triangle first, bridge across, answer triangle second. That order is the entire secret.
