Hey, it's Mandy — I know a page of compound-angle identities looks like a wall of nearly identical formulae designed to be confused with each other. Breathe. The wall is really two sentences and one substitution.

Sentence one, for sine: mix the functions, keep the sign. Sine of alpha plus beta is sine alpha cos beta plus cos alpha sine beta. Sentence two, for cosine: pair the functions, flip the sign. Cosine of alpha plus beta is cos alpha cos beta minus sine alpha sine beta. That is all four compound identities, because the minus versions just follow the same sentences. And if panic wipes them out mid-exam, rebuild trust with numbers: sine of 90 must equal 1, and only the true expansion of sine of 60 plus 30 delivers it.

The substitution: let beta equal alpha, and the doubles appear free of charge. Sine 2 alpha is 2 sine alpha cos alpha. Cos 2 alpha is cos squared minus sine squared, and the Pythagorean identity dresses it two more ways — 2 cos squared minus 1, or 1 minus 2 sine squared. Three faces, one identity. Choosing a face is not a lottery: look at the neighbours. Sine terms nearby means wear the sine face; cosines nearby, the cosine face; a lone 1 that must cancel, the face whose 1 kills it.

For the given-one-ratio questions, the only trap is a sign: the Pythagorean identity returns a square, so the quadrant in the question chooses plus or minus. Second quadrant, cosine negative. Draw the triangle if unsure — the sign is visible.

And equations like cos 2 x plus sine x equals 0 are just costume changes: convert the double to match the single, factorise the quadratic, then quadrants and reference angles as always.

Two sentences, one substitution, one sign check. The wall was never solid. You've got this!
