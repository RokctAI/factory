Hey, it's Mandy — I know a 3D diagram with towers and observers and dotted lines looks like an architecture exam gatecrashing a maths paper. It is not. This whole lesson is three tools and one bookkeeping habit.

Tool one, the sine rule: side over sine of the opposite angle, all fractions equal. It switches on the moment you hold a complete pair — a side and the angle directly across from it. Tool two, the cosine rule: two sides and the hinge angle between them gives the far side; all three sides gives any angle. It is just Pythagoras with a hinge correction — narrow hinge shortens the far side, wide hinge stretches it. Tool three, the area rule: half times two sides times sine of the angle clamped between them, no height required.

Choosing is a card count, not a talent. Complete pair anywhere? Sine rule. Side-angle-side or side-side-side? Cosine rule. And always harvest the free fact first: the three angles sum to 180, so the third angle costs one subtraction.

The 3D questions are the same tools wearing a costume. There is no 3D triangle — only two flat triangles sharing one side, like two pages of an open book. Find the shared side, the spine. Solve the page where the data lives, usually the ground triangle, and only then carry the spine's length into the standing page, where a right angle and a tangent finish the height. Solve the data page first, every time; the answer page always starts empty.

Sanity checks are your safety net: the biggest angle faces the longest side, a negative cosine just means an obtuse angle, and if your tower comes out taller than the distances on the ground, revisit the tangent.

Three tools, one card count, two pages and a spine. Every mark in this topic is a routine, and routines can be drilled. You've got this!
