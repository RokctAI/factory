**Mandy:** Class questions before we close! First, the one everyone whispers: why does the tempting shortcut fail? Why is sine of a sum not just the sum of the sines?

**Tutor:** Because sine does not measure the angle; it measures the HEIGHT the angle produces, and heights from rotations do not stack linearly. Picture the ramp. The first 45 degree tilt lifts the far end a certain amount from flat ground. Now tilt 45 degrees further. The second rotation starts from a slope already leaning, so some of its turning no longer converts into height — it spends itself pulling the ramp backwards over the top. Adding sine 45 to sine 45 pretends both rotations started from flat ground, which is why the naive answer 1,41 overshoots the true sine of 90, which is exactly 1. The genuine expansion records the interaction: each angle contributes its sine SCALED by the other angle's cosine — scaled, that is, by how upright the situation remains when the other rotation has done its work. That is why all four identities contain products, never bare sums. Whenever the temptation returns, run the numbers test in the margin: one known triple like 90 equals 45 plus 45 demolishes the shortcut in one line, and the demolition itself is worth remembering.

**Mandy:** Next: with three faces of cos 2 alpha available, how do we choose the right one under exam pressure without trying all three?

**Tutor:** Let the rest of the line choose for you — the face is always the one that removes what you do not want. Three situations cover everything. Situation one: the expression or equation beside cos 2 alpha contains sine. Wear 1 minus 2 sine squared alpha, and the whole line speaks sine only — that is exactly what we did in cos 2 x plus 3 sine x minus 2, which collapsed into a quadratic in sine x. Situation two: the neighbour is a cosine, or you are GIVEN only cos alpha. Wear 2 cos squared alpha minus 1; in the worked example with cos alpha equal to minus 5 over 13, that face delivered cos 2 alpha as minus 119 over 169 without ever needing sine. Situation three: a proof contains the expression 1 plus cos 2 alpha or 1 minus cos 2 alpha. Choose the face whose constant cancels the 1 — with 1 plus cos 2 alpha, the face 2 cos squared minus 1 turns it into a clean 2 cos squared alpha, and with 1 minus cos 2 alpha, the face 1 minus 2 sine squared turns it into 2 sine squared alpha. Match the function, kill the constant. Two instructions, and the choosing takes five seconds.

**Mandy:** Third one: in the minus 5 over 13 question, why did we have to stop and think about quadrants before writing sine alpha?

**Tutor:** Because the Pythagorean identity only ever reports a SIZE, and the quadrant supplies the sign. From cos alpha equals minus 5 over 13, the identity gives sine squared alpha equals 144 over 169, so sine alpha is plus or minus 12 over 13 — the algebra genuinely cannot tell which, because squaring erased the sign. The question can tell: alpha lies between 90 and 180 degrees, the second quadrant, where sine is positive and cosine negative. So sine alpha is positive 12 over 13, and only then do the doubles compute honestly: sine 2 alpha equals minus 120 over 169 and cos 2 alpha equals minus 119 over 169. Choose the wrong sign and BOTH final answers flip incorrectly, so the quadrant call is worth real marks. If the letters ever swim, draw the little reference triangle in the correct quadrant — side 5 on the negative x side, 12 up, hypotenuse 13 — and read the signs off the picture. And take the free consistency check at the end: both doubles negative places 2 alpha in the third quadrant, exactly where doubling an angle just past 90 should land. When the answer's geography agrees with the algebra, you can hand the work in with confidence.

**Mandy:** Last one: for exact values, how do we know which two special angles to build from? Some people tried 60 minus 45 for 15 degrees and got tangled.

**Tutor:** Both routes are legal — 45 minus 30 and 60 minus 45 are equally true statements about 15 — but they are not equally kind. The kindness test is: which pair keeps the surds simplest and the arithmetic shortest? The angles 45 and 30 bring root 2 over 2, root 3 over 2 and a half, whose products give root 6 over 4 and root 2 over 4 — two terms with the same denominator, subtract and done: root 6 minus root 2, over 4. The pair 60 and 45 arrives at the same destination, as it must, but its products mix differently and most people mis-multiply somewhere in the middle. So the working rule: reach first for a pair involving 30 or 45 with the smallest surds, write the identity out in full BEFORE substituting, then substitute in one careful pass. The same discipline powers the reverse direction: when you meet a four-term spread like cos 50 cos 20 plus sine 50 sine 20, do not compute anything — pattern-match it. Cos cos plus sine sine is the cosine-of-a-difference recipe already written out, so it equals cos 30, root 3 over 2. Build forwards with friendly angles; collapse backwards by pattern. Either direction, the identity is the recipe card.
