**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First one, and it comes up a lot: how are we supposed to remember four compound identities plus three versions of cos 2 alpha without mixing them up in the exam?

**Tutor:** Do not memorise seven things — memorise two patterns and one factory. Pattern one, sine: the functions MIX, sine cos then cos sine, and the middle sign copies the bracket's sign. Pattern two, cosine: the functions PAIR, cos cos then sine sine, and the middle sign flips the bracket's sign. Those two sentences generate all four compound identities on demand. Then the factory: set beta equal to alpha, and the doubles manufacture themselves. Sine of alpha plus alpha becomes sine alpha cos alpha plus cos alpha sine alpha — two identical terms, so 2 sine alpha cos alpha. Cosine of alpha plus alpha becomes cos squared minus sine squared, and the Pythagorean identity converts that into the other two faces whenever you need them. So the honest memory load is: two patterns, one substitution, one Pythagorean rewrite. In the exam, if doubt strikes, run a number check in ten seconds: your candidate identity must survive alpha equals 30 and beta equals 60, because sine of 90 is 1, and only the correct expansion — sine 30 cos 60 plus cos 30 sine 60, a quarter plus three quarters — delivers exactly 1.

**Mandy:** Next: in the question where sine alpha is 3 over 5 and alpha is between 90 and 180 degrees, why did cos alpha HAVE to be negative? Some of us took plus 4 over 5 and lost everything after that.

**Tutor:** Because the Pythagorean identity only ever reports a square. From sine squared plus cos squared equals 1, you get cos squared alpha equals 16 over 25 — and a square has forgotten the sign of the thing that made it. Both plus and minus 4 over 5 square to 16 over 25, so the algebra alone cannot choose. The quadrant information is the tiebreaker, and it is given in the question precisely because it is needed. Between 90 and 180 degrees is the second quadrant: x coordinates there are negative, and cosine is the x coordinate on the unit circle, so cosine is negative — minus 4 over 5, no debate. If the CAST picture ever feels slippery, draw the actual triangle: put the angle's arm in the second quadrant, opposite side 3 up, hypotenuse 5, and the horizontal leg runs 4 units in the NEGATIVE x direction. The sign is visible on the page. And the sign matters downstream, which is why the whole question collapses if it is missed: sine 2 alpha equals 2 sine alpha cos alpha inherits that minus, giving minus 24 over 25. A wrong sign there is not a one-mark slip; it poisons every later line, so spend the ten seconds on the quadrant call every single time.

**Mandy:** Third one, from the homework: with three faces of cos 2 alpha available, how do we know which one to use in a proof? Trying all three wastes half the exam.

**Tutor:** Let the REST of the expression choose, because the face is never picked in isolation. Three situations cover essentially every question. Situation one: the expression beside cos 2 alpha involves sine — sine squared terms, or a sine x standing alone in an equation. Wear 1 minus 2 sine squared alpha, so everything speaks sine. That is why the equation cos 2 x plus sine x equals 0 became a clean quadratic in sine x. Situation two: the neighbours are cosines. Wear 2 cos squared alpha minus 1. Situation three: the expression pairs cos 2 alpha with nothing but wants factorising — then the face cos squared minus sine squared is gold, because it is a difference of squares and splits into cos alpha minus sine alpha, times cos alpha plus sine alpha. There is also a constant-hunting trick: when the expression contains a lone 1 that needs to die, pick the face whose 1 cancels it. In the proof of 1 minus cos 2 alpha over sine 2 alpha, choosing 1 minus 2 sine squared made the numerator's 1 vanish instantly, leaving 2 sine squared over 2 sine cos, which is tan alpha. One glance at the neighbours, one face, one line — never trial and error.

**Mandy:** Last one: when we solved cos 2 x plus sine x equals 0 and got sine x equals minus a half, how do the answers 210 and 330 actually come out? The reference angle step keeps going wrong.

**Tutor:** Slow the step down into three decisions, and it stops going wrong. Decision one: which quadrants? Sine x equals minus a half is NEGATIVE, and sine is negative where y coordinates are negative — quadrants three and four. Not two, not one; write the pair down before touching the calculator. Decision two: the reference angle, which is the acute angle whose sine is the SIZE of the value, ignoring sign. Sine of what acute angle is a half? Thirty degrees — an old friend, no calculator required. Decision three: place the reference angle into the chosen quadrants using the frame angles 180 and 360. Third quadrant angles are 180 plus the reference: 180 plus 30 is 210. Fourth quadrant angles are 360 minus the reference: 360 minus 30 is 330. So sine x equals minus a half gives 210 and 330 in the demanded interval, and the other factor, sine x equals 1, contributes 90 directly, since sine peaks at exactly 90 degrees. Then the certificate, which costs one line: substitute 210 back into the ORIGINAL equation — cos of 420 is cos of 60, a half; sine of 210 is minus a half; the sum is zero, as demanded. The reference-angle routine is always those three decisions in that order: sign chooses quadrants, size chooses the reference, frame angles place it.
