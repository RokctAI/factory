# Part 1 — Expert

Right-angled triangles surrender to sine, cosine and tangent directly. But the triangles of the real world — survey lines, roof trusses, cellphone tower shadows — are rarely right-angled. Grade eleven met the three rules that handle ANY triangle; grade twelve revises their proofs and then pushes them into three dimensions. Four pieces of equipment by the end: the decision procedure for choosing a rule, the sine rule at work, the cosine rule at work, and the two-triangle strategy that cracks every 3D problem.

## Subtopic: Choosing the Rule

The standard labelling first, because everything hangs on it: in triangle A B C, the side named little a lies OPPOSITE the angle named capital A, little b opposite capital B, little c opposite capital C. Every rule is written in that pairing.

The sine rule says: a over sine A equals b over sine B equals c over sine C. It connects PAIRS — a side with its opposite angle. The cosine rule says: a squared equals b squared plus c squared minus 2 b c cos A. It connects three sides and ONE angle, the angle wedged between two known sides. The area rule says: area equals a half a b sine C — two sides and the INCLUDED angle between them, no height needed.

Choosing is a stocktake. Count what the question gives. Holding a complete pair — a side and its opposite angle — plus one more piece? Sine rule. Holding two sides and the included angle, or all three sides and hunting an angle? Cosine rule. Asked for area with two sides and the angle between them? Area rule. The proofs of all three are examinable and each starts the same way: drop a perpendicular height inside the triangle, split it into right-angled pieces, and the rule assembles from basic trig ratios and, for the cosine rule, Pythagoras.

Pause here — the questions for this section are with you now. Match side to opposite angle, count your data, and let the stocktake choose the rule.

## Subtopic: The Sine Rule at Work

Use the sine rule when a matched pair is on the table. In triangle A B C, angle A is 40 degrees, angle B is 60 degrees, and side little a is 10 units. The pair a and A is complete, so the rule runs. B over sine B equals a over sine A: b equals 10 times sine 60, over sine 40, which computes to 13,47 units correct to two decimals. Sanity check first, calculator second: angle B is bigger than angle A, so side b MUST come out longer than 10 — bigger angles sit opposite longer sides — and 13,47 obeys.

Angle C comes free of charge: angles of a triangle sum to 180 degrees, so C is 80 degrees, and side c follows from the same rule if wanted. Always harvest the third angle by subtraction before hunting more sides — it is the cheapest fact in the triangle.

One caution carried over from grade eleven: when the sine rule is used to find an ANGLE, the calculator returns an acute answer, but sine is also positive in the second quadrant, so an obtuse triangle can hide a second possibility. The ambiguous case appears when the given information permits two different triangles; check whether the obtuse alternative still fits under the 180 degree ceiling, and state both when both survive.

Stop for this section's questions now — complete pair plus one more piece, longest side opposite biggest angle, and interrogate the calculator's angle before trusting it.

## Subtopic: The Cosine Rule at Work

No matched pair? Then the cosine rule, in one of its two jobs. Job one: two sides and the included angle, find the third side. Sides 7 and 9 with a 60 degree angle wedged between them. A squared equals 7 squared plus 9 squared, minus 2 times 7 times 9 times cos 60. That is 49 plus 81 minus 126 times a half: 130 minus 63, which is 67. So the side is the square root of 67, about 8,19 units. Note the shape of the formula: Pythagoras plus a correction term. At exactly 90 degrees the cosine is zero and the correction vanishes — the cosine rule IS Pythagoras with an adjustment for how far the angle leans from square.

Job two: all three sides known, find any angle. Rearranged: cos A equals b squared plus c squared minus a squared, all over 2 b c. Sides 5, 7 and 8, hunting the angle opposite the 5: cos A equals 49 plus 64 minus 25, over 2 times 7 times 8 — that is 88 over 112, which is 11 over 14. Inverse cosine gives 38,21 degrees. The rearranged rule needs no quadrant debate: unlike sine, cosine is negative for obtuse angles, so the calculator's single answer is always the correct one — a negative cosine simply announces an obtuse angle honestly.

And the area rule completes the kit: the same 7 and 9 sides with the 60 degree included angle enclose an area of a half times 7 times 9 times sine 60, about 27,28 square units. Two sides and the wedge between them — no height, no base hunting.

Quick pause — the questions on the cosine and area rules are with you now. Included angle for a side, three sides for an angle, and read a negative cosine as an obtuse verdict, not an error.

## Subtopic: The Two-Triangle Strategy in Three Dimensions

Every 3D examination question is two 2D triangles sharing an edge, and the shared edge is the bridge. The routine: identify the horizontal triangle lying flat on the ground, identify the vertical triangle standing upright, find the side they share, cross the bridge.

Worked case. A vertical tower stands at point A. Two observers stand at ground points B and C. The angle A B C is 70 degrees, the angle A C B is 50 degrees, and the distance B C is 100 metres. From B, the angle of elevation of the tower's top is 25 degrees. Find the tower's height.

Step one: the ground triangle A B C. Its third angle, at A, is 180 minus 70 minus 50, which is 60 degrees. The bridge is the side A B — the horizontal distance from observer B to the tower's base — and it sits opposite the 50 degree angle at C. Sine rule: A B equals 100 times sine 50, over sine 60, which is 88,46 metres.

Step two: the vertical triangle. It stands on A B, right-angled at the tower's base, with the 25 degree elevation angle at B. The height is opposite that angle, and the bridge A B is adjacent, so height equals A B times tan 25, which is 88,46 times 0,4663 — 41,25 metres.

The discipline is bookkeeping: mark every right angle — vertical structures meet horizontal ground at 90 degrees even when the sketch hides it — write the elevation angle inside the vertical triangle, and name the bridge side explicitly before calculating anything. Nearly every mark lost in 3D trigonometry is lost by solving the wrong triangle first: the bridge must be computed in the triangle where enough is known, then carried to the other.

The final questions of this part are with you now — flat triangle, standing triangle, shared bridge, and cross it in the right direction.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same three rules from a football pitch, a fenced field and a flagpole — same rules, same answers.

## Subtopic: Which Tool Comes Out of the Box

Three tools live in the triangle toolbox, and each has a grip shaped for a specific hand of data. Before any calculation, lay out what the question dealt you, like checking your cards.

The sine rule is the matching-pairs tool. It works in fractions: each side over the sine of the angle staring at it from across the triangle, all equal. To turn its handle you need one COMPLETE pair — a side together with its opposite angle — plus any one more piece. The cosine rule is the wedge tool: two sides with the angle clamped between them gives the far side, or all three sides gives any angle. The area rule is the paint estimate: two sides and the clamped angle, half a b sine C, and no ladder needed to measure a height.

The labelling convention makes the pairing visible: little letters for sides, capitals for angles, and little a always faces capital A across the room. Mispair them and every rule misfires — the most common zero in this topic is a side divided by the sine of the WRONG angle.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on tool choice are coming to you right now. Lay out your cards, find a pair or find a wedge, and pick the tool with the matching grip.

## Subtopic: A Field You Cannot Walk Across

A farmer owns a triangular field. Two fences run from the same corner post: one 7 units long, one 9 units, opening at 60 degrees to each other. The far boundary crosses a marsh nobody can walk. How long is that unreachable fence?

This is the wedge tool's moment: two sides and the angle clamped between them. The far side squared equals 7 squared plus 9 squared minus 2 times 7 times 9 times cos of 60. Compute in pieces: 49 plus 81 is 130; the correction is 126 times a half, 63; and 130 minus 63 is 67. Square root: about 8,19 units of fence.

Look at what the correction term does, because it is the whole idea. If the corner opened at exactly 90 degrees, cos would be zero and the rule would collapse to Pythagoras — 130, root about 11,4. At 60 degrees the corner is squeezed narrower than square, the far side shortens, and the minus 63 records exactly how much. A corner wider than 90 has a negative cosine, the correction ADDS, and the far side stretches. Pythagoras with a hinge — that is the cosine rule.

And the marsh's area, for the record: half times 7 times 9 times sine 60 — about 27,28 square units — measured entirely from the dry corner.

Your questions for this part are up now. Two fences and a hinge angle: square both, subtract the swing correction, and root.

## Subtopic: The Flagpole and Its Two Triangles

A flagpole problem in 3D undoes everyone until they see the trick: there is no such thing as a 3D triangle question — only two flat triangles pretending, one lying on the ground, one standing upright, joined along one shared side like two pages of an open book meeting at the spine.

The flagpole stands at A. Two friends stand at B and C on flat ground, 100 metres apart. From where they stand, the ground angles at B and C measure 70 and 50 degrees. Friend B looks up at the top of the pole at 25 degrees above the horizontal. The height hides in the standing page; the given distances live on the lying page. The spine — the side both pages share — is A B, the flat distance from friend B to the pole's base.

Solve the lying page first, because that is where the data is. Its angles are 70, 50, and by subtraction 60 at the pole. A B faces the 50 degree angle, so the matching-pairs tool gives A B equals 100 sine 50 over sine 60 — 88,46 metres. Now carry that number up the spine into the standing page: a right-angled triangle, base 88,46, elevation angle 25. Height equals 88,46 times tan 25, which is 41,25 metres.

Every 3D question is this dance: find the spine, solve the page that holds the data, carry the spine's length to the page that holds the answer. Draw the two pages as separate flat triangles if the 3D sketch swims — flattening the book loses nothing and saves everything.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: lying page, standing page, one shared spine, and always solve the data-rich page first.
