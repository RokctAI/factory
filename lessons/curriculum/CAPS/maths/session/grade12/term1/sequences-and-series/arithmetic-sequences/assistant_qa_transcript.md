**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First one, and it comes up a lot: why is the formula a plus n minus one times d, and not just a plus n d? Half the class keeps writing the wrong one.

**Tutor:** Count what actually happens on the way to a term. Term one is where you start — no d has been added yet. Term two has one d added. Term three has two. The number of additions always trails the position by exactly one, because the first term is a starting point, not a step. So term n has n minus one additions, and the formula records that. If you write a plus n d instead, you are adding one step too many — your term two hundred would really be term two hundred and one. There is a quick way to protect yourself in an exam: build the formula, then test it on a term you can see with your own eyes. For 3; 7; 11; 15, the formula T n equals 4 n minus 1 should give T4 equal to 15, and it does — four fours minus one. If your version gives 19 there, you have the extra step and you fix it before it costs anything. Ten seconds of checking, and the off-by-one error, which is the most common error in this whole topic, never reaches the marker.

**Mandy:** Next: when a question asks, is 130 a term of the sequence, and n comes out as a decimal, some of us just round it off. Why is that wrong?

**Tutor:** Because n is not a measurement — it is a position in a list, and positions only come in whole numbers. There is a first term, a second term, a thirty-second term, but there is no term thirty-two comma seven five; the sequence simply does not have a slot there. When you solve 4 n minus 1 equals 130 and get n equal to 32,75, the algebra is telling you something useful: 130 falls BETWEEN two genuine terms. Term thirty-two is 127 and term thirty-three is 131, and the sequence jumps clean over 130 without landing on it. Rounding n to 33 and answering yes would claim that 131 and 130 are the same number. The correct answer is a sentence, not a number: since n is not a natural number, 130 is not a term of the sequence. Write exactly that, because the reasoning is what the final mark is for. And notice the contrast with the salary type of question, where a decimal n means the target is passed DURING a year — there you take the next whole n up, because the question asks when a level is first reached, not whether it is hit exactly. Read which question you are in before you touch the decimal.

**Mandy:** Third one, from the homework: given T3 equals 10 and T7 equals 26, why does subtracting the two equations give d so quickly?

**Tutor:** Because subtraction measures the climb between the two known positions. Write both facts through the general term: a plus 2 d equals 10, and a plus 6 d equals 26. Both equations carry the same a, so subtracting removes it entirely and leaves 4 d equals 16 — four jumps of size d lifted the sequence from 10 to 26, a rise of sixteen shared over four jumps, so each jump is 4. That picture is worth keeping, because you can use it without writing equations at all: from term three to term seven is seven minus three, four jumps, and the value changed by 26 minus 10, sixteen, so d is sixteen over four. Once d is known, substitute back into either equation to find a — here a plus 8 equals 10, so a is 2. Then run the safety check on the OTHER given term: T7 should be 2 plus six fours, which is 26. It agrees, so both a and d are certified. The pattern generalises: the difference between any two terms equals d times the number of positions between them. That single sentence solves every two-terms-given question in the topic.

**Mandy:** Last one: in the fence post question, why do three inserted means make four gaps? People keep dividing by three.

**Tutor:** Picture it physically, because the mistake dies the moment you draw it. Two corner posts already stand — those are your given numbers, 5 and 25. Now plant three new posts between them. Walk the line and count the spaces: corner to first new post, one; first to second, two; second to third, three; third to the far corner, four. Three posts, four spaces — the walk always has one more space than the number of posts planted, because both ends are already occupied. In sequence language, inserting three means between 5 and 25 builds a five-term sequence, and a five-term sequence has four jumps, so 25 equals 5 plus 4 d and d is 5, not 20 over 3. Dividing by three is really the error of forgetting that the far corner post also needs a jump to reach it. The general rule to memorise: k means inserted gives k plus one jumps, so d equals the total gap divided by k plus one. And after computing d, list the full sequence — 5; 10; 15; 20; 25 — because seeing all five numbers climb evenly is the check that costs nothing and confirms everything.
