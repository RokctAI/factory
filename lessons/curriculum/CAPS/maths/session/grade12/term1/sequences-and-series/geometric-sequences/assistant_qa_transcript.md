**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First one, asked three different ways: how do I tell quickly whether a sequence is arithmetic or geometric, and what if it is neither?

**Tutor:** Run both tests, in that order, and let the numbers decide. Subtract neighbours: a constant answer means arithmetic. If the differences disagree, divide neighbours: a constant answer means geometric. If both tests fail, the sequence may still be the quadratic pattern from earlier grades — check the SECOND differences, and if those are constant you are back in grade eleven territory with a general term of the form a n squared plus b n plus c. Take 2; 6; 18. Differences are 4 and 12 — not arithmetic. Ratios are 3 and 3 — geometric. Now take 2; 6; 12; 20. Differences are 4, 6, 8 — not constant. Ratios are 3, 2, and then 20 over 12 — not constant. Second differences are 2 and 2 — quadratic pattern. The order of testing matters for speed, because subtraction is quicker than division, but the real discipline is testing along the WHOLE list. Many exam sequences are built to pass the first check and fail the second, precisely to catch a learner who tests once and assumes. Two checks minimum, three if the list allows it, and name the type in writing before you build any formula, because the formula you reach for depends entirely on the verdict.

***

**Mandy:** Next: in the two-terms-given questions, why do we divide the equations for geometric but subtract them for arithmetic?

**Tutor:** Because in each case you are choosing the operation that makes the unknown starting value disappear. In an arithmetic sequence the start is ADDED to some number of d's — T3 is a plus 2 d, T7 is a plus 6 d — and the operation that removes an added a is subtraction: the a's cancel and you are left with pure d's. In a geometric sequence the start is MULTIPLIED by powers of r — T2 is a r, T5 is a r to the fourth — and the operation that removes a multiplied a is division: a over a is one, and the powers of r subtract to leave r cubed. Each structure dictates its own eraser. There is also a meaning hiding in the leftover equation. In the arithmetic case, 4 d equals 16 says four jumps climbed sixteen. In the geometric case, r cubed equals 8 says three multiplications grew the value eightfold. In both, the exponent or coefficient equals the number of position steps between the two given terms — five minus two, or seven minus three. Once you see that, you can skip the simultaneous equations entirely: the factor between two known terms equals r raised to the number of steps between them. Divide, take the appropriate root, and only then recover a from either given term.

**Mandy:** Third: in the ball question I wrote 8 times 0,75 to the fourth but my friend wrote it to the power five and got a different answer. Who indexed it right?

**Tutor:** You both might have, and that is exactly why the examiner sets this question — the answer depends on what you decide term one means, and the marks depend on saying it out loud. If you call the drop height of 8 metres term one, then bounce one is term two, and bounce four is term five: T5 equals 8 times 0,75 to the power five minus one, which is 0,75 to the FOURTH — about 2,53 metres. If instead you let the exponent count bounces directly — height after n bounces equals 8 times 0,75 to the power n — then bounce four uses the power four, and you get the same 2,53 metres by a different label. The wrong answer comes from mixing the two schemes: calling the drop term one AND using the power five for bounce four counts an extra bounce, giving about 1,90 metres, which loses the accuracy marks. So the rule is not which indexing to use — both are legal — but to write one clear sentence fixing it, such as: let T1 be the drop height, so bounce n is term n plus one. Then trust that sentence all the way. The formula never makes the off-by-one error; only an unstated indexing does.

**Mandy:** Last one: when r squared equals 9 came up, why must we give minus 3 as well? A negative ratio feels like it should be illegal.

**Tutor:** It feels illegal because most textbook examples grow tidily, but the definition only demands a constant ratio, and minus 3 passes that test perfectly. Build the sequence and watch it: start at 2, multiply by minus 3 repeatedly, and you get 2; minus 6; 18; minus 54. Divide any neighbour pair and you get minus 3 every time — constant, therefore geometric. The sequence alternates in sign while growing in size, which is unusual to the eye but flawless to the definition. Now, why must you OFFER both? Because taking the square root of both sides of r squared equals 9 is exactly the situation from the algebra of earlier grades: two numbers square to 9, and discarding one without a reason is an incomplete solution — typically a lost mark labelled both answers required. The question itself is the only thing that can eliminate a sign. If it says all terms are positive, minus 3 dies, because a negative ratio forces alternating signs. If it says the sequence is increasing, same verdict. If it says nothing, both ratios stand, and each generates its own middle term in a means question — so you present two sequences, not one. The habit to build: every even root in this topic triggers the sentence, r equals plus or minus, followed by a check of which signs the question allows.
