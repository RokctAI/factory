**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First one, and it comes up a lot: why is a exactly HALF the second difference? Some of us just memorise it and then panic when we forget which way the halving goes.

**Tutor:** Build it once and you will never need the memory again. Write the general term at the first three positions: T1 is a plus b plus c, T2 is 4 a plus 2 b plus c, T3 is 9 a plus 3 b plus c. Now subtract to get the first differences: T2 minus T1 is 3 a plus b, and T3 minus T2 is 5 a plus b. Subtract again for the second difference: 5 a plus b, minus 3 a plus b, leaves 2 a. So the second difference IS 2 a — always, for every quadratic pattern — and that is why a is half of it. If the halving direction ever deserts you in an exam, test with the simplest quadratic you know: T n equals n squared gives 1; 4; 9; 16, first differences 3; 5; 7, second difference 2. There a is 1 and the second difference is 2 — the second difference is the doubled one. Ten seconds of reconstruction beats a memorised rule that can flip in your head, and the same subtraction hands you the other two equations for free: 3 a plus b is the first gap, and a plus b plus c is the first term.

**Mandy:** Next: when a question says find which term equals 190, we get two answers from the quadratic equation, like 9 and minus 10,5. People lose a mark just for how they write the rejection. What exactly should go on the page?

**Tutor:** One short sentence next to the discarded root: n must be a natural number, so n equals minus 10,5 is rejected, and n equals 9. The reason matters, not just the crossing out, because the marker wants evidence that you know positions live in the counting numbers — there is a first term, a ninth term, never a minus-ten-comma-fifth term. Notice the rejection has two possible triggers, and you should name the one that applies. A negative root fails because positions cannot be negative. A fractional root, like 11,5 when testing whether 300 is a term, fails because positions cannot sit between whole numbers — and in that case the fraction is the entire answer: since n is not a natural number, 300 is not a term. Then, if you have a moment, show the jump: term eleven is 276 and term twelve is 325, so the pattern steps clean over 300. That one line of evidence turns a bald statement into an argument, and it is exactly the reasoning the memo rewards. What you may never do is round 11,5 up to 12 — that claims 325 and 300 are the same number.

**Mandy:** Third one, from the homework: in the pattern x; 13; 30; 53, why are we allowed to subtract terms that still have an x inside? It feels illegal.

**Tutor:** It is completely legal, because subtraction does not care whether a symbol or a number rides along — it only cares about the structure. The first difference is 13 minus x whether or not you know x yet; the next two are 17 and 23, pure numbers. Then the second differences are 17 minus the quantity 13 minus x, which simplifies to 4 plus x, and 23 minus 17, which is 6. The question TELLS you the pattern is quadratic with second difference 6, and a quadratic pattern has one constant second difference, so 4 plus x must equal 6, and x is 2. The letter was never an obstacle — it was a passenger that the difference table carried until an equation formed around it. The discipline is the same as everywhere else in this topic: substitute back and read the honest table. Terms 2; 13; 30; 53, first differences 11; 17; 23, second differences 6 and 6. If the second differences had come out unequal, that would be the signal of an algebra slip — usually a dropped sign when subtracting the bracket, because minus the quantity 13 minus x is minus 13 plus x, both signs flipped.

**Mandy:** Last one: for the smallest term of n squared minus 12 n plus 40, someone in class used the parabola's turning point and got position six. But a pattern is dots, not a smooth curve — why is the vertex allowed at all?

**Tutor:** The vertex is allowed as a guide, and here it happens to be exact. The pattern's values sit ON the parabola y equals x squared minus 12 x plus 40, but only at the natural-number positions — the dots, as you say. The parabola bottoms out where n equals minus b over 2 a, which is 12 over 2, position six. Because six is itself a natural number, one of the dots sits exactly at the bottom of the curve, and T6 equals 36 minus 72 plus 40, which is 4. That is the smallest term, and the values on either side confirm it: term five is 5 and term seven is 5, both larger. The care comes when the vertex lands BETWEEN whole positions — say the formula gives n equals 6,5. Then no dot sits at the bottom, and the smallest term is whichever of T6 or T7 is smaller; by symmetry they may even tie. So the drill is: compute the vertex position, send it through the natural-number gate, and if it fails, test the whole numbers on either side. The parabola is the map; the pattern only lives at the numbered stops.
