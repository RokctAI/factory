# Part 1 — Expert

The arithmetic sequence climbs by adding. Its sibling multiplies instead, and that single change of operation produces the mathematics of interest, inflation, population growth and radioactive decay. This session builds the geometric sequence properly: the ratio test that certifies it, the general term with its exponent, the algebra of geometric means and hidden unknowns, and the modelling questions where multiplication runs the world.

## Subtopic: The Constant Ratio Test

A geometric sequence is a list in which each term is the previous term times a fixed number. That fixed number is the common ratio, written r, and it is found by dividing: any term divided by the term before it. Take 2; 6; 18; 54. Six over two is 3. Eighteen over six is 3. Fifty-four over eighteen is 3. Constant ratio, so the sequence is geometric with r equal to 3.

The same disciplines as the arithmetic case apply, translated from subtraction to division. Direction: r is the second term over the first, never the reverse. And thoroughness: one matching ratio proves nothing — check the quotient along the list. The sequence 1; 2; 4; 6 starts geometric and then breaks, because 6 over 4 is 1,5, not 2.

The ratio does not have to be a whole number, and it does not have to grow the sequence. The sequence 80; 40; 20; 10 has r equal to a half — each term is half the one before, and the sequence decays. A negative ratio is also legal: 3; minus 6; 12; minus 24 has r equal to minus 2, and the signs alternate forever. What r cannot be is zero, and no term of a geometric sequence may be zero, because dividing by zero would destroy the ratio test itself.

Pause here — the questions for this section are with you now. Divide neighbour by neighbour, check the quotient more than once, and let fractions, negatives and decay all pass the test on equal terms.

## Subtopic: The General Term T n Equals a r to the Power n Minus One

Watch the structure grow. T1 is a. T2 is a times r. T3 is a times r squared. T4 is a times r cubed. The exponent on r trails the position by one, for the same reason as the arithmetic n minus one: the first term is a start, not a step. So T n equals a times r to the power n minus one.

Use it forwards. For 2; 6; 18; 54, T n equals 2 times 3 to the power n minus one. Test on a visible term: T4 should be 54, and 2 times 3 cubed is 2 times 27, which is 54. Confirmed. Term ten is 2 times 3 to the ninth — 3 to the ninth is 19 683, so T10 is 39 366. Notice the size: multiplication runs away from addition very quickly, which is the whole character of this topic.

Use it backwards. Which term of the sequence equals 1458? Set 2 times 3 to the power n minus one equal to 1458, so 3 to the power n minus one equals 729. Now 729 is 3 to the sixth — force both sides to the same base, exactly the exponential equation technique from earlier grades. So n minus one is 6 and n is 7: 1458 is the seventh term.

And recover a and r from two given terms. Suppose T2 is 6 and T5 is 48. Then a r equals 6, and a r to the fourth equals 48. DIVIDE the equations — division is to geometric what subtraction is to arithmetic — and a cancels: r cubed equals 8, so r is 2, and a is 3. From position two to position five is three multiplications, and the value grew by a factor of eight: two, cubed. The general term is T n equals 3 times 2 to the power n minus one, and checking on the other given term, T5 equals 3 times 16, which is 48. Correct.

Stop for this section's questions now — build the general term, test it on a term you can see, and when two terms are given, divide them and read off a power of r.

## Subtopic: Geometric Means and Unknowns in the Terms

Three consecutive terms of a geometric sequence obey one equation: the ratio between the first pair equals the ratio between the second pair. Cross-multiplied, the middle term squared equals the product of its neighbours. That is the geometric counterpart of the equal-gaps rule.

Worked case. The terms x; x plus 6; x plus 9 are consecutive terms of a geometric sequence — find x. Middle squared equals product of neighbours: the quantity x plus 6, squared, equals x times the quantity x plus 9. Expand: x squared plus 12 x plus 36 equals x squared plus 9 x. The x squared terms cancel, leaving 3 x equal to minus 36, so x is minus 12. Substitute back — always — and the terms are minus 12; minus 6; minus 3. Check the ratio both ways: minus 6 over minus 12 is a half, and minus 3 over minus 6 is a half. A genuine geometric sequence with r equal to one half, living entirely in negative numbers, which is perfectly legal.

Geometric means are the inserted numbers that make a list multiply evenly. Insert two geometric means between 4 and 108. Two insertions make 4 the first term and 108 the fourth, so 108 equals 4 times r cubed, giving r cubed equal to 27 and r equal to 3. The sequence is 4; 12; 36; 108, and the means are 12 and 36. As with arithmetic means, count jumps as means plus one — two means, three multiplications.

One caution the marker watches for: when r comes from an even root, both signs may be possible. If r squared equals 9, then r is 3 or minus 3, and each sign produces its own valid sequence. State both unless the question forces one — for instance by saying all terms are positive.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — middle squared equals neighbour product, substitute back to see the real terms, and respect both signs when an even root sets the ratio.

## Subtopic: Multiplication Models the World

Geometric sequences appear wherever change is proportional to size: interest on money, inflation on prices, populations breeding, substances decaying, a ball losing a fixed fraction of its bounce. The translation questions are the same three as before. Starting value: a. Multiplier per step: r. And what is asked — a term's value or a position.

Worked case. A ball is dropped from 8 metres, and each bounce reaches three quarters of the previous height. The heights form a geometric sequence: 8; 6; 4,5; and so on, with a equal to 8 and r equal to 0,75. The height after the fourth bounce is T5 in sequence language — the drop height is term one, so bounce four is position five. T5 equals 8 times 0,75 to the fourth. Now 0,75 to the fourth is 0,31640625, so T5 is 2,53125 — about 2,53 metres. The off-by-one trap here is vicious: decide explicitly whether the starting value is term one or term zero style, write that decision down, and the exponent looks after itself.

Second case, growth. A culture of 500 bacteria doubles every hour, so after n hours the count is 500 times 2 to the power n — here the natural indexing starts at zero hours, and the exponent equals the number of doublings, cleanly. After 10 hours: 500 times 1024, which is 512 000. The lesson inside the lesson: choose the indexing that matches the story, state it, and stay consistent.

Percentages convert to ratios in one move: growth of 6 percent per year means r equal to 1,06; decay of 15 percent means r equal to 0,85. The ratio is one plus or one minus the rate — the same object that finance calls one plus i, which is exactly where this topic is heading later in the year.

The final questions of this part are with you now — name the multiplier, fix the indexing in writing, and convert every percentage into its ratio before touching the formula.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same geometric sequences from a photocopier and a chain of forwarded messages — same rules, same answers, built from things you can picture.

## Subtopic: The Photocopier Set to Seventy-Five Percent

Put a poster on a photocopier set to reduce to 75 percent, copy the copy, then copy that. Each page is three quarters the size of the one before. That is a geometric sequence: not equal steps like a staircase, but equal SHRINKS — or equal stretches, if the machine enlarges. The fixed setting on the machine is the common ratio r.

How do you check a sequence is geometric? Divide each term by the one before it, the way you would compare each copy with its original. Same quotient everywhere: geometric. In 2; 6; 18; 54, every division gives 3 — the machine is set to triple. In 1; 2; 4; 6, the first divisions give 2 but the last gives 1,5 — someone changed the setting, so the sequence is not geometric.

The setting can shrink: 80; 40; 20; 10 divides to a half every time. The setting can even flip the page over: 3; minus 6; 12; minus 24 multiplies by minus 2, so the sign alternates like a page copied front-back-front-back. The only forbidden setting is zero — one zero term and every later term is zero, and division can no longer even ask the question.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on spotting the ratio are coming to you right now. Divide neighbour by neighbour, and expect fractions, negatives and shrinking as normal citizens.

## Subtopic: One Message, a Chain of Forwards

A message goes to 2 people. Each of them forwards it to 3 more. Round one: 2 people. Round two: 6. Round three: 18. To ask how many people receive the message in round ten, you do not need to count rounds one by one — you need the start, 2, times the multiplier 3 applied nine times. That is the general term: T n equals a times r to the power n minus one. Nine, not ten, because round one is the start, and the forwarding only begins after it.

The exponent is what separates this from the arithmetic world. Adding 3 nine times gives 27 more. MULTIPLYING by 3 nine times gives 19 683 times more — round ten reaches 39 366 people from a start of two. Multiplication does not climb; it explodes. That explosion is why the same mathematics prices compound interest.

The formula also runs in reverse. Which round reaches exactly 1458 people? Set 2 times 3 to the power n minus one equal to 1458. Divide by 2: 3 to the power n minus one equals 729. Now count your threes: 729 is 3 to the sixth. So n minus one is 6, and n is 7 — round seven. Same-base thinking, straight out of the exponents toolbox you already own.

And when two rounds are known but the start is not — say round two reached 6 and round five reached 48 — divide the big fact by the small one. The start cancels, leaving r cubed equal to 8: three forwards multiplied the reach by eight, so each forward multiplies by 2, and the start must have been 3.

Your questions for this part are up now. Start times multiplier to the power jumps, and when two terms are known, divide them and let the start cancel itself.

## Subtopic: The Fair Middle, Multiplied

The arithmetic world had a fair middle: the number halfway between two others. The geometric world has one too, but fairness now means equal MULTIPLICATION. Between 4 and 108, what numbers make the list multiply evenly? Two inserted numbers make three multiplications, so the machine setting r obeys r cubed equals 27 — r is 3, and the list is 4; 12; 36; 108. The inserted 12 and 36 are geometric means.

For three terms in a row there is a shortcut worth owning: the middle term squared equals the product of its neighbours. Check it on 4; 12; 36 — twelve squared is 144, and 4 times 36 is 144. That identity is the engine for every question with an x inside the terms. Given x; x plus 6; x plus 9 geometric, write the quantity x plus 6, squared, equals x times the quantity x plus 9. The x squared on each side cancels and x is minus 12, making the real terms minus 12; minus 6; minus 3 — each term half the one before. Negative terms, ratio one half, all perfectly healthy. The habit that saves the mark is substituting back and dividing to SEE the constant ratio.

One last care. If the algebra gives r squared equals 9, the machine setting could be 3 or minus 3, and both build honest sequences — one steady, one alternating in sign. Offer both unless the question rules one out.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: middle squared equals neighbours multiplied, jumps count as means plus one, and an even root on r means two possible machines until the question chooses.
