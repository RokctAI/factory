**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First, the one asked most often: from k equals 3 to k equals 20, why is it eighteen terms and not seventeen? Subtraction says seventeen.

**Tutor:** Subtraction measures the DISTANCE between the two counter values, and distance is not the same as a count of occupants. Walk it slowly: k takes the values 3, 4, 5, and so on up to 20. If it were only the values 3 and 4, subtraction gives one, but two values of k were used — the starting value itself is a term, and subtraction never counts the place you started from. So the count is top minus bottom, plus one for the starting post. From 3 to 20: seventeen steps of travel, eighteen values visited. It is the same fence-post fact from the arithmetic means question — three posts between two corners make four spaces — just running in the other direction: spaces are subtraction, posts are subtraction plus one, and sigma counts posts. A concrete check whenever you are unsure: shrink the limits to something you can list on your fingers. From k equals 3 to k equals 5 gives 3, 4, 5 — three terms, and five minus three plus one is three. If your rule survives the tiny case, trust it on the big one. That thirty-second sanity test settles every counting dispute this notation ever causes.

**Mandy:** Next: for the how-many-terms questions, my quadratic gave n equals 10 and n equals about minus 10,3. I know to reject the negative, but must I really write a reason?

**Tutor:** Yes, and the habit pays in two currencies: a mark, and protection against the rarer case where the rejection is less obvious. The variable n was DEFINED as a number of terms — a count of envelopes, bricks, payments. A count lives in the natural numbers: positive whole values only. So minus 10,3 fails on two grounds at once, negativity and non-wholeness, and the marker wants the sentence, n must be a positive natural number, therefore n equals 10. Here is the subtle case that makes the habit worth building: sometimes a quadratic in n delivers two POSITIVE roots, say 4 and 15, and both are whole. Then you cannot reject either on sign, and the question decides — a sum can genuinely reach the same total at two different lengths when the terms turn negative partway, because the running total climbs, peaks, and comes back down through the same value. In an examination, both answers stand unless the context forbids one. If you have trained yourself to interrogate every root instead of automatically crossing one out, you bank that question too. The mechanical version of the discipline: solve the quadratic fully, test each root against the definition of n, and write one line per root — kept or rejected, with the reason.

**Mandy:** Third: the derivation of the geometric sum formula, where we multiply by r and subtract. How does that not lose information, and what should we actually memorise?

**Tutor:** Watch what the two lines contain, term by term, because the trick is precision, not magic. S n is a, plus a r, plus a r squared, up to a r to the power n minus one. Multiply every term by r and the line shifts one position along: r S n is a r, plus a r squared, up to a r to the power n. The two lines now share almost everything — a r through a r to the n minus one appear in both. Subtracting cancels exactly that shared middle, leaving only the head of the first line, a, and the tail of the second, a r to the n. No information is lost; the shared terms genuinely contribute nothing to the DIFFERENCE of the two sums. Then factorising both sides gives S n times one minus r equals a times one minus r to the n, and dividing by one minus r — legal precisely because r is not one — delivers the formula. As for memorising: memorise the MOVE, not the final line. The derivation is examinable in its own right, stated as, derive the formula for the sum of a geometric series, and learners who only memorised the result score zero on it. The move is three sentences: write the sum, multiply through by r, subtract and factorise. Practise writing it out twice and it becomes the easiest guaranteed marks in the paper — and if the formula ever slips your memory mid-exam, thirty seconds of the move rebuilds it from nothing.

**Mandy:** Last one: the sum to infinity feels like a trick. How can infinitely many positive numbers possibly stop at 16 — should the total not grow forever?

**Tutor:** The total does grow forever — it just does not grow WITHOUT BOUND, and the difference between those two ideas is the entire concept. Look at the running totals of 8 plus 4 plus 2 plus 1: they are 8, then 12, then 14, then 15, then 15 comma 5. Growing at every step, yes. But measure what remains to 16: first 8, then 4, then 2, then 1, then a half. Each step closes exactly half the remaining gap, so the gap never reaches zero in finitely many steps — and also never stops shrinking. The totals climb toward 16 like someone walking half the remaining distance to a wall: always moving, never arriving, never passing. Mathematics defines the sum of the infinite series as the value the running totals approach, and that value is exactly 16 — not roughly, exactly, because no smaller number is ever exceeded for good and every number below 16 eventually is. The formula a over one minus r computes that destination in one line: 8 over one minus a half. Now flip the condition around to see why it matters: with ratio 1, the takings are 8, 8, 8, and the totals march past every wall ever built — no destination, no sum. The condition minus one less than r less than one is precisely the requirement that the leftover r to the n dies out. And the honest miracle you can hold in your hand is zero comma five five five repeating: infinitely many contributions, sum exactly five ninths, and your calculator has believed it all along.
