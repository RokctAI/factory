# Part 1 — Expert

Grade eleven ended with quadratic number patterns — sequences whose second difference is constant. Grade twelve opens by returning to the simplest pattern of all and arming it properly: the arithmetic sequence, where the FIRST difference is constant. Four pieces of equipment by the end: the test that certifies a sequence as arithmetic, the general term that reaches any position without counting, the algebra for unknowns hiding inside a sequence, and the discipline of turning a word problem into a term number.

## Subtopic: The Constant Difference Test

An arithmetic sequence is a list of numbers in which each term is the previous term plus a fixed number. That fixed number is the common difference, written d, and it is found by subtracting: any term minus the term before it. Take the sequence 3; 7; 11; 15. Seven minus three is four. Eleven minus seven is four. Fifteen minus eleven is four. Constant difference, so the sequence is arithmetic with d equal to 4.

Two disciplines attach to that subtraction. First, direction: d is the second term minus the first, never the first minus the second. The sequence 20; 17; 14 has d equal to minus 3 — a negative difference is perfectly legal and simply means the sequence is decreasing. Second, thoroughness: check the difference more than once. The sequence 2; 4; 8 passes the first check, since 4 minus 2 is 2, but fails the second, because 8 minus 4 is 4. One matching gap proves nothing; a constant gap across the whole list is the definition.

The standard labels, used in every question: the first term is a, the common difference is d, and the term in position n is called T n. So for 3; 7; 11; 15, a is 3, d is 4, T1 is 3 and T4 is 15.

Pause here — the questions for this section are with you now. Subtract neighbouring terms in the correct order, check the gap at least twice, and let a negative d decrease in peace.

## Subtopic: The General Term T n Equals a Plus n Minus One, Times d

Listing terms one by one works until a question asks for term two hundred. The general term is the machine that jumps straight there. Build it by watching the structure: T1 is a. T2 is a plus one d. T3 is a plus two d. T4 is a plus three d. The number of d's is always one less than the position, so T n equals a plus the quantity n minus one, times d.

Use it forwards. For 3; 7; 11; 15, T n equals 3 plus the quantity n minus one, times 4, which expands to 4 n minus 1. Test it on a term you can see: T4 should be 15, and four times four minus one is 15. Always run that test — it costs seconds and catches almost every slip. Now term two hundred is four times two hundred, minus one: 799. No listing, no counting.

Use it backwards, which is where the marks live. Is 99 a term of this sequence, and if so, which one? Set 4 n minus 1 equal to 99, so 4 n is 100 and n is 25. Yes: 99 is the twenty-fifth term. Now try 130: 4 n minus 1 equals 130 gives n equal to 131 over 4, which is 32,75. A position must be a natural number — there is no term thirty-two comma seven five — so 130 is NOT in the sequence. Writing that sentence, n is not a natural number so 130 is not a term, earns the final mark.

Third use: recovering a and d from two given terms. Suppose T3 is 10 and T7 is 26. Then a plus 2 d equals 10, and a plus 6 d equals 26. Subtract the equations: 4 d equals 16, so d is 4 and a is 2. From term three to term seven is four jumps, and the value climbed sixteen — the subtraction says exactly that. The general term is T n equals 4 n minus 2, and checking, T7 is four times seven minus two, 26. Correct.

Stop for this section's questions now — build T n, test it against a visible term, and when a question asks whether a number belongs, demand that n comes out a natural number.

## Subtopic: Unknowns in the Terms and Arithmetic Means

Examiners love handing you a sequence with a variable inside. The terms x plus 1; 2 x minus 3; x plus 9 are consecutive terms of an arithmetic sequence — find x. The whole solution is one sentence: in an arithmetic sequence, consecutive gaps are equal. Second term minus first term equals third term minus second term. So 2 x minus 3, minus the quantity x plus 1, equals x plus 9, minus the quantity 2 x minus 3. The left side simplifies to x minus 4. The right side simplifies to minus x plus 12. Set them equal: x minus 4 equals minus x plus 12, so 2 x equals 16 and x is 8.

Never stop at x. Substitute back: the terms are 9; 13; 17, with common difference 4 — a genuine arithmetic sequence, which is the check and usually a mark. The classic error in this question type is dropping a sign when subtracting a bracket: minus the quantity x plus 1 is minus x minus 1, both signs flipped.

The same equal-gaps idea powers arithmetic means — the numbers inserted between two given values to make an arithmetic sequence. Insert three arithmetic means between 5 and 25. With three numbers inserted, 5 is the first term and 25 is the fifth, so 25 equals 5 plus 4 d, giving d equal to 5. The sequence is 5; 10; 15; 20; 25, and the means are 10, 15 and 20. Count carefully: inserting three means creates a sequence of five terms and FOUR jumps. The number of jumps is always the number of means plus one.

A single arithmetic mean between two numbers is their halfway point — the mean of 4 and 10 is 7, which is also their average. That is why the word mean appears at all.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — equal gaps give the equation, substitution gives the check, and inserted means always create one more jump than mean.

## Subtopic: From Word Problem to Term Number

Arithmetic sequences price anything that grows by a fixed amount per step: wages with a fixed annual increase, stacked chairs, seats per row in a stadium, a contract that adds the same fee each month. The translation is always the same three questions. What is the starting value? That is a. What is the fixed change per step? That is d. What is being asked — a term's value, or the position where a value is reached?

Worked case. A technician earns 120 000 rand in her first year, and her salary rises by 8 000 rand every year. What does she earn in year six, and in which year does the salary first reach 200 000 rand? Here a is 120 000 and d is 8 000, so T n equals 120 000 plus the quantity n minus one, times 8 000. Year six: T6 equals 120 000 plus five times 8 000, which is 160 000 rand. For the second part, set T n equal to 200 000: the quantity n minus one, times 8 000, equals 80 000, so n minus one is 10 and n is 11. The salary reaches 200 000 rand in year eleven.

Watch two traps. The first is the off-by-one: year six uses five increases, not six, because the first year carries no increase — the formula's n minus one handles it, but only if you trust the formula rather than your fingers. The second is the inequality version: if the question asks when the salary first EXCEEDS some amount that no term hits exactly, n comes out as a decimal and the answer is the next whole year up. Position numbers round up to the next term, never to the nearest.

The final questions of this part are with you now — name a, name d, write the general term, and make n answer the actual question asked.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same arithmetic sequences from a taxi rank and a payslip — same rules, same answers, built from things you can picture.

## Subtopic: Steps of the Same Size

Picture a long staircase where every step is exactly the same height. That is the whole idea of an arithmetic sequence. The bottom step is the first term. The height of each step is the common difference, d. Climb the staircase and the numbers you pass are the terms: 3, then 7, then 11, then 15 — every step lifts you by 4.

How do you check a staircase? You measure the gaps. Subtract each number from the one after it. If every gap measures the same, the sequence is arithmetic. If even one gap disagrees — like 2; 4; 8, where the first gap is 2 but the next is 4 — the staircase is broken and the sequence is not arithmetic. One measurement is never enough; a builder checks every step.

Stairs can also go down. The sequence 20; 17; 14 drops by 3 each time, so d is minus 3. A negative d is not an error — it is a staircase into a cellar, and the subtraction must be done in the right order to see it: second number minus first number. Seventeen minus twenty is minus three. Do it backwards and you will climb when you should be descending.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on spotting the constant step are coming to you right now. Measure the gap, measure it again, and keep the subtraction in order: the later term minus the earlier one.

## Subtopic: Jumping Straight to Term Two Hundred

Suppose the taxi fare from the rank is 3 rand for the first kilometre and 4 rand for every kilometre after that. One kilometre costs 3. Two kilometres cost 7. Three cost 11. You could count your way to any distance — or you could think like the driver: start with 3, then add 4 for every kilometre AFTER the first. Two hundred kilometres is 3 plus one hundred and ninety-nine fours.

That sentence is the general term. T n equals a plus the quantity n minus one, times d. The n minus one is not a decoration — it is the fact that the first kilometre is not an add-on; the adding only starts after it. Count the additions on your fingers for small cases: term four is the start plus THREE additions, and four times four minus one is indeed 15.

The formula also answers the reverse question, which is the one exams prefer. You have 99 rand — how far can you ride? Set 4 n minus 1 equal to 99: n is 25, so exactly twenty-five kilometres. But try 130 rand: 4 n minus 1 equals 130 gives n equal to 32,75. There is no kilometre number thirty-two comma seven five. The fare meter simply never reads 130 — it jumps from 127 to 131. When n refuses to be a whole number, the value is not in the sequence, and saying so is the answer.

Your questions for this part are up now. Start plus jumps times step, test the formula on a term you can already see, and let a broken n tell you the number was never on the meter.

## Subtopic: The Missing Rung and the Fair Middle

Sometimes a question hands you a ladder with a rung painted over: three terms in a row, but with an x hiding inside each, like x plus 1; 2 x minus 3; x plus 9. The rescue is one fact — on a true ladder, the gap below a rung equals the gap above it. Middle minus first must equal last minus middle. Write that equation, solve it, and x is 8, making the real rungs 9; 13; 17. Then always look at the actual numbers you found: gaps of 4 and 4 — the ladder is straight, the answer is confirmed.

The same fairness idea explains arithmetic means. Insert three numbers between 5 and 25 so the whole list climbs evenly, like planting three fence posts between two corner posts so all the spacing matches. Five posts means four equal spaces, and 20 metres of run shared over four spaces is 5 each: 5; 10; 15; 20; 25. The inserted numbers, 10, 15 and 20, are the arithmetic means. The counting trap lives here — three inserted posts create FOUR gaps, always one more gap than posts.

And a single mean between two numbers is just the fair middle: the mean of 4 and 10 is 7, exactly the average you have calculated since primary school. Same word, same idea — a number chosen so nobody can say the spacing favours either side.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: equal gaps above and below the middle term, substitute x back to see the real sequence, and count one more gap than the number of means you insert.
