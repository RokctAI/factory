**Mandy:** Class questions before we finish! Top of the pile: from k equals 4 to 25, why are there twenty-two terms and not twenty-one? The subtraction says twenty-one.

**Tutor:** The subtraction measures the distance between the endpoints; the count includes both endpoints, and those are different things. Walk it physically: point at box four and start counting — four is one, five is two, six is three. By the time your finger lands on box twenty-five, you have counted twenty-two boxes, because the first box was counted as one, not as zero. Twenty-five minus four is twenty-one steps BETWEEN boxes, but a step count and a box count always differ by exactly one. Hence the rule: top minus bottom, plus one. It is the same fence-post arithmetic that ruled inserted means — posts versus spaces, boxes versus steps. In an exam, the plus one is worth checking twice, because a sum computed over the wrong number of terms is wrong everywhere afterwards even if every formula is right. When in doubt, shrink the range until you can count on fingers: from k equals 4 to 6 is 4, 5, 6 — three terms, and six minus four plus one is three. The small case certifies the rule, and the rule then scales to any range too long to touch.

**Mandy:** Next: in the geometric sum proof, what exactly cancels when we subtract r S n from S n, and why does that even help?

**Tutor:** Line the two rows up and watch the columns. S n is a, plus a r, plus a r squared, all the way to a r to the n minus one. Multiply by r and every term slides one column right: r S n is a r, plus a r squared, up to a r to the n. Now subtract. The a r terms cancel, the a r squared terms cancel, every middle term finds its twin in the other row and vanishes — a telescope folding shut. Two survivors remain: the a at the front of the first row, which the second row never had, and the a r to the n at the back of the second row, which the first row never reached. So S n minus r S n equals a minus a r to the n, and factorising both sides hands you the formula in one line. The help is enormous: a sum of n terms became an expression with just two. And the proof carries a warning label — if r equals one, the second row is identical to the first, the subtraction gives zero equals zero, and the formula's denominator collapses. That is not a defect; it is the algebra telling you that with r equal to one every term is a, and the sum is simply n times a.

**Mandy:** Third one: how many terms of 3 plus 7 plus 11 make 210 — the quadratic gave two answers, and someone asked why we don't keep both like we usually do.

**Tutor:** Because n is not an ordinary unknown; it is a headcount. The equation n over 2, times 6 plus 4 n minus 4, equals 210 tidies to 2 n squared plus n minus 210 equals zero, and the quadratic formula with the root of 1681, which is 41, delivers n equals 10 or n equals minus ten and a half. In pure algebra both satisfy the equation, and if this were a geometry parabola we would keep both. But the question asks how many terms are being added, and you cannot add minus ten and a half terms — a count is a positive natural number or it is nothing. So the negative root is rejected, and the rejection must be WRITTEN: something like, n must be a positive natural number, so n equals 10. That sentence is not decoration; it is usually a mark, because it shows the marker you interrogated the algebra instead of copying it. Then close the loop with the check: S 10 equals 5 times the quantity 6 plus 36, which is 210. Ten envelopes, ten terms, certified. The pattern to memorise: every how-many question ends with a quadratic or an exponent equation, and every one of them ends with a sentence executing the impossible root.

**Mandy:** Last one: the sum to infinity feels like cheating. How can infinitely many positive numbers stop at 40,5 instead of growing forever?

**Tutor:** Because growing forever requires the pieces to keep their size, and these pieces do not — each is a third of the one before. Watch the running totals for 27 plus 9 plus 3 plus 1: they read 27, then 36, then 39, then 40, then 40 and a third. Each new term closes two thirds of the gap that remains to 40,5, so the total is always moving and never arriving — trapped below 40,5 yet creeping as close as anyone demands. That trapped-but-creeping behaviour is what convergence means, and the formula a over one minus r names the ceiling exactly: 27 over two thirds is 40,5. The condition minus one strictly less than r strictly less than one is the entire boundary between this and chaos. At r equal to one the terms stop shrinking and the total marches off to infinity; at r equal to minus one the totals bounce between two values forever and never choose. So before promising any infinite total, check the ratio at the door. And enjoy the payoff: zero comma seven seven seven repeating is a converged series with a equal to seven tenths and r equal to one tenth, and the formula quietly returns seven ninths — infinity, domesticated into a fraction.
