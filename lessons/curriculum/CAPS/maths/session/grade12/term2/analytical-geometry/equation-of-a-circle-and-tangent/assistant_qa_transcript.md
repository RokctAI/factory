**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First one, and it comes up a lot: in the circle equation, why do the signs of the centre flip? Half the class reads x minus 3 squared and writes the centre as minus 3.

**Tutor:** Go back to what the equation actually records, because the flip stops being a rule and becomes obvious. The circle equation is the distance formula: the distance from a roaming point x and y to the centre. Distance is measured by SUBTRACTION — roaming coordinate minus centre coordinate. So if the centre's x coordinate is 3, the equation must contain x minus 3; the 3 you see inside the bracket already has the subtraction applied to it. Reading it back, you ask: what value of x makes this bracket zero? x equals positive 3 — that is where the centre sits. Same logic for y plus 2: rewrite it as y minus negative 2, and the bracket dies at y equals minus 2, so the centre's y coordinate is minus 2. The safest habit is exactly that question — what makes each bracket zero? — asked twice, every time. It never fails, and it also protects you on the radius side of the equation, where the second classic misread lives: the right side is r SQUARED, so 25 means a radius of 5. Bracket-killers for the centre, square root for the radius.

***

**Mandy:** Next: when we complete the square, where do those extra numbers on the right side actually come from? Some of us just memorise add 9, add 4 without knowing why, and then forget one.

**Tutor:** They come from the brackets being slightly dishonest, and the extra numbers are the honesty payment. When you replace x squared minus 6 x with the bracket x minus 3 squared, expand that bracket and see what it really contains: x squared minus 6 x PLUS 9. The bracket includes a 9 that the original expression never had — you did not simplify, you smuggled. To keep the equation true, that smuggled 9 must be paid back, either by writing minus 9 next to the bracket or by adding 9 to the other side; the two are the same accounting. The y bracket smuggles a 4 the same way. That is why the right side of our example grew from 12 to 12 plus 9 plus 4, which is 25. Forget one payment and the equation still LOOKS respectable but describes a different circle — the centre survives, but the radius silently changes, and every later answer inherits the damage. The self-check is cheap: expand your completed form fully and match it against the original, term by term, or substitute one convenient point into both versions. If the accounting balances, the circle is genuine.

***

**Mandy:** Third one, from the homework: why does the tangent get its gradient from the radius, of all things? The connection between the two feels like it appears from nowhere.

**Tutor:** The connection is one geometric theorem, and the wheel makes it visible. A tangent touches the circle at exactly one point, and the theorem says the radius drawn to that touching point is perpendicular to the tangent — spoke square to the road. Why must it be? Because the radius measures the shortest path from the centre to the tangent line, and the shortest path from a point to a line is always the perpendicular one. If the spoke met the road at any other angle, some point of the road would sit closer to the hub than the touching point — closer than one radius — meaning the road had cut INSIDE the wheel and crossed it twice, contradicting the single touch. So perpendicularity is not decoration; it is what touching once means. Coordinates then translate the theorem: perpendicular lines have gradients multiplying to minus 1, so tangent gradient equals the negative reciprocal of radius gradient. The radius gradient costs one rise-over-run from centre to touching point, and the tangent inherits everything else. That is why the routine never varies: point on circle, radius gradient, flip and negate, point-gradient form.

***

**Mandy:** Last one: a test question asked whether the line y equals minus 3 over 4 x plus 13 over 2 is a tangent to our circle, and some of us did not know what would count as a full answer. What is the marker looking for?

**Tutor:** The marker wants perpendicularity at a verified touching point — two facts, each earned. Route one, the geometric route, is fastest when the touching point is known or suspected. First verify the point lies on BOTH the line and the circle: 6 and 2 satisfies the circle since 9 plus 16 is 25, and satisfies the line since minus 4,5 plus 6,5 is 2. Then verify the right angle: radius gradient from 3 and minus 2 to 6 and 2 is 4 over 3; the line's gradient is minus 3 over 4; their product is minus 1. Point shared, angle square — tangent confirmed, both facts stated in words. Route two, the algebraic route, needs no candidate point: substitute the line into the circle equation and show the resulting quadratic has exactly ONE solution — a zero discriminant. One solution means one meeting point, which is the definition of tangency. Either route earns full marks; what earns half marks is showing only the gradient product, because a line can be perpendicular to some radius without ever touching the circle, or only the shared point, because a line through one circle point could be a secant leaning through it. Touching once and standing square: prove both, and say both.
