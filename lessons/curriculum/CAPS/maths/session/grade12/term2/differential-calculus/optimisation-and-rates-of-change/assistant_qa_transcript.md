**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First: in the motion questions, I mix up which derivative answers which question. Is there a fixed map from the words to the symbols?

**Tutor:** There is, and it is worth writing at the top of every motion answer. Three rows: position s of t — where the object is; velocity, the first derivative — how fast position changes, sign carrying direction; acceleration, the second derivative — how fast velocity changes. Now map the exam phrases onto the rows. Distance after three seconds: substitute into s itself, no differentiation. Velocity after three seconds: differentiate once, then substitute. Initial velocity: differentiate once, substitute t equals zero. Momentarily at rest, or stationary for an instant: set the FIRST derivative to zero and solve for t. Maximum height, for a projectile: also first derivative zero, because height is the position function and its peak is a turning point. When is velocity constant, or when does acceleration vanish: second derivative zero. The pattern underneath: the question's noun tells you which row you are answering about, and the question's verb — is, changes, at rest, maximum — tells you whether to substitute or to set something to zero. One more disambiguation that catches people: rate of change of the velocity is acceleration, not velocity. Whenever the phrase rate of change appears, it commands one differentiation of whatever noun follows it. Build the three-row table in the margin, place the question in a row, and the calculus writes itself.

**Mandy:** Next: in optimisation, how do I know which formula to differentiate and which to use as the constraint? I sometimes differentiate the wrong one.

**Tutor:** Ask two identifying questions of every formula in the problem. Question one: which quantity does the problem want biggest or smallest? That is your OBJECTIVE — the formula to be differentiated, eventually. The words maximise, minimise, largest, cheapest, most, least point straight at it: area in the kraal, surface material in the tin, profit in the vetkoek model. Question two: which quantity is FIXED by the story? That is your constraint — the formula you solve for one variable and substitute, and it never gets differentiated. Fixed fence length, fixed volume, fixed total of two numbers: the giveaway words are has only, must hold, adds up to. The workflow then locks in: write both formulas; solve the constraint for whichever variable is easier to isolate; substitute into the objective so it holds a single variable; and only THEN differentiate. Differentiating a two-variable expression is the checkpoint failure — if your objective still contains both x and h when you reach for the derivative, you skipped the substitution, and the recipe stops you. There is also a quick sanity test for having chosen correctly: imagine deleting the constraint. If the problem becomes silly — the kraal grows infinite, the tin shrinks to nothing — you had the right constraint, because constraints are exactly the facts that keep reality finite. When deleting a formula changes nothing, it was decoration, and some questions do include decorative information precisely to test the sorting.

**Mandy:** Third: why is the derivative of 2000 over x negative, when 2000 over x is clearly a positive quantity for the box?

**Tutor:** Separate the VALUE of a function from its RATE of change — they are different animals, and the wall-cost story shows it cleanly. The quantity 2000 over x is the material in the four walls, always positive, yes. But the derivative does not report the material; it reports what happens to the material AS THE BASE WIDENS. Widen the base and the height 500 over x squared drops, so the walls shrink: a positive quantity, decreasing. Decreasing means negative derivative. The algebra says the same thing in symbols: 2000 over x is 2000 x to the minus one, the exponent minus one falls forward as a multiplier bringing its sign, giving minus 2000 x to the minus two — negative for every positive x. So the sign is not an error to be corrected; it is the mathematics agreeing with the physics. And that negative term is what makes the whole problem work: total material is x squared, whose derivative two x is positive and growing, plus 2000 over x, whose derivative is negative and fading. The minimum sits exactly where the two rates cancel — where two x equals 2000 over x squared, giving x cubed equals 1000. Lose the minus sign and the equation becomes two x plus 2000 over x squared equals zero, which has no positive solution at all — your algebra literally cannot finish. So when a derivative sign looks wrong, translate it into a sentence about the story: is this quantity really shrinking as x grows? If the sentence rings true, the sign is right.

**Mandy:** Last one: my friend found the derivative zero at two different points in the profit question and reported the first one. Why was that wrong when the derivative was genuinely zero there?

**Tutor:** Because derivative-zero is a necessary credential, not a sufficient one — it gets a candidate into the interview, it does not give it the job. A cubic profit model has a falling-rising-falling or rising-falling-rising shape, so its derivative vanishes twice: once at a local MINIMUM and once at a local maximum. In the vetkoek model the derivative is zero near n equals one comma eight four and near eight comma one six. Classify before you report: the second derivative, minus twelve n plus sixty, is positive at the first candidate — concave up, a local minimum, the WORST nearby sales level, the valley where the business bleeds — and negative at the second, the genuine peak. Reporting the first point answers the question, where is profit momentarily flat, which nobody asked. The complete discipline for any applied extremum has three stamps. Stamp one: the candidate lies in the stated domain — here n between zero and twelve, and both do. Stamp two: its type matches the request — maximum wanted, concavity negative delivered. Stamp three, easy to forget: compare against the ENDPOINTS when the domain is a closed range, because a boundary can beat every interior candidate without any zero derivative — a business capped at twelve hundred vetkoek might do best at the cap. Only a candidate carrying all three stamps becomes the reported answer, in the units and the form the question requested — sales level, or the profit itself, whichever was actually asked.
