Hey, it's Mandy — I know the word problems in this chapter feel like a double exam: first decode the story, then do the calculus. But every single one of them, from bouncing particles to fenced kraals to tin boxes, is one recipe wearing different overalls, and the recipe has only four moves.

Move one: write the thing you care about as a formula. The area, the material, the profit, the distance. Move two: use the fixed fact in the story — the roll of fence, the required volume — to squeeze the formula down to ONE letter. If two letters remain when you want to differentiate, you skipped this move; go back. Move three: differentiate and set to zero. Flat ground is where the best and worst live. Move four: interpret. Check the candidate is in the sensible range, check it is the maximum or minimum the question wants — one second-derivative substitution settles it — and then answer the question actually asked, in its own units.

For the motion questions, keep the dashboard in your head: position is the odometer, velocity is the speedometer, acceleration is the push in your back. Differentiate once per gauge. At rest means speedometer zero — that phrase alone unlocks half the motion marks in past papers.

Two small snakes to step over: the derivative of anything over x carries a minus sign, because those wall-costs shrink as x grows; and a derivative-zero point can be the worst spot, not the best — classify before you celebrate.

Formula, one letter, flat ground, interpret. Say the four moves out loud before each problem, and the story questions become the most predictable marks in the paper. You've got this!
