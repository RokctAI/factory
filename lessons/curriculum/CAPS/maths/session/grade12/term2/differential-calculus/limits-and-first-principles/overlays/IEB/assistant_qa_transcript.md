**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! The first one several of you typed almost word for word: if the function has no value at x equals 4, how can we claim to know anything about it there? It feels like making the answer up.

**Tutor:** Nothing is made up, because the limit never makes a claim ABOUT the point — it reports on everything around the point, and everything around the point is on solid ground. At every input except 4, the expression x squared minus sixteen over x minus four exists and equals x plus four. So a perfectly fair question can be asked: as the inputs creep closer to 4, what do the outputs do? They step through 7,9 and 7,99 and 8,01, tightening onto 8 from both directions. The limit is the destination of that tightening, and every step of it happens on defined territory. We never assign a value at 4; the sentence, the limit as x approaches 4 is 8, speaks about approach, never arrival. The mark-losing mistake is merging the two sentences: f of 4 equals 8 is false, because f of 4 does not exist, while the limit statement is precisely true. Keep them separate and the concept is yours. And it matters beyond this section — the derivative's own definition is a fraction with a hole at h equals zero that behaves beautifully near it. Befriend the hole now and first principles will feel inevitable.

**Mandy:** Next: the recipe divides by h and then puts h equal to zero. Is that not dividing by zero and quietly hoping nobody notices?

**Tutor:** It would be, if both things happened at once — but they happen in sequence, and the sequence is the entire legality of the subject. At the moment we divide, h is small but strictly non-zero; the limit process means h approaches zero and never lands on it. Division by a non-zero number is blameless algebra, so turning two x h plus h squared over h into two x plus h is honest at every stage of the shrink. Only once the division is finished, holding the tidy expression two x plus h, do we pose the limit question: where is this heading as h fades? Since two x plus h contains no division by h any more, it answers by plain substitution: two x. Notice the original fraction was never evaluated AT h equals zero — we evaluated its simplified twin, which matches it at every h except the one value the limit is designed to sidestep. It is the pothole all over again: the difference quotient has a hole at h equals zero, and the derivative is what the neighbourhood points at. That is also why step three must always succeed — the cancelling of h is the algebraic moment the hole gets filled, and when it fails, the fault sits upstream in your expansion, never in the method.

**Mandy:** Third: honestly, why bother with first principles when the shortcut rule produces two x in a single line?

**Tutor:** Three answers, each stronger than the last. The practical answer: assessments ask for it by name. When a question says, differentiate from first principles, the marks sit in the definition being stated and executed — a shortcut answer earns almost nothing there. The family to drill is small: a constant, a straight line, a x squared plus b, and one over x. The mathematical answer: the shortcut is a theorem, and first principles is its birthplace. Watching two x h plus h squared over h collapse into two x is watching WHY the rule reads the way it does; the rule is just that computation, done once in general and then remembered. Someone who owns the definition can rebuild any forgotten rule from scratch, and can spot the situations where a rule does not apply. The conceptual answer, the one that pays all year: the definition IS the meaning. F prime of x is the limit of average gradients over shrinking intervals — the speedometer assembled from stopwatch readings. Optimisation, rates of change, and the shape of cubic graphs all work only because the derivative means instantaneous rate of change, and that meaning lives nowhere except in this definition. Drill the recipe until it bores you — in this topic, boredom is mastery.

**Mandy:** Last one: the one over x computation defeated half the homework group — the h would not cancel and everything dissolved. Where does it usually go wrong?

**Tutor:** In one place, nearly every time: the subtraction of the two fractions, so slow down right there. One over x plus h, minus one over x, have different denominators and must be combined over the common denominator x times x plus h before anything else is attempted. The first classic slip is subtracting the numerators without adjusting them — writing one minus one is zero on top and concluding the derivative of one over x is zero, which the falling graph immediately contradicts. The correct combination cross-multiplies: x minus the quantity x plus h, all over x times x plus h. The second slip hides inside that numerator: writing x minus x plus h instead of x minus the QUANTITY x plus h. That bracket is everything — subtracting the whole quantity yields x minus x minus h, which is minus h, and that minus sign is the final answer's minus sign entering the world. After the bracket survives, the road is smooth: minus h over h times x times x plus h, the h cancels on schedule, minus one over x times x plus h remains, and h to zero delivers minus one over x squared. Then run the picture test: each branch of the hyperbola y equals one over x falls from left to right, so the gradient must be negative everywhere, and minus one over x squared is. When algebra and picture shake hands, hand the answer in; when they argue, trust the picture and recheck the brackets.
