Hey, it's Mandy — I know the word calculus arrives carrying a reputation, and the h's and limits look like the subject finally left earth. Breathe. This lesson contains exactly two ideas, and you already use both every time you glance at a speedometer.

Idea one: the limit. Some expressions have a pothole — one input where they give zero over zero and refuse to answer. The limit is what all the neighbouring outputs point at. The routine is two steps: substitute, and if zero over zero appears, factorise, cancel, substitute again. That is the entire limits section.

Idea two: the derivative. Average speed over a trip is easy — distance over time. The speedometer's reading is the same fraction over a trip shrunk to nothing. In symbols: f at x plus h, minus f at x, over h, then let h shrink to zero. Four steps every single time. Write f of x plus h. Subtract f of x. Divide by h and cancel it — it ALWAYS cancels, and if it will not, an algebra slip is hiding in your expansion, usually a lost bracket. Then set h to zero in what is left.

Practise the recipe on the four functions the exam draws from: a constant gives zero, a straight line gives its own slope, x squared gives two x, and one over x gives minus one over x squared. The reciprocal one needs a common denominator and rewards you with a minus sign — check it against the falling hyperbola and smile when they agree.

Write the definition at the top of every first-principles answer before touching the specific function; the definition itself carries a mark, and it steadies your hand for the rest.

Pothole, speedometer, four steps. That is all of it, and by the third practice run it will feel mechanical. You've got this!
