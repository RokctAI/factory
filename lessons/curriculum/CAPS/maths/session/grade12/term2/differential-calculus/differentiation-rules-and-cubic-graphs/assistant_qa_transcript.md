**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First one, from at least four people: when do I use the original function and when do I use the derivative? I keep feeding the wrong machine.

**Tutor:** Sort every quantity in the topic into two boxes, and the confusion ends. Box one, POSITION questions: what is the height, where does the graph cut an axis, what are the coordinates? Those feed the original function — f tells you where the curve IS. Box two, DIRECTION questions: what is the gradient, where is it increasing, where is the tangent flat, where is the turning point? Those feed the derivative — f prime tells you where the curve is GOING. The trap is that most exam questions need both boxes in sequence, and the classic example is the turning point. Finding WHERE it happens is a direction question — set f prime to zero, solve for x. Finding its COORDINATES is then a position question — feed that x back into the ORIGINAL f for the height. The mark-losing move is feeding the x into f prime again and reporting zero as the height; of course it gives zero, that was the condition you solved. Same discipline with tangents: the touch point's height comes from f, the slope from f prime, and the two numbers then build the line together. Before every substitution this term, ask one word: position or direction? Height or slope? The right machine announces itself.

**Mandy:** Next: my derivative of three over x squared came out as six over x. Where does that go wrong, and how do I stop it happening under pressure?

**Tutor:** Two separate errors usually conspire there, so break the computation into slow motion once, and the fast version becomes safe forever. Start by dressing the function properly: three over x squared is three times x to the power MINUS two. Error one is differentiating the disguise instead of the power form — trying to apply the rule to a fraction directly. The rule reads only powers of x, so the rewrite is not optional decoration; it is the step where the differentiation actually becomes possible. Now apply the rule to three x to the minus two: the exponent minus two falls forward and multiplies the three, giving minus six — note the SIGN falls forward too, that is half of error two — and then the exponent steps down by one. Stepping down from minus two means more negative: minus three. Not minus one. Picture the number line: down is leftward, deeper into the negatives. So the derivative is minus six x to the minus three, which in fraction clothing is minus six over x cubed. Your answer of six over x made both mistakes at once: it lost the falling minus sign and stepped the exponent the wrong way. The under-pressure protection is a two-check habit that costs five seconds: after differentiating any negative power, confirm first that the sign of the answer flipped relative to the coefficient, and second that the new exponent is more negative than the old. Fail either check and you re-do just that term.

**Mandy:** Third: for the cubic sketch, why does the graph only TOUCH the x axis at a double root instead of crossing it?

**Tutor:** Look at the factor's behaviour on either side of the root, because the sign story explains everything. Take f of x equals x times the quantity x minus three, squared. Near x equals 3, the factor x is positive and roughly constant, so the graph's sign is controlled by the squared factor. A squared quantity is never negative: just below 3 it is a small positive number, just above 3 also a small positive number. So the function comes down to zero at 3 and lifts off again WITHOUT changing sign — it touches and turns. Compare a single root like the one at zero: there the factor x changes sign as you pass it, negative to positive, so the graph must cross. The rule of thumb: odd multiplicity crosses, even multiplicity touches. And here is the beautiful cross-check built into this specific cubic: the calculus found a local minimum at exactly three, zero. The algebra says touch, the calculus says minimum on the axis — same geometric event, described by two different tools. When your sketch shows the turning point sitting precisely on the x intercept, that is not coincidence, it is consistency, and examiners often design cubics so that this agreement rewards learners who check. If your algebra and calculus disagree about such a point, one of the two computations holds an error, and finding it before sketching saves the whole question.

**Mandy:** Last one: is the second derivative actually needed, or is it a luxury? Some of us classify turning points with a sign table instead.

**Tutor:** Both methods are legal, and a strong candidate carries both, choosing by situation. The sign table interrogates the FIRST derivative on each side of the stationary point: gradient positive before and negative after means the trail climbed then dropped — a maximum; the reverse pattern gives a minimum. It always works, and when the question already demands increasing and decreasing intervals, the table is sitting on your page anyway, free of charge. The second derivative test interrogates curvature at the point itself: f double prime negative means the curve is arching over like a frown — concave down, so a stationary point there is a maximum; positive means cupped upward, a minimum. For our cubic, f double prime is six x minus twelve: at x equals 1 it is minus six, maximum; at x equals 3, plus six, minimum. Two substitutions, no table — usually faster. But the second derivative earns its place in the syllabus for a bigger reason: it locates the point of INFLECTION, where f double prime is zero and the concavity flips — for a cubic, always midway between the turning points, at two, two in our example. Exam questions ask for the inflection by name, ask for the interval where the graph is concave up, and ask for the x value where the gradient itself is steepest on a descent — all second-derivative territory that a sign table cannot reach. So: table for intervals, second derivative for speed and for concavity questions, and the wisdom to deploy each is itself part of what the paper is testing.
