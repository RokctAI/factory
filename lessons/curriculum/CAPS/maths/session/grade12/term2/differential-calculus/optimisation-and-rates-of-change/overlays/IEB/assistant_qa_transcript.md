**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First one: velocity, speed, acceleration — three words that keep swapping places in our answers. Can you untangle them once and for all?

**Tutor:** Untangle them with the chain and the sign. The chain: position, velocity, acceleration — each is the derivative of the one before, so one differentiation moves you down a link. Velocity answers, how fast and in which direction is the position changing; it is allowed to be negative, and the negative IS information — it says the motion is towards the reference point rather than away. Speed is what remains of velocity when you strip the sign: magnitude only, direction discarded. So a velocity of minus five metres per second is a speed of five metres per second, travelling backwards. Acceleration answers a different question entirely: how fast is the VELOCITY changing? A common trap is reading negative acceleration as slowing down — not necessarily. A particle with negative velocity and negative acceleration is speeding up, backwards; slowing down happens when velocity and acceleration carry OPPOSITE signs, because the push acts against the motion. In our example the acceleration twelve t minus thirty is zero at two comma five seconds — the instant the braking hands over to the pushing. When a question asks for velocity, answer with the sign; asked for speed, without it; asked whether the particle speeds up or slows down, compare the two signs. Three words, three precise questions.

**Mandy:** Next, the recipe. Some of us freeze at step two — using the constraint. How do you actually see which equation kills the extra variable?

**Tutor:** Look for the sentence in the problem that states a FIXED total, and trust that it is the constraint wearing plain clothes. Every optimisation problem contains exactly two mathematical sentences. One describes what you want to be best — biggest area, least material, most profit. That sentence becomes the formula you will eventually differentiate. The other states what is fixed — one roll of fencing totalling 160 metres, a tin that must hold 108 cubic centimetres, a budget that cannot move. That sentence is the constraint, and its job is execution: solve it for one variable and substitute into the first sentence, so that only one letter survives. In the camp problem, the fixed roll gives two x plus the front equals 160, so the front is 160 minus two x — substitute, and the area speaks in x alone. In the tin, the fixed volume gives x squared h equals 108, so h is 108 over x squared — substitute, and the surface speaks in x alone. If you catch yourself trying to differentiate a formula that still holds two letters, you have skipped the constraint; go back and spend it. And notice why it must exist: without the fixed total, the best area would be infinite fence and the cheapest tin would hold nothing. The constraint is what makes best mean something.

**Mandy:** Third: in the tin problem, the marking of that minus sign in the derivative of 432 over x seemed to matter enormously. Why is one sign worth so much?

**Tutor:** Because the whole answer passes through it. Watch what the sign is doing. The surface is x squared plus 432 over x — one term that grows with x and one that shrinks as x grows. The physics of the problem lives in that opposition: bigger base costs more base but saves wall, and the minimum is their truce. Differentiate correctly and the algebra keeps the opposition: 432 over x is 432 x to the minus one, the exponent falls forward carrying its sign, and the derivative is two x MINUS 432 over x squared — one positive influence, one negative, and setting them equal locates the truce at x cubed equals 216. Now drop the minus sign, and the derivative becomes two x plus 432 over x squared: two positive terms, a sum that can never be zero for positive x. The equation has no solution, the method appears to fail, and a candidate under time pressure either invents an answer or abandons the question — all because of one sign. So build the habit: rewrite every fraction as a power BEFORE differentiating, write the new exponent down explicitly, and let the sign travel with it. Then run the sanity check at the end: S double prime is two plus 864 over x cubed, positive, so the truce is genuinely a minimum. One sign, carefully carried, is the difference between 108 square centimetres and a blank page.

**Mandy:** Last one: the koeksister profit model gave two places where the derivative is zero, and only one of them was the answer. How do we avoid reporting the wrong one in an exam?

**Tutor:** By refusing to let a zero derivative speak for itself — it only ever says level ground, never which kind. The profit curve minus n cubed plus twelve n squared minus twenty-one n flattens at n equals 1 and n equals 7, and the two flat spots mean opposite things. Classify before reporting, using either tool. The second derivative: minus six n plus twenty-four is plus eighteen at n equals 1 — concave up, a local MINIMUM of profit, the worst nearby sales level, arguably the one to avoid — and minus eighteen at n equals 7 — concave down, the local maximum, the answer. Or the sign of the first derivative: negative before 1, positive between 1 and 7, negative after 7 — profit falls, rises, falls, so the peak is at 7. Then match the report to the question: asked for the sales level, report seven hundred koeksisters; asked for the maximum profit, substitute n equals 7 into P and report the rand amount; asked to justify, quote the classification line. And keep one eye on the boundaries: this domain ran from zero to ten, and on a closed range the best value can sit at an endpoint where the derivative never bothers to be zero — evaluate the ends when they exist. The full discipline in one breath: candidates from the derivative, classification from the second derivative or a sign line, eligibility from the domain, and the final number from the question's own wording.
