# Part 1 — Expert

First principles built the derivative; this session industrialises it. One rule differentiates every power of x in a single line, and with it the cubic function — the star graph of grade twelve — opens up completely: its turning points, its shape, its tangents, and the way its derivative narrates its every rise and fall.

## Subtopic: The Power Rule and Rewriting First

The rule: the derivative of x to the power n is n times x to the power n minus one. Bring the exponent down as a multiplier, then knock the exponent down by one. It holds for every real exponent — positive, negative, fractional. Alongside it, two service rules: the derivative of a constant times a function is the constant times the derivative, and the derivative of a sum is the sum of the derivatives — differentiate term by term.

Worked run. For y equals three x to the fourth, minus two x, plus five: term one gives twelve x cubed, term two gives minus two, and the lone constant five dies to zero. So d y by d x is twelve x cubed minus two.

The rule only reads powers of x, so the real skill is REWRITING before differentiating. Three disguises appear constantly. Surds: the square root of x is x to the power one half, so its derivative is a half x to the power minus a half — one over two root x. Reciprocals: three over x squared is three x to the power minus two, with derivative minus six x to the power minus three. And quotients that are secretly sums: the expression two x cubed minus x, all over x squared, splits into two x minus one over x — rewrite as two x minus x to the minus one, and differentiate to two plus x to the minus two. No quotient rule exists in this course, and none is needed: every quotient in the syllabus splits or simplifies.

Watch the negative exponent arithmetic: knocking minus two down by one gives minus three, not minus one. Down means more negative.

Pause here — the questions for this section are with you now. Rewrite every surd and quotient as powers first, then let the exponent fall forward and step down.

## Subtopic: Tangents to a Curve

The derivative at a point is the gradient of the tangent there — which turns tangent questions into a fixed routine. Find the equation of the tangent to f of x equals x cubed minus three x squared plus two, at x equals 1. Step one, the point: f of 1 is one minus three plus two — zero, so the point is one, zero. Step two, the gradient function: f prime of x is three x squared minus six x. Step three, the gradient number: f prime of 1 is three minus six, minus three. Step four, the line: y minus zero equals minus three times x minus one, so y equals minus three x plus three.

Every variation reorders the same four ingredients: point, derivative, gradient, line. If the question gives the gradient instead — find where the tangent to this curve has gradient 9 — set the derivative equal to 9 and solve: three x squared minus six x equals nine gives x squared minus two x minus three equals zero, factorising to x minus three, times x plus one: x is 3 or minus 1. Two points share that tangent gradient, one on each arm of the cubic.

A tangent parallel to the x axis has gradient zero — remember that phrase; it is the exam's favourite disguise for the turning-point condition coming next. And a tangent parallel to a given line shares that line's gradient, so read the gradient off the line first.

Stop for this section's questions now — point, derivative, gradient, line, in whichever order the question deals them.

## Subtopic: The Cubic Graph — Stationary Points and Sketch

The cubic f of x equals a x cubed plus b x squared plus c x plus d is the canvas for the rest of the term, and its geography is governed by its derivative. Where f prime is zero, the tangent lies flat: a stationary point — a local maximum, a local minimum.

Full worked sketch: f of x equals x cubed minus six x squared plus nine x. Stationary points first. F prime of x is three x squared minus twelve x plus nine — set it to zero and divide by three: x squared minus four x plus three equals zero, factorising to x minus one, times x minus three. Stationary at x equals 1 and x equals 3. Heights: f of 1 is one minus six plus nine — 4. F of 3 is twenty-seven minus fifty-four plus twenty-seven — zero. So the stationary points are one, four and three, zero.

Which is which? Test the sign of f prime around them, or trust the cubic's shape: a positive x cubed coefficient rises, turns, dips, and rises again — first stationary point a local maximum, second a local minimum. Intercepts next. The y intercept: f of zero is 0. The x intercepts: factorise f itself — x times x squared minus six x plus nine, which is x times the quantity x minus three, squared. Roots at zero and at three, the second one doubled. A double root means the graph TOUCHES the x axis there and turns away — and notice the consistency: the local minimum three, zero sits exactly on the axis, touching it.

Sketch from the data: rising from the far lower left, through the origin, up to the peak at one, four, down to touch the axis at three, zero, then up forever. When a cubic resists factorising, remember the factor theorem from earlier in the year: test small values for a zero, then divide the factor out.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — derivative for the turning points, function for the intercepts, and let a double root touch rather than cross.

## Subtopic: Reading f Through f Prime, and Concavity

The derivative is not only a turning-point finder; it narrates the whole graph. Where f prime of x is positive, f is increasing; where negative, decreasing. For our cubic, f prime is three times x minus one, times x minus three — positive outside the roots, negative between them. So f increases until x equals 1, decreases between 1 and 3, increases after 3. Exam questions ask exactly this: for which values of x is the function decreasing? Answer: one strictly less than x strictly less than three — an interval, read straight off the derivative's sign.

Differentiate again and you get the second derivative, f double prime — the gradient of the gradient. For our cubic: six x minus twelve, zero at x equals 2. That point, two, two, is the point of INFLECTION: where the curve changes concavity, from arching downwards like a frown to curving upwards like a cup. For any cubic with distinct turning points, the inflection point sits exactly midway between them — one and three average to two. The second derivative also classifies stationary points without sign tables: f double prime negative at a stationary point means concave down, a local maximum; positive means a local minimum. At x equals 1: six minus twelve is negative — maximum, confirming the shape. At x equals 3: eighteen minus twelve is positive — minimum.

The complete grade twelve reading list for any cubic: zeros of f give intercepts; zeros of f prime give turning points; the sign of f prime gives increase and decrease; zeros of f double prime give the inflection; and the sign of a, the leading coefficient, orients the whole journey — positive rises to the right, negative falls.

The final questions of this part are with you now — f for position, f prime for direction, f double prime for curvature: three functions telling one story.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same derivatives and cubics from a hiking trail and a bag of tricks for lazy calculators — same rules, same answers, built from things you can picture.

## Subtopic: The Exponent Falls Forward

First principles was honest work, and you never have to do it again. The power rule is the payoff: to differentiate x to any power, the exponent falls forward to become a multiplier, and the power steps down by one. X squared becomes two x. X cubed becomes three x squared. X to the tenth becomes ten x to the ninth. Each term in a sum is handled on its own, constants riding along in front, and any lone number — a plus five, a minus eleven — differentiates to zero, because a constant changes at no rate at all.

The rule's one demand is that everything be DRESSED as a power of x. Most exam marks lost here are lost before differentiation starts, in the wardrobe. The square root of x is x to the half. One over x cubed is x to the minus three. And a fraction like two x cubed minus x, over x squared, is not one item but a sum wearing a coat: split it, term over denominator, into two x minus one over x, then dress the last piece as minus x to the minus one. Only then does the rule fire: two plus x to the minus two.

One arithmetic habit protects the negative exponents: stepping DOWN from minus two lands on minus three. Down the number line, always — more negative, never less.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the rule are coming to you right now. Dress everything as powers, let the exponent fall forward, step the power down.

## Subtopic: The Hiking Trail

A cubic graph is a hiking trail crossing a valley range, and the derivative is your climbing sense — the slope under your boots at each step. Walk the cubic x cubed minus six x squared plus nine x from the far left: the trail climbs, levels off at a viewpoint, drops into a valley, touches the valley floor, and climbs out forever. Every feature of that hike is a derivative fact.

The viewpoints and valley floors are where the slope is exactly zero — level ground, the tangent lying flat. Set the derivative to zero: three x squared minus twelve x plus nine factorises to three times x minus one times x minus three, so level ground occurs at x equals 1 and x equals 3. Plug into the ORIGINAL function for the altitudes: height 4 at the peak, height 0 at the valley floor. Peak first, valley second — because a right-way-up cubic always climbs, dips, climbs, in that order.

Where the trail climbs, the derivative is positive; where it drops, negative. Asked where the function is decreasing, answer with the stretch of trail between the peak and the valley: x strictly between 1 and 3. And halfway down that descent there is a spot every hiker knows — where the path stops curving over the top and starts curving into the bowl of the valley. That is the inflection point, at x equals 2 here, always midway between a cubic's two level spots. The slope there is the steepest of the whole descent.

Your questions for this part are up now. Level ground means derivative zero, altitude comes from the original function, and the trail's story — climb, drop, climb — is written in the derivative's sign.

## Subtopic: Laying a Ruler on the Curve

A tangent is a ruler laid against the curve at one point, matching the trail's direction exactly there. Every tangent question needs the same two numbers — where the ruler touches, and what slope it holds — and the derivative supplies the slope on demand.

The routine, on f of x equals x cubed minus three x squared plus two at x equals 1. Touch point: feed 1 into the original function — height zero, so the ruler touches at one, zero. Slope: feed 1 into the derivative three x squared minus six x — minus three. Line: start from y equals m x plus c with m equal to minus three, push the touch point through it, and the tangent is y equals minus three x plus three. Two feeds, one line, every time — the only care needed is feeding the right machine: the FUNCTION gives heights, the DERIVATIVE gives slopes, and mixing them up is the classic wrong turn.

Questions also run in reverse: where on the curve does a ruler hold a slope of 9? Set the derivative equal to 9 and solve the quadratic — x is 3 or minus 1 — two spots on the trail, one on each climbing stretch, steep in the same measure. And a ruler lying level, parallel to the x axis? Slope zero: that is the level-ground condition again, and it means tangent questions and turning-point questions are the same question in different jackets.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: function for the touch point, derivative for the slope, and read every parallel clue as a statement about gradient.
