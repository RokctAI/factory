**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First up, the wardrobe issue: several of you differentiate beautifully and still lose the marks. Why does the rewriting before the rule matter so much, and where does it usually go wrong?

**Tutor:** It matters because the power rule is a narrow machine: it reads one shape only, a coefficient times x to a power. Anything else — a surd, a reciprocal, a quotient — must be translated into that shape first, and the translation is where the topic quietly awards or removes its marks. Three translations cover nearly everything. A root becomes a fraction exponent: four root x is four x to the half. A reciprocal becomes a negative exponent: five over x cubed is five x to the minus three. And a quotient with a single term underneath splits term by term: three x to the fourth plus x, over x squared, becomes three x squared plus x to the minus one — two clean powers where there seemed to be a fraction. The two recurring slips both involve the negative exponents. Slip one: differentiating five x to the minus three and forgetting that the falling exponent brings its sign — the coefficient is minus fifteen, not fifteen. Slip two: stepping the power down the wrong way — minus three reduces to minus four, further down the number line, not up to minus two. My advice is to make the rewriting a separate written line every time. One line of wardrobe, then the rule; merging the two steps in your head is exactly where the signs get dropped.

***

**Mandy:** Next: in the cubic sketch, some of us substitute the turning x values into the derivative and get zero, then wonder where the heights went. Can you settle the which-machine question once and for all?

**Tutor:** Let me give you the sorting sentence: the function knows WHERE you are; the derivative knows which WAY you are leaning. Heights, points on the graph, intercepts — those are position questions, and position lives in the original f. Slopes, gradients, increasing or decreasing, level ground — those are direction questions, and direction lives in f prime. Now watch the sketch routine alternate between the machines. You differentiate to FIND the special x values — that is a direction question, level ground, f prime equals zero, giving minus one and three for our cubic. But the moment you want the altitude of those spots, you have changed to a position question, so you feed minus one and three back into the ORIGINAL function, getting thirty-two and zero. Substituting them into the derivative instead returns zero both times — of course it does, that is the very equation you solved, and it tells you nothing new. So before every substitution in this topic, ask one word: height or slope? Height, feed f. Slope, feed f prime. Learners who install that single question stop making the chapter's most common error permanently.

***

**Mandy:** Third: the double root. Why does the graph touch the axis at x equals 3 instead of crossing it, and why does that same point turn out to be a turning point?

**Tutor:** Look at the factorised form and track its sign. Our cubic is x minus three, squared, times x plus three. Near x equals 3, the squared factor is a square — it cannot be negative. So on both sides of 3, that factor is positive, and the sign of the whole function is carried by x plus three, which is safely positive there. The function is therefore positive just left of 3, zero at 3, and positive just right of 3: it comes down to the axis, touches, and lifts away without ever changing sign. That is what touching means algebraically — no sign change. A single root like minus three behaves oppositely: the factor x plus three changes sign as you pass it, so the graph genuinely crosses. And now the beautiful consistency: a point where the graph descends to a value, stops, and rises again is by definition a local minimum — level ground, derivative zero. So the double root at three, zero and the turning point at three, zero are not two coincidental facts; they are one geometric event reported by two different branches of your working. When your factorising and your calculus deliver the same point independently, treat it as a free correctness certificate for the whole page.

***

**Mandy:** Last one: what exactly is the point of inflection? It gets asked for, we compute it, but half of us could not say what it IS.

**Tutor:** It is the spot where the curve changes the way it bends. Follow our cubic over its summit at minus one, thirty-two: up there the curve arches over like a dome — concave down. Down at the valley around three, zero it opens upward like a bowl — concave up. Somewhere on the descent, the arch must hand over to the bowl, and the handover point is the inflection. The second derivative finds it, because f double prime measures bending the same way f prime measures climbing: here six x minus six is zero at x equals 1, height sixteen. Geometrically, notice two things worth saying in an answer. First, for any cubic with two turning points, the inflection sits exactly midway between them — minus one and three average to one, a built-in arithmetic check. Second, the inflection is where the descent is at its steepest: the slope has been getting more negative all the way down the dome, and starts recovering once the bowl begins, so the most negative slope occurs precisely at the handover. That is also why the second derivative classifies turning points: at the summit the curve is dome-shaped, f double prime negative, a maximum; at the valley it is bowl-shaped, positive, a minimum. Position, direction, bending — f, f prime, f double prime — one curve telling its story three ways.
