**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First, the one half the class whispered: if the function is undefined at x equals 3, how can we say anything at all about it there? It feels like inventing an answer.

**Tutor:** The honesty of the limit is that it never claims anything ABOUT the point itself — it reports on the neighbourhood, and the neighbourhood is fully defined. At every x except 3, the expression x squared minus nine over x minus three exists and equals x plus three. So we can ask a legitimate question: as inputs get closer and closer to 3, what do the outputs do? They march through 5,9 and 5,99 and 6,01, closing in on 6 from both sides. The limit is the destination of that march, and the march happens entirely on defined ground. No value at 3 is invented; the statement, the limit as x approaches 3 is 6, is a statement about approach, not arrival. Where learners lose marks is collapsing the two ideas into one: writing f of 3 equals 6 is false, because f of 3 does not exist; writing the limit as x approaches 3 of f of x equals 6 is precisely true. Keep the two sentences separate and you have understood the concept. And this is not pedantry for its own sake — the entire derivative is built on exactly this situation, a fraction that is undefined AT h equals zero but perfectly behaved NEAR it. Making peace with the pothole now is what makes first principles feel natural next.

***

**Mandy:** Next: in first principles, we divide by h and then set h to zero. Is that not dividing by zero and then pretending we did not?

**Tutor:** It would be, if the two steps happened at the same moment — but they happen at different moments, and the order is the whole legality of calculus. When we divide by h, we are working with a small but NON-ZERO h; the limit process means h approaches zero, never equals it. For a non-zero number, division is perfectly legal, so simplifying two x h plus h squared over h into two x plus h is honest algebra at every stage of the approach. Only after the division is complete, with the expression two x plus h in hand, do we ask the limit question: what does this approach as h shrinks? And two x plus h, being a polynomial in h with no division left in it, answers by simple substitution: two x. Notice we never evaluated the ORIGINAL fraction at h equals zero — we evaluated its simplified twin, which agrees with it everywhere except at the single point that the limit deliberately avoids. This is the pothole story again: the difference quotient has a hole at h equals zero, and the derivative is the value that the neighbourhood points at. That is why the h must always cancel in step three. The cancellation is not a lucky accident; it is the algebraic event that fills the pothole, and if it fails to happen, the error is upstream in the expansion, not in the method.

***

**Mandy:** Third: why must we learn first principles at all when the power rule gives the same answers in one line?

**Tutor:** Three reasons, in rising order of importance. The examinable reason: the syllabus commands it, and every paper contains a question that says, differentiate from first principles — those marks are awarded only for the definition, written and executed, and a power-rule answer scores close to nothing there. Expect the small family: a constant, a straight line, a x squared plus b, and one over x. The mathematical reason: the power rule is a theorem, not a fact of nature, and first principles is where it comes from. When you compute the derivative of x squared and watch two x h plus h squared over h collapse to two x, you are watching WHY the rule says what it says; the rule is the memorised residue of that computation done once for all powers. A person who owns the definition can rebuild any rule they forget, and can also recognise when a rule does NOT apply — which matters as soon as functions get complicated. The conceptual reason, the deepest: the definition is the meaning. F prime of x is not a symbol-shuffling outcome; it is the limit of average gradients over shrinking intervals — the speedometer built from stopwatch readings. Optimisation, rates of change, the shape of cubic graphs, everything later in the term works because the derivative MEANS instantaneous rate of change, and that meaning lives only in the first-principles definition. Learn the recipe until it is boring; boredom here is fluency.

***

**Mandy:** Last one: with one over x, my h refused to cancel and the algebra turned into porridge. What goes wrong for most people?

**Tutor:** Almost always the same two slips, both in the subtraction step, so slow down exactly there. The difference is one over x plus h, minus one over x — two fractions with different denominators, and they must be combined over the common denominator x times x plus h before anything else happens. Slip one is subtracting numerators without adjusting them: writing one minus one equals zero on top and concluding the derivative is zero. The correct combination multiplies each numerator by the other fraction's denominator: x minus the quantity x plus h, all over x times x plus h. Slip two lives inside that numerator: x minus x plus h instead of x minus the QUANTITY x plus h. The bracket matters totally — subtracting the whole quantity gives x minus x minus h, which is minus h, and that minus sign is the answer's minus sign being born. From there the method is downhill: the difference quotient is minus h over h times x times x plus h, the h cancels as it must, leaving minus one over x times x plus h, and sending h to zero gives minus one over x squared. Sanity-check the sign against the graph: one over x falls from left to right on each branch of the hyperbola, so its gradient must be negative everywhere — and minus one over x squared is negative for every non-zero x. When your algebra and the picture agree, submit with confidence; when they disagree, the picture is usually telling the truth.
