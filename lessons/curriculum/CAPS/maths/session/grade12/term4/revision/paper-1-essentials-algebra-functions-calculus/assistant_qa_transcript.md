**Mandy:** Revision questions from the class! First: with six topics on one paper, how do we split the remaining revision days without just doing everything thinly?

**Tutor:** Split by weight times weakness. Start from the weighting: functions thirty-five, calculus thirty-five, algebra twenty-five, patterns twenty-five, finance fifteen, probability fifteen. That is the value of each district. Then rate your own comfort in each, honestly, from a past paper attempt rather than a feeling — work one full Paper 1 cold and mark it against the memo. Where the district is heavy AND the marks leaked, that is where the days go; a weak thirty-five-mark topic outranks a weak fifteen-mark one every time. Two cautions. Do not spend days polishing a topic already scoring near full marks — the last two marks in a strong topic cost more effort than ten marks in a weak one. And do not revise by rereading notes: redo questions cold, then check against the memo, because the exam tests production, not recognition. A useful rhythm for the final stretch is one topic per day in rotation, each day ending with a mixed set of five questions from earlier days so nothing learned gets to evaporate.

**Mandy:** The quadratic formula question keeps saying correct to two decimal places, and people still lose a mark there. What exactly goes wrong?

**Tutor:** Almost always premature rounding, and it is a self-inflicted wound. The formula for three x squared minus five x minus one gives the square root of thirty-seven in the numerator. A learner who writes the root as six comma one and carries on gets one comma eight five if lucky — but often one comma eight four or eight six, and the accuracy mark is gone even though every step was understood. The discipline: the calculator holds the entire expression, five plus the root of thirty-seven, all over six, evaluated in one go, and rounding happens exactly once, on the final line, to exactly the accuracy the question names. There is a second, quieter leak in the same question: writing only one root. The formula's plus or minus is two answers, and the memo allocates to both. And a third: candidates who reach for the formula when the trinomial factorises in five seconds, spending triple the effort for the same two answers. The tell is in the instruction — two decimal places means formula; a plain solve invites thirty seconds of factor-hunting first, formula second. Read the instruction as the strategy hint it is.

**Mandy:** Inverses confuse people at the sketching stage — which points to swap, what happens to domain and range. Is there a reliable drill?

**Tutor:** One picture and one sentence carry the whole topic. The sentence: the inverse undoes the function, so every input-output pair reverses — a semicolon b becomes b semicolon a, domain and range trade places wholesale. The picture: the line y equals x as a mirror, with the function and its inverse as reflections. The drill that installs it: take g of x equals three to the x, tabulate three easy points — nought semicolon one, one semicolon three, two semicolon nine — swap each pair, plot both sets, and draw the mirror line through the middle. Do that once per function family — exponential to log, restricted parabola to root — and sketching questions become mechanical. The two marks that still escape people: first, the equation work is swap then solve, so y equals three to the x becomes x equals three to the y, and making y the subject forces the logarithm, y equals log base three of x — the log is not memorised, it is manufactured by the swap. Second, the parabola only earns an inverse function after its domain is restricted, and the question expects that restriction stated, x greater than or equal to nought or the mirror choice, because without it the reflection is not a function at all.

**Mandy:** Last one: any final-week advice specific to Paper 1 — the night before, the morning of, the first ten marks?

**Tutor:** Three layers. Final week: mixed past papers at examination pace, full memo marking after each, and a one-page error log — every mark lost, written as a rule for next time, like round once at the end or check both surd candidates. By the third paper the log stops growing, and that shrinking is confidence you can measure. Night before: no new topics. Read the error log, redo three questions you previously got wrong to prove the repair held, rewrite the information sheet's finance and calculus entries from memory once, and stop early — a rested brain out-earns a crammed one on complex procedure marks. Morning of: the information sheet is printed in the paper, so rehearse finding formulas on it rather than fearing the memory. In the exam itself, open with the algebra question and take it slowly on purpose — the first ten marks set the paper's rhythm, and rushing routine marks is how strong candidates donate them. Then trust the map: a stall is a signal to move, never to camp, because somewhere ahead sits a fifteen-mark probability question asking politely to be banked.
