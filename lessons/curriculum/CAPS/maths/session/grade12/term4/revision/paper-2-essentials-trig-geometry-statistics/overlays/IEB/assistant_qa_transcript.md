**Mandy:** Geometry revision questions from the class! First: everyone fears the Euclidean rider at the end. Is there a method for a proof you cannot immediately see?

**Tutor:** There is, and it is a pincer, not a lightning bolt. Work the diagram forwards: transfer every given onto the figure and let each one announce its theorem — a diameter announces ninety degrees, a tangent announces tan-chord, equal chords announce equal angles, a cyclic quadrilateral announces supplementary opposites. Write each discovered angle onto the diagram as you find it, because discoveries compound: two facts that meant nothing alone often collide into a third. Then work the target backwards: if the question wants two angles equal, list the ways angles get equal — same segment, tan-chord, isosceles triangle, parallel lines, corresponding angles of similar triangles — and ask which of those the diagram could plausibly supply. The proof lives where the forward wave and the backward wave meet, and in most riders they meet after two or three moves each. If they refuse to meet, add the classic constructions one at a time — join two marked points, draw the diameter from a point of contact — because a construction is a new given, and new givens restart the forward wave. And when the proof finally closes, write it as a clean chain, statement and reason, in order — the messy exploration stays on scrap paper. The pincer is slower than inspiration, but it works on days when inspiration stays home, and the final stretch is about methods that work every day.

**Mandy:** Next: the three faces of cos two x. How do we choose the right one under pressure instead of trying all three?

**Tutor:** Let the neighbouring term choose for you — the face is always picked to annihilate or match whatever sits beside it. If the expression contains a lone one being added or subtracted, pick the face that contains its own one: one plus cos two x wants two cos squared x minus one, so the ones cancel into two cos squared x; one minus cos two x wants one minus two sin squared x, collapsing into two sin squared x. Today's identity did exactly that. If the expression is drowning in sines, pick the sine face; drowning in cosines, the cosine face — the goal is always a single trig species on the page, because a single species cancels and factorises. And if the question gives you a value — cos x equals three fifths — take the face built from what you hold: cos two x equals two cos squared x minus one goes straight there without ever finding sin x, though the three-four-five triangle hands you sin x in one line anyway. The wrong face never gives a wrong answer, by the way — all three are true — it just gives a longer journey, and in an exam the longer journey is where slips breed. One drill makes the choice automatic: take a page of identity fragments and, without solving anything, write next to each which face you would choose and why. Ten minutes of that beats an hour of full proofs for training the reflex itself.

**Mandy:** In analytical geometry, the questions with many subquestions feel like mazes. How do we keep from getting lost halfway through part four?

**Tutor:** Respect the architecture: a long analytical question is a staircase, not a maze — every part is built from the parts below it. Part one asks for a gradient; part two uses that gradient for a perpendicular or a parallel line; part three intersects that line with something; part four asks for an area or an angle that spends everything earned so far. So the first discipline is bookkeeping: as each subquestion resolves, write its result onto the diagram — the gradient beside the line, the point beside its dot — because part four's difficulty is usually not conceptual, it is retrieval, and a labelled diagram retrieves instantly. The second discipline is the checkpoint habit: before using an earlier answer, glance at its plausibility on the figure — a midpoint should look like the middle, a gradient's sign should match the slope's direction, an angle of one hundred and twenty-six degrees should look obtuse. That one glance catches the transplanted sign error, which is the single most common way a staircase collapses. Third: when a middle step refuses to come out, do not abandon the staircase — take the general strategy from the structure. Later parts often accept a method built on the unproved result, and clean reasoning from a flawed number still earns its method marks. The staircase rewards the candidate who climbs it in order, hands on the rail, checking each tread.

**Mandy:** Last one: how should the final week of revision for this material be structured?

**Tutor:** Rotate the four territories daily rather than blocking one territory per day — retrieval strengthens through spacing, and a territory untouched for five days arrives at the exam colder than you expect. A working template: each day holds one full trigonometry item, because that territory compounds fastest; one geometry rider worked with the pincer, alternating Euclidean and analytical days; ten minutes of statistics calculator drill, because mode errors and register clutter are rust that forms quickly; and one toolkit page written from memory every second day, checked word for word against yesterday's page — especially the theorem reasons, which must be verbatim reflexes by the end of the week. Add one habit rehearsal per day from the list of five: Monday the reason column, Tuesday degree mode, and so on — one habit at a time until each is automatic. Keep every session short and honest: forty focused minutes with a blank page beats three hours of rereading, because rereading feels warm and measures nothing. And the day before the paper, taper: one gentle rider, one identity, the toolkit page, the reasons out loud, and sleep. The mathematics is already in you by then; the final week's job is keeping the retrieval paths warm and the habits loaded.
