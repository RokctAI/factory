# Part 1 — Expert

This session is the whole of Paper 1 folded into one revision sweep — algebra and equations, sequences and series, functions with their inverses, finance, differential calculus, and probability. Every mark on that paper belongs to a method you have already used this year; today the methods get reorganised into exam order, each one carrying its worked example and its classic trap. The weighting tells you where the money is: functions and calculus carry thirty-five marks each, algebra and patterns twenty-five each, finance and probability fifteen each, one hundred and fifty in total.

## Subtopic: Algebra and Equations — the Opening Twenty-Five Marks

The paper opens with algebra, and the opening question is deliberately the friendliest on the paper — factorise if the factors show themselves, formula if they do not. Solve three x squared minus five x minus one equals zero, correct to two decimal places. The phrase two decimal places is itself the instruction: the trinomial will not factorise, so go straight to the quadratic formula. X equals minus b, plus or minus the square root of b squared minus four a c, all over two a. Substitute: five, plus or minus the square root of twenty-five plus twelve, over six — the square root of thirty-seven. Two answers: x is approximately one comma eight five, or minus nought comma one eight. Keep every decimal in the calculator until the final line; rounding the square root early drags the second decimal off target and costs the accuracy mark.

The k-method, matric's own trick: solve three to the power two x, minus ten times three to the power x, plus nine, equals zero. Let k equal three to the power x. The equation becomes k squared minus ten k plus nine equals zero, which factorises as k minus one, times k minus nine. So three to the x equals one, giving x equals nought, or three to the x equals nine, giving x equals two. The substitution turns an alien equation into a familiar quadratic — that is the whole method.

Surd equations demand a check. Solve: the square root of x plus five, equals x minus one. Square both sides: x plus five equals x squared minus two x plus one. Bring everything left: x squared minus three x minus four equals zero, so x minus four times x plus one equals zero, giving x equals four or x equals minus one. Now test both in the original. X equals four: square root of nine is three, and four minus one is three — valid. X equals minus one: square root of four is two, but minus one minus one is minus two — rejected, because a square root is never negative. Squaring manufactures candidates; the original equation vets them, and the memo reserves a mark for the vetting.

Quadratic inequalities live in the world of graphs: solve x minus four, times x plus two, greater than zero. The parabola through roots minus two and four opens upward, so it is positive outside the roots: x less than minus two, or x greater than four. Sketch the parabola every time — the picture chooses between outside and between.

Last of the set, simultaneous equations, one linear and one quadratic: y equals x plus two, and x squared plus y squared equals ten. Substitute the linear into the quadratic: x squared, plus x plus two all squared, equals ten. Expand and tidy: two x squared plus four x minus six equals zero, divide by two, x squared plus two x minus three equals zero, factorise to x plus three times x minus one. X equals one with y equals three, or x equals minus three with y equals minus one. Pairs, always — an x without its y is half an answer.

This section's questions are with you now — formula, k-method, surd check, and the parabola picture.

## Subtopic: Sequences and Series — Patterns With a Formula

Twenty-five marks of patterns, and the first skill is recognition by differences. The pattern three; eight; fifteen; twenty-four has first differences five, seven, nine — not constant — and second differences two, two — constant. Constant second difference means quadratic: T n equals a n squared plus b n plus c, with two a equal to the second difference, so a equals one. Fit the first terms: T n equals n squared plus two n. Check it: one plus two is three, four plus four is eight, nine plus six is fifteen — the formula is verified against the data before it is trusted. Which term equals one hundred and sixty-eight? Set n squared plus two n minus one hundred and sixty-eight equal to zero, factorise as n plus fourteen times n minus twelve, and since n counts terms, n equals twelve. Reject the negative root out loud — that rejection is a mark.

Geometric sequences run on a constant ratio. The series one hundred and twenty-eight, plus sixty-four, plus thirty-two, and so on, has a equal to one hundred and twenty-eight and r equal to one half. Because r sits strictly between minus one and one, the sum to infinity exists: a over one minus r, which is one hundred and twenty-eight over a half — two hundred and fifty-six. The convergence condition is not decoration; state it, because the memo looks for minus one less than r less than one before it accepts the sum.

The exam's favourite twist: for which smallest n does S n first exceed two hundred and fifty-five? S n equals two hundred and fifty-six times, one minus a half to the power n. Exceeding two hundred and fifty-five forces a half to the power n below one over two hundred and fifty-six, which is a half to the power eight. Powers of a half shrink as n grows, so n must be greater than eight: the smallest n is nine. Check: S nine is two hundred and fifty-five comma five — past the target, as promised.

Sigma notation is a set of instructions, not a monster: the sum from k equals one to twenty of three k minus one means substitute k from one to twenty and add. Arithmetic with first term two, common difference three, twenty terms: S n equals n over two, times two a plus n minus one d — ten times, four plus fifty-seven — six hundred and ten. The classic sigma trap is the count: a sum from k equals three to twenty has eighteen terms, not seventeen — upper minus lower, plus one.

Take this section's questions now — differences to diagnose, formulas to fit, and every count of terms done twice.

## Subtopic: Functions, Inverses and Finance

Functions carry thirty-five marks, and every graph question walks the same checklist: shape, asymptotes, intercepts, then the special points. Take f of x equals two over x minus one, plus three. Hyperbola. Vertical asymptote where the denominator dies: x equals one. Horizontal asymptote from the shift: y equals three. Y-intercept at x equals nought: two over minus one, plus three, equals one. X-intercept where f is zero: two over x minus one equals minus three, so x minus one equals minus two thirds, x equals one third. Four facts, four marks, before any sketching begins.

Inverses are the matric upgrade. The inverse undoes the function, and graphically it is the reflection across the line y equals x — every point a semicolon b becomes b semicolon a. For the exponential g of x equals three to the power x, the inverse is the logarithm: g inverse of x equals log base three of x. The point two semicolon nine on g becomes nine semicolon two on g inverse. Domain and range swap wholesale: g takes all real x to positive y, so g inverse takes positive x to all real y. And remember the restriction ritual for the parabola: y equals x squared only has an inverse function once the domain is cut to x greater than or equal to nought, or x less than or equal to nought — otherwise the reflection fails the vertical line test.

Finance, fifteen marks, two skills. Skill one, nominal to effective: eight percent per annum compounded quarterly means two percent per quarter, so the effective annual rate is one comma zero two to the power four, minus one — eight comma two four percent. Compounding more often than yearly always beats the nominal label. Skill two, the annuity formulas. A loan of nine hundred thousand rand at ten comma five percent per annum compounded monthly, repaid by two hundred and forty equal monthly payments: the present value formula gives x equals P times i, over one minus, one plus i to the power minus n. The monthly i is nought comma zero zero eight seven five. The payment lands at eight thousand nine hundred and eighty-five rand and forty-two cents, and multiplying by two hundred and forty shows the loan's true cost: about two comma one six million rand — more than double the amount borrowed. That comparison sentence is a favourite final subquestion.

This section's questions are ready — checklist the graph, swap the coordinates, and let the formula sheet carry the finance.

## Subtopic: Calculus and Probability — the Big Finish

Calculus opens with first principles, and the definition earns its marks verbatim: f prime of x equals the limit as h tends to nought of, f of x plus h, minus f of x, all over h. For f of x equals three x squared minus two: f of x plus h is three times x plus h squared minus two, which expands to three x squared plus six x h plus three h squared minus two. Subtract f of x, and the surviving terms are six x h plus three h squared. Divide by h: six x plus three h. Let h tend to nought: f prime of x equals six x. Writing the limit symbol on every line until it is used is not pedantry — it is a mark.

Then the rules, after rewriting: y equals four x cubed, minus the square root of x, plus two over x. Rewrite the square root as x to the half and the fraction as two x to the minus one. Differentiate term by term: twelve x squared, minus a half x to the minus a half, minus two x to the minus two. The rewriting step is where the marks hide — the rule only applies to powers of x, never to roots and fractions left in disguise.

Cubic graphs, the centrepiece. F of x equals x cubed minus six x squared plus nine x factorises as x times x minus three squared: x-intercepts at nought and at three, the double root meaning the graph touches at three. Turning points where f prime, three x squared minus twelve x plus nine, is zero: x equals one and x equals three. F of one is four — local maximum; f of three is nought — local minimum, sitting exactly on the intercept. The point of inflection, where concavity changes, at x equals two, y equals two — the midpoint of the turning points for any cubic. Optimisation questions are this same machinery pointed at a story: write the quantity as a function, differentiate, set the derivative to zero, and answer in the units of the story.

Probability closes the paper, fifteen friendly marks. Independent events A and B with P of A equal to nought comma four and P of B equal to nought comma five: independence means P of A and B is the product, nought comma two. The addition rule gives P of A or B: nought comma four, plus nought comma five, minus nought comma two — nought comma seven. The counting principle: the letters of the word REVISION, eight letters with the letter I appearing twice, arrange in eight factorial over two factorial ways — twenty thousand one hundred and sixty. Repeated letters divide; that division is the whole trick.

The final questions of this part are with you now — the limit written in full, the cubic anatomised, and every arrangement counted with its repeats divided out.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same paper as a route map — where the marks sit, which question shapes repeat every single year, and the short list of donations that weaker scripts make to the markers.

## Subtopic: The Map of Paper One

Picture Paper 1 as a city with six districts, and the mark weighting as the size of each district. Functions and graphs: thirty-five marks. Calculus: thirty-five. Algebra and equations: twenty-five. Number patterns: twenty-five. Finance: fifteen. Probability: fifteen. One hundred and fifty in total, and the order on the paper usually runs from algebra through patterns and functions to finance, calculus, and probability at the end.

The map changes how you travel. Seventy of the one hundred and fifty marks live in just two districts — functions and calculus — so revision effort should lean there. But the first twenty-five, the algebra opener, are the most routine marks on the paper: solve, solve, solve, inequality, simultaneous. Bank them calmly and the paper opens with momentum.

The map also says what every question is allowed to ask. Patterns can only be linear, quadratic, arithmetic or geometric — four species, no more. Finance is growth, decay, nominal versus effective, and the two annuity formulas — all printed on the information sheet. Probability is Venn diagrams, tree diagrams, the addition rule, independence, and the counting principle. Districts, not wilderness: each one has a fence around what can be examined, and revision is walking the fenced ground until it is familiar.

At examination pace the rule is simple: never camp in one district. A question that stalls gets a mark in the margin and a promise to return; the districts ahead hold marks that need no help from the one that stalled.

Your questions for this section are ready — six districts, their sizes, and the route through them.

## Subtopic: Five Question Shapes That Repeat Every Year

Same shapes, new numbers — that is a final paper. Shape one: solve to two decimal places. Translation: the quadratic formula, because two decimal places is code for does not factorise. Shape two: an equation with x in the exponent twice, like three to the two x minus ten times three to the x plus nine — the k-substitution, turning it into a quadratic wearing a costume.

Shape three: here is a pattern, find the general term, then find which term equals some number. Differences diagnose the species — constant first difference, linear; constant second difference, quadratic; constant ratio, geometric. Then the second half is always the same move in reverse: set the formula equal to the number and solve for n, rejecting any negative or fractional n because term numbers count.

Shape four: the graph question, and its checklist never changes — shape, asymptotes, intercepts, special points, in that order. A hyperbola question and an exponential question ask the same four things wearing different equations. Inverses add one move: swap x and y, then make y the subject, and on the sketch reflect across the line y equals x.

Shape five: the cubic. Factorise for intercepts, differentiate and solve for turning points, and remember the double root touches while a single root cuts. Every year the same five shapes carry the majority of the paper; a learner who can perform them cold walks in owning most of the marks before the first page turns.

Here are this section's questions — five shapes, five reflexes, no surprises.

## Subtopic: The Donation List — Marks Given Away and How to Keep Them

Markers speak of donations: marks the script gives away that the mathematics never required. The Paper 1 donation list is short and stable, and knowing it is as valuable as knowing a theorem.

Donation one: rounding too early. The square root of thirty-seven gets rounded to six comma one on line two, and by the final line the answer misses the memo's one comma eight five. Keep everything in the calculator; round once, at the end, to exactly the accuracy the question names.

Donation two: the unchecked surd answer. Squaring an equation invents candidates, and one of them usually fails the original. The check is one substitution per candidate — a single line that carries a full mark and, better, certainty.

Donation three: the missing second answer. Quadratics have two roots; simultaneous systems have two pairs; a general term question wants the n rejected as well as the n accepted. Whenever a square appeared anywhere in the working, ask where the second answer went.

Donation four: finance without a timeline. Payments that start one month after the loan, or a deposit that grows for a different number of months than the question implies — one small timeline sketch, dots for months, arrows for payments, prevents nearly every finance error worth preventing.

Donation five: notation in calculus. The limit written once and then abandoned, or d y d x used as if it were a fraction of its own accord. Write the definition in full, keep the limit symbol until h actually goes to nought, and the notation marks stay home.

The habit that collects them all: after every answer, one check that costs a line. Substitute back, multiply back, count again, or test a value. The paper rewards accuracy it can see, and the check makes accuracy visible.

The final questions of the lesson are with you now — the donation list read twice, and every check performed while the marks are still yours.
