## Subtopic: Statistics and Regression — Twenty Marks of Method

Statistics opens the paper with the most calculator-driven marks of the year. Take ten test marks: twelve, forty-five, fifty-two, fifty-eight, sixty-one, sixty-four, sixty-eight, seventy-two, seventy-five, ninety-four. The mean: the sum, six hundred and one, over ten — sixty comma one. The standard deviation, straight from the calculator's statistics mode: twenty comma five. Then the five-number summary for the box and whisker diagram: minimum twelve, lower quartile fifty-two, median sixty-two comma five, upper quartile seventy-two, maximum ninety-four.

Outliers are policed by the fence rule: lower quartile minus one comma five times the interquartile range. The interquartile range is seventy-two minus fifty-two — twenty. The lower fence: fifty-two minus thirty, which is twenty-two. Twelve sits below the fence, so twelve is an outlier, and the sentence naming the fence is the mark. One more favourite: how many marks lie within one standard deviation of the mean? The interval runs from thirty-nine comma six to eighty comma six, and counting the list gives eight of the ten marks.

Regression pairs two variables. Temperature against cans of cooldrink sold: six pairs, from fifteen degrees with twelve cans up to thirty degrees with twenty-nine cans. The calculator delivers the least squares line: y hat equals minus five comma four three, plus one comma one five x, with correlation coefficient r equal to nought comma nine nine nine — very strong positive linear association. Interpret the gradient in context: about one more can sold for each degree warmer. Predict at twenty-five degrees: about twenty-three cans — trustworthy, because twenty-five sits inside the data range. Predict at forty degrees and the duty changes: calculate if asked, then flag it as extrapolation beyond the observed range and decline to trust it. In statistics the refusal is a scoring answer.

## Subtopic: Analytical Geometry — the Forty-Mark Grid

Analytical geometry is four formulas plus discipline. The angle of inclination is the grid's compass: theta is the angle the line makes with the positive x-axis, and tan theta equals the gradient. ## Subtopic: Trigonometry — the Fifty-Mark Kingdom

Fifty marks make trigonometry the largest single territory across both papers, and it splits into identities, equations, and triangle rules. First the compound and double angle machinery. Cos of seventy-five degrees, exactly: split it as forty-five plus thirty. Cos of A plus B equals cos A cos B minus sin A sin B — the formula sheet carries it. Substituting the special values gives root six minus root two, all over four — approximately nought comma two six, and the exactness is the point: no calculator decimal earns the marks.

Double angles compress: sin two x equals two sin x cos x, and cos two x wears three faces — cos squared minus sin squared, two cos squared minus one, one minus two sin squared. Choosing the right face is the skill. Given sin x equals three fifths with x acute, the three-four-five triangle gives cos x equals four fifths, so sin two x equals two times three fifths times four fifths — twenty-four over twenty-five — and cos two x equals sixteen over twenty-five minus nine over twenty-five — seven over twenty-five. Identities reward the same discipline: prove sin two x, over one minus cos two x, equals cos x over sin x. Replace sin two x with two sin x cos x, and one minus cos two x with two sin squared x — the face chosen precisely because it kills the one. Cancel two sin x, and the left side becomes cos x over sin x. Start from one side, state restrictions if asked, never multiply across the equals sign.

Equations want all solutions. Cos x equals minus a half: the reference angle is sixty degrees, cosine is negative in the second and third quadrants, so the general solution is x equals one hundred and twenty degrees plus k times three hundred and sixty, or x equals minus one hundred and twenty degrees plus k times three hundred and sixty, k an integer. The general solution first, always — then restrict to the demanded interval by choosing k.

Triangles close the kingdom. Sine rule when a side faces a known angle; cosine rule when two sides trap an angle or three sides are known. Two sides seven and nine with a sixty-degree angle between them: the cosine rule gives the third side as the square root of sixty-seven, about eight comma one nine. The area rule from the same data: a half times seven times nine times sin sixty — about twenty-seven comma two eight square units. Height problems chain the tools: forty-five metres from a building's base, angle of elevation thirty-eight degrees, the height is forty-five tan thirty-eight — about thirty-five comma one six metres. In three-dimensional problems, solve one triangle at a time and carry the shared side across.

## Subtopic: Euclidean Geometry — Theorems, Reasons, Riders

Euclidean geometry pays its forty marks in a currency of reasons: every statement in a proof earns only when its reason is stated in the accepted wording. The examinable proofs are a closed list of seven, and two appear most often: the angle at the centre is double the angle at the circumference, and the tangent-chord theorem. Learn those proofs as performances — diagram, construction, statements, reasons — because a proof question is the closest thing to free marks the paper offers a prepared candidate.

The working theorems, with their memo wording. Angle at centre: a central angle of one hundred degrees forces fifty degrees at the circumference on the same arc — reason, angle at centre equals two times angle at circumference. Angles in the same segment are equal. The angle in a semicircle is ninety degrees. Opposite angles of a cyclic quadrilateral are supplementary, and the exterior angle of a cyclic quadrilateral equals the interior opposite angle. Tangents from a common point are equal. The tangent-chord theorem: the angle between a tangent and a chord equals the angle in the alternate segment. Then the proportionality pair that rules the similarity questions: a line parallel to one side of a triangle divides the other two sides proportionally, and equiangular triangles are similar.

Riders are solved backwards and forwards at once. Read the diagram and mark everything given — equal sides, tangents, diameters — because each given is a theorem invitation: a diameter whispers ninety degrees, a tangent whispers tan-chord, a cyclic quadrilateral whispers supplementary. Then read the target and ask what would prove it: equal angles come from the same segment, from tan-chord, from isosceles triangles, from parallel lines. The proof is the corridor where the two searches meet, and it is written statement by statement, each with its reason, no steps assumed. When a similarity question asks for a ratio, name the triangles with letters in matching order — the order is the mapping, and a scrambled order scrambles the ratio.

The final questions of this part are with you now — reasons in memo wording, givens converted to invitations, and the seven proofs rehearsed as performances.

## Subtopic: Reasons, Rounding and the Paper Two Habits

The habits that decide Paper 2 scripts are small, repeatable, and all drillable in the final stretch.

Habit one: the reason column. In Euclidean geometry a correct statement with a missing or invented reason is a mark lost, every time. The memo accepts specific wordings — tan chord theorem, angle at centre equals two times angle at circumference, opposite angles of cyclic quad — and revision means writing those exact phrases until they arrive without thought. A useful drill: cover the reasons in a worked rider, and supply them from memory.

Habit two: degrees on the calculator. One general solution computed in radian mode poisons every answer downstream. Check the mode at the start of the trigonometry section — a two-second glance that protects a dozen marks.

Habit three: exact versus decimal. When a question says show that, or asks for cos seventy-five in surd form, a calculator decimal earns nothing — the exact value is the answer. When a question names an accuracy, round exactly there and nowhere earlier. Analytical lengths stay as simplified surds — four root five, not eight comma nine four — unless decimals are demanded.

Habit four: the diagram as ledger. Every fact discovered — an angle of fifty degrees, a pair of equal tangents — gets written onto the diagram immediately. Later subquestions almost always spend what earlier subquestions earned, and the ledger keeps the earnings visible.

Habit five: the general solution reflex. Trigonometric equations produce families, not single answers; write the family with its k, then harvest the members inside the demanded interval. Order matters — family first, harvest second — because the memo pays for the family even when the harvest slips.

Five habits, none of them mathematics, all of them marks. The territory knowledge is already in your hands; the habits are how it reaches the page intact.

The final questions of the lesson are with you now — reasons word-perfect, modes checked, surds kept exact, and every diagram earning like a ledger.
