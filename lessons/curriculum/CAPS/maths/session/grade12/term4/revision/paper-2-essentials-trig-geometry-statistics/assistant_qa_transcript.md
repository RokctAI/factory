**Mandy:** Paper 2 revision questions from the class! First: everyone fears the Euclidean geometry rider at the end. Is there a method for a proof you cannot immediately see?

**Tutor:** There is, and it is a pincer movement rather than a lightning bolt. Work from both ends. From the front: transfer every given onto the diagram and let each one whisper its theorems — a diameter offers ninety degrees in the semicircle, a tangent offers the tan-chord theorem and equal tangents from a common point, a cyclic quadrilateral offers supplementary opposites and the exterior angle. Write the discovered angles onto the diagram as you go, because riders are cumulative: subquestion three spends what subquestion one earned. From the back: look at what must be proved and list what would deliver it — equal angles arrive via the same segment, tan-chord, isosceles triangles or parallel lines; a cyclic quadrilateral is certified by converse theorems. Usually the two searches meet in the middle within a step or two, and the proof writes itself statement-plus-reason down the corridor they built. When the pincer stalls, the classic unblocker is to chase angles: give one unknown angle a name, x, and express everything reachable in terms of it. And if it still will not open, bank the margin mark and move — the statistics and analytical marks ahead are routine and must not be sacrificed to pride.

**Mandy:** Next: in trigonometry, how do we know which version of cos two x to use? Three faces and people freeze choosing.

**Tutor:** Let the rest of the expression choose for you — that is the entire skill. The three faces are cos squared minus sin squared, two cos squared minus one, and one minus two sin squared, and each exists to cooperate with different company. If the line contains one minus cos two x, pick the face one minus two sin squared, because the ones annihilate and leave pure sine — that is exactly the move in the identity sin two x over one minus cos two x, which collapses to cos x over sin x. If the line contains one plus cos two x, pick two cos squared minus one so the ones cancel into pure cosine. If the expression around it is already all in sines, pick the sine face; all in cosines, the cosine face — the goal is always one function name on the page instead of two. In equation questions the choice is about structure: cos two x equals sin x becomes solvable when cos two x wears one minus two sin squared, turning the whole equation into a quadratic in sin x. So the drill for the final weeks: take each face and write next to it the trigger that summons it. Freezing comes from asking which is correct; fluency comes from asking which face makes something cancel.

**Mandy:** Third: the statistics question should be easy marks, but people still leak them. Where exactly does the leaking happen?

**Tutor:** Four leaks, all preventable. Leak one: calculator entry. Ten data values typed with one slip poisons the mean and the standard deviation together — enter the list, then scroll back through it once before computing; thirty seconds insures four marks. Leak two: the interpretation sentence. The memo wants numbers wearing units and context — a gradient of one comma one five means about one more can sold per degree warmer, with the word predicted or on average included; a bare number is not an interpretation. Leak three: the outlier fence. Candidates spot the outlier by eye and claim the mark, but the memo pays for the calculation: quartile minus one comma five times the interquartile range, shown, then the verdict. Intuition finds it; arithmetic earns it. Leak four: extrapolation. A prediction outside the data range must be flagged as unreliable, and if it is also impossible — sales below zero, marks above a hundred — say so. In statistics the refusal to trust a number is itself the scoring answer. Fix those four and the opening twenty marks of Paper 2 become the safest marks of your final examinations.

**Mandy:** Last one: with limited days left, how should Paper 2 revision be structured differently from Paper 1 revision?

**Tutor:** Two differences in kind. First, Paper 2 is diagram-driven, so revise with a pen that draws. For analytical geometry, plot the points roughly every time — half the errors marked every year are impossible answers a sketch would have vetoed, obtuse inclinations reported as acute, tangents drawn through centres. For Euclidean geometry, redraw the diagram yourself rather than staring at the printed one; the hand teaches the eye where the equal angles live. Second, Paper 2 has a memorisation layer Paper 1 lacks: the theorem reasons in their accepted wording, and the seven examinable proofs. Those need spaced repetition, not understanding — understanding you already have. A deck of cards, theorem on one side, memo wording on the other, shuffled daily, and the two most examined proofs — angle at centre, tan-chord — performed on blank paper until the performance is boring. Then the shared machinery: alternate days, Paper 1 and Paper 2, both at examination pace, both marked against the memo, both feeding the same error log. The log's shrinkage is the honest measure of readiness — not hours spent, not pages read, but errors that stopped recurring.
