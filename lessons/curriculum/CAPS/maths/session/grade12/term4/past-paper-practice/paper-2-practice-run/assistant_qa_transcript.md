**Mandy:** Questions after the Paper 2 run! First: the analytical triangle question suddenly used a circle theorem — the semicircle idea — inside an analytical question. Is that mixing allowed in the real paper?

**Tutor:** Not just allowed — it is announced. The examination guidelines state directly that candidates are expected to integrate Euclidean geometry axioms and theorems into analytical geometry problems, and the midpoint part you met is the classic case: prove a right angle with gradients, then let the converse of the angle-in-a-semicircle theorem crown the hypotenuse's midpoint as the circumcentre, equidistant from all three vertices. No distance formula needed for the final claim — the theorem does it in one sentence. The traffic runs both ways: an analytical question may ask for the length of a tangent from an external point, which is Pythagoras wearing the tangent-perpendicular-to-radius theorem, exactly as in the circle question. So when revising analytical geometry, keep the theorem list open beside the formula list, and when a question feels like it needs an ugly calculation, pause and ask which theorem would make it elegant. Examiners design those pauses deliberately: the complex-procedure marks sit precisely where the two toolboxes connect, and the candidates who never open both at once end up doing triple the arithmetic for half the marks.

***

**Mandy:** Next: in the general solution question, why is writing plus k times three hundred and sixty non-negotiable? Some of us just listed the angles.

**Tutor:** Because the equation's answer is a family, and the listed angles are only the family members who happen to live nearby. Cos x equals minus a half is solved by one hundred and twenty degrees — and by four hundred and eighty, and by minus two hundred and forty, and by infinitely many others, one pair for every full turn of the circle. The general solution, plus or minus one hundred and twenty plus k times three hundred and sixty with k an integer, is the only notation that captures all of them, which is why the memo allocates marks to the k-statement itself: omit the k and you have answered a different, smaller question. The practical workflow never changes: reference angle first — sixty degrees — then quadrant reasoning — cosine negative in the second and third quadrants — then the family, and only then, if an interval is demanded, substitute integer values of k and harvest the members inside the fence. Doing it in that order also protects you from the classic interval accident: a solution like minus one hundred and twenty degrees looks outside a nought-to-three-sixty interval, but k equals one relocates it to two hundred and forty, squarely inside. The family always finds its members; the listed angles miss them.

**Mandy:** The proof question — five whole marks for a theorem we can read in the textbook. How do we make sure those marks are actually safe on the day?

**Tutor:** Treat the proof as a performance with a fixed choreography, because that is how it is marked. The guidelines name a closed list of examinable proofs — among them the angle at the centre, the tangent-chord theorem you performed today, the proportionality theorem, and equiangular triangles being similar — so there is no mystery about the repertoire; it is published. For each proof the memo pays the same skeleton: the construction named, the auxiliary facts established with reasons, and the chain closed. In the tan-chord performance that meant: draw the diameter from the point of contact and join it across — construction mark; tangent perpendicular to radius — reason mark; angle in a semicircle — reason mark; then the two subtractions from ninety degrees meeting in the middle, and finally angles in the same segment carrying the result to the general chord. Rehearse each proof on blank paper, out loud, until the construction line arrives before you have consciously thought of it — the construction is where most candidates stall, because without the extra line the proof cannot start. And label your diagram exactly as your statements name it: a proof whose letters wander is a proof the marker cannot follow. Five rehearsals per proof, spread across the remaining weeks, and those marks move from hopeful to guaranteed.

**Mandy:** Last one: any Paper 2 specific advice for the day itself — the order, the calculator, the diagrams?

**Tutor:** Four rituals. Ritual one, before question one: check the calculator is in degree mode and clear the statistics registers — a radian-mode general solution or a leftover data list from practice poisons silently. Ritual two: statistics first, exactly as the paper presents it, because those are the most routine marks of the day and finishing them settles the pulse for the geometry ahead. Ritual three: every geometry question, analytical or Euclidean, begins by transferring the givens onto the diagram — and if no diagram is printed, drawing one is the first line of working, not a luxury. Angles discovered in early subquestions get written onto the figure immediately, because Paper 2 questions are cumulative: part three spends what part one earned, and the diagram is the bank account. Ritual four: at the end of every trigonometry answer, glance at plausibility — a triangle's largest side must face its largest angle, an inclination from a negative gradient must be obtuse, a tower is not four metres tall from forty-five metres away. Each glance costs seconds and has vetoed thousands of impossible answers in marking history. Then the general discipline from every practice run applies unchanged: no camping on a stubborn rider, margin mark, return visit, and trust that the eighty-plus routine marks come first to those who collect them calmly.
