**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First one, and it comes up a lot: why do the residuals get SQUARED before adding? Why not just add the misses, or take their absolute values?

**Tutor:** Try plain adding first and watch it fail. Residuals carry signs — dots above the line miss positively, dots below negatively — so a terrible line through a balanced cloud can score a total of zero, with huge up-misses cancelling huge down-misses. Plain addition cannot tell a perfect fit from a disaster with good manners, so it is useless as a judge. Absolute values fix the cancelling, and there is a genuine branch of statistics that uses them, but they treat a miss of 10 as exactly ten times a miss of 1. Squaring changes the pricing: a miss of 10 costs 100 while ten misses of 1 cost only 10 together, so squaring makes one large betrayal far more expensive than many small ones — the line is forced to stay close to EVERY point rather than abandoning one dot to please the rest. Squares are also smooth to minimise mathematically, which is why the calculator can produce the unique best a and b instantly, and why the answer has clean properties like passing through the mean point. So: squares kill the cancelling, punish big misses hardest, and make the best line unique and computable. That is three jobs with one operation.

**Mandy:** Next: the gradient came out as b equal to 4,57. What is the difference between just quoting that and interpreting it? The memo seems to want a sentence.

**Tutor:** The memo wants evidence that the number has meaning outside the calculator, and the sentence is that evidence. The gradient of any line is rise over run — how much y changes per unit of x. In a regression line the units come from the data, so b equal to 4,57 says: for each additional HOUR studied, the PREDICTED mark rises by about 4,57 PERCENTAGE POINTS. Three ingredients make the sentence scoreworthy: the unit of x, one hour; the unit of y, percentage points; and the word predicted or on average, because the line describes the trend, not a guarantee for any individual learner — one particular learner may study an extra hour and improve by 2 points or 8. Same discipline for the intercept: a equal to 46 is the predicted mark at zero hours of study, and here that reading is sensible because zero hours sits just outside a data range that starts at 1. In another data set — say mass of cars against fuel use — the intercept would describe a car of zero mass, and the correct interpretation is that a is just the line's anchor with no physical meaning. Numbers plus units plus context: that is interpretation, and it is usually two of the easiest marks on the paper.

**Mandy:** Third one, from the homework: what exactly does r add that the regression line does not already tell us? The line already shows the direction.

**Tutor:** The line tells you the direction and the rate; r tells you how much to TRUST the line. Picture two data sets sharing the identical regression line, y hat equals 46 plus 4,57 x. In the first, the six dots sit almost on the line — that is our study data, r equal to 0,997. In the second, dots are sprayed all over the board, and the least squares recipe still dutifully produces the same broomstick — but predictions from it would be little better than guessing. The slope cannot see the difference; r exists precisely to report it. Its sign copies the lean, its size measures the hug: above about 0,9 in size, very strong; 0,7 to 0,9, strong; 0,5 to 0,7, moderate; 0,3 to 0,5, weak; below that, essentially none. So a full commentary always pairs the two: the line for WHAT the trend is, r for HOW FAITHFULLY the data follows it, both in words and in context. And one caution keeps r honest: it only understands straight lanes. Dots on a perfect semicircle can score near zero, not because there is no relationship but because the relationship is not linear — which is why the scatterplot is inspected before either the line or r is quoted. The picture licences the numbers, never the other way round.

**Mandy:** Last one: the 15 hours question. The line predicted 114,55 percent, some of us wrote 100, some wrote it as is, and everyone apparently lost something. What was the fully correct answer?

**Tutor:** The fully correct answer is a calculation PLUS a refusal, and the refusal carries the marks. Show the substitution honestly: 46 plus 4,57 times 15 gives about 114,55. Then reject it, for two named reasons. Reason one: 15 hours is extrapolation — the data covered 1 to 6 hours, and the line's straightness was only ever verified inside that fence. Beyond it, real study behaviour bends: fatigue, diminishing returns, a natural ceiling. The line knows nothing past 6 hours; it simply continues straight because straight is all a line can do. Reason two: the prediction is impossible on its face — no test mark exceeds 100 percent — so the model has visibly broken. Writing 100 instead is not a repair; it invents a number the data never supported. Writing 114,55 without comment is worse: it presents an impossibility as a prediction. The examiner's mark sits on the sentence: this is extrapolation far outside the observed range, and the predicted value exceeds 100 percent, so the prediction is not valid. The general habit: every prediction question, first locate the x value relative to the data range. Inside, predict and round sensibly — interpolation, like the 4,5 hours giving about 67 percent. Outside, calculate if asked, then flag and refuse. The refusal is not a weakness; in statistics it is the correct result.
