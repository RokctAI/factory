**Mandy:** Class questions before we close! First one: why do the residuals get SQUARED in least squares? Adding the plain gaps seems simpler.

**Tutor:** Try the plain gaps and watch the method collapse. Residuals carry signs: a dot above the line files a positive miss, a dot below files a negative one. Add them raw and they cancel — in fact for any line through the mean point the raw misses sum to zero, so every such line would claim a perfect score and the criterion could not choose between a beautiful fit and a terrible one. The first fix you might try is absolute values, which stops the cancelling — but it treats one miss of ten the same as ten misses of one, and it makes the minimisation mathematically awkward. Squaring does both jobs at once: every squared miss is positive, so nothing cancels, and the penalty grows with the square of the miss, so one miss of ten costs one hundred while ten misses of one cost only ten — the line is forced to care most about the dots it would otherwise betray worst. That is why the least squares line settles into the cloud the way it does: it is the unique line for which the total squared complaint cannot be reduced by any nudge or tilt. And the balance shows up in the property we checked: the line passes through the mean point, the see-saw pivot of the data — tilt it off that pivot and the squared total immediately rises.

***

**Mandy:** Next: the intercept says a learner doing zero practice papers should score about 41 percent. Do we always interpret a like that, as a real prediction?

**Tutor:** Only when zero deserves the courtesy — and the test is whether x equals zero lives near the data. Our papers ran from two to seven, so zero sits just left of the observed range: a learner doing no practice papers is a real, nearby scenario, and reading a as roughly forty-one percent for no practice is a fair, lightly-flagged statement. But shift the context and the courtesy dies. Fit a line to the masses and heights of teenagers and the intercept describes a person of zero height — the line's anchor on the y-axis, nothing more. The honest habit is to ask two questions of every intercept: is x equal to zero physically meaningful, and is it inside or near the range I actually observed? Two yeses: interpret in context, with the usual caution that it is still a prediction. Any no: write that the intercept is only the constant of the line and has no practical reading here. The gradient never suffers this ambiguity — b is the change in predicted y per unit of x anywhere along the lane — which is why b earns interpretation credit in almost every question and a only sometimes. Reserve real-world sentences for numbers that describe the real world.

***

**Mandy:** Third one: if r is 0,99 — practically perfect — why can we still not say that doing practice papers CAUSES higher marks?

**Tutor:** Because r sees only that two columns of numbers march in step, and marching in step has at least three choreographies. Choreography one: practice genuinely improves marks — plausible here. Choreography two: the arrow points backwards — perhaps learners already scoring well enjoy the subject and therefore do more papers; the same dots would appear. Choreography three: a third variable pulls both strings — motivated learners both practise more AND revise better, so motivation lifts both columns while the columns merely watch each other. The scatterplot cannot distinguish these; neither can r, however close to one it climbs. My favourite demonstration is the shoe-size data: measure children across a primary school and shoe size correlates impressively with reading level — yet nobody argues that big feet read. Age drives both, and it is invisible in the two columns. That is why the licensed sentence is: there is a very strong positive linear association between practice papers and marks. Cause needs a different tool entirely — a controlled experiment, where you assign the practice and hold the rest still. Association is what the data shows; causation is a claim about the world's machinery, and our two columns never saw the machinery.

***

**Mandy:** Last one: for prediction, where exactly is the boundary between a trustworthy answer and one we must flag?

**Tutor:** The boundary is the edge of the observed x-values — the fence, and it is sharper than most people expect. Between two and seven papers, the data itself testified: we SAW the dots run straight there, and r told us how faithfully. Inside that window, the line speaks with borrowed authority from the evidence — 5,5 papers predicting about sixty-two percent is interpolation, quoted with confidence. One step past the fence, the authority is gone, not merely reduced: at eight papers the line is already guessing that the lane continues straight, a guess the data never tested; at twenty papers the guess has compounded into the absurd — one hundred and seventeen percent, a mark that cannot exist. Notice the two separate sins at twenty: extrapolation, predicting where straightness was never verified, and impossibility, predicting outside the variable's own ceiling. Both deserve their own sentence in an answer, and the second is non-negotiable: an impossible prediction is rejected, not rounded down. The wisdom behind the fence is that real relationships bend — returns diminish, fatigue arrives, ceilings loom — and a straight ruler only ever photographed one stretch of a curving road. So my routine for every prediction: locate the x inside or outside the observed range; inside, compute and state; outside, compute if asked, then flag as unreliable and, when the value is impossible, reject it in words. The flagging sentence is not decoration — it is the demonstration that you know what the line does and does not know.
