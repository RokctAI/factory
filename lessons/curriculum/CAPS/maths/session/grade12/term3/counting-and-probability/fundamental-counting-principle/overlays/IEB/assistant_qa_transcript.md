**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First up, the one I hear every year: how do we decide when two counts get MULTIPLIED and when they get ADDED? Half the class wanted to add the shirts to the jeans.

**Tutor:** Put one question to the two numbers: do they belong to the word AND, or to the word OR? A shirt AND a pair of jeans make an outfit — both decisions happen, one after the other, and every shirt choice leaves both jeans choices open. Sequential decisions multiply: 5 times 2 is 10 outfits. But bus OR bicycle to school — only one of them happens, the two worlds never meet, and the total is simply each world's pile of options stacked side by side: add. The grid settles any lingering doubt: shirts along the top, jeans down the side — the outfits are the CELLS, rows times columns, and nobody counts the cells of a grid by adding a row to a column. Adding 5 and 2 would count a wardrobe where you wear either a shirt or jeans but never both, which is a different and much colder wardrobe. So run the test in the question's own wording: things that happen together in sequence multiply; rival alternatives add. And in longer problems the two operations combine — multiply along each scenario, then add the scenarios — which is why writing the slots in order matters more than memorising any formula.

**Mandy:** Next one: in the photo question, why is together 4 factorial TIMES 2 and not just 4 factorial? That times 2 keeps disappearing from people's work.

**Tutor:** Because the glue creates one unit but not one PERSON — and the unit has an inside. Walk it through slowly. Tie the cousins with the invisible string: the row now holds four things to arrange — three loose debaters plus one knot — and four things arrange in 4 factorial ways, 24. But each of those 24 photographs still hides a decision inside the knot: which cousin is on the left of the pair and which on the right? Those are two genuinely different photographs sharing every other feature, so each of the 24 arrangements doubles: 48. The times 2 is really 2 factorial, the arrangements INSIDE the glue, and the idea scales: glue three people and the interior contributes 3 factorial, 6, multiplied by the 3 factorial for the three units outside. The dependable habit is to count in two layers and say both aloud: arrange the units, then arrange within each unit, then multiply the layers. Leaving out the interior exactly halves the answer, and 24 written where 48 belongs is the single most common slip in this entire topic.

**Mandy:** Third, from the homework: for the code with all different digits, some of us wrote 10 to the power 5 and then tried to repair it afterwards. Why does 10, 9, 8, 7, 6 work instead?

**Tutor:** Because the slot method counts options AT THE MOMENT each decision is made, and the no-repeat rule reshapes those moments. First digit: nothing has been used, all 10 digits stand ready. Second digit: exactly one digit is spent, and the rule bans reusing it — 9 remain. And no matter WHICH digit was spent, always exactly 9 remain; that uniformity is what allows the multiplication principle to fire at all. Third digit: 8 remain; fourth: 7; fifth: 6. Multiply: 10 times 9 times 8 times 7 times 6 is 30 240. The 10 to the power 5 version answers a different question entirely — codes where repetition is welcome, 100 000 of them — and no after-the-fact patch converts one count into the other, because they differ slot by slot rather than by one tidy factor. Notice the pattern that stitches the topic together: repetition allowed keeps every slot at full strength; repetition forbidden weakens each slot by one — precisely the factorial shrinkage that appeared with people in the photo row, since people are simply symbols that can never repeat. Read the repetition rule FIRST, write honest numbers over the slots, and the product needs no repair.

**Mandy:** Last question: in the probability step, favourable over total — how do we make sure the top and the bottom are counted in the same world? That is exactly where my fractions fall apart.

**Tutor:** That worry is the right worry: the fraction only carries meaning when numerator and denominator count the SAME kind of object under the SAME rules. Fix the world before counting anything: decide what one outcome IS. For the unlock code, one outcome is a complete 5-digit code with repetition allowed — so the bottom is 100 000, and the top must count codes of exactly that kind which happen to have five distinct digits: 30 240. Probability 0,3024. The classic mismatch is a bottom counted with repetition and a top counted without noticing, or the reverse — the fraction then compares apples to bakkies. Same discipline at the photo: one outcome is a complete line-up of all five debaters; bottom 120, top the 48 line-ups where the cousins touch, giving two fifths. It would be nonsense, for example, to put the cousins' 2 swaps over 120, because a swap is not a line-up. Two protections keep the world consistent. One: write a single sentence defining the outcome before either count, and use it for both. Two: sanity-check the finished fraction — it must sit between 0 and 1, and its complement should make sense: three fifths for the cousins separated matched the direct count of 72 over 120, and when the complement route and the direct route agree, the world was consistent from the start.
