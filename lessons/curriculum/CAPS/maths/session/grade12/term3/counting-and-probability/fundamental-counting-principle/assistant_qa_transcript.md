**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First one, and it comes up a lot: how do we know when to MULTIPLY counts and when to ADD them? Half the class adds the shirts to the trousers.

**Tutor:** Ask one question of the two numbers: do they answer to the word AND, or to the word OR? A shirt AND a trouser make an outfit — both decisions happen, one after the other, and every shirt choice leaves all trouser choices open. Sequential decisions multiply: 4 times 3 is 12 outfits. But taxi OR walking to school — only one happens, the two worlds never meet, and the total is just the pile of options from each world stacked together: add. The grid picture settles any doubt: shirts along the top, trousers down the side — the outfits are the CELLS of the grid, rows times columns, and nobody counts cells by adding a row to a column. Adding 4 and 3 would count as if you wear either a shirt or a trouser, never both, which is a different and colder wardrobe. So run the test in the question's own words: if the counted things happen together in sequence, multiply; if they are rival alternatives, add. And in long problems the two combine — multiply along each scenario, then add the scenarios — which is why writing the slots down in order matters more than any formula.

**Mandy:** Next: in the photo question, why is it 5 factorial TIMES 2 for the friends standing together, and not just 5 factorial? The times 2 keeps being forgotten.

**Tutor:** Because the glue makes one unit but not one PERSON, and the unit has an inside. Walk the count through slowly. Tie the two friends with the invisible string: the row now contains five things to arrange — four loose learners plus one knot — and five things arrange in 5 factorial ways, 120. But each of those 120 photographs is still ambiguous about the knot's interior: is the taller friend on the left of the knot or the right? Two genuinely different photographs share every other feature, so every one of the 120 arrangements doubles: 240. The times 2 is 2 factorial, the arrangements INSIDE the glue, and the principle scales: glue three people together and the interior contributes 3 factorial, 6, times the 4 factorial for the four units outside. The reliable habit is to count in two layers and say both out loud: arrange the units, then arrange within each unit, and multiply the layers. Forgetting the interior is exactly halving your answer, and markers see 120 where 240 belongs more often than any other slip in this topic.

**Mandy:** Third one, from the homework: for the PIN with all different digits, some of us wrote 10 times 10 times 10 times 10 and then tried to fix it afterwards. Why does 10, 9, 8, 7 work instead?

**Tutor:** Because the slot method counts options AT THE MOMENT each decision is made, and the no-repeat rule changes those moments. First digit: nothing is used yet, all 10 digits available. Second digit: one digit is now spent, and the rule forbids reusing it — 9 remain. Whichever digit was spent, ALWAYS exactly 9 remain, and that uniformity is what lets the multiplication principle fire. Third digit: 8 remain; fourth: 7. Multiply: 10 times 9 times 8 times 7 is 5 040. The 10 to the power 4 version answers a different question — codes where repetition is welcome, 10 000 of them — and no afterwards-fix can turn one count into the other, because the counts differ slot by slot, not by a tidy factor. Notice the contrast that runs through the whole topic: repetition allowed keeps every slot full strength; repetition forbidden makes each slot one weaker than the last, which is exactly the factorial pattern that appeared with people in the photo row — people are just symbols that can never repeat. So read the question for the repetition rule FIRST, then write the slots with honest numbers, and the product needs no repair.

**Mandy:** Last one: in the probability step, favourable over total — how do we make sure the two counts are counted in the same world? That is where my fractions keep going wrong.

**Tutor:** That instinct is exactly right: the fraction only means something when top and bottom count the SAME kind of object under the SAME rules. Fix the world first, before any counting: what is one outcome? For the PIN question, one outcome is a complete 4-digit code with repetition allowed — so the bottom is 10 000, and the top must count codes of precisely that kind which happen to have distinct digits: 5 040 of them. Probability 0,504. The classic mismatch is counting the bottom with repetition and the top without noticing, or the reverse — the fraction then compares apples to bakkies. Same discipline in the photo: one outcome is a full line-up of all six learners; bottom 720, top the 240 line-ups where the friends touch, giving one third. It would be wrong, for instance, to put the pair's 2 swaps over 720, because a swap is not a line-up. Two protections keep the world consistent. One: write a sentence defining the outcome before counting anything, and use it for both counts. Two: sanity-check the final fraction — it must land between 0 and 1, and its complement should make sense: two thirds for the friends separated matched the direct count of 480 over 720, and when a direct count and a complement count agree, the world was consistent all along.
