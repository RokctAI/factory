**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First: with four formulae on the table, people freeze choosing one. Is there a foolproof sorting method under exam pressure?

**Tutor:** Two questions, asked in order, sort every case. Question one: is the value climbing or falling — growth or decay? That fixes the sign inside the bracket: plus for growth, minus for decay. Question two: does the change compound — does each period's change act on the CURRENT value — or is it calculated forever on the original amount only? Compound means the bracket is raised to the power n; simple means i n sits inside the bracket, unraised. The vocabulary of the question usually answers question two for you: compounded, reducing balance, and depreciation on the book value all say compound; straight line and simple interest say simple. If the wording is silent, the context leans compound for banks and inflation, and the question will usually say straight-line explicitly when it wants it, because that method is the unusual one in the real world. Then one further check before computing: the units. The i and the n must speak the same period — an annual rate compounded monthly becomes a monthly rate, annual rate over twelve, with n counted in months. Write the conversion line before the formula every time; more finance marks die on mismatched periods than on wrong formulae. Sign, power, period: three decisions, and the formula assembles itself.

**Mandy:** Next: in the rate questions, why do we divide before taking the root? I have seen classmates take the cube root of both prices separately and get nonsense.

**Tutor:** Because the thing that was repeatedly multiplied is the FACTOR, not the prices, and the root must interrogate exactly the thing that repeated. Watch the structure: 128 000 equals 250 000 times the mystery factor cubed. The equation says the old price, multiplied by one unknown number three times over, lands on the new price. To expose the cubed factor you must first strip away the 250 000, and division is what strips a multiplier: the factor cubed equals 128 000 over 250 000, which is 0,512 — a pure, unitless ratio saying 51,2 percent of the value survived in total. Only now does the cube root make sense: it splits the three-year survival into three equal yearly survivals of 0,8 each. Rooting the prices separately computes the cube root of 128 000 and of 250 000 — two meaningless numbers with strange units, whose ratio accidentally equals the right answer only if you then divide them anyway, having done the work in the wrong order with more button presses and more places to slip. There is also a conceptual dividend in dividing first: the ratio A over P is the total growth or decay factor, and glancing at it tells you instantly whether your setup is sane — a decay question must produce a ratio below one, a growth question above. If 0,512 had come out as 1,953, you would know immediately the fraction was upside down, before any root obscured the evidence. Divide, inspect, then root: the order is the method.

**Mandy:** Third: logarithms scared everyone in term one, and now they are back. What exactly is the log doing in the how-long questions, in plain words?

**Tutor:** In plain words: a logarithm counts multiplications. That is its entire job. When you ask, how many times must I multiply by 1,09 to reach 2, the answer to that question IS log base 1,09 of 2 — the logarithm is not a trick applied to the problem, it is the NAME of the number you are looking for. The term one definition says the same thing formally: y equals log base b of x means b to the y equals x — the log is the exponent. Now, your calculator has no button for base 1,09, which is where the change of base rule earns its keep: log base 1,09 of 2 equals log 2 divided by log 1,09, using the log button you do have, any base, as long as top and bottom use the same one. Punch it: 8,04. And here is the way to make peace with the rule rather than memorising it blindly — check it in reverse. Raise 1,09 to the power 8,04 and the calculator hands back 1,9999 and small change: the count of multiplications was correct. That reverse check is worth doing once in the exam too, because it catches the classic inversion error, log 1,09 over log 2, which gives 12,4 — a number you would otherwise happily write down. The final skill is respecting the calendar: 8,04 counts fractional multiplications, but annual compounding only performs whole ones, at year ends. After eight multiplications the money sits just below double; the ninth crediting carries it past. So the mathematics says 8,04 and the bank account says end of year nine — give the examiner both sentences and the interpretation mark is yours.

**Mandy:** Last one: on timeline questions with a rate change and a late deposit, my two attempts gave different answers and I cannot tell which bookkeeping is legal.

**Tutor:** Both bookkeepings are legal — walking the balance and growing each deposit separately — and when they disagree, one of three specific errors has crept in, so audit for those rather than doubting the methods. Error one, the skipped segment: every rand must earn through every period between its arrival and the target date. The 5 000 deposited at the end of year two must ride through year three at 8 percent AND years four and five at 9 — three years of growth in total. The commonest slip gives it only the new-rate years, quietly parking it during year three. Error two, the misplaced regime boundary: the rate changed at the END of year three, so year three itself still runs at 8 percent. Write the regime spans explicitly — 8 percent covers years one to three, 9 percent covers years four and five — before any arithmetic, because off-by-one on a boundary shifts a whole year of interest. Error three, compounding across the boundary incorrectly: an amount crossing a rate change carries its accumulated VALUE across, then grows under the new factor — you multiply by 1,08 cubed and then by 1,09 squared; you never average the rates or restart the principal. Run the audit on the worked case: balance method gives 10 000 to 11 664, plus 5 000 makes 16 664, times 1,08 is 17 997,12, times 1,1881 is 21 382,38. Separate method: 10 000 times 1,08 cubed times 1,09 squared, plus 5 000 times 1,08 times 1,09 squared — same 21 382,38, guaranteed by the distributive law. When both roads meet, the answer is safe; when they differ, the difference itself tells you which deposit or which year went missing, because each error leaves a fingerprint of exactly one skipped factor.
