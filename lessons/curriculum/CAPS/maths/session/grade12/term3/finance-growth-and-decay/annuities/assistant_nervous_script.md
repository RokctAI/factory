Hey, it's Mandy — I know annuity questions are the longest paragraphs in the whole maths paper, and the two formulas look like typing errors. Here is what actually happens in every single one: equal payments stand in a row on a timeline, and you either push them all forward to a pile, or pull them all back to a debt. That is the entire chapter.

Push forward — future value — when you are SAVING: each payment grows for however many months remain after it lands, the row of values is a geometric series from term one, and its sum is the formula. Pull backward — present value — when you OWE: each promised payment shrinks to its today-value, and the loan equals the shrunken sum. The never-fails test: find where the lump sits. Lump at the end, push forward. Lump at the start, pull back.

Before any formula, do two small rituals. Convert the rate to the payment period — 12 percent per annum compounded monthly is one percent per month — and draw the timeline, marking the first payment, the last payment, and the valuation date. Every off-by-one error in this topic is a payment standing on the wrong tick of that line, and the drawing costs thirty seconds.

The scary-sounding extras are the same two moves dressed up. Sinking fund: inflate the future machine, depreciate the old one for scrap, subtract to get the target, then ordinary future value. Settlement or outstanding balance: the today-value of the payments you have not yet made — ordinary present value with the remaining months.

And keep the consumer insight as your reward: multiply out any instalment stream and compare it with the lump. That gap is the true cost of credit, and you are now one of the few people in the shop who can see it.

Push forward or pull back, convert the rate, draw the line. Three habits, two formulas, one geometric series you already own. You've got this!
