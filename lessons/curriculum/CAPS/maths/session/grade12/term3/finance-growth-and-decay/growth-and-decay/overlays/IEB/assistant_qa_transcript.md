**Mandy:** Let's take the class questions before we finish! First: why does compound interest beat simple interest by so much over long periods? Over one year they look almost identical.

**Tutor:** Because simple interest is paid on a frozen base while compound interest is paid on a moving one. Simple interest looks at the original deposit — 20 000 rand — and pays 7 percent of THAT, 1 400 rand, every single year until the end of time. Compound interest looks at the CURRENT balance, which already contains every earlier interest payment, so the interest itself starts earning interest. Year one, the two schemes pay the same 1 400. Year two, compound pays 7 percent of 21 400 — already 98 rand ahead — and the gap widens every year after that, because each year's advantage joins the base for the next. That is the escalator against the staircase: the staircase climbs by the same step forever; the escalator's speed grows with its height. Run the six-year numbers: stairs reach 20 000 plus six steps of 1 400 — 28 400 rand — while the escalator reaches 30 014,61. Over six years, a sixteen-hundred-rand gap; over twenty years the gap becomes the majority of the money. The sequence language makes it precise: arithmetic sequences grow linearly, geometric sequences grow exponentially, and no straight line outruns an exponential for long. That is also why loans hurt: debt rides the same escalator, just with you standing underneath it.

**Mandy:** Next: in the van question, someone divided 25 percent by three and said the van loses about 8 percent a year. Why is that wrong?

**Tutor:** Because percentage losses do not divide across years — they multiply. Losing a total of 57,8 percent over three years is NOT losing a third of that each year, since each year's percentage acts on a different base. Watch the actual mechanics: the van kept 0,421875 of its value over three years, and that keeping happened as factor times factor times factor. To find one year's factor you need the number whose cube is 0,421875 — the cube root, 0,75 — not some average of percentages. Check the wrong route honestly: shedding 19,3 percent a year — a third of the total 57,8 — would keep 0,807 cubed, about 0,525 of the value, not 0,422. The error is invisible in the language but glaring in the multiplication. This is exactly why the working rule is: divide end by start FIRST, then root by the number of years, then translate the factor into a rate by its distance from one. The factor is the honest currency of compound change; percentages are just its costume. And the same trap runs in reverse for growth: money that grows 33 percent over three years did not grow 11 percent a year — the yearly rate is the cube root of 1,33, about 9,97 percent. Whenever years and percentages meet, reach for factors and roots, never for division.

**Mandy:** Third one: why do we round the doubling time UP to the end of year 10, when 9,58 is clearly closer to 9 and a half?

**Tutor:** Because the bank's calendar is not a continuous line — it is a row of paydays. The formula 1,075 to the power n treats n as if interest flowed smoothly, and on that idealised line the balance would cross double at 9,58 years. But under annual compounding, the account is frozen between year ends: all of year ten, until its final day, the balance sits at 1,075 to the ninth — about 1,92 times the start — stubbornly short of double. Only when year ten's interest lands does the balance jump to about 2,06 times the start, past double at last. So the question when does the money first reach double has a calendar answer, not a decimal one: at the end of year 10. Nearest-number rounding would hand you a moment — nine and a half years in — when the money demonstrably had NOT doubled, and a wrong statement rounded prettily is still wrong. The rule generalises cleanly: find the raw n from the logarithm, then round UP to the next crediting moment — the next year end for annual compounding, the next month end for monthly. The one exception to watch: if the log lands exactly on a whole period, no rounding is needed, the crossing IS the payday. Always end a how-long answer by naming the schedule: at the end of year ten, at the end of month one hundred and fifteen — that phrasing shows you understood the machinery, not just the button sequence.

**Mandy:** Last question: on the timeline problem, why can't we just average the 7 percent and 8 percent into 7 and a half and grow everything at that?

**Tutor:** Because exponents do not average — they accumulate, and each rand only experiences the rates that ruled while it was in the account. Look at what the average destroys. The 12 000 deposit lived through four years of 7 percent and two of 8: its true factor is 1,07 to the fourth times 1,08 squared. The 6 000 deposit arrived at the end of year three and lived through one year of 7 and two of 8 — a completely different diet of rates. A single averaged rate would feed both deposits six years of 7,5 percent, overpaying the early years of the first deposit, overcharging its late ones, and inventing three phantom years for the second deposit entirely. Sometimes the errors partly cancel and the answer looks plausible — which makes the habit more dangerous, not less. The timeline is the honest bookkeeping: mark each event, cut the line at every rate change, and grow each amount through each regime it actually occupied, either walking the balance forward event by event — 14 700,52, then 20 700,52, then 22 149,55, then 25 835,24 — or growing each deposit separately to the end and adding. Both roads agree because the distributive law says they must. The averaged rate agrees with neither, except by accident. The principle beneath it all: compound growth is multiplication of factors, and the order and duration of the factors is the whole story — an average forgets both.
