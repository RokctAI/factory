**Mandy:** Class questions before we wrap up! First one, and it is the classic: how do we decide between the future value formula and the present value formula? Several people used future value on the bakkie loan and got a monster instalment.

**Tutor:** Anchor yourself to WHERE the lump sits on the timeline. Future value answers: I pay a stream now and collect a pile LATER — the lump lives at the end, so every payment gets grown forward to meet it. Present value answers: I collect a lump NOW and pay a stream later — the lump lives at the start, so every payment gets shrunk backward to meet it. The question's verb betrays the geometry every time: save, invest, accumulate all point forward — future value; borrow, repay, settle, buy on credit all point backward — present value. Using future value on a loan grows the repayments AWAY from the money you received, comparing values at two different dates, which is why the instalment came out grotesque. If the verb feels ambiguous, draw the timeline and mark the lump with a cross: payments must all be moved TO the cross, and the direction of that movement chooses the formula for you. One more tell: the future value bracket reads (1 + i)^n minus one — powers positive; the present value bracket reads one minus (1 + i)^(−n) — powers negative. Negative exponent, backward look, borrowed money.

**Mandy:** Next: in the sinking fund, why can't we just use one interest rate? Three rates in one question feels like three questions.

**Tutor:** It IS three questions, wearing one story — and that is the entire design. Each rate governs a different quantity that lives its own life. The inflation rate answers: what will the NEW machine cost in six years? That is compound growth on the price tag: 800 000 times 1,07 to the sixth, about 1,2 million rand. The depreciation rate answers: what will the OLD machine fetch as scrap? Reducing balance decay on the asset: 800 000 times 0,88 to the sixth, about 371 500 rand. Neither of those rates ever touches the fund. The fund's own rate answers only the third question: how hard do the monthly deposits work? Subtract scrap from future cost to get the target — roughly 829 000 rand — then run the future value annuity formula at 0,0075 per month to find the deposit, about 8 726 rand. The discipline that keeps marks safe is lane hygiene: write three separate headings, finish each lane's calculation inside its lane, and only let the numbers meet at the subtraction. The moment a depreciation rate wanders into an annuity factor, or the fund rate inflates the machine, the question is lost — not because the mathematics is hard, but because the lanes were crossed.

**Mandy:** Third one, from the homework: after 18 of the 48 payments we owe about 121 000 rand of the original 180 000. Why does paying off a loan start so slowly?

**Tutor:** Because interest is charged on the CURRENT balance, and at the start the balance is at its maximum. Follow one instalment in month one. The bank first computes interest: 0,875 percent of 180 000, which is 1 575 rand. Your 4 608,61 instalment must clear that toll before touching the debt, so only 3 033,61 lands as an actual reduction. Next month the balance is a whisker lower, the toll a whisker smaller, the landing portion a whisker bigger — and that whisker-by-whisker shift is the whole story of amortisation. Early payments are mostly toll; late payments are mostly demolition. That is why the balance after 18 months is not eighteen forty-eighths gone — the debt was being nibbled while the interest was feasting. And notice what the balance actually IS: not the original loan minus what you paid, but the present value of the thirty payments you still owe, discounted at the loan rate — that is the formula the bank runs when you request a settlement figure. The practical edge: any extra amount paid early lands entirely on the balance at its fattest, cancelling every future month of interest that slice would have generated. A modest early overpayment quietly deletes months off the far end of the loan.

**Mandy:** Last one: for the envelopes, why does the future value formula break if the payments start today instead of at month-end? The n is the same either way.

**Tutor:** The n is the same, but the EARNING TIME of every envelope is not — and the formula is really a ledger of earning times. The standard formula assumes the first payment arrives at the end of month one and the valuation happens at the moment of the last payment: earning times run from n minus one months down to zero. Shift the first payment to today and every single envelope gains exactly one extra month in the account: earning times now run from n months down to one. The tidy fix is to grow the whole standard answer by one factor of 1,0075 — same series, whole table shifted one month deeper into interest. The same logic covers the other classic twist: money valued three months AFTER the final deposit means the finished pile sits untouched and compounds three more months, so multiply by 1,0075 cubed. My rule for the class: never adjust n by instinct. Draw the timeline, place every payment as a dot, place the valuation date as a cross, and count each dot's months to the cross. If the dots match the standard pattern, use the formula raw; if they are shifted, use the formula and then grow or shrink by the mismatch. The formula is honest — it just answers exactly the timeline it was built for, and no other.
