# Part 1 — Expert

Grade eleven priced growth and decay and unmasked nominal rates. Grade twelve completes the toolkit: the four formulae revised and drilled, and then the two questions grade eleven could not answer — given the start and the end, what was the RATE, and, the true novelty of this year, how LONG does money need? The second question is solved by logarithms, and it is the reason logarithms are in the syllabus at all.

## Subtopic: The Four Formulae on One Timeline

The full kit, all sharing the cast: P the present or starting value, A the accumulated or ending value, i the rate per period as a decimal, n the number of periods. Simple growth: A equals P times the quantity one plus i n. Compound growth: A equals P times the quantity one plus i, raised to the power n. Simple decay: A equals P times the quantity one minus i n. Compound decay, also called reducing balance: A equals P times the quantity one minus i, raised to the power n.

The geometry behind the algebra: simple interest adds the same rand amount each period — an arithmetic sequence — while compound interest multiplies by the same factor each period — a geometric sequence with ratio one plus i. Term one of this year's mathematics and term three are the same subject wearing different clothes.

Drill one worked case. Invest 15 000 rand at 8 percent per annum, compounded annually, for 5 years. A equals 15 000 times 1,08 to the power 5. The power is 1,469328 to six decimals, so A is 22 039,92 rand. Two disciplines from grade eleven still rule: match i and n to the compounding period — monthly compounding divides the annual rate by twelve and counts n in months — and keep every decimal until the final rounding, because rounding 1,08 to the fifth too early costs rand-level accuracy.

When compounding is more frequent than annual, convert honestly: 9 percent per annum compounded monthly is zero comma zero zero seven five per month, and two years is twenty-four months.

Pause here — the questions for this section are with you now. Choose the formula by growth or decay, simple or compound, then match the rate and the clock to the same period.

## Subtopic: Solving for the Rate

An exam favourite: the start and end values are given, the time is given, and the RATE is the unknown. The compound formula then contains an unknown base raised to a known power, and the tool is the n-th root — the inverse of raising to the power n.

Worked case. A bakkie bought for 250 000 rand is worth 128 000 rand after 3 years on reducing balance. Find the annual depreciation rate. Set up: 128 000 equals 250 000 times the quantity one minus i, cubed. Divide: one minus i cubed equals 128 000 over 250 000, which is 0,512. Take the cube root: one minus i equals 0,8 — the cube root of 0,512 exactly, since 0,8 cubed is 0,512. So i is 0,2: the bakkie sheds 20 percent per annum.

The growth twin runs identically. If 10 000 rand grows to 13 604,89 rand in 4 years compounded annually, then one plus i to the fourth equals 1,360489, whose fourth root is 1,08 — the rate was 8 percent per annum. On the calculator, the fourth root is the power one quarter: raise 1,360489 to the power 0,25.

Two disciplines. First, divide BEFORE rooting: the root applies to the ratio A over P, never to A alone. Second, sign sense: reducing-balance answers should give one minus i between zero and one — if your root comes out above one in a decay question, the fraction was inverted. And state the final answer as a percentage, because the question asked for a rate, not a decimal.

Stop for this section's questions now — isolate the bracket, root it with the reciprocal power, and read decay rates off one minus i, not off the root itself.

## Subtopic: Solving for Time — Enter the Logarithm

Now the year's genuine novelty. How long will 5 000 rand take to double at 9 percent per annum compounded annually? Set up: 10 000 equals 5 000 times 1,09 to the power n, so 1,09 to the n equals 2. The unknown is in the EXPONENT, and no root can bring it down. This is precisely the question the logarithm answers: the log of a number, to a given base, is the exponent that base needs to reach that number. The definition from term one: y equals log base b of x means b to the y equals x.

Apply it: n equals log base 1,09 of 2, and by the change-of-base rule, that is log 2 divided by log 1,09. Compute: 0,693147 over 0,086178 in natural logs, or the same ratio in common logs — either way n is 8,04 years to two decimals.

Now interpret, because the decimal is not yet an answer. With ANNUAL compounding, interest arrives only at year ends: after 8 years the factor is 1,09 to the eighth, about 1,9926 — not yet doubled — and after 9 years about 2,1719. So the money first exceeds double at the END OF YEAR 9. The calculation says the crossing happens during the ninth year; the crediting schedule says the balance actually jumps past double when year nine's interest lands. When compounding is monthly, the same logic rounds n UP to the next month.

Inflation runs on the same mathematics: at 6 percent per annum, prices double in log 2 over log 1,06 years — 11,9 years. Every doubling question, money or prices or populations, is the same logarithm.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — unknown exponent means logarithm, change of base turns it into a division, and the decimal answer must be rounded to the crediting schedule before it means anything.

## Subtopic: Changing Conditions on One Timeline

Real accounts misbehave: rates change midstream, deposits arrive late, withdrawals interrupt. The master technique is the TIMELINE — mark every event at its time, grow each amount through each regime separately, and add value at the target date.

Worked case, fully. Deposit 10 000 rand at 8 percent per annum compounded annually. At the end of year 2, add 5 000 rand. At the end of year 3, the rate changes to 9 percent. Find the value at the end of year 5. Walk the line. Years zero to two: 10 000 times 1,08 squared is 11 664. Add the deposit: 16 664 at time two. Year two to three, still at 8 percent: 16 664 times 1,08 is 17 997,12. Years three to five at the new rate: 17 997,12 times 1,09 squared. Now 1,09 squared is 1,1881, and the product is 21 382,38 rand.

The alternative bookkeeping grows each deposit separately to the end — 10 000 through three years of 8 percent and two of 9, plus 5 000 through one year of 8 and two of 9 — and adds. Same answer, by the distributive law; choose whichever keeps your page tidy.

The rules that make timelines safe. Every amount travels through every period between its arrival and the target date — no amount skips a segment. Rate changes cut the line into regimes, and an amount crossing a boundary carries its accumulated value across, then grows under the new rules. Withdrawals subtract at their date and simply stop growing for the departed amount. Draw the line, mark the arrows, and a four-event question becomes bookkeeping instead of acrobatics.

The final questions of this part are with you now — draw the timeline before touching the calculator, grow through each regime in turn, and let every rand account for every period it was present.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same growth and decay from a stokvel book and a second-hand car lot — same formulae, same answers, built from things you can picture.

## Subtopic: Escalator Up, Escalator Down

Money on compound interest rides an escalator: each year multiplies by the same factor. At 8 percent, the factor is 1,08 — this year's whole balance, including all past interest, times 1,08. After five years, five multiplications: 15 000 rand becomes 15 000 times 1,08 to the fifth — 22 039,92 rand. Compare simple interest, the stairs: the same fixed step of 1 200 rand each year, calculated forever on the original 15 000 only. Stairs add; escalators multiply. That is the entire difference between simple and compound, and it is the arithmetic-versus-geometric split from term one wearing a bank uniform.

Decay is the same escalator, heading down. A machine losing 15 percent a year on reducing balance keeps 85 percent of its CURRENT value each year: times 0,85, times 0,85, again and again. It never quite reaches zero — 85 percent of something is always something — while straight-line decay, the downward stairs, marches by equal steps and does hit the floor.

Choosing the formula is two questions. Up or down — growth or decay? Stairs or escalator — simple or compound? Four combinations, four formulae, no memorising beyond the pattern: stairs put i n inside the bracket with plus or minus; escalators raise the bracket to the power n.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the four formulae are coming to you right now. Ask up or down, stairs or escalator, and the formula chooses itself.

## Subtopic: The Car Lot Detective

A second-hand dealer says: this bakkie cost 250 000 rand new, three years ago; it is priced at 128 000 today. What yearly percentage did it shed? That is rate-detective work, and the method is to un-multiply.

Three years of reducing balance means the original was multiplied by the same mystery factor three times. Total damage: 128 000 over 250 000 — 0,512 of the value kept. One year's factor is the number that multiplies by itself three times to give 0,512: the cube root. Cube root of 0,512 is 0,8, because 0,8 times 0,8 is 0,64, and 0,64 times 0,8 is 0,512. So each year kept 80 percent — the bakkie shed 20 percent per annum.

The detective's two habits. Habit one: always divide end by start FIRST, to get the total factor, before any rooting — the root un-does the repeated multiplication, and it must act on the factor, not on the raw price. Habit two: sense-check which side of one the factor lands. Decay keeps less than everything, so the yearly factor must sit below one; growth's factor must sit above. A decay answer above one means the fraction went in upside down — catch it yourself before the marker does.

Growth detective work is identical with the root on the other side of one: money that turned 10 000 into 13 604,89 over four years kept a yearly factor of the fourth root of 1,360489 — exactly 1,08, so 8 percent per annum growth.

Your questions for this part are up now. Divide end by start, root by the years, and read the percentage off the factor's distance from one.

## Subtopic: How Long Until It Doubles

The last question money ever asks: how long? At 9 percent compounded annually, when does 5 000 rand become 10 000? The setup collapses to 1,09 to the power n equals 2 — and here multiplication runs out of ordinary tools, because the unknown n is upstairs in the exponent. Roots pull down known powers; they cannot pull down unknown ones.

The logarithm is the tool built for exactly this. A log answers one question: how many times must I multiply by the base to reach this number? Log base 1,09 of 2 asks: how many rides on the 9 percent escalator double my money? Your calculator computes it as a division: log of 2 divided by log of 1,09 — about 8,04.

But 8,04 is a raw reading, not an answer, because the bank only pays interest at year ends. Check the ledger: after 8 years the factor is about 1,99 — agonisingly short of double. The balance crosses double only when year nine's interest lands. So the honest answer is: at the end of year 9. Round to the crediting schedule, not to the nearest number — with monthly compounding you would round the months up instead.

The same tool answers every how-long question. Prices doubling at 6 percent inflation: log 2 over log 1,06, about 11,9 years — keep money under a mattress for twelve years and it buys half. A population, a debt, a rumour spreading at a fixed percentage: same logarithm, same division, same careful rounding at the end.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: unknown exponent calls the logarithm, the log of the target factor over the log of the yearly factor, and the calendar answer honours when interest actually arrives.
