Hey, it's Mandy — I know finance questions read like bank small print, and the sight of logarithms coming back for a second round is nobody's favourite news. Let me shrink this lesson to its true size: one escalator, three unknowns.

The escalator: compound growth multiplies the whole current balance by the same factor every period — 1,08 for 8 percent up, 0,85 for 15 percent reducing-balance down. Simple interest is just stairs, the same fixed step each year on the original amount. Choose by asking two questions: up or down, stairs or escalator. Then match the rate and the clock to the same period — monthly rate with months, yearly with years — in a written conversion line before the formula. That line alone rescues more marks than any other habit in this chapter.

Three things can be unknown, and each has its own key. Unknown ending value: substitute and calculate, keeping every decimal until the end. Unknown rate: divide end by start FIRST to get the total factor, then take the n-th root — power one over n on your calculator — and sense-check that decay factors land below one, growth factors above. Unknown time: the mystery number is upstairs in the exponent, and the tool is the logarithm, which simply counts multiplications. Log of the target factor divided by log of the yearly factor, then round to the crediting schedule — annual interest arrives at year ends, so 8,04 becomes end of year nine.

For messy stories — deposits arriving late, rates changing midstream — draw the timeline before touching the calculator. Every rand grows through every period it is present, and rate changes cut the line into regimes.

Escalator, divide-then-root, count-the-multiplications, draw the line. Four keys, and every finance door in the paper opens. You've got this!
