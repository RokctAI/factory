**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First one, asked by nearly everyone: future value or present value — is there a test that never fails when the question is long and wordy?

**Tutor:** There is, and it ignores the wording almost entirely: find WHERE THE LUMP SUM SITS on the timeline. Every annuity question has a stream of equal payments and one lump. If the lump sits at the END — a savings target, a fund that must reach some amount, money available after the last deposit — the payments grow FORWARD toward it: future value. If the lump sits at the START — a loan received today, a cash price enjoyed now, a settlement amount owed at this moment — the payments are pulled BACKWARD to it: present value. Savings and sinking funds are future value; loans, bonds, hire purchase and settlement figures are present value. The verb test is the quick version — save points forward, owe points backward — but the lump-position test survives even deliberately confusing wording, such as a question that talks about saving while actually asking what lump today would replace the stream. One more discriminator for the truly ambiguous cases: ask what the interest is DOING. If interest is being earned on money you own, accumulating, that is the forward direction. If interest is being charged on money you owe, and your payments fight it, that is the backward direction. Position of the lump, direction of the pull — settle those two and the formula chooses itself before you read the question a second time.

**Mandy:** Next: in the derivation, why does the FIRST payment earn n minus one months and not n? That off-by-one costs people the whole derivation.

**Tutor:** Put actual dates on it and the off-by-one evaporates. Say the annuity is twelve monthly payments, first one at the end of January, valuation at the moment of the December payment. The January payment is deposited at the end of January and the clock stops at the end of December: February through December, eleven months of interest — twelve minus one. The December payment is deposited at the very instant the valuation happens: zero months. Count the earning periods for each payment and they run n minus one, n minus two, down to zero — never n, because no payment exists at time zero in this arrangement; the stream starts one period in. That is what payments at the end of each period means, and it is the default the formula encodes. Now, questions can and do change the arrangement, and the derivation is exactly what lets you adapt instead of panic. First payment TODAY, valuation at the last payment: every payment slides one month earlier, every exponent lifts by one, and the whole sum is the standard formula multiplied by one plus i. Payments stop but the money sits untouched for four more months: the standard pile grows by one plus i to the fourth. The examiners are not testing whether you memorised one formula; they are testing whether you can stand each payment on the timeline and count its months honestly. Do the count for the first payment, the last payment, and one in the middle, and the right exponents declare themselves — the geometric series does the rest, exactly as it did in term one.

**Mandy:** Third: the sinking fund question uses three different rates in one problem. How do I stop them contaminating each other?

**Tutor:** Give each rate a lane, and never let a number cross lanes without a passport. Lane one, the future cost: equipment inflation prices the REPLACEMENT machine. It touches only the current price and the years: 500 000 times 1,06 to the fifth gives 669 112,79. Note it compounds annually, because inflation is quoted annually — five as the exponent, not sixty. Lane two, the scrap value: reducing-balance depreciation prices the OLD machine at sale time: 500 000 times 0,85 to the fifth is 221 852,66. Also annual, also exponent five, and the sign inside the bracket is minus because value is decaying. Lanes one and two meet only once, in a subtraction: the fund's target is the shortfall, 447 260,13. Lane three, the fund itself: the bank's 8 percent compounded monthly grows the PAYMENTS — and only here do you convert to a monthly rate, 0,08 over twelve, with sixty as the count. The classic contaminations are using the fund's monthly rate to inflate the machine price, using an exponent of sixty on the inflation lane, or forgetting the subtraction and saving toward the full future price as though the scrap sale were worthless. The bookkeeping that prevents all three: before calculating anything, write three labelled lines — future cost equals, scrap equals, target equals future cost minus scrap — each with its own rate and its own clock, and only then bring out the annuity formula for lane three. Three lanes, one merge point, one formula at the end. The question is long, but no single step in it is harder than the previous lesson.

**Mandy:** Last one: the outstanding balance result upset people. After nearly half the payments, two thirds of the debt remains — why do the numbers punish the borrower like that, and is there a second way to compute the balance?

**Tutor:** The numbers are not punishing anyone; they are charging rent on money for exactly as long as it is borrowed, and early in the loan you are renting a great deal of money. Look inside month one: the balance is the full 250 000, so the month's interest is one percent of that, 2 500 rand. Your instalment of 5 561,11 must pay the rent first; only the leftover 3 061,11 buys back debt. By the final months the balance is a few thousand, the rent is pennies, and almost the entire instalment is demolition. So the debt-reduction power of your payments RAMPS UP over time — equal instalments do unequal work, weakest first. That asymmetry, called amortisation, is why 24 payments clear only about a third of the principal, and it is also why any EXTRA rand paid early is so powerful: it removes balance at the moment balance is most expensive to hold. Yes, there is a second computation, and using both is the professional's check. Method one, retrospective: grow the original loan forward 24 months as though untouched — 250 000 times 1,01 to the twenty-fourth — and subtract the future value of the 24 payments you actually made, grown at the same rate. Method two, prospective, the one from the lesson: the present value of the 36 remaining payments — 5 561,11 times one minus 1,01 to the minus thirty-six, over 0,01 — about 167 431 rand. The two methods must agree, because both express the same fairness equation sliced at month 24. When an exam asks for a settlement figure after some payment number, the prospective method is usually two lines; when it asks how much of the loan has been repaid, the retrospective view answers directly. Carry both, check one against the other when the marks justify the minute, and the balance questions lose their sting entirely.
