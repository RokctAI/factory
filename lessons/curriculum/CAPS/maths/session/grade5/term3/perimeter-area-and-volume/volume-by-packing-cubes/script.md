# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers finding the volume or capacity of objects by packing or filling them, to develop an understanding of cubic units. The anchor is a Grade 5 class in Paarl that helps a local fudge maker pack gift boxes for a school fair: every piece of fudge is a cube 1 cm long on each side, and the boxes come in different sizes. The governing idea is that volume is the amount of space an object takes up or a container holds, measured by counting how many equal cubes fill it with no gaps, and that boxes are counted most easily in layers. Packing boxes with cubes, counting layers, comparing volumes and filling containers are examinable.

## Subtopic: Volume and Cubic Units

Volume is the amount of space something takes up. Capacity is the amount a container can hold. A box of fudge has a volume, and the inside of the box has a capacity: how much fudge it can hold.

To measure volume, we use cubes, all the same size, packed with no gaps. Each cube is one cubic unit. The fudge pieces are centimetre cubes: each one is 1 cm long, 1 cm wide and 1 cm high. One centimetre cube has a volume of 1 cubic centimetre.

Length is measured in units like centimetres, area in square units, and volume in cubic units. A line has length only. A flat shape has length and width. A solid object has length, width and height, so it needs a unit that has all three: a cube.

Cubes work well because they stack and fit together with no gaps, like bricks in a wall. Balls or marbles would leave gaps, so counting them would not give the true volume.

Volume and capacity use the same kind of unit, but for different jobs. The fudge itself has a volume. The empty gift box has a capacity. When the box is packed full, the volume of the fudge is the same as the capacity of the box.

A centimetre cube is small, about the size of a sugar cube. Bigger objects can be measured with bigger cubes, as long as all the cubes are the same size. A cardboard box packed with 30 matchboxes, all the same size, has a volume of 30 matchboxes, but that unit is less useful than cubic centimetres, because matchboxes are not all made the same.

The questions for this section are with you now: what volume and capacity mean, and why we use cubic units.

## Subtopic: Packing Boxes with Cubes

The small gift box is packed one layer at a time. The bottom layer has 4 rows of 3 cubes, which is 12 cubes. The box is 2 cubes high, so there are 2 layers. 12 plus 12 is 24, or 2 times 12 is 24. The volume of the box is 24 cubic centimetres, and it holds 24 pieces of fudge.

Counting in layers is quicker than counting every cube one by one, and it helps you count the cubes you cannot see. In a packed box, the cubes in the middle are hidden, but they still take up space and must be counted.

The large gift box has a bottom layer of 5 rows of 4 cubes, which is 20 cubes, and it is 3 layers high. 3 times 20 is 60 cubes, so its volume is 60 cubic centimetres.

The steps are always the same: count the cubes in one layer, count the layers, and multiply or add. Check by imagining the box being packed: does each layer fit exactly, with no gaps?

The fudge maker also has a box with a bottom layer of 3 rows of 3 cubes, which is 9 cubes, and 3 layers. 3 times 9 is 27 cubes. This box is a cube itself: 3 cubes long, 3 cubes wide and 3 cubes high.

The questions for this section are with you now: counting cubes in layers and finding the volume of packed boxes.

## Subtopic: Comparing Volumes and Filling Containers

Different boxes can have the same volume. A box with a layer of 4 by 3 cubes, 2 layers high, holds 24 cubes. A long box with a layer of 6 by 2 cubes, 2 layers high, also holds 24 cubes. They look different, but they hold the same amount of fudge. A box 3 by 3 by 3 holds 27 cubes, so it holds 3 more than either of them.

Capacity can also be found by filling. The fudge maker's jug is filled with sugar, one cup at a time. If 8 cups fill it, its capacity is 8 cups. To compare two containers, fill them with the same unit, such as the same cup, and count. The container that takes more cups has the bigger capacity.

A box 10 cm long, 10 cm wide and 10 cm high holds 1 000 centimetre cubes, because each layer has 100 cubes and there are 10 layers. That box would hold exactly 1 litre of water, which links volume to the capacity units you will meet in Term 4.

Filling can also compare containers of different shapes. The fudge maker pours dry rice into the 24-piece box and then into a small round tin, using the same spoon. The box takes 5 spoonfuls and the tin takes 8, so the tin has the bigger capacity, even though it is a different shape.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: counting only the cubes you can see from the front, 8, instead of all 24. Two: counting only the bottom layer, 12, and forgetting the second layer. Three: using cubes of different sizes, so the count does not compare fairly. Four: writing the volume as 24 square centimetres; volume uses cubic units.

The questions for this section are with you now: comparing volumes, and finding capacity by filling.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the fudge boxes in Paarl — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Space Inside

Volume is the space something takes up. Capacity is how much it can hold.

One fudge cube: 1 cubic centimetre.

## Subtopic: Layer by Layer

Bottom layer: 4 rows of 3 is 12 cubes. 2 layers: 24 cubes.

Big box: 20 in a layer, 3 layers: 60 cubes.

Hold the layer-times-layers habit in mind and try this part's questions: packing boxes.

## Subtopic: Which Holds More?

4 by 3 by 2 and 6 by 2 by 2: both 24. 3 by 3 by 3: 27.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Volume is the amount of space an object takes up, and capacity is how much a container holds. Measure volume by packing equal cubes with no gaps and counting them, in cubic units such as cubic centimetres. Count the cubes in one layer, count the layers, and multiply, remembering the hidden cubes. Different boxes can have the same volume. Find capacity by filling a container with a unit, such as a cup, and counting.

Hold the count-the-hidden-cubes rule in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: comparing volumes and capacity.
