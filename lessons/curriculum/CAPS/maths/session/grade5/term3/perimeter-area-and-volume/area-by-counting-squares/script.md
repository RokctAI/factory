# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers finding the areas of regular and irregular shapes by counting squares on grids, to develop an understanding of square units. The anchor is a Grade 5 class in Thohoyandou designing a carpeted reading corner: they draw possible carpet shapes on grid paper, where each square stands for one carpet tile, and count how many tiles each design needs. The governing idea is that area is the amount of flat surface inside a shape, measured by counting how many equal squares cover it with no gaps and no overlaps. Counting squares, using half squares, estimating curved areas and comparing areas are examinable.

## Subtopic: Area and Square Units

Area is the amount of flat surface a shape covers. The carpet tiles are squares, so the class measures area by counting how many tiles fit inside each design. Each square on the grid paper is one square unit.

The first design is a rectangle 5 squares long and 3 squares wide. Count the squares: there are 3 rows with 5 squares in each row. 5 plus 5 plus 5 is 15, or 3 times 5 is 15. So the area is 15 square units, and the corner needs 15 carpet tiles.

Square units must all be the same size, and they must cover the shape with no gaps and no overlaps. If some tiles overlapped, or there were gaps between them, the count would not tell you the true area.

A square centimetre is a square 1 cm long and 1 cm wide. On centimetre grid paper, each small square is 1 square centimetre. A 5 by 3 rectangle drawn on that paper has an area of 15 square centimetres. Area always needs the word square in its unit, because it counts squares, not lengths.

The questions for this section are with you now: what area means, counting squares in rectangles and squares, and square units.

## Subtopic: Counting Squares in Irregular Shapes

The second design is L-shaped, so it fits around a bookshelf. On the grid it is made of a 4 by 2 rectangle with a 2 by 2 square on top of one end. Count the squares: 8 in the rectangle and 4 in the square, which is 12 square units. Counting in rows works too: the bottom two rows have 4 squares each and the top two rows have 2 squares each, so 4 plus 4 plus 2 plus 2 is 12.

To avoid counting a square twice or missing one, mark each square with a dot as you count it, or count row by row.

Some designs have slanted edges that cut squares in half. Two half squares make one whole square. The third design is a triangle with two straight sides of 4 squares and a slanted side joining them. Inside it there are 6 whole squares and 4 half squares. The 4 halves make 2 whole squares, so the area is 6 plus 2, which is 8 square units.

Count whole squares first, then pair up the halves. Write the area with its unit: 8 square units.

The questions for this section are with you now: counting squares in L shapes and other irregular shapes, and using half squares.

## Subtopic: Comparing Areas and Estimating

Shapes that look different can have the same area. A 4 by 3 rectangle has 12 squares. A 6 by 2 rectangle also has 12 squares. The L-shaped design has 12 squares too. All three need 12 carpet tiles.

Shapes with the same area can have different perimeters. The 4 by 3 rectangle has a perimeter of 14 units, but the 6 by 2 rectangle has a perimeter of 16 units. Area counts the squares inside; perimeter measures the distance around the outside.

For a shape with curved edges, like a round rug, you cannot count exactly, so you estimate. Count every whole square inside. Then look at the part squares: count each one that is half or more as 1, and leave out each one that is less than half. For the round rug, the class counts 21 whole squares and 8 part squares of half or more, so the area is about 29 square units.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: counting the dots where the grid lines cross instead of the squares, so a 5 by 3 rectangle seems to have 24. Two: counting half squares as whole squares, so the triangle seems to have 10 square units instead of 8. Three: mixing up area and perimeter, so the 4 by 3 rectangle is given an area of 14. Four: writing the area as 15 with no unit, or as 15 cm; area needs square units.

The questions for this section are with you now: comparing areas, area and perimeter, and estimating areas of curved shapes.

## Subtopic: Same Area, Different Shape

4 by 3 and 6 by 2 both have 12 squares, but different perimeters.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Area is the amount of flat surface inside a shape. Measure it by counting equal squares that cover the shape with no gaps and no overlaps, and write it in square units, such as square centimetres. Count row by row and mark each square. Two half squares make one whole. For curved shapes, count whole squares, count part squares of half or more as 1, and leave out smaller ones, to estimate. Different shapes can have the same area, and the same area can have different perimeters.

Hold the square-units rule in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: comparing and estimating areas.
