### subtopic_1 — Volume and Cubic Units
**Assistant:** Why do I need to count the cubes I cannot see?

**Tutor:** Because they are there, taking up space, even though they are hidden inside. When you look at a packed box of fudge from the outside, you see only the cubes on the front, top and sides. The cubes in the middle are covered by the others. But each one is a piece of fudge, and each one fills a cubic centimetre of space. If you count only what you can see, you will get the wrong volume. For the small box, the front shows only 8 cubes, 4 across and 2 high, but the box actually holds 24. Counting in layers solves this problem. You count the cubes in one layer, including the ones in the middle of the layer, which you can see before the next layer is put on top. Then you count how many layers there are and multiply. If you are looking at a picture of a stack of cubes, imagine taking the top layer off to see the layer below. Every layer in a box is the same, so once you know one layer, you know them all.

### subtopic_2 — Packing Boxes with Cubes
**Assistant:** What is the difference between volume and capacity?

**Tutor:** Volume is the amount of space an object takes up, and capacity is the amount a container can hold. They are closely linked. A solid block of fudge 4 cm by 3 cm by 2 cm has a volume of 24 cubic centimetres: that is the space it fills. An empty gift box with the same inside measurements has a capacity of 24 cubic centimetres: that is how much it can hold. So you can think of capacity as the volume of the space inside a container. Capacity is often measured by filling, especially for liquids or things that pour, like sugar, rice or water. You fill the container with a unit, such as a cup or a spoon, and count how many it takes. Volume of a solid is often measured by packing, counting cubes. In Term 4 you will measure capacity in millilitres and litres. A cube 10 cm on each side holds 1 000 centimetre cubes and exactly 1 litre of water, which shows how the two ideas fit together.

### subtopic_3 — Comparing Volumes and Filling Containers
**Assistant:** How can two boxes that look different hold the same amount?

**Tutor:** Because volume depends on the total number of cubes, not on how the box looks. A box with a bottom layer of 4 rows of 3 cubes, 2 layers high, holds 12 times 2, which is 24 cubes. A long, thin box with a bottom layer of 6 rows of 2 cubes, 2 layers high, also holds 12 times 2, which is 24 cubes. Even a tall box, 2 by 3 at the bottom and 4 layers high, holds 6 times 4, which is 24 cubes. They are different shapes, but each holds 24 pieces of fudge. This is like area, where a 4 by 3 rectangle and a 6 by 2 rectangle both cover 12 squares. To compare the volumes of two boxes, count the cubes in each, or work out layer times number of layers, and compare the totals. Do not judge by the height alone or the length alone, because a tall narrow box can hold less than a short wide one. For the fudge maker, this means she can choose any of these box shapes for a 24-piece gift box, depending on which looks best.
