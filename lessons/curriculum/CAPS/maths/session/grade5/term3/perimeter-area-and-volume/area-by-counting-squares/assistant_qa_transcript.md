### subtopic_1 — Area and Square Units
**Assistant:** Why is area measured in square units and not in centimetres?

**Tutor:** Because area measures how much flat surface a shape covers, and you cover a surface with squares, not with lines. A centimetre is a length: it measures how far along a line something goes. That is perfect for perimeter, which is the distance around a shape. But area is about space spread out in two directions, long and wide at the same time. To measure it, you need a unit that also spreads out in two directions, and a square does exactly that. A square centimetre is a small square 1 cm long and 1 cm wide. When you say a rectangle has an area of 15 square centimetres, you mean that 15 of those little squares would cover it exactly. If you wrote 15 cm, a reader would think of a line 15 cm long, which is something quite different. Squares are used because they fit together with no gaps and no overlaps, like floor tiles. Circles would leave gaps between them, so they would not measure area properly. That is why grid paper, made of equal squares, is the perfect tool for learning about area.

### subtopic_2 — Counting Squares in Irregular Shapes
**Assistant:** How do I count half squares?

**Tutor:** Count the whole squares first, then count the half squares, and remember that every two halves make one whole. In the triangle design, there are 6 whole squares and 4 half squares along the slanted edge. Pair up the halves: 4 halves make 2 wholes. Add these to the whole squares: 6 plus 2 is 8 square units. If you have an odd number of halves, there will be one half left over, and the area will include a half, such as 9 and a half square units. A common slip is to count every part square as a whole square, which would give 10 for the triangle. Another slip is to ignore the halves completely, which would give 6. Neither is right. It helps to mark the whole squares with one colour and the half squares with another before you count, so you can see them clearly. If a part square is not exactly half, like the pieces cut by a curve, you can only estimate: count pieces that are half or more as 1 and leave out smaller pieces.

### subtopic_3 — Comparing Areas and Estimating
**Assistant:** Can two shapes have the same area but different perimeters?

**Tutor:** Yes. Area and perimeter measure different things, so they do not have to change together. A rectangle 4 squares long and 3 squares wide has an area of 12 square units, because it has 3 rows of 4 squares. Its perimeter is 4 plus 3 plus 4 plus 3, which is 14 units. Now rearrange those same 12 squares into a rectangle 6 squares long and 2 squares wide. The area is still 12 square units, because you used the same 12 squares. But the perimeter is now 6 plus 2 plus 6 plus 2, which is 16 units. A long, thin shape has a bigger perimeter than a squarer shape with the same area. If you put all 12 squares in one row, 12 by 1, the perimeter would be 26 units. For the reading corner, this matters. The carpet needed depends on the area, but if the class wanted to put a border strip around the carpet, the amount of border would depend on the perimeter. So a long, thin design would need more border, even though it uses the same number of tiles.
