### subtopic_1 — 3D Objects and Data
**Assistant:** There is so much in Term 3. What should I revise first?

**Tutor:** Start with the ideas that link several topics, because each one helps you in more than one place. First, units. Length, perimeter, area and volume all depend on using the right unit: centimetres and metres for length and perimeter, square units for area and cubic units for volume. Knowing the three conversion facts, 10 mm in a centimetre, 100 cm in a metre and 1 000 m in a kilometre, will help you in every measurement question. Second, careful counting. Tallies, pictographs, squares on a grid and cubes in a box all need counting that does not miss or double anything, so practise counting in rows and layers. Third, reading displays: always read the title, key or scale and labels before the numbers. Then go through each topic and do two or three questions from it, rather than many from one. Keep a list of mistakes you made, with the right method next to each, and read it the night before the test. Spend most time on the topics that feel uncomfortable, because that is where the extra marks are.

### subtopic_2 — Probability and Length
**Assistant:** How do I keep perimeter, area and volume apart?

**Tutor:** Think about what each one measures and what unit it uses. Perimeter is the distance around the edge of a flat shape, like the lace around a tablecloth. It is a length, so it uses units like centimetres and metres, and you find it by adding the sides. Area is the amount of flat surface inside a shape, like the cloth itself. It uses square units, and you find it by counting the squares that cover it, row by row. Volume is the amount of space a solid takes up or a container holds, like the sweets packed in a box. It uses cubic units, and you find it by counting the cubes that fill it, layer by layer. One way to remember: perimeter is a line, so it has one direction; area is flat, so it spreads in two directions, length and width; and volume is solid, so it spreads in three directions, length, width and height. If a question asks about a fence, a frame or a border, it is perimeter. If it asks about carpet, paint or a cloth, it is area. If it asks about filling or packing, it is volume.

### subtopic_3 — Perimeter, Area and Volume
**Assistant:** How do I check a data handling answer?

**Tutor:** Use the totals, the key or scale, and common sense. First, check that the frequencies in a table add up to the number of people asked or the number of trials: food 15, games 11, crafts 8 and books 6 make 40, which matches the 40 visitors. If the total does not match, something was counted twice or missed. Second, when you read a pictograph, multiply by the key, and remember that half a symbol is half of the key: with one star for 2 visitors, 7 and a half stars is 15 visitors. When you read a bar graph, slide across from the top of the bar to the scale, and check that the scale goes up in equal steps from 0. Third, for the mode, give the value or category that appears most often, not how many times it appears: the mode is food, not 15. Finally, check your conclusions. Do not claim more than the data shows. A survey of 40 visitors at one fun day tells you about those visitors, and any prediction should use words like probably.
