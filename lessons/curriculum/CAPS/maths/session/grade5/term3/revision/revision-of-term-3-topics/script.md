# Part 1 — Expert

This session revises the Term 3 topics: properties of 3D objects; data handling, from collecting and organising data to drawing and reading graphs and finding the mode; probability with coins, dice and spinners; length, including measuring and converting units; and perimeter, area and volume. The anchor is a school fun day in Port Shepstone, with gift boxes and tins at the prize table, a survey of 40 visitors' favourite stalls, a lucky spinner, a sack race track, tablecloths for the stalls, and boxes of sweets. The governing idea is that every Term 3 skill comes down to looking carefully, counting correctly and using the right unit. All the Term 3 topics are examinable in the test.

## Subtopic: 3D Objects and Data

At the prize table, a gift box is a rectangular prism with 6 rectangle faces, 12 edges and 8 vertices. A small gift box with 6 equal square faces is a cube. Tins of sweets are cylinders, with 2 circle faces and 1 curved surface. The shade tent is a square-based pyramid with 5 faces: 1 square and 4 triangles. Cutting open a gift box and pressing it flat gives its net: 6 rectangles in 3 matching pairs.

The class asks 40 visitors which stall they liked best and tallies the answers in bundles of five: food 15, games 11, crafts 8 and books 6. The total, 40, matches the number asked. Ordered from smallest to largest: books 6, crafts 8, games 11, food 15.

In a pictograph with the key one star stands for 2 visitors, food needs 15 divided by 2, which is 7 and a half stars. In a bar graph with a scale counting in 2s from 0, the food bar goes halfway between 14 and 16.

Reading the data: food was the most popular stall, and books the least. Food had 9 more votes than books, because 15 minus 6 is 9. The modal stall is food. A sensible conclusion is that next year the school should plan for more food stalls, and a prediction is that food will probably be popular again.

Always read a display before its numbers: the title, the key or scale, and the labels. A bar graph of the votes needs a scale that starts at 0 and goes up in equal steps, with bars of equal width. A pie chart of the same votes would show food as a little more than a third of the circle, because 15 out of 40 is more than a third.

The questions for this section are with you now: naming 3D objects, tallies and tables, pictographs and bar graphs, and the mode.

## Subtopic: Probability and Length

The lucky spinner has 4 equal parts: red, blue, green and yellow, so there are 4 possible outcomes, all equally likely. In 20 spins, the results are red 6, blue 4, green 5 and yellow 5. The total is 20. The results are not exactly equal, and that is normal with only 20 trials.

The sack race track is measured with a trundle wheel: 25 clicks, so it is 25 m long. Short things, like the width of a prize ribbon, are measured with a ruler in centimetres and millimetres, starting at the 0 mark.

Conversions use three facts: 10 mm is 1 cm, 100 cm is 1 m and 1 000 m is 1 km. Multiply to change to a smaller unit and divide to change to a bigger unit. The fun walk is 1 and 1/2 km, which is 1 000 m plus 500 m, or 1 500 m. A 4 m roll of ribbon is 400 cm, so it cuts into 400 divided by 50, which is 8 rosettes of 50 cm each.

When ordering lengths, compare the biggest unit first: 1 m 98 cm is shorter than 2 m 8 cm, and 2 m 8 cm is shorter than 2 m 35 cm.

A spinner with unequal parts behaves differently. A spinner that is half red gives red more often, because the red part is bigger. Before deciding whether a difference in results is surprising, ask whether the parts of the spinner are equal.

The questions for this section are with you now: outcomes and trials, measuring tools, and converting and ordering lengths.

## Subtopic: Perimeter, Area and Volume

Perimeter: each stall table has a cloth 2 m long and 1 m wide. Its perimeter is 2 plus 1 plus 2 plus 1, which is 6 m, so 6 m of lace edging goes around it.

Area: a tablecloth design on grid paper is 6 squares long and 4 squares wide. It has 4 rows of 6 squares, which is 24 square units. A flag shape on the grid covers 3 whole squares and 2 half squares, so its area is 3 plus 1, which is 4 square units.

Area and perimeter are different. The 6 by 4 design has an area of 24 square units, but its perimeter is 6 plus 4 plus 6 plus 4, which is 20 units.

Volume: a box of sweets is packed with centimetre cubes. The bottom layer has 3 rows of 2, which is 6 cubes, and there are 2 layers, so the volume is 12 cubic centimetres.

Choose the measure that fits the question. Lace around the cloth is perimeter. Paint to cover the stall sign is area. Sweets to fill the box is volume. Naming the measure first tells you which unit to write: metres, square units or cubic units.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: calling a tin a circle; the circle is one face, and the tin is a cylinder. Two: counting half a star as a whole star in a pictograph. Three: changing 1 and 1/2 km to 150 m; it is 1 500 m. Four: giving the 6 by 4 design an area of 20, which is its perimeter; the area is 24 square units.

The questions for this section are with you now: perimeter, area by counting squares, and volume by counting cubes.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again, walking around the Port Shepstone fun day — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Prizes and Votes

Gift box: rectangular prism. Tin: cylinder. Tent: square-based pyramid.

Votes: food 15, games 11, crafts 8, books 6. Total 40. The mode is food.

Keep the prize table and the votes in mind and try the questions for this part: 3D objects and data.

## Subtopic: Spin and Measure

Spinner: 4 equal parts, 4 outcomes. 20 spins: 6, 4, 5, 5.

1 and 1/2 km is 1 500 m. 4 m of ribbon is 400 cm: 8 rosettes.

Hold the same-unit-first rule in mind and try this part's questions: probability and length.

## Subtopic: Around, Inside and Filling

Cloth 2 m by 1 m: 6 m around. Design 6 by 4: 24 squares. Box: 6 in a layer, 2 layers, 12 cubes.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Name 3D objects by their faces and surfaces. Tally data in fives, check the total, order it, draw it with a key or an even scale from 0, read it carefully and find the mode. List all possible outcomes and expect small differences in real trials. Measure length with the right tool from zero, and convert by multiplying or dividing by 10, 100 or 1 000. Perimeter goes around the edge in length units, area counts squares inside in square units, and volume counts cubes in cubic units.

Hold the right-unit rule in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: perimeter, area and volume.
