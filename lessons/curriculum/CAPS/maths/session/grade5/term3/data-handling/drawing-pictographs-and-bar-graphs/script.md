# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers drawing pictographs with many-to-one correspondence and drawing bar graphs. The anchor is a Grade 5 class in Mthatha that helps in the school library: they record how many books are borrowed each day of one week, and they also survey 36 learners about their favourite sport. The governing idea is that a graph must let a reader see the data correctly at a glance, so a pictograph needs a key that says what each symbol stands for, and a bar graph needs a title, labelled axes and an even scale that starts at 0. Drawing pictographs and bar graphs, choosing keys and scales, and checking graphs are examinable.

## Subtopic: Pictographs with a Key

The library records for the week are: Monday 20 books, Tuesday 35, Wednesday 15, Thursday 40 and Friday 25. Drawing one book symbol for every book would mean 135 little pictures, which is far too many. Instead, one symbol stands for several books. This is called many-to-one correspondence.

The class chooses a key: one book symbol stands for 10 books. Monday's 20 books need 2 symbols. Thursday's 40 need 4. Tuesday's 35 is 3 tens and a 5, so it needs 3 whole symbols and half a symbol. Wednesday's 15 needs 1 and a half symbols, and Friday's 25 needs 2 and a half.

To work out the number of symbols, divide each frequency by the value of one symbol. 40 divided by 10 is 4. If the answer is a half, draw half a symbol. Every symbol must be the same size, and they must line up neatly, so that longer rows really mean more books.

A pictograph needs a title, such as Library books borrowed in one week, and the key written clearly underneath. Without the key, a reader might think Thursday had only 4 books.

The questions for this section are with you now: using a key, working out the number of symbols, and drawing half symbols.

## Subtopic: Drawing Bar Graphs

The sports survey gives: soccer 14, netball 9, cricket 6, rugby 4 and athletics 3, which totals 36. The class draws a bar graph.

First draw two axes: a horizontal line along the bottom and a vertical line up the left side. Along the bottom, write the categories, soccer, netball, cricket, rugby and athletics, with equal spaces. Up the side, write the scale: the numbers of learners.

The scale must start at 0 and go up in equal steps. The biggest number is 14, so the class counts in 2s: 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 14. Each step is the same distance apart, measured with a ruler.

Draw a bar for each category, all the same width, with equal gaps between them. The soccer bar goes up to the line for 14. The netball bar goes halfway between 8 and 10, because 9 is halfway. Label the bottom axis Sport and the side axis Number of learners, and give the graph a title: Favourite sports of 36 Grade 5 learners.

The questions for this section are with you now: the parts of a bar graph, drawing scales, and the heights of bars.

## Subtopic: Choosing Keys and Scales

A good key or scale fits the data. If the numbers are small, a key of 1 or 2 works. If they are bigger, a key of 5, 10 or 20 keeps the graph a sensible size. The library numbers all end in 0 or 5, so a key of 10 needs only whole and half symbols. A key of 5 would also work but would need twice as many symbols.

For a bar graph, look at the largest value and choose a step that reaches it in about 5 to 10 equal steps. For the sports data, steps of 2 reach 14 in 7 steps. Steps of 1 would make the graph tall; steps of 5 would make small values, like 3 and 4, hard to read.

The scale must be even. A scale of 0, 2, 4, 8, 10 skips 6, so the bars would be the wrong heights. A scale that starts at 2 instead of 0 makes the small bars look much smaller than they really are. Honest graphs use even scales from 0.

Before you finish, check your graph against the table: count the symbols or read each bar back, and make sure every category is there.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: leaving out the key, so the reader thinks Thursday had 4 books instead of 40. Two: drawing a full symbol for 35 books with a key of 10, so Tuesday shows 40. Three: an uneven scale, 0, 2, 4, 8, 10, so the bars are the wrong heights. Four: bars of different widths, which makes some categories look bigger than they are.

The questions for this section are with you now: choosing a key or scale and checking a graph for mistakes.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the Mthatha library and the sports survey — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: One Picture, Many Books

Key: one book symbol means 10 books.

40 books: 4 symbols. 35 books: 3 and a half symbols.

Always write the key.

Keep the key in mind and try the questions for this part: pictographs.

## Subtopic: Build the Bars

Title, two axes, labels, and a scale from 0 in equal steps.

Steps of 2: 0, 2, 4, 6, up to 14. Netball 9 is halfway between 8 and 10.

Same width for every bar, same gaps.

Hold the even-scale rule in mind and try this part's questions: bar graphs.

## Subtopic: Pick a Good Step

Big numbers: bigger key. Pick steps that reach the top in 5 to 10 jumps.

Pocket summary of the lesson. In a pictograph, one symbol stands for several things, so divide each frequency by the key, draw half symbols when needed, and always show the key. A bar graph needs a title, labelled axes, a scale that starts at 0 and goes up in equal steps, and bars of equal width with equal gaps. Choose a key or scale that fits the data, and check the finished graph against the table.

Hold the check-against-the-table habit in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: choosing keys and scales.
