# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers analysing data by answering questions about its categories, sources and contexts, summarising data in words and in short written paragraphs, drawing conclusions and making predictions, and examining ungrouped numerical data to find the mode. The anchor is a Grade 5 class of 32 learners in Mahikeng that surveys how its learners spend break time, and records the shoe sizes of 20 learners in the school relay squad so that the right sports shoes can be ordered. The governing idea is that data is only useful when you interpret it carefully: say what it shows, say who it describes, and use it to make sensible decisions. Analysing data, writing summaries, making predictions and finding the mode are examinable.

## Subtopic: Asking Questions About Data

The break-time survey asked all 32 learners in the class one question: what do you do most often at break? The results are soccer 12, skipping 8, chatting 7 and reading 5. The total, 32, matches the class size.

Analysing starts with questions about the categories. Which activity is most popular? Soccer, with 12. Which is least popular? Reading, with 5. How many more learners play soccer than read? 12 minus 5 is 7. What fraction of the class skips? 8 out of 32, which is a quarter.

Then ask about the source. The data comes from one Grade 5 class, asked by their classmates. It describes those 32 learners well, but it cannot tell us about Grade 1 learners or about learners at another school.

Then ask about the context: when and why was the data collected? The survey was done in winter, when cold mornings might keep some learners inside. In summer the results could be different. Thinking about the source and the context stops us from claiming more than the data shows.

The questions for this section are with you now: answering questions about categories, sources and the context of data.

## Subtopic: Summarising and Predicting

A summary tells the main story of the data in a few sentences. Here is one for the break-time survey: We asked all 32 learners in our Grade 5 class what they do most often at break. Soccer was the most popular, chosen by 12 learners, and reading was the least popular, with 5. A quarter of the class skips. More learners are active, playing soccer or skipping, than are quiet, chatting or reading: 20 against 12.

A conclusion is a sensible decision or statement based on the data. Because soccer is the most popular, the class concludes that a second soccer ball would be used the most. Because 8 learners skip, two or three skipping ropes would also be well used.

A prediction says what is likely to happen in a similar situation. If another Grade 5 class at the same school is asked, soccer will probably be popular there too. A prediction is a sensible guess, not a certainty, so use words like probably and likely.

A good summary uses numbers from the data, compares groups, and does not claim things the data does not show.

The questions for this section are with you now: writing summaries, drawing conclusions and making predictions.

## Subtopic: The Mode

Numerical data is data made of numbers, like shoe sizes. The shoe sizes of the 20 relay runners are: 3, 4, 4, 5, 3, 4, 6, 5, 4, 3, 4, 5, 2, 4, 5, 3, 4, 6, 5, 4.

The mode is the value that appears most often. To find it, order the data or tally it. Ordered, the sizes are 2, then four 3s, eight 4s, five 5s and two 6s. Size 4 appears 8 times, more than any other size, so the mode is size 4.

The mode is useful for decisions. The shop should send more size 4 shoes than any other size. The mode answers the question what is most common, which is often exactly what you need to know.

Be careful to give the value, not how many times it appears. The mode is size 4, not 8. Sometimes two values tie for most often, and then the data has two modes. The mode can also be used with categories: the modal break-time activity is soccer.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: giving the biggest value, size 6, as the mode; the mode is the most common value, not the largest. Two: giving the frequency, 8, as the mode instead of the value, size 4. Three: claiming that soccer is the most popular break-time activity for all children in South Africa, from one class of 32. Four: predicting that the next class will certainly choose soccer; it probably will, but it is not certain.

The questions for this section are with you now: finding the mode of numerical data and using it to make decisions.
