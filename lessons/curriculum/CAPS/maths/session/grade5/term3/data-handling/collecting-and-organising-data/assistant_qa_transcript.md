### subtopic_1 — The Data Cycle and Asking Questions
**Assistant:** Why do we draw the fifth tally mark across the other four?

**Tutor:** Because bundles of five make counting fast and accurate. If you drew 17 separate strokes in a row, you would have to count every one, and it is easy to lose your place or count one twice. With bundles, 17 shows as three bundles and two single strokes. You count the bundles in fives, 5, 10, 15, and then add the singles, 16, 17. That takes a few seconds and is much harder to get wrong. Bundles also make it easy to compare categories at a glance. Walk, with three bundles, clearly beats taxi, with one bundle and four singles, before you even count. Five is a good bundle size because it matches the fingers on a hand and fits easily into counting in fives and tens. When you are recording answers quickly, as people call them out, the bundles also keep the tallies tidy, so that you can read them later. After tallying, always write the number in the frequency column as well, because a number is easier to use in calculations and graphs than a row of strokes.

### subtopic_2 — Tally Marks and Frequency Tables
**Assistant:** What makes a good survey question?

**Tutor:** A good survey question is clear, has answers that can be sorted, and asks about something that will help you. Clear means everyone understands it the same way. How do you travel to school is clearer than how do you come, which might mean what time you come. Answers that can be sorted means you have categories that cover everyone and do not overlap. The class used walk, taxi, bus, car and bicycle. If they had used car and lift from a parent, a learner driven by a parent could choose either, so the counts would not be reliable. If they had not included bicycle, the cyclists would have nowhere to go. Sometimes an other category catches unusual answers. It also helps to decide in advance how to deal with tricky answers, like the learner who walks to the taxi rank and then takes a taxi. Finally, a useful question links to a decision. The class asked about transport so that the school could plan a safe drop-off area, so the answers have a real purpose. Before collecting, test your question on two or three people to see whether the answers fit your categories.

### subtopic_3 — Ordering and Organising Data
**Assistant:** Why should I order data from smallest to largest?

**Tutor:** Because ordered data is much easier to read and to think about. In the transport survey, the table in the order the categories were first written is walk 17, taxi 9, bus 6, car 5, bicycle 3. That happens to be in order already, from largest to smallest. Reversed, from smallest to largest, it is bicycle 3, car 5, bus 6, taxi 9, walk 17. Either way, you can see straight away which way is most popular and which is least, and how the groups compare. With a list of numbers, ordering is even more helpful. The household sizes 4, 6, 3, 5, 4, 7, 4, 2, 5, 4, 6, 3 look jumbled. Ordered, they become 2, 3, 3, 4, 4, 4, 4, 5, 5, 6, 6, 7. Now you can see the smallest and largest values at the ends and the number that appears most often, 4, grouped together in the middle. Ordering is also the first step for drawing a neat graph and for finding the mode, which you will meet later in this topic. When you order, cross off each value as you use it and count both lists to check that you have not lost or added any numbers.
