### subtopic_1 — Reading Pictographs and Data in Words
**Assistant:** How do I read half a symbol in a pictograph?

**Tutor:** Find half of what the key says one whole symbol stands for. In the tomato pictograph, one basket stands for 4 kilograms, so half a basket stands for half of 4, which is 2 kilograms. In week 3 there are 4 whole baskets and half a basket. The 4 whole baskets are 4 times 4, which is 16 kilograms. Add the 2 kilograms for the half basket, and week 3 has 18 kilograms. A common slip is to count the half basket as a whole one, which gives 20 kilograms, or to ignore it, which gives 16. Both are wrong. If the key were one symbol for 10 kilograms, half a symbol would be 5 kilograms. If the key were one symbol for 2 kilograms, half a symbol would be 1 kilogram. Sometimes a pictograph uses quarter symbols too, and then a quarter of the key is used in the same way. The rule never changes: whatever fraction of a symbol you see, take that fraction of the key. Check your total against any other information you have, such as the report in words.

### subtopic_2 — Reading Bar Graphs
**Assistant:** Why is a pie chart useful if a bar graph can show the same data?

**Tutor:** Because a pie chart shows how one whole is shared out, and you can see at a glance what fraction each part is. The garden's pie chart shows that half of all the beds grow spinach. You can see the half immediately, because the spinach slice is exactly half the circle. A bar graph of the same data would show bars of 20, 10, 5 and 5 beds, and you could compare them, but you would have to work out that 20 is half of 40. So the pie chart is best when the question is about parts of a whole: what fraction of the garden is spinach? The bar graph is best when the question is about comparing amounts, especially when the categories are not parts of one whole, like volunteers on different Saturdays. A pie chart of volunteers on different days would make less sense, because the Saturdays are not parts of one thing. To read numbers from a pie chart, you need to know the total. Then find the fraction of the total for each slice: half of 40 is 20, a quarter of 40 is 10, and an eighth of 40 is 5.

### subtopic_3 — Reading Pie Charts and Reading Critically
**Assistant:** What does it mean to read a graph critically?

**Tutor:** It means you do not just accept what the picture seems to show; you check that the graph is fair and that you are reading it correctly. Start with the title: does it say what the data is about, where it comes from and when it was collected? Then look at the key or scale. A pictograph without a key cannot be read at all. A bar graph whose scale starts at 10 instead of 0 makes small differences look huge. In the neighbouring garden's graph, the bars for 12 and 18 volunteers looked like 2 and 8, so it seemed as if four times as many people came, when only 6 more did. Look at the widths of the bars and the sizes of the symbols too: they should all be equal. Then ask whether the data answers the question being asked. A graph of volunteers on five Saturdays cannot tell you how many tomatoes were picked. Finally, check the numbers against other information, like the total in a written report. Reading critically protects you from being fooled, whether a mistake was an accident or done on purpose to make something look better.
