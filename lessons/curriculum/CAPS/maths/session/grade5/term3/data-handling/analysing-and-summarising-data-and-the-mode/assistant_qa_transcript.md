### subtopic_1 — Asking Questions About Data
**Assistant:** What is the difference between the mode and the frequency?

**Tutor:** The mode is a value from the data, and the frequency is how many times a value appears. In the shoe sizes, size 4 appears 8 times. So the frequency of size 4 is 8, and because no other size appears that often, the mode is size 4. If someone says the mode is 8, they have mixed the two up, and that would mean size 8 shoes, which nobody in the squad wears. A good way to keep them apart is to ask what the answer is measured in. The mode of shoe sizes is a shoe size. The mode of the number of people in a household is a number of people. The frequency is always a count of how many times. When you write your answer, include the unit or the word that goes with it: the mode is size 4. Then add the frequency as a reason: because it appears 8 times, more than any other size. That sentence shows the reader both numbers and makes it clear which is which. It also makes your answer easy to check.

### subtopic_2 — Summarising and Predicting
**Assistant:** How can a survey of one class lead to a wrong conclusion?

**Tutor:** A survey describes the people who were asked, and it can be wrong to stretch it to people who were not asked. The break-time survey asked 32 Grade 5 learners in one class in Mahikeng. It tells us a lot about those 32 learners: soccer is their most popular activity. But Grade 1 learners might prefer playing on swings, and Grade 7 learners might prefer chatting. A school in a different town might have no soccer field at all. So claiming that soccer is the most popular break-time activity for all South African children goes far beyond the data. The context matters too. The survey was done in winter, and on a hot summer day more learners might read in the shade. A careful summary says exactly who was asked and when, such as in our Grade 5 class in winter. Conclusions and predictions should stay close to that group. If you want to know about the whole school, you must ask learners from every grade. Thinking about sources and contexts in this way is what makes data handling honest.

### subtopic_3 — The Mode
**Assistant:** Why should a prediction use words like probably?

**Tutor:** Because data shows what is likely, not what is certain. The break-time survey showed that soccer was the most popular activity in one class. If you ask a similar class at the same school, soccer will probably be popular there too, because the learners are the same age, share the same field and have the same break times. But you cannot be sure. That class might have a skipping champion who has got many friends to skip, or it might be raining that week. Saying soccer will probably be the most popular is honest. Saying soccer will definitely be the most popular is claiming more than you know. Weather forecasters do the same thing. They study a lot of data and say there is a good chance of rain, not it will rain. When you make a prediction, you can also say why you think so: soccer will probably be popular in the other Grade 5 class, because it was chosen by 12 of the 32 learners in ours, more than any other activity. Giving a reason makes your prediction stronger and shows that it comes from the data.
