# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers collecting data using tally marks and tables, and ordering data from the smallest group to the largest group, as the first steps of the data cycle. The anchor is a Grade 5 class in George that asks 40 learners how they travel to school, so that the school can plan a safe drop-off area. The governing idea is that data answers a question only when it is collected carefully and organised clearly: every answer counted once, grouped into tallies of five, totalled, and then ordered. Posing questions, tallying, completing frequency tables and ordering data are examinable.

## Subtopic: The Data Cycle and Asking Questions

Data is information collected to answer a question. The data cycle is the path from question to answer: pose a question, collect the data, organise it, represent it in a graph, analyse it, and report what you found. The report often raises a new question, and the cycle starts again.

The class begins with a question: how do Grade 5 learners at our school travel to school? A good question is clear and has answers that fit into groups, called categories. The class chooses five categories: walk, taxi, bus, car and bicycle. Every learner must fit into exactly one category, so they agree that a learner who uses two kinds of transport gives the one used for the longest part of the trip.

The class also decides on the source of the data, meaning who or where it comes from. Here the source is the learners themselves, asked in person. Other sources include records, such as the school register, or measurements, such as the temperature every morning.

The class decides to ask all 40 Grade 5 learners, so the answers describe the whole grade. Asking only the learners in the front row would not give a fair picture.

Before the real survey, the class tries the question on five learners from another class. One says she comes by bus and then taxi, which shows why the class needed the longest-part rule. Testing a question like this is called a trial run, and it catches problems before all 40 answers are collected.

The questions for this section are with you now: the steps of the data cycle, good questions and categories, and sources of data.

## Subtopic: Tally Marks and Frequency Tables

As each learner answers, a recorder makes a tally mark next to the category. Tally marks are short strokes. Every fifth stroke is drawn across the four before it, making a bundle of five. Bundles make counting quick: two bundles and three single strokes is 5 plus 5 plus 3, which is 13.

The completed tallies are counted and written in a frequency table. Frequency means how many times something happens. The table for the class has three columns: category, tally and frequency. The results are walk 17, taxi 9, bus 6, car 5 and bicycle 3.

Always check the total. 17 plus 9 plus 6 plus 5 plus 3 is 40, which matches the 40 learners asked. If the total is too big, someone was counted twice; if it is too small, someone was missed.

Tallying is fast and neat because you do not have to cross out and rewrite a number each time a new answer comes in. You just add one stroke. Walk with 17 learners shows as three bundles of five and two single strokes.

The class checks the table by reading the tallies again. Taxi shows one bundle and four singles, which is 9. Bus shows one bundle and one single, which is 6. Car is one bundle, 5, and bicycle is three singles, 3. Every tally matches its frequency.

The questions for this section are with you now: reading and making tally marks, completing frequency tables and checking totals.

## Subtopic: Ordering and Organising Data

Next the class orders the categories from the smallest group to the largest group: bicycle 3, car 5, bus 6, taxi 9, walk 17. Ordering makes it easy to see the most popular way, walking, and the least popular way, cycling.

Data can also be numbers rather than categories. The class asks 12 learners how many people live in their homes and gets 4, 6, 3, 5, 4, 7, 4, 2, 5, 4, 6, 3. Ordered from smallest to largest, the list is 2, 3, 3, 4, 4, 4, 4, 5, 5, 6, 6, 7. Ordering shows at once that the smallest household has 2 people, the largest has 7, and 4 people appears most often.

When ordering a list, cross off each number as you write it, and count at the end to make sure the new list has as many numbers as the old one: 12 in, 12 out.

An organised table is ready for the next steps of the cycle: drawing a graph and answering questions about the data.

Ordering also makes comparisons easy. Are there more walkers than learners who come by taxi, bus and car together? Those three groups make 9 plus 6 plus 5, which is 20, so there are 3 fewer walkers than learners in those three groups. Comparisons like this help the school plan the drop-off area.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: drawing five or more single strokes without bundling them, so counts are slow and easy to get wrong. Two: not checking the total, so a learner counted twice makes 41 answers from 40 learners. Three: ordering categories alphabetically, bicycle, bus, car, taxi, walk, instead of by size. Four: using categories that overlap, such as car and lift from a parent, so one learner could fit both.

The questions for this section are with you now: ordering categories and number data from smallest to largest.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Question, collect, organise, draw, analyse, report: the data cycle.

Five clear groups: walk, taxi, bus, car, bicycle. Everyone fits in one group.

One stroke per answer. The fifth stroke goes across the four.

Walk 17, taxi 9, bus 6, car 5, bicycle 3. Total 40.

Hold the check-the-total habit in mind and try this part's questions: tallies and tables.

## Subtopic: Put Them in Order

Smallest to largest: bicycle 3, car 5, bus 6, taxi 9, walk 17.

Pocket summary of the lesson. The data cycle starts with a clear question, collects data from a good source, organises it, represents it, analyses it and reports. Choose categories so that every answer fits exactly one group. Tally with bundles of five, write the frequencies in a table, and check that the total matches the number of answers. Order groups and number lists from smallest to largest, and count to make sure nothing is lost.

Hold the smallest-to-largest order in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: ordering data.
