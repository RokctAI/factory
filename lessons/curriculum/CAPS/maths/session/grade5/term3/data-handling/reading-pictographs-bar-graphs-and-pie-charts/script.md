# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers critically reading and interpreting data presented in words, pictographs, bar graphs and pie charts. The anchor is a community vegetable garden in Polokwane that shares a report with the local school: a paragraph about the garden, a pictograph of tomatoes harvested, a bar graph of volunteers and a pie chart of the garden beds. The governing idea is that before you read any number from a display, you read the title, the key or the scale, and the labels, and then you ask whether the display tells the story fairly. Reading values, comparing them, calculating totals and differences, and reading displays critically are examinable.

## Subtopic: Reading Pictographs and Data in Words

The report begins in words: the garden has 40 beds and grows four vegetables, and in four weeks it harvested 58 kilograms of tomatoes. Data in words must be read slowly, picking out each number and what it describes.

The pictograph shows tomatoes harvested each week, with the key: one basket symbol stands for 4 kilograms. Week 1 shows 3 baskets, so 3 times 4, which is 12 kilograms. Week 2 shows 5 baskets, which is 20 kilograms. Week 3 shows 4 and a half baskets: 4 baskets are 16 kilograms and half a basket is 2 kilograms, so 18 kilograms. Week 4 shows 2 baskets, 8 kilograms.

To answer questions, turn symbols into numbers first. Which week was best? Week 2, with 20 kilograms. How many more kilograms in week 2 than week 4? 20 minus 8 is 12. Altogether? 12 plus 20 plus 18 plus 8 is 58 kilograms, which matches the report in words.

Checking a display against the words is part of reading critically. If the numbers had not matched, either the pictograph or the paragraph would contain a mistake.

The questions for this section are with you now: reading data in words and turning pictograph symbols into numbers.

## Subtopic: Reading Bar Graphs

The bar graph shows the number of volunteers on five Saturdays. Its scale counts in 3s: 0, 3, 6, 9 and so on up to 21. The bars show 12, 18, 9, 15 and 21 volunteers.

To read a bar, follow the top of the bar straight across to the scale. If the top is between two marks, find the halfway point: halfway between 12 and 15 is 13 and a half, but people come in whole numbers, so check whether the top is really on a mark.

Questions use the values in different ways. The most volunteers came on Saturday 5, with 21. The fewest came on Saturday 3, with 9. The difference is 21 minus 9, which is 12. The total over five Saturdays is 12 plus 18 plus 9 plus 15 plus 21, which is 75 volunteers.

Bar graphs are good for comparing at a glance. Without reading any numbers, you can see that Saturday 5 had more than twice as many volunteers as Saturday 3, because its bar is more than twice as tall.

The questions for this section are with you now: reading values from bar graphs, finding the most and fewest, and calculating differences and totals.

## Subtopic: Reading Pie Charts and Reading Critically

A pie chart is a circle divided into slices, where each slice shows a part of the whole. The garden's pie chart shows its 40 beds. Spinach takes half the circle, tomatoes take a quarter, and onions and carrots take an eighth each.

To find how many beds each slice stands for, find that fraction of the whole. Half of 40 is 20 spinach beds. A quarter of 40 is 10 tomato beds. An eighth of 40 is 5 beds, so there are 5 onion beds and 5 carrot beds. Check: 20 plus 10 plus 5 plus 5 is 40. A pie chart is best for showing how a whole is shared out.

Reading critically means asking whether a display is fair. A neighbouring garden shows its volunteers in a bar graph whose scale starts at 10, not 0. Its bars for 12 and 18 look like 2 and 8, so 18 seems four times as many as 12 when it is really only 6 more. Always check where the scale starts, what the key says, and whether the title matches the data.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: counting baskets instead of kilograms, saying week 2 had 5 kilograms of tomatoes instead of 20. Two: counting half a basket as a whole, so week 3 becomes 20 kilograms instead of 18. Three: reading the top of the bar for 9 as the next mark up, 12, instead of 9. Four: saying the onion slice is 8 beds because it is an eighth; an eighth of 40 is 5.

The questions for this section are with you now: reading pie charts, and checking displays for unfair scales and missing keys.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the community garden in Polokwane — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Baskets to Kilograms

Key: one basket means 4 kilograms.

Week 2: 5 baskets, 20 kilograms. Week 3: 4 and a half baskets, 18 kilograms.

Keep the key in mind and try the questions for this part: pictographs and words.

## Subtopic: Read the Bars

Scale in 3s. Saturdays: 12, 18, 9, 15, 21 volunteers.

Most: 21. Fewest: 9. Difference: 12. Total: 75.

Hold the read-across-to-the-scale habit in mind and try this part's questions: bar graphs.

## Subtopic: Slices of the Garden

Half of 40 is 20 spinach beds. A quarter is 10. An eighth is 5.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Read the title, the key or scale, and the labels before reading any numbers. In a pictograph, multiply the number of symbols by the key, and count half symbols as half the key. In a bar graph, read across from the top of each bar to the scale. In a pie chart, each slice is a fraction of the whole, so find that fraction of the total. Then answer most, fewest, how many more and altogether questions, and check whether the display is fair, especially where the scale starts.

Hold the read-the-key-first habit in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: pie charts and reading critically.
