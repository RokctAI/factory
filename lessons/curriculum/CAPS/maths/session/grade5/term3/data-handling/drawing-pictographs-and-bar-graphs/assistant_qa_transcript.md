### subtopic_1 — Pictographs with a Key
**Assistant:** Why does a pictograph need a key?

**Tutor:** Because without a key, the reader does not know how many things each symbol stands for. In the library pictograph, each book symbol stands for 10 books. Thursday shows 4 symbols, which means 40 books. If the key were missing, a reader might count the symbols and think only 4 books were borrowed, which is wrong by a lot. Another reader might guess that each symbol stands for 5 books and say 20. The key removes the guessing. It also makes half symbols meaningful. Half a book symbol stands for half of 10, which is 5 books, so 3 and a half symbols on Tuesday means 35 books. Keys are what make pictographs useful for large numbers. Drawing 135 single book pictures would take forever and be impossible to read, but with a key of 10, the whole week fits into 13 and a half symbols. When you read any pictograph, find the key first, before you look at anything else. When you draw one, write the key before you draw the first symbol, so you never forget it.

### subtopic_2 — Drawing Bar Graphs
**Assistant:** How do I choose the scale for a bar graph?

**Tutor:** Look at the biggest number and choose a step that gets there in about 5 to 10 equal jumps, starting from 0. For the sports survey, the biggest number is 14. Counting in 2s gives 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 14, which is 7 jumps, so the graph is neither too tall nor too squashed. If the biggest number were 45, counting in 5s would work well: 0, 5, 10 and so on up to 45. If it were 300, counting in 50s would work. Once you have chosen a step, every jump must be the same, and every step must be the same distance apart on the paper. Use a ruler and mark them carefully. Values that fall between the marks, like 9 when you count in 2s, go halfway between 8 and 10. A scale that skips a number, or starts above 0, gives bars of the wrong heights, and the graph tells a false story. Finally, label the scale axis with what it counts, such as Number of learners, so the reader knows what the numbers mean.

### subtopic_3 — Choosing Keys and Scales
**Assistant:** Why must all the bars be the same width?

**Tutor:** Because the reader should compare only the heights of the bars, and different widths would distract them and mislead them. In a bar graph, the height of each bar shows the number. If the soccer bar is twice as wide as the netball bar, it looks much bigger than it really is, even if you only meant to make it stand out. The eye notices the area of a bar, not just its height. Equal widths and equal gaps make the graph fair, so that the only difference between bars is the thing being measured. The same idea applies to pictographs: every symbol must be the same size and evenly spaced, or a row with big symbols will look longer than a row with more, smaller symbols. A good test is to show your graph to a friend and ask which category is biggest and by how much. If they answer correctly without looking at the table, your graph is doing its job. If they are fooled, look for uneven widths, uneven gaps or an uneven scale.
