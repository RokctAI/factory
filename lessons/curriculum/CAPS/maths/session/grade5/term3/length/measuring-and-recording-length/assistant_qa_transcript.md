### subtopic_1 — Units and Tools
**Assistant:** Why must I start at the 0 mark and not at the end of the ruler?

**Tutor:** Because on many rulers the 0 mark is not at the very end. There is often a small strip of plastic or wood before the 0, to protect the marks from wearing away. If you put the end of the ruler at the start of the object, you are really starting a few millimetres before 0, and every measurement you take will be wrong by that amount. For example, if the gap is 3 mm, a pencil that is really 124 mm long will seem to end at the 127 mm mark. To avoid this, look for the 0 mark and line it up exactly with one end of the object. Then read the mark at the other end. If the 0 mark is worn away or the ruler is broken, you can start at another mark, such as 1 cm, and subtract it at the end: a pencil from the 1 cm mark to the 13 cm 4 mm mark is 12 cm 4 mm long. Tape measures have a metal hook at the end, and the hook is made so that its edge is at 0. Trundle wheels have an arrow that must point straight down at the start line.

### subtopic_2 — Estimating and Measuring
**Assistant:** How do I know which unit to use?

**Tutor:** Choose the unit that gives a sensible number, not too big and not too small. The thickness of a coin is about 2 mm. In metres it would be a tiny fraction, which is hard to picture, so millimetres suit it. A pencil is about 17 cm. In millimetres that is 170 mm, which is fine for exact measuring, but centimetres are easier to picture. A classroom is about 8 m long. In centimetres that is 800 cm, which is a big number, so metres suit it better. The distance to the next town might be 25 km. In metres that would be 25 000 m, which is much harder to picture. A good habit is to think of benchmarks: a millimetre is the thickness of a paper clip wire, a centimetre is the width of a small fingernail, a metre is about a big stride, and a kilometre is about 10 to 15 minutes of walking. Compare the object with these and choose the closest. Sometimes you need two units together, like 2 m 35 cm for a long jump, because one unit alone is not exact enough.

### subtopic_3 — Recording, Comparing and Ordering
**Assistant:** Why is 2 m 8 cm shorter than 2 m 35 cm when 8 is bigger than 3?

**Tutor:** Because the 8 and the 35 are both centimetres, and you must compare the whole number of centimetres, 8 against 35, not single digits. Both jumps have 2 whole metres, so the metres are equal. Then look at the centimetres. 2 m 8 cm has only 8 extra centimetres. 2 m 35 cm has 35 extra centimetres. 35 is more than 8, so 2 m 35 cm is longer. The mistake happens when people compare the 8 with the 3 in 35, as if they were in the same place. One way to avoid it is to write the centimetres with two digits: 2 m 08 cm and 2 m 35 cm. Now it is clear that 08 is less than 35. Another way is to change both to centimetres: 2 m is 200 cm, so 2 m 8 cm is 208 cm and 2 m 35 cm is 235 cm. Now you are comparing two whole numbers, and 235 is clearly bigger. In the long jump, the order from shortest to longest is 198 cm, 208 cm, 235 cm and 250 cm. Writing all the lengths in the same unit is the safest way to order them.
