# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers estimating and practically measuring 2D shapes and 3D objects using rulers, metre sticks, tape measures and trundle wheels, and recording, comparing and ordering lengths in millimetres, centimetres, metres and kilometres. The anchor is a Grade 5 class in Ladysmith getting ready for a sports day: they measure pencils and books in class, the long jump pit, the running track and the distance from the school to the sports ground. The governing idea is that every measurement needs a sensible unit, the right tool, an estimate first, and a careful reading that starts from zero. Choosing units and tools, estimating, measuring, recording and ordering lengths are examinable.

## Subtopic: Units and Tools

Length is measured in four units. A millimetre, mm, is tiny: about the thickness of the wire in a paper clip. A centimetre, cm, is about the width of your smallest fingernail. A metre, m, is about one big stride of an adult, or the height of a kitchen counter. A kilometre, km, is a long distance: about 10 to 15 minutes of walking.

Choose the unit to suit the length. The thickness of a coin is measured in millimetres. A pencil is measured in centimetres. A classroom is measured in metres. The distance between two towns is measured in kilometres.

Choose the tool to suit the length too. A ruler, usually 30 cm long and marked in centimetres and millimetres, is good for short things like books and pencils. A metre stick is good for desks and doors. A tape measure bends, so it measures curved things like a waist or a tree trunk, and it is long enough for a room. A trundle wheel is a wheel on a handle that clicks once every metre as you push it, so it is good for long distances like the length of a field.

The class uses a ruler for the pencil, a metre stick for the classroom door, a tape measure for the long jump, and a trundle wheel for the running track.

The questions for this section are with you now: choosing sensible units and the right measuring tools.

## Subtopic: Estimating and Measuring

Before measuring, estimate. Compare the object with something you know. A school desk looks a little longer than a metre stick, so a good estimate is about 120 cm. Estimating first helps you spot a measurement that is badly wrong.

To measure with a ruler, line up the 0 mark, not the edge of the ruler, with one end of the object. Many rulers have a small gap before the 0. Then read the mark at the other end. A pencil that ends at the fourth small mark after 12 is 12 cm and 4 mm long, which is 124 mm, because each centimetre has 10 millimetres.

The class measures 2D shapes, like the cover of an exercise book, and 3D objects, like a shoebox. For the book cover, they measure the length and the width: 297 mm by 210 mm. For the shoebox, they measure its length, width and height, each along an edge.

With a trundle wheel, set the arrow at the start, push the wheel in a straight line, and count the clicks. The running track straight measures 100 clicks, so it is 100 m long.

The questions for this section are with you now: estimating lengths and reading rulers, tape measures and trundle wheels.

## Subtopic: Recording, Comparing and Ordering

Always record a length with its unit. Writing only 124 could mean 124 mm, 124 cm or 124 m, which are very different lengths.

At the long jump, four learners jump: Sipho 2 m 35 cm, Amahle 2 m 8 cm, Kayla 1 m 98 cm and Thabo 2 m 50 cm. To compare, look at the metres first. Kayla has only 1 m, so her jump is the shortest. The other three all have 2 m, so compare their centimetres: 8, 35 and 50. Ordered from shortest to longest: Kayla 1 m 98 cm, Amahle 2 m 8 cm, Sipho 2 m 35 cm, Thabo 2 m 50 cm.

Be careful with 2 m 8 cm. The 8 is only 8 centimetres, so 2 m 8 cm is shorter than 2 m 35 cm. Writing every jump in centimetres makes this clear: 198 cm, 208 cm, 235 cm, 250 cm.

The distance from school to the sports ground is 2 km, and the running track around the field is 400 m. When you compare lengths in different units, change them to the same unit first, which the next lesson covers in detail.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: measuring from the edge of the ruler instead of the 0 mark, adding a few millimetres. Two: thinking 2 m 8 cm is longer than 2 m 35 cm because 8 looks bigger than 3; compare 8 cm with 35 cm. Three: recording 124 without a unit. Four: choosing a 30 cm ruler to measure the school field.

The questions for this section are with you now: recording lengths with units, comparing them and putting them in order.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Use millimetres for tiny lengths, centimetres for small objects, metres for rooms and fields, and kilometres for long distances.
