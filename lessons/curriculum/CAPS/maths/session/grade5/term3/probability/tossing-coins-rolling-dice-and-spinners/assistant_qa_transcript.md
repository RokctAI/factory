### subtopic_1 — Possible Outcomes
**Assistant:** If heads and tails are equally likely, why did I not get 10 of each?

**Tutor:** Because equally likely does not mean the results will come out exactly even. Each toss is separate, and each one has the same chance of heads or tails, so the pattern of results is a bit different every time. In 20 tosses you might get 11 heads and 9 tails, or 8 heads and 12 tails, or even 13 and 7. Getting exactly 10 and 10 happens only now and then. Think of it like this: if you and 9 friends each tossed a coin 20 times, you would get 10 different sets of results, most of them close to 10 and 10 but not exactly. That is normal. What equally likely does tell you is that, over a very large number of tosses, heads and tails will come out close to half each. With 1 000 tosses, the results would usually be much closer to half and half than with 20. So when you compare your actual results, do not expect perfection. Say that heads and tails came up about the same number of times, and that the small difference is what you would expect from chance.

### subtopic_2 — Doing Trials and Counting Results
**Assistant:** After three heads in a row, is tails more likely next time?

**Tutor:** No. The coin has no memory. It does not know what happened on the last toss, so each toss is a fresh start with the same chance of heads or tails. Many people feel that tails is due after a run of heads, and this feeling is very common, even among adults. But the coin is just a piece of metal spinning in the air. Whether it lands on heads or tails depends on how it is tossed and how it bounces, not on earlier tosses. Runs of the same result are actually quite normal. If you toss a coin 20 times, you will often see 3 or 4 heads or tails in a row somewhere in the list. The same idea applies to dice. If you have not rolled a 6 for a long time, the next roll is still exactly as likely to be a 6 as it always was. Knowing this helps you think clearly in games and avoid wrong decisions, such as believing that you are owed a lucky result.

### subtopic_3 — Comparing Actual Results
**Assistant:** How can I tell whether a spinner is fair?

**Tutor:** Look at the spinner first, and then look at the results. A spinner is fair if all its parts are the same size, because then the arrow is equally likely to stop in each part. A spinner with 4 equal parts, red, blue, green and yellow, is fair. A spinner that is half red, a quarter blue and a quarter green is not fair between the colours, because the red part is twice as big as each of the others, so red is more likely. Then do trials and compare the frequencies. With the unequal spinner, the class got red 11 times, blue 5 and green 4 in 20 spins. Red came up about twice as often as each other colour, which matches the sizes of the parts. If a spinner looked fair but one colour came up far more often in many trials, you might check whether it is bent or whether the arrow sticks. But with only 20 trials, small differences are normal, so do not decide a spinner is unfair because one colour came up a little more often. Looking at the sizes of the parts gives the clearest answer.
