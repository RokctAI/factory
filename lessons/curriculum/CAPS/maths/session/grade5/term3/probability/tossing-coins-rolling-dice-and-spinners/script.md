# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers performing simple repeated events, such as tossing a coin, rolling a die and spinning a spinner, listing the possible outcomes, and counting and comparing the frequency of the actual outcomes for up to 20 trials. The anchor is a Grade 5 class in Bethlehem in the Free State holding a maths games afternoon, with stations for tossing a R5 coin, rolling a die and spinning cardboard spinners. The governing idea is that the possible outcomes tell you what could happen, while the trials tell you what actually happened, and that real results vary even when every outcome has the same chance. Listing outcomes, recording trials with tallies, and comparing frequencies are examinable.

## Subtopic: Possible Outcomes

An outcome is one result of an experiment. When you toss a coin, there are 2 possible outcomes: heads or tails. When you roll an ordinary die, there are 6 possible outcomes: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 or 6. When you spin a spinner with 4 equal parts coloured red, blue, green and yellow, there are 4 possible outcomes.

Listing all the possible outcomes is the first step. Make sure none is left out and none is listed twice. For the die, there is no 0 and no 7.

For a fair coin, a fair die and a spinner with equal parts, each outcome has the same chance. Heads and tails are equally likely. Each number on the die is equally likely. A 6 is no harder to roll than a 1, even though it might feel that way in a board game when you are waiting for one.

Some spinners have unequal parts. The class makes a spinner that is half red, a quarter blue and a quarter green. It still has 3 possible outcomes, but red is more likely than blue or green, because the red part is bigger.

The questions for this section are with you now: listing the possible outcomes for coins, dice and spinners, and deciding whether outcomes are equally likely.

## Subtopic: Doing Trials and Counting Results

A trial is one go at the experiment: one toss, one roll or one spin. At the coin station, Lindiwe tosses the coin 20 times and her partner tallies each result. The tallies show heads 11 times and tails 9 times. The frequencies add up to 20, the number of trials.

At the die station, Pieter rolls the die 20 times. His results are: 1 came up 3 times, 2 came up 4 times, 3 came up 2 times, 4 came up 5 times, 5 came up 3 times and 6 came up 3 times. Check the total: 3 plus 4 plus 2 plus 5 plus 3 plus 3 is 20.

Recording carefully matters. Do one trial, record it straight away with a tally mark, and only then do the next trial. Keep a count of trials so you stop at exactly 20.

A frequency table for the die has a row or column for every possible outcome, even if one outcome never comes up. Then a 0 is written in its frequency space, so the reader knows it was not forgotten.

The questions for this section are with you now: recording trials with tallies, completing frequency tables and checking that the total equals the number of trials.

## Subtopic: Comparing Actual Results

Now the class compares the results. Heads came up 11 times and tails 9 times. That is not exactly 10 and 10, even though heads and tails are equally likely. With only 20 trials, results are almost never perfectly even. A difference of 2 is normal and does not mean the coin is unfair.

For the die, 4 came up most often, 5 times, and 3 came up least often, 2 times. That does not mean 4 is a lucky number. If Pieter rolled another 20 times, a different number might come up most.

The unequal spinner, half red and a quarter each blue and green, was spun 20 times: red 11, blue 5 and green 4. Red came up most often, which matches the bigger red part. Here the difference has a reason.

To compare, use words like more often, less often and about the same. Compare the results with what you expected: equal parts should give roughly equal frequencies, and a bigger part should usually give a bigger frequency.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking that after 3 heads in a row, tails is due; the coin does not remember, so each toss is still equally likely. Two: thinking 6 is harder to roll than other numbers; every number has the same chance. Three: listing 0 to 6 as the outcomes of a die; there are 6 outcomes, 1 to 6. Four: deciding the coin is unfair because heads came up 11 times out of 20; small differences are normal.

The questions for this section are with you now: comparing frequencies, and deciding whether a difference is normal or has a reason.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the games afternoon in Bethlehem — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: What Could Happen?

Coin: heads or tails, 2 outcomes.

Die: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 6 outcomes.

Equal spinner with 4 colours: 4 outcomes.

Keep the full list in mind and try the questions for this part: possible outcomes.

## Subtopic: Try It 20 Times

One toss, one tally. Heads 11, tails 9. Total 20.

Die: 3, 4, 2, 5, 3, 3. Total 20.

Hold the one-trial-one-tally habit in mind and try this part's questions: recording trials.

## Subtopic: What Did Happen?

Heads 11, tails 9: close to even, and that is normal.

Pocket summary of the lesson. List every possible outcome first: 2 for a coin, 6 for a die, and one for each part of a spinner. Outcomes on a fair coin, a fair die or an equal spinner are equally likely. Do one trial at a time, tally it, and check that the frequencies add up to the number of trials. Real results are seldom perfectly even, so small differences are normal. A bigger part on a spinner should come up more often.

Hold the small-differences-are-normal idea in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: comparing results.
