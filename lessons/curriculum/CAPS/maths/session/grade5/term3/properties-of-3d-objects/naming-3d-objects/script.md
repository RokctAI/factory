# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers recognising, visualising and naming 3D objects: rectangular and other prisms, cubes, cylinders, cones and pyramids, and the similarities and differences between cubes and rectangular prisms. The anchor is a spaza shop in Pietermaritzburg, where a Grade 5 learner, Zanele, packs the shelves on Saturday mornings with cereal boxes, sugar cubes, tins of beans, ice-cream cones and a box of tea bags shaped like little pyramids. The governing idea is that a 3D object is named by looking at its faces: what shape they are, how many there are, and whether any surfaces are curved. Naming 3D objects, comparing cubes with rectangular prisms, and telling prisms from pyramids are examinable.

## Subtopic: Prisms and Cubes

A 3D object is solid: it has length, width and height, so you can hold it and it takes up space. Its flat surfaces are called faces, the lines where two faces meet are edges, and the corners where edges meet are vertices.

A prism has two end faces that are the same shape and size, facing each other, joined by rectangles. A prism is named by the shape of its end faces. A cereal box is a rectangular prism, because its ends are rectangles. A tent or a long chocolate box with triangle ends is a triangular prism. An unsharpened pencil, with hexagon ends, is a hexagonal prism.

If you cut a prism anywhere straight across, parallel to its ends, the cut face is the same shape and size as the ends. That is why a loaf of bread cut into slices gives slices of the same shape.

A cube is a special rectangular prism. All six of its faces are equal squares. A sugar cube and a die are cubes. A box of matches is a rectangular prism, but it is not a cube, because its faces are not all squares.

The questions for this section are with you now: naming prisms by their end faces and recognising cubes.

## Subtopic: Cubes and Rectangular Prisms Compared

Zanele puts a sugar cube next to a cereal box and compares them. They are alike in many ways. Each has 6 flat faces, 12 straight edges and 8 vertices. Every face is a rectangle, because a square is a special rectangle. Opposite faces are the same shape and size, and both objects stack neatly on a shelf without gaps.

They are also different. All 6 faces of a cube are equal squares, and all 12 edges are the same length. A rectangular prism that is not a cube has faces of different sizes: the cereal box has a big front and back, narrower sides, and a small top and bottom. Its edges come in three different lengths.

So every cube is a rectangular prism, but not every rectangular prism is a cube. In the same way, every square is a rectangle, but not every rectangle is a square.

Visualising helps. Picture a cube and stretch it upwards: it becomes a tall rectangular prism, like a box of long-life milk. Squash a rectangular prism until all its edges are equal, and it becomes a cube.

The questions for this section are with you now: similarities and differences between cubes and rectangular prisms.

## Subtopic: Cylinders, Cones and Pyramids

Some objects have curved surfaces. A tin of beans is a cylinder: it has 2 flat faces that are circles, one at each end, and 1 curved surface around the middle. It rolls on its side but stands still on its end. An ice-cream cone, turned the other way up, is a cone: it has 1 flat face that is a circle, 1 curved surface and 1 point at the top, called the apex.

A pyramid has one base and triangular faces that meet at the apex. A pyramid is named by the shape of its base. A square-based pyramid has a square base and 4 triangles: 5 faces, 8 edges and 5 vertices. A triangular-based pyramid has a triangle base and 3 more triangles: 4 faces, 6 edges and 4 vertices. The pyramid tea bags are triangular-based pyramids.

Prisms and pyramids are often mixed up. A prism has two matching ends joined by rectangles. A pyramid has one base and triangles that come to a point. A triangular prism, like a tent, has 5 faces; a triangular-based pyramid has 4.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: calling a die a square; a square is flat, so the die is a cube. Two: calling a tin a circle; the circle is only one face, and the whole tin is a cylinder. Three: calling a tent a pyramid; it has two triangle ends joined by rectangles, so it is a triangular prism. Four: saying a cube is not a rectangular prism; it is a special one with all faces square.

The questions for this section are with you now: cylinders, cones and pyramids, and telling prisms from pyramids.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again on the shelves of Zanele's spaza shop — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Boxes and Cubes

Two matching ends joined by rectangles: a prism. Name it by its ends.

Cereal box: rectangular prism. Tent: triangular prism. Pencil: hexagonal prism.

Sugar cube: a cube, all faces equal squares.

Keep the matching-ends idea in mind and try the questions for this part: prisms and cubes.

## Subtopic: Cube or Box?

Both have 6 faces, 12 edges and 8 vertices.

A cube has all faces equal squares. A cereal box has faces of different sizes.

Every cube is a rectangular prism.

Hold the alike-and-different list in mind and try this part's questions: cubes and rectangular prisms.

## Subtopic: Round and Pointed

Tin: cylinder. Ice-cream cone: cone. Tea bag: pyramid.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A 3D object is solid, with faces, edges and vertices. A prism has two matching ends joined by rectangles and is named by its ends. A cube is a rectangular prism with all faces equal squares. A cylinder has two circle faces and a curved surface. A cone has one circle face, a curved surface and an apex. A pyramid has one base and triangles meeting at the apex, and is named by its base.

Hold the look-at-the-faces habit in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: cylinders, cones and pyramids.
