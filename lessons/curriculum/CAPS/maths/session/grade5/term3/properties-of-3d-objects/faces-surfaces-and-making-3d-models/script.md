# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers describing, sorting and comparing 3D objects in terms of the shape of their faces, the number of faces, and flat and curved surfaces, making 3D models from cut-out polygons, and cutting open boxes to trace and describe their nets. The anchor is a Grade 5 class in Rustenburg running a recycling project: they collect old boxes, tins and balls, sort them, build new models from card shapes and cut the boxes open flat. The governing idea is that every 3D object can be described by its faces and surfaces, and that the flat net of an object shows all its faces at once. Describing and sorting objects, comparing them, making models and describing nets are examinable.

## Subtopic: Faces and Surfaces

The class empties the recycling bags onto a table: cereal boxes, a toothpaste box with triangle ends, tins, a funnel shaped like a cone, a card pyramid with a square base, a tea bag shaped like a triangular-based pyramid, a tennis ball and a die.

Every object has surfaces. A flat surface is called a face. A cereal box has 6 flat faces, all rectangles. The toothpaste box is a triangular prism with 5 flat faces: 2 triangles and 3 rectangles. A tin has 2 flat faces, both circles, and 1 curved surface. A cone has 1 flat face, a circle, and 1 curved surface. A ball is a sphere: it has 1 curved surface and no flat faces at all.

The surfaces tell you how an object moves. Objects with only flat faces slide and stack. Objects with a curved surface can roll. A tin rolls on its curved surface but stands still on a flat face. A ball rolls in every direction.

To describe an object fully, say how many flat faces it has, what shapes they are, and whether it has any curved surfaces. For example: a square-based pyramid has 5 flat faces, 1 square and 4 triangles, and no curved surfaces.

The questions for this section are with you now: counting faces, naming face shapes and spotting curved surfaces.

## Subtopic: Sorting and Comparing 3D Objects

The class sorts the objects into three groups by their surfaces. Only flat faces: the boxes, the die and the two pyramids. Only curved: the ball. Both flat and curved: the tins and the cone.

Then they sort the flat-faced objects by the number of faces. 4 faces: a triangular-based pyramid. 5 faces: the toothpaste box and the square-based pyramid. 6 faces: the cereal box and the die.

Comparing means saying what is the same and what is different. The toothpaste box and the square-based pyramid both have 5 flat faces. But the toothpaste box has 2 triangles and 3 rectangles, while the pyramid has 1 square and 4 triangles. The toothpaste box has two matching ends; the pyramid comes to a point. So the same number of faces does not mean the same object.

A cylinder and a cone both have a circle face and a curved surface. But the cylinder has 2 circle faces and the cone has only 1, and the cone has an apex.

The questions for this section are with you now: sorting objects by surfaces and by number of faces, and comparing two objects.

## Subtopic: Models and Nets

The class makes models from cut-out polygons. To build a cube, they cut out 6 equal squares and tape the edges together. A square-based pyramid needs 1 square and 4 matching triangles. A triangular prism needs 2 matching triangles and 3 rectangles. Counting and naming the polygons first tells you exactly what to cut.

Next they cut open an old cereal box along some of its edges and press it flat. The flat shape is called a net: a flat pattern that folds up into the 3D object. Tracing around it on paper shows 6 rectangles in 3 matching pairs: the front and back, the two sides, and the top and bottom.

A cube net has 6 equal squares. One common net is a cross: a row of 4 squares with one square above and one below the second square. But not every arrangement of 6 squares works. A straight row of 6 squares rolls into a tube with two faces overlapping and two open ends, so it is not a net of a cube. Test a net by folding it in your mind or on paper.

To describe a net, say how many faces it has, what shapes they are and how they are joined.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: counting the curved surface of a tin as a face, and saying it has 3 faces; it has 2 flat faces and 1 curved surface. Two: saying a ball has 1 face; it has no flat faces, only a curved surface. Three: thinking two objects with 5 faces must be the same; the shapes of the faces matter. Four: thinking any 6 squares make a cube net; a row of 6 does not fold into a cube.

The questions for this section are with you now: making models from polygons and describing nets.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the Rustenburg recycling project — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Flat or Curved

Cereal box: 6 flat faces. Tin: 2 flat faces and 1 curved surface.

Cone: 1 flat face and 1 curved surface. Ball: only curved.

Curved surfaces roll. Flat faces slide and stack.

Keep flat and curved in mind and try the questions for this part: faces and surfaces.

## Subtopic: Sort Them Out

Only flat, only curved, or both.

Then count the faces: 4, 5 or 6.

Same number of faces does not mean the same object. Look at the shapes.

Hold the sort-by-one-thing idea in mind and try this part's questions: sorting and comparing.

## Subtopic: Open the Box

Cut a box open and press it flat: that is a net.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Describe a 3D object by the number of flat faces, their shapes, and any curved surfaces. Sort objects into only flat, only curved, or both, and then by number of faces. Compare objects by saying what is the same and what is different, including the shapes of the faces. Build models from the right cut-out polygons. A net is the flat pattern of an object; describe it by its faces and how they join, and fold it in your mind to check it.

Hold the fold-it-in-your-mind habit in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: models and nets.
