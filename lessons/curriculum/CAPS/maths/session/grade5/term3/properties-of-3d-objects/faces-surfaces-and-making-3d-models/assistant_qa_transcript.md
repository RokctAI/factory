### subtopic_1 — Faces and Surfaces
**Assistant:** Is the curved part of a cylinder a face?

**Tutor:** In Grade 5 we call only the flat surfaces faces, and we call the round part a curved surface. So a cylinder, like a tin, has 2 faces, which are the circles at the top and bottom, and 1 curved surface around the middle. A cone has 1 face, the circle at the bottom, and 1 curved surface. A sphere, like a ball, has no faces at all, only 1 curved surface. Keeping these words separate makes descriptions clear. If you say a tin has 3 faces, someone might picture three flat shapes, which is not what a tin looks like. If you say it has 2 circle faces and 1 curved surface, they can picture it exactly. You can see the curved surface very clearly if you peel the label off a tin. The label covers the curved surface, and when you flatten it, it becomes a rectangle. So the net of a cylinder is made of 2 circles and 1 rectangle. But while it is wrapped around the tin, it is curved, not flat, and that is why it is not counted as a face.

### subtopic_2 — Sorting and Comparing 3D Objects
**Assistant:** How do I know whether a shape made of 6 squares is the net of a cube?

**Tutor:** Fold it in your mind, one square at a time, or cut it out and fold it for real. Choose one square to be the bottom of the cube. Then fold up the squares joined to it as sides. Keep going until every square has a place. For a real cube net, every square ends up as a different face, with no two squares on top of each other and no face left open. The cross shape works: a row of 4 squares becomes the bottom, back, top and front as it wraps around, and the two squares sticking out on either side fold up to become the left and right sides. A straight row of 6 squares does not work: it wraps around the cube and keeps going, so the fifth and sixth squares land on top of the first two, and the left and right sides stay open. There are 11 different nets of a cube altogether, so there are many correct answers. But checking by folding is always the safe way to be sure. With practice, you will start to see quickly which arrangements will fold up and which will not.

### subtopic_3 — Models and Nets
**Assistant:** Two objects both have 5 faces. Are they the same object?

**Tutor:** Not necessarily. The number of faces is only one part of the description. You also need the shapes of the faces and how they fit together. A triangular prism, like the toothpaste box, has 5 faces: 2 matching triangles at the ends and 3 rectangles around the sides. A square-based pyramid also has 5 faces: 1 square base and 4 triangles that meet at a point. They look completely different. The prism has two matching ends and the same shape all the way along. The pyramid has one base and comes to an apex. They also have different numbers of edges and vertices: the triangular prism has 9 edges and 6 vertices, while the square-based pyramid has 8 edges and 5 vertices. So when you compare objects, list everything: the number of faces, the shapes of the faces, any curved surfaces, and the numbers of edges and vertices. If two objects match on every point, they are the same kind of object. If even one point is different, they are different objects.
