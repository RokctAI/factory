### subtopic_1 — Slide, Turn and Flip
**Assistant:** How can I tell a translation from a reflection?

**Tutor:** Look at which way the copy faces. In a translation, the shape slides without turning or flipping, so the copy faces exactly the same way as the original. If the original letter F has its arms pointing to the right, the translated F still has its arms pointing to the right. In a reflection, the shape is flipped over a mirror line, so the copy is a mirror image and faces the opposite way. A reflected F has its arms pointing to the left, like an F seen in a mirror. With shapes that are symmetrical, like a square, it can be hard to tell, because a square looks the same whether it is slid or flipped. Then you need to look at a mark or a corner. If you label one corner of the square with a dot, a slide keeps the dot in the same corner, while a flip moves it to the matching corner on the other side. When you describe a move, say what happened: slid 4 squares to the right, or flipped over the dotted line.

### subtopic_2 — Making Composite Shapes
**Assistant:** Why do some shapes tessellate and others do not?

**Tutor:** A shape tessellates when copies of it can fit around every corner point with no gaps and no overlaps. At each point where corners meet, the corners must fill a full turn exactly. Squares have square corners, and four square corners fill a full turn, so squares fit perfectly, four meeting at each point. Equilateral triangles have smaller corners, and six of them fill a full turn, so triangles tessellate with six meeting at each point. Regular hexagons have wider corners, and three of them fill a full turn, which is why a honeycomb works. Regular pentagons have corners that are too big for four to fit but too small for three to fill the turn, so a gap is always left. Circles have no corners and curved sides, so there are always little gaps between them, like the spaces between coins on a table. You do not need to measure angles to test a shape: trace it several times, fitting the copies together, and see whether gaps appear.

### subtopic_3 — Tessellations
**Assistant:** How do I make a composite shape with a line of symmetry?

**Tutor:** Use a reflection. Trace your shape, then choose one of its straight sides as a mirror line. Flip the card shape over that side so that the side stays in exactly the same place, and trace again. The two shapes together make a composite shape, and the mirror line runs right through the middle of it as a line of symmetry. For example, a right-angled triangle reflected over one of its shorter sides makes a bigger triangle with two equal sides, and the line of symmetry runs from its top corner to the middle of its base. A square with a triangle on top, reflected over its bottom side, makes a shape like an arrow pointing both ways, with a line of symmetry across the middle. Check with a fold: if you cut out the composite shape and fold it along the mirror line, the two halves should match exactly. Translations and rotations can also make symmetrical shapes, but reflection is the surest way, because the reflection line is always a line of symmetry.
