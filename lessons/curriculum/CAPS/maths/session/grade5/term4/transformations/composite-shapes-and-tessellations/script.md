# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers making composite 2D shapes, including shapes with line symmetry, by tracing and moving a 2D shape by rotation, translation or reflection, and making tessellated patterns, including some with line symmetry. The anchor is a Grade 5 class in Polokwane designing a mosaic for the floor of the school entrance: they cut out card shapes, trace them, move them and trace again to build up designs. The governing idea is that a shape can be moved in three ways, by sliding, turning or flipping, and that it keeps its size and shape every time, so moved copies can be fitted together into new shapes and patterns. Describing transformations, making composite shapes and making tessellations are examinable.

## Subtopic: Slide, Turn and Flip

A transformation moves a shape to a new position. There are three kinds, and in each one the shape keeps exactly the same size and shape.

A translation is a slide. Every point of the shape moves the same distance in the same direction, and the shape does not turn. Slide a card square 5 squares to the right on grid paper, and the copy looks exactly like the first square, just in a new place.

A rotation is a turn around a fixed point. You can turn a shape a quarter turn, a half turn or a full turn, clockwise like the hands of a clock, or anticlockwise. A full turn brings the shape back to where it started. Turn a card triangle a quarter turn clockwise around one corner, and it points in a new direction.

A reflection is a flip over a mirror line. The copy is a mirror image: every point is the same distance from the mirror line as the matching point on the original, but on the other side. Reflect the letter F, and the copy faces the other way.

A rotation needs a turning point and an amount of turn. A quarter turn is like the minute hand moving from 12 to 3. A half turn is from 12 to 6, and a three-quarter turn is from 12 to 9. Saying a quarter turn clockwise about the bottom corner tells someone exactly how to move the shape.

The questions for this section are with you now: naming and describing translations, rotations and reflections.

## Subtopic: Making Composite Shapes

A composite shape is made by joining shapes together. The class makes composite shapes by tracing a card shape, moving it, and tracing it again.

Reflection makes symmetrical shapes. Trace a right-angled triangle, then reflect it over one of its two shorter sides. The two triangles together make a bigger triangle with two equal sides, and the mirror line is its line of symmetry. Reflecting any shape over one of its sides always gives a composite shape with a line of symmetry along that side.

Translation makes longer shapes. Trace a square, slide it one side length to the right, and trace again: the two squares make a rectangle. Slide again and you get a longer rectangle.

Rotation makes shapes that turn around a point. Trace a square, then rotate it a quarter turn around one corner three times, tracing each time. The four squares make one big square, with the turning point in the middle.

To describe what you did, name the transformation and give details: reflected over the long side, translated 3 squares up, or rotated a half turn around the top corner.

A composite shape can use more than one move. The class traces a square, reflects a triangle onto its top side to make a house shape, and then translates the whole house along to make a row of houses for the mosaic border. Each house is exactly the same size and shape.

The questions for this section are with you now: making composite shapes by tracing and moving, and finding their lines of symmetry.

## Subtopic: Tessellations

A tessellation is a pattern of shapes that fit together to cover a flat surface with no gaps and no overlaps, like the tiles on a floor. The mosaic needs to tessellate, or there will be holes in the floor.

Some shapes tessellate on their own. Squares tessellate by translation, sliding in rows. Rectangles tessellate like bricks in a wall. Equilateral triangles tessellate when every second triangle is rotated a half turn, so they fit point up, point down. Regular hexagons tessellate like a honeycomb.

Some shapes do not tessellate on their own. Circles always leave gaps between them. Regular pentagons leave gaps too, because their corners do not fit together around a point.

Many tessellations have lines of symmetry. A floor of squares has lines of symmetry along the edges and through the middles of the squares. A tessellation made by reflecting a shape over its sides, again and again, has lines of symmetry along the reflection lines.

The class tests shapes before choosing tiles. They trace a regular hexagon several times, fitting the copies together, and find no gaps. They try regular pentagons the same way and find thin gaps that cannot be filled. So the mosaic uses hexagons and squares, not pentagons.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking a rotated triangle is a different shape; it is the same triangle, turned. Two: reflecting a shape but putting the copy at a different distance from the mirror line, so the halves do not match. Three: thinking circles tessellate; they always leave gaps. Four: calling a slide a flip, when the copy faces the same way as the original.

The questions for this section are with you now: which shapes tessellate, how they are moved to tessellate, and symmetry in tessellations.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the Polokwane mosaic floor — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Three Ways to Move

Translation: slide. Rotation: turn. Reflection: flip.

The shape stays the same size and the same shape.

Keep slide, turn and flip in mind and try the questions for this part: transformations.

## Subtopic: Trace, Move, Trace

Flip a triangle over its side: a symmetrical shape.

Slide a square: a rectangle. Turn a square four times: a big square.

Hold the trace-move-trace habit in mind and try this part's questions: composite shapes.

## Subtopic: No Gaps, No Overlaps

Squares, rectangles, triangles and hexagons tessellate. Circles do not.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A translation slides a shape, a rotation turns it around a point, and a reflection flips it over a mirror line, and the shape keeps its size and shape each time. Trace, move and trace again to make composite shapes; reflecting over a side gives a line of symmetry. A tessellation covers a surface with no gaps and no overlaps. Squares, rectangles, equilateral triangles and regular hexagons tessellate; circles and regular pentagons do not.

Hold the no-gaps rule in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: tessellations.
