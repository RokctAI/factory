# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers describing patterns in nature, modern everyday life and our cultural heritage, referring to lines, 2D shapes, 3D objects, lines of symmetry, rotations, reflections and translations. The anchor is a Grade 5 class in Makhanda in the Eastern Cape making a pattern scrapbook: each learner photographs or draws patterns at home, at school and in town, and writes a short mathematical description under each one. The governing idea is that the geometry you have learned gives you exact words for describing any pattern: what shapes it uses, how they are repeated, and whether it has symmetry. Describing patterns in nature, in everyday life and in cultural heritage with mathematical words is examinable.

## Subtopic: Patterns in Nature

Nature is full of patterns. A butterfly's wings are reflections of each other: the line down the middle of its body is a line of symmetry, and the left wing is a mirror image of the right. Many leaves also have one line of symmetry along the middle vein.

Some patterns in nature use rotation. A flower with 5 equal petals looks the same each time it is turned a fifth of a full turn. A sea star with 5 arms is similar, and it also has 5 lines of symmetry, one through each arm. A spider's web has lines spreading out from the centre like the spokes of a wheel, crossed by rings that go around and around.

Bees build honeycomb from regular hexagons that tessellate with no gaps, which is a very efficient way to store honey. A zebra's stripes are curved lines repeated across its body, and a pineapple's skin is covered in a pattern of shapes arranged in rows that curve around it.

To describe a pattern in nature, name the shapes or lines you can see, say how they repeat, and say whether there is a line of symmetry or a turning pattern.

Some patterns in nature use translation. The scales on a fish and the rows of kernels on a mealie cob are shapes repeated in rows, each row like a slide of the one before. A snake's skin often has the same shape repeated all along its body.

The questions for this section are with you now: describing symmetry, rotation and tessellation in nature.

## Subtopic: Patterns in Everyday Life

Modern everyday life is full of patterns made by people. A brick wall is a tessellation of rectangles, and each row is a translation of the row two below it. The dashed white line down the middle of a road is a translation: the same rectangle repeated at equal spaces.

A car wheel's hubcap often has a rotation pattern. If it has 6 equal spokes, it looks the same after every sixth of a turn. Many company logos have one or more lines of symmetry so that they look balanced.

3D objects make patterns too. Tins of food stacked in a shop make a pattern of cylinders, and boxes on a shelf make rows of rectangular prisms. A palisade fence is a translation of the same upright bar, again and again.

Floor tiles and paving bricks are tessellations. Some paving uses rectangles in a zigzag called herringbone, where each brick is rotated a quarter turn compared with its neighbour.

Road signs use shapes and symmetry too. A stop sign is an octagon, with 8 sides. A yield sign is a triangle with 3 equal sides, so it has 3 lines of symmetry. Clear shapes help drivers recognise signs quickly, even from far away.

The questions for this section are with you now: describing translations, rotations, tessellations and 3D objects in everyday patterns.

## Subtopic: Patterns in Our Cultural Heritage

South Africa has a rich heritage of patterns. Ndebele house painting, from Mpumalanga and Gauteng, uses bold black lines to outline rectangles, triangles and steps, filled with bright colours. Many designs have a line of symmetry down the middle of a wall or a doorway, so one half is a reflection of the other.

Zulu beadwork often uses triangles and diamond shapes in bands that are translated along a necklace or belt. The colours and shapes can carry meanings, like a message in beads. Basotho women decorate the mud walls of their homes with litema, patterns pressed into wet plaster, often made of one design reflected and rotated to fill a square.

Woven baskets use triangles that are rotated around the centre of the basket. A traditional rondavel is made of 3D objects: a cylinder for the walls and a cone for the roof.

When you describe a heritage pattern, use exact words and show respect for its meaning. A good description might be: the beadwork band repeats a red triangle and a white triangle by translation, and each diamond has two lines of symmetry.

The class adds its own design to the scrapbook: a beadwork-style band of red and white triangles, with each red triangle pointing up and each white triangle pointing down beneath it, so that each pair makes a diamond. The diamond is translated 6 times along the strip, and each diamond has 2 lines of symmetry.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: calling a turning pattern a line of symmetry; a 5-petal flower turns into place, and it also has lines of symmetry, but they are different ideas. Two: saying every repeating pattern must have a line of symmetry; a row of the letter F translated has none. Three: describing a pattern only as nice or colourful instead of naming the shapes and transformations. Four: forgetting 3D objects, such as describing a rondavel as a circle and a triangle instead of a cylinder and a cone.

The questions for this section are with you now: describing Ndebele painting, beadwork, litema and other heritage patterns.
