# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers estimating and practically measuring capacity and volume using measuring spoons, cups and jugs, and recording, comparing and ordering capacity and volume in millilitres and litres. The anchor is a Grade 5 class in Stellenbosch making fruit punch for the end-of-year concert: they measure juice, water and syrup, read the scales on jugs and compare bottles of different sizes. The governing idea is that liquids are measured in millilitres and litres, with 1 000 millilitres in 1 litre, and that a measuring tool is read carefully at eye level with the right unit written down. Choosing units and tools, reading jugs, and comparing and ordering amounts are examinable.

## Subtopic: Millilitres, Litres and Measuring Tools

Capacity is how much a container can hold, and volume is how much liquid is actually in it. Both are measured in millilitres, ml, and litres, l. A millilitre is tiny: a few drops. A litre is a carton of milk. 1 litre is 1 000 millilitres.

Benchmarks help you estimate. A teaspoon holds 5 ml. A tablespoon holds 15 ml. A measuring cup holds 250 ml. A can of cooldrink holds 330 ml. A small water bottle holds 500 ml. A large cooldrink bottle holds 2 l.

Choose the tool to suit the amount. Measuring spoons are for small amounts, like 5 ml of vanilla. Measuring cups are for amounts like 250 ml of juice. Measuring jugs, marked with a scale, are for larger amounts up to 1 l or 2 l. For a whole bucket of punch, the class fills a 2 l jug several times.

Choose the unit to suit the amount too. Medicine and spices are measured in millilitres. Buckets, tanks and swimming pools are measured in litres.

Recipes often mix tools. The punch recipe says 2 tablespoons of lemon juice, 1 cup of syrup and 2 l of water. 2 tablespoons is 30 ml, and 1 cup is 250 ml. Knowing what each tool holds lets you write the whole recipe in millilitres.

Estimating with benchmarks is quick. A jug of juice that looks about the size of four measuring cups holds about 1 000 ml, which is 1 l. A glass that looks a little smaller than a cooldrink can holds about 250 ml. After estimating, the class measures to check how close they were.

The questions for this section are with you now: millilitres and litres, benchmarks and choosing measuring tools.

## Subtopic: Reading Measuring Jugs

A measuring jug has a scale of lines up the side. The class's 1 l jug has a long line every 100 ml, labelled 100, 200, 300 and so on up to 1 000, and a short line halfway between each, for the 50s.

To read a jug, put it on a flat table and bend down so that your eyes are level with the top of the liquid. Looking from above or below makes the level seem higher or lower than it is. Then find the line the liquid reaches. The orange juice reaches the short line between 700 and 800, so it is 750 ml.

Not every line is labelled. Another jug has labels every 200 ml: 200, 400, 600, 800 and 1 000, with one unlabelled line halfway between each. Halfway between 400 and 600 is 500, so the unlabelled line there is 500 ml. Work out what each small step is worth before you read.

Measuring spoons and cups are filled level to the top. A heaped spoon or a half-filled cup gives the wrong amount.

Measuring cups have scales too. A cup marked 50 ml, 100 ml, 150 ml, 200 ml and 250 ml is read in the same way as a jug. The class measures 150 ml of syrup by filling it to the third line and checking at eye level.

The questions for this section are with you now: reading jug scales, labelled and unlabelled lines, and measuring at eye level.

## Subtopic: Recording, Comparing and Ordering

Always record a capacity with its unit: 750 ml, not 750. Without the unit, 750 could mean 750 l, which would fill a small pond.

To compare amounts, change them to the same unit. Is 750 ml more or less than 1 l? 1 l is 1 000 ml, so 750 ml is less, by 250 ml. Is 2 l more than 500 ml? 2 l is 2 000 ml, so it is much more, even though 2 is a smaller number than 500.

The class lines up their containers from smallest to largest: can 330 ml, small bottle 500 ml, juice jug 750 ml, milk carton 1 l, cooldrink bottle 2 l. Writing them all in millilitres, 330, 500, 750, 1 000 and 2 000, makes the order clear.

Estimate before you measure. The punch bowl looks as if it holds about 5 large cooldrink bottles, so a good estimate is 10 l. Then measure by filling it with the 2 l jug and counting.

Containers that look different can hold the same amount. A tall thin bottle and a short wide jug both hold 500 ml, but the tall one looks bigger. The class pours water from one into the other to check, and it fits exactly. Shape can fool your eyes, so measure.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: reading the jug from above, so 750 ml looks like 800 ml. Two: thinking 2 l is less than 500 ml because 2 is less than 500; 2 l is 2 000 ml. Three: recording 750 with no unit. Four: using a tablespoon when the recipe says a teaspoon, so 15 ml goes in instead of 5 ml.

The questions for this section are with you now: recording with units, comparing and ordering capacities.
