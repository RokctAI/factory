### subtopic_1 — Millilitres, Litres and Measuring Tools
**Assistant:** Why must my eyes be level with the liquid when I read a jug?

**Tutor:** Because looking from above or below makes the liquid seem to be at a different line. The scale is on the side of the jug, and the liquid surface is a little way behind it. If you look down from above, your line of sight passes the top of the liquid and hits the scale higher up, so the liquid seems to be at a higher mark than it really is. If you look up from below, it seems lower. The only way to read it correctly is to look straight across, with your eyes at the same height as the top of the liquid. That is why you put the jug on a flat table and bend down, instead of holding it up in the air or looking down into it. Holding a jug in your hand also tilts it, which makes the liquid higher on one side than the other. A good habit is to take your reading, then lift your head and check again from a different angle, to see how much the reading changes. That shows you why eye level matters.

### subtopic_2 — Reading Measuring Jugs
**Assistant:** How do I read a line on a jug that has no number?

**Tutor:** Work out what each small step is worth by looking at the labelled lines around it. On a jug labelled 200, 400, 600, 800 and 1 000, the labels go up by 200 ml each time. If there is one unlabelled line halfway between each label, it splits each 200 ml into two steps of 100 ml. So the unlabelled line between 400 and 600 is 500 ml. If there were four small lines between labels, they would split 200 ml into five steps of 40 ml each. On the class's other jug, labelled every 100 ml with one short line halfway between, each short line is worth 50 ml, so the line between 700 and 800 is 750 ml. Always count the spaces, not the lines, between two labels, and divide the jump between the labels by the number of spaces. Then count up from the label below the liquid. This works for every scale, including those on measuring cups, thermometers and rulers.

### subtopic_3 — Recording, Comparing and Ordering
**Assistant:** Why is 2 litres more than 500 millilitres when 2 is less than 500?

**Tutor:** Because litres and millilitres are different sizes, and a litre is much bigger than a millilitre. Comparing 2 with 500 is like comparing 2 buckets with 500 drops: the number of buckets is smaller, but the buckets hold far more. To compare properly, change both amounts into the same unit. 1 litre is 1 000 millilitres, so 2 litres is 2 000 millilitres. Now compare 2 000 ml with 500 ml: 2 000 is clearly bigger, so 2 litres is more, by 1 500 ml. In fact, a 2 l cooldrink bottle holds as much as four 500 ml bottles. The same mistake can happen with other measurements, such as comparing 3 kg with 900 g, or 2 km with 800 m. In each case, the bigger unit can hide a lot more than its small number suggests. So whenever two amounts have different units, change them to the same unit first, usually the smaller one, and then compare the numbers.
