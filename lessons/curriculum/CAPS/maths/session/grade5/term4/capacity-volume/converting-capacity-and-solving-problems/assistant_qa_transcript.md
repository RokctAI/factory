### subtopic_1 — Litres to Millilitres and Back
**Assistant:** Why is a quarter of a litre 250 ml and not 25 ml?

**Tutor:** Because a litre is 1 000 ml, so a quarter of a litre is a quarter of 1 000, not a quarter of 100. Dividing 1 000 by 4 gives 250. You can check this by putting four quarters together: 250 plus 250 plus 250 plus 250 is 1 000 ml, which is exactly 1 litre. If a quarter were only 25 ml, four quarters would make 100 ml, which is only a small glass of water, not a litre. The mistake usually happens when people mix up litres with metres, where there are 100 centimetres in a metre, or with money, where there are 100 cents in a rand. Each kind of measurement has its own conversion number, and for litres it is 1 000. A measuring cup is a good reminder: most measuring cups hold 250 ml, and four cups fill a 1 litre jug. Whenever you find a fraction of a litre, start with 1 000 ml and find that fraction of it.

### subtopic_2 — Fractions of a Litre
**Assistant:** How do I change 4 500 ml into litres?

**Tutor:** Divide by 1 000, because every 1 000 ml makes 1 litre. 4 500 divided by 1 000 is 4, with 500 left over. So 4 500 ml is 4 litres and 500 millilitres, written 4 l 500 ml. You can also write the 500 ml as a fraction of a litre. 500 ml is half of 1 000 ml, so it is half a litre, and 4 500 ml is 4 and 1/2 l. The mistake to avoid is dropping zeros carelessly, which can turn 4 500 ml into 45 l, a hundred times too big. A good check is to think about real containers. 4 500 ml is a bit more than four 1 l milk cartons, which sounds right. 45 l is enough to fill several buckets, which is far too much. Another check is to go back the other way: 4 l is 4 000 ml, and 4 000 plus 500 is 4 500 ml, which matches the amount you started with.

### subtopic_3 — Solving Capacity Problems
**Assistant:** How can I work out how much water a dripping tap wastes in a day?

**Tutor:** Measure how much it drips in a short time, then multiply up to a longer time. Lwazi put a 250 ml cup under the tap and found it filled in 10 minutes. An hour has 60 minutes, which is 6 lots of 10 minutes, so in 1 hour the tap fills 6 cups. 6 times 250 is 1 500 ml, which is 1 and 1/2 l. A day has 24 hours, so in a day the tap wastes 24 times 1 and 1/2 l. 24 times 1 is 24, and 24 times a half is 12, so 24 plus 12 is 36 l. That is more than 7 big buckets of 5 l each, wasted by one small drip. In a week, it would be 7 times 36, which is 252 l. Working it out step by step like this shows why small leaks matter, especially in a city that has run short of water. It is also a good example of using conversions in a real problem: millilitres for the small measurement, and litres for the big answer.
