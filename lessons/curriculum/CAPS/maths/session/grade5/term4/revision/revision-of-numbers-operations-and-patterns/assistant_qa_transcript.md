### subtopic_1 — Whole Numbers, Adding and Subtracting
**Assistant:** How should I prepare for a test that covers the whole year?

**Tutor:** Start with the skills that every topic depends on, then work through the topics in order. The times tables and place value come first, because multiplication, division, fractions, rounding and flow diagrams all use them. Practise them a little every day until they are quick. Next, practise the checking habits: estimate before you calculate and check with the opposite operation afterwards. Those habits catch many mistakes without much time. Then go through each term's topics and do two or three questions from each, rather than many from just one. For each question, write the number sentence, estimate, calculate and check, so you practise the full routine. Keep a page of the mistakes you made, with the correct method next to each, and read it the night before the test. Spend most of your time on the topics that feel uncomfortable, because that is where the extra marks are. Finally, sleep well and eat breakfast on the day of the test: a rested brain remembers far more than a tired one.

### subtopic_2 — Multiplying, Dividing and Fractions
**Assistant:** How do I decide what to do with a remainder?

**Tutor:** Read the question again and ask what the remainder means in the story. 750 programmes tied in bundles of 40 gives 18 bundles with 30 programmes left over. If the question asks how many bundles are needed to tie up all the programmes, the 30 left over still need a bundle, so the answer is 19. If it asks how many full bundles of 40 can be made, the answer is 18. If it asks how many programmes are left over, the answer is 30. If the thing being shared can be cut, like cakes, the remainder can become a fraction: 7 cakes shared among 2 tables is 3 and 1/2 cakes each. So the same division can give different answers depending on the question. Always finish with a sentence that answers the question, such as 19 bundles are needed. Writing that sentence makes you think about whether your answer makes sense.

### subtopic_3 — Patterns and Number Sentences
**Assistant:** Why does the order of steps in a flow diagram matter?

**Tutor:** Because doing the same operations in a different order usually gives a different answer. The ticket rule is times 35, then plus 5. For 4 tickets, that is 4 times 35, which is 140, and then 140 plus 5, which is 145, so the order costs R145. If you add first, 4 plus 5 is 9, and then 9 times 35 is 315, which would charge the family for 9 tickets. The rule in words, multiply the number of tickets by 35 and add the booking fee, makes the order clear. The number sentence 4 times 35 plus 5 equals 145 follows the same order, because multiplication is done before addition. When you work backwards, you undo the steps in the opposite order: first take away the 5, then divide by 35. For R180, 180 minus 5 is 175, and 175 divided by 35 is 5 tickets. Check by going forwards again: 5 times 35 is 175, plus 5 is 180. The order matters in both directions.
