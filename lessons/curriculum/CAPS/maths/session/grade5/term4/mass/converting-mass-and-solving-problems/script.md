# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers converting between grams and kilograms, with whole numbers and fractions, and solving problems in contexts involving mass. The anchor is a Grade 5 class in Kroonstad baking for the school bake sale: their recipes are in grams, but the flour and sugar come in bags marked in kilograms. The governing idea is that 1 kilogram is 1 000 grams, so you multiply by 1 000 to change kilograms to grams and divide by 1 000 to change grams to kilograms, and that you change every mass to the same unit before you calculate. Converting whole numbers and fractions of kilograms, and solving mass problems, are examinable.

## Subtopic: Kilograms to Grams and Back

1 kilogram is 1 000 grams. To change kilograms to grams, multiply by 1 000. A 3 kg bag of potatoes is 3 times 1 000, which is 3 000 g. A 10 kg bag of maize meal is 10 000 g.

Mixed masses work the same way. A bag of flour weighs 2 kg 350 g. 2 kg is 2 000 g, and 2 000 plus 350 is 2 350 g. A tub of butter weighs 1 kg 50 g. 1 kg is 1 000 g, so the tub is 1 050 g, not 150 g.

To change grams to kilograms, divide by 1 000 and write what is left over in grams. 5 000 g is 5 kg. 4 600 g is 4 kg 600 g. 1 250 g is 1 kg 250 g.

Check the size of your answer. Changing to grams, the smaller unit, makes the number bigger. Changing to kilograms, the bigger unit, makes the number smaller.

Conversions help you compare packets. A bag of rice says 2 kg, and a box of rice says 500 g. 2 kg is 2 000 g, and 4 times 500 is 2 000, so the bag holds 4 times as much as the box.

Benchmarks help you check conversions. A 1 kg bag of sugar holds 1 000 g, so a 3 kg bag of potatoes is about as heavy as three bags of sugar. If you ever convert 3 kg and get 300 g, picture three bags of sugar: 300 g would be less than one bag, so the answer must be wrong.

The questions for this section are with you now: changing kilograms to grams and grams to kilograms.

## Subtopic: Fractions of a Kilogram

Many foods are sold in fractions of a kilogram. To change a fraction of a kilogram to grams, find that fraction of 1 000 g.

Half a kilogram is half of 1 000, which is 500 g. A quarter of a kilogram is 1 000 divided by 4, which is 250 g. A block of butter is often 250 g. Three quarters of a kilogram is 3 times 250, which is 750 g. A fifth of a kilogram is 200 g, and a tenth is 100 g.

Mixed numbers work too. A bag of flour marked 2 and 1/2 kg holds 2 000 plus 500, which is 2 500 g. 1 and 3/4 kg is 1 000 plus 750, which is 1 750 g.

Going the other way, 1 500 g is 1 kg 500 g, which is 1 and 1/2 kg. 250 g is 1/4 kg.

Recipes sometimes use fractions. The muffin recipe says 1/4 kg of butter and 1/2 kg of flour. In grams, that is 250 g of butter and 500 g of flour, which the class weighs on the kitchen scale.

The questions for this section are with you now: changing fractions and mixed numbers of kilograms to grams, and back.

## Subtopic: Solving Mass Problems

One batch of scones needs 500 g of flour. The bag holds 2 and 1/2 kg. Change to grams: 2 500 g. 2 500 divided by 500 is 5, so the class can bake 5 batches.

The class has 1 kg of sugar and uses 350 g for the muffins. Change to the same unit: 1 kg is 1 000 g. 1 000 minus 350 is 650 g left.

They bake 12 muffins, each with a mass of about 75 g. 12 times 75 is 900 g, so the tray of muffins weighs about 900 g, just under 1 kg.

Two bags of flour weigh 2 kg 400 g and 1 kg 750 g. Change both to grams: 2 400 g and 1 750 g. Together that is 4 150 g, which is 4 kg 150 g.

The steps are always the same: change every mass to the same unit, usually grams, choose the operation, calculate, and write the answer with its unit. Check that the answer makes sense.

The finished biscuits are packed in packets of 250 g. The class bakes 3 kg of biscuits. 3 kg is 3 000 g, and 3 000 divided by 250 is 12, so they fill 12 packets.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: changing 3 kg to 300 g by multiplying by 100; a kilogram is 1 000 g, so it is 3 000 g. Two: saying a quarter of a kilogram is 25 g; it is 250 g. Three: writing 1 kg 50 g as 150 g; it is 1 050 g. Four: subtracting 350 g from 1 kg as 1 minus 350; change 1 kg to 1 000 g first.

The questions for this section are with you now: solving baking problems with conversions.
