### subtopic_1 — Grams, Kilograms and Scales
**Assistant:** How do I work out what each small mark on a scale is worth?

**Tutor:** Look at two numbered marks next to each other, find the jump between them, and share it equally among the spaces in between. On Rendani's kitchen scale, the numbers go 0, 100, 200, so the jump between numbers is 100 g. Between 100 and 200 there are 4 small marks, which make 5 equal spaces. Remember to count the spaces, not the marks. 100 divided by 5 is 20, so each small mark is worth 20 g. Now read the pointer: if it stops 2 marks past 300, that is 300 plus 2 times 20, which is 340 g. On the bathroom scale, the numbers go up in 10s and there are 9 small marks between them, making 10 spaces, so each mark is 1 kg. Different scales use different steps, so work this out every time you use a new scale. A common slip is to assume every small mark is worth 10 or 1. Checking first takes only a few seconds and avoids wrong readings.

### subtopic_2 — Reading Scales
**Assistant:** Why is mass not the same as size?

**Tutor:** Because how heavy something is depends on what it is made of and how tightly it is packed, not only on how big it is. A big bag of chips is mostly air, so it is light even though it takes up a lot of space. A small bag of potatoes is packed with solid food, so it is heavy even though it is smaller. A large pillow is lighter than a small brick. That is why you cannot always tell which of two objects is heavier just by looking. You can hold one in each hand and feel which pulls down more, which is how a balance works too: the heavier side goes down. To know the actual mass, you need to weigh it on a scale. At the stall, Rendani has learned that a few large avocados can weigh less than a smaller bag of potatoes, so she always weighs the items rather than guessing the price. This idea also helps in science, where different materials of the same size can have very different masses.

### subtopic_3 — Recording, Comparing and Ordering
**Assistant:** How does a balance measure mass?

**Tutor:** A balance compares the mass on one pan with the mass on the other. If one side goes down, that side is heavier. If the two pans are level, the masses are equal. To measure the mass of something, put it on one pan, and put mass pieces of known mass, such as 100 g, 200 g and 500 g pieces, on the other pan. Add or swap the pieces until the pans are exactly level. Then add up the mass pieces: that total is the mass of the object. When Rendani puts tomatoes on one side, a 500 g piece on the other side is not enough, because the tomato side stays down. Adding a 200 g piece makes the pans level. So the tomatoes have a mass of 500 plus 200, which is 700 g. A balance is useful because it does not need a dial to be read, and it can compare two objects directly, even without mass pieces, to tell you which is heavier. Old shops and markets used balances like this before electronic scales were common.
