### subtopic_1 — Kilograms to Grams and Back
**Assistant:** Why does 1 kg 50 g become 1 050 g and not 150 g?

**Tutor:** Because the 1 kg is 1 000 g, and you add the 50 g to it. 1 000 plus 50 is 1 050. The mistake of writing 150 g comes from putting the digits side by side, as if 1 kg were 100 g. But a kilogram is much bigger than that. Think of a 1 kg bag of sugar with a small 50 g packet of sweets on top. Together they weigh a little more than 1 kg, so the answer must be a little more than 1 000 g. 150 g would be much lighter than the bag of sugar alone, which cannot be right. A good habit is to write the grams with three digits when you change a mixed mass: 1 kg 050 g. Then 1 kg becomes 1 000 g and you can see the 050 sitting in the right places, giving 1 050 g. The same care is needed for 2 kg 5 g, which is 2 005 g, and 3 kg 80 g, which is 3 080 g. Always ask whether your answer is close to the number of whole kilograms times 1 000.

### subtopic_2 — Fractions of a Kilogram
**Assistant:** How do I solve a problem when the recipe is in grams and the bag is in kilograms?

**Tutor:** Change the kilograms to grams first, so both amounts are in the same unit. The scone recipe needs 500 g of flour for each batch, and the bag is 2 and 1/2 kg. 2 kg is 2 000 g and half a kilogram is 500 g, so the bag holds 2 500 g. Now both numbers are in grams, and you can divide: 2 500 divided by 500 is 5 batches. If you tried to divide without converting, you would get a strange answer, like 2 and a half divided by 500, which is tiny and does not make sense. The same idea works for subtracting. The class has 1 kg of sugar and uses 350 g. 1 kg is 1 000 g, and 1 000 minus 350 is 650 g left. Grams are usually the best unit to work in, because they are whole numbers for most recipes. If the answer is big, you can change it back to kilograms at the end, such as 4 150 g, which is 4 kg 150 g.

### subtopic_3 — Solving Mass Problems
**Assistant:** How do I change a fraction of a kilogram into grams?

**Tutor:** Find that fraction of 1 000 g, because 1 kg is 1 000 g. For half a kilogram, half of 1 000 is 500 g. For a quarter, divide 1 000 by 4 to get 250 g. For three quarters, take a quarter, 250 g, and multiply by 3 to get 750 g. For a fifth, divide 1 000 by 5 to get 200 g. For a tenth, divide by 10 to get 100 g. For a mixed number, change the whole kilograms first and then add the fraction. 1 and 3/4 kg is 1 000 g plus 750 g, which is 1 750 g. 2 and 1/2 kg is 2 000 g plus 500 g, which is 2 500 g. This is exactly the same method you used for fractions of a litre, with 1 000 ml, and for fractions of a kilometre, with 1 000 m. All three units have 1 000 of the smaller unit inside them, so once you know the method for one, you know it for all three.
