### subtopic_1 — Straight Sides and Curved Sides
**Assistant:** Where does a semicircle go when I sort by straight and curved sides?

**Tutor:** It goes in the group for shapes with both straight and curved sides. A semicircle is half a circle, cut straight across the middle. Its outline has two parts: the straight cut, which is one straight side, and the round part, which is one curved side. Because it has a straight side, some learners put it with the polygons. But a polygon must have only straight sides, so the curved side keeps it out. Because it has a curved side, some put it with the circles. But it is not only curved. That is why a sorting table with three groups is useful: only straight, only curved, and both. The semicircle fits neatly in the both group, together with shapes like a door with a round top, a slice of a round cake, or a quarter circle. When you sort, trace the outline with your finger all the way around. Each time you pass along a straight part or a curved part, say so. When you get back to the start, you will know exactly which group the shape belongs to.

### subtopic_2 — Sorting and Comparing by Number of Sides
**Assistant:** Why does the size of a shape not matter when I sort by number of sides?

**Tutor:** Because making a shape bigger or smaller does not change how many sides it has. A triangle drawn on a postage stamp and a triangle painted on a soccer field both have 3 sides, so both are triangles. When you sort by number of sides, you are only asking one question: how many sides? Size, colour, thickness and the way the shape is turned are not part of that question, so you ignore them. This is what sorting means in maths: you choose one property and look at that property only. You could sort the same shapes in a different way later, for example by size, and then the big triangle and the big square might go together. Neither way is wrong; they answer different questions. In tests, shapes are often drawn in different sizes, colours and positions to check that you really count the sides. Learners who are tricked usually put a tall thin triangle in a different group from a short wide one. Trace and count, and you will put every triangle with the other triangles.

### subtopic_3 — Drawing Shapes on Grid Paper
**Assistant:** How do I make sure my rectangle on grid paper is the right size?

**Tutor:** Count the squares along each side, not the lines you cross. To draw a side 5 squares long, put your pencil on a point where two lines meet and move along a line, counting each small square you pass: one, two, three, four, five. Stop at the point at the end of the fifth square. A common slip is to count the lines instead, starting with the line you are on as one. If you stop at the fifth line, the side is only 4 squares long. Counting the squares themselves avoids the problem. After drawing all four sides, check that the opposite sides are the same length: the top and bottom should both be 5, and the two upright sides should both be 3. Also check that the last side ends exactly where the first side began, so that the shape is closed. Grid paper is helpful because the lines are already straight and the squares are all equal, so you can draw accurate shapes without measuring with a ruler. You can draw squares, triangles, pentagons and hexagons the same way, by planning the corners first.
