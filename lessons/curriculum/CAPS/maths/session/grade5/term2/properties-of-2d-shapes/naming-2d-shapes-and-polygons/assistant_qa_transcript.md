### subtopic_1 — Polygons and Circles
**Assistant:** Why is a circle not a polygon?

**Tutor:** Because a polygon must have only straight sides, and a circle has no straight sides at all. Its outline is one smooth curved line that bends the same amount all the way around. A polygon also has vertices, the corners where two straight sides meet, and a circle has none. Some people think a circle is a polygon with very many sides, because a polygon with lots of short sides can look round from far away. But look closely at that shape and you will see the tiny straight sides and corners, while a true circle never has any. The same rule applies to other shapes with curves. A semicircle, which is half a circle, has one straight side and one curved side, so it is not a polygon. A shape like a running track, with two straight sides and two curved ends, is not a polygon either. So when you are deciding, check every part of the outline. If even one part is curved, the shape is not a polygon. Circles are still important 2D shapes, and you will find them everywhere: in coins, wheels, plates and the beads on Ndebele designs.

### subtopic_2 — Regular and Irregular Polygons
**Assistant:** Is a rectangle a regular polygon?

**Tutor:** Usually not. A regular polygon must pass two tests: all its sides are the same length, and all its angles are the same size. A rectangle passes the second test, because all four of its corners are square corners. But a rectangle that is longer than it is wide fails the first test, because its long sides are not the same length as its short sides. So it is an irregular quadrilateral. There is one rectangle that passes both tests: the square. A square has four equal sides and four square corners, so a square is a regular quadrilateral. A rhombus fails in the other way. It has four equal sides, but two of its angles are narrow and two are wide, so it is irregular too. This is why you must always check both sides and angles. Regular does not mean normal or neat-looking. It has an exact meaning in maths. A regular hexagon, like a cell in a honeycomb, has six equal sides and six equal angles. A hexagon with one long side or one squashed corner is still a hexagon, but it is irregular.

### subtopic_3 — Recognising Shapes in Any Position
**Assistant:** If I turn a square so that it stands on a corner, does it become a different shape?

**Tutor:** No. Turning, flipping or sliding a shape does not change its sides or its angles, so it does not change its name. A square standing on one corner still has four equal sides and four square corners. Many people call it a diamond because of the way it looks, and that is fine in everyday talk, but in maths it is still a square. The same is true for every shape. A triangle balanced on its point is still a triangle. A hexagon turned so that a point faces up is still a hexagon. When you are not sure, imagine turning the page until the shape sits in the way you are used to seeing it, or trace it and turn the tracing. Then count the sides again and check whether they are equal. Questions in tests often show shapes turned in unusual ways on purpose, to check that you look at the sides and angles, not just at how the shape is sitting. If you count the sides and check the angles, the position of the shape will never trick you.
