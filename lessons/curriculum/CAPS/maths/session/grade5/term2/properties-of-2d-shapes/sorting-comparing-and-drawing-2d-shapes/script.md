# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers describing, sorting and comparing 2D shapes in terms of straight and curved sides and the number of sides, and drawing 2D shapes on grid paper. The anchor is a Grade 5 craft club in Gqeberha making a hanging mobile from coloured card shapes: they sort a pile of cut-outs, compare them, and draw new shapes on grid paper before they cut. The governing idea is that you sort shapes by choosing one property at a time, such as the kind of sides or the number of sides, and that the lines of grid paper help you draw straight sides and equal lengths accurately. Describing and sorting shapes, comparing them, and drawing shapes on grid paper are examinable.

## Subtopic: Straight Sides and Curved Sides

The club tips out a box of card shapes: triangles, squares, rectangles, a pentagon, a hexagon, circles, an oval, a semicircle and a shape like a door with a round top. The first job is to sort them by the kind of sides.

Some shapes have only straight sides: the triangles, squares, rectangles, the pentagon and the hexagon. These are the polygons. Some shapes have only curved sides: the circles and the oval. Some have both straight and curved sides: the semicircle has one straight side and one curved side, and the door shape has three straight sides and one curved top.

A sorting table with three columns works well: only straight, only curved, and both. Every shape fits into exactly one column. To describe a shape fully, say what kind of sides it has and how many. For example: the door shape has 3 straight sides and 1 curved side.

Comparing two shapes means saying what is the same and what is different. A circle and an oval both have only one curved side, but a circle is equally round all the way around, while an oval is longer one way than the other.

The questions for this section are with you now: describing shapes by their sides and sorting them into straight, curved and both.

## Subtopic: Sorting and Comparing by Number of Sides

Next the club sorts the polygons by the number of sides. Three sides go together: all the triangles, big or small, thin or wide. Four sides go together: the squares, the rectangles and a kite. Then the pentagon with 5, and the hexagon with 6.

Size, colour and position do not change the group. A tiny blue triangle and a huge red triangle both have 3 sides, so they belong together. When you sort by number of sides, look only at the sides.

You can compare shapes by counting. A hexagon has 3 more sides than a triangle. A pentagon has 1 more side than a quadrilateral. Two shapes can have the same number of sides and still look very different, like a square and a kite, which both have 4 sides.

Sometimes you sort by two properties at once. Among the 4-sided shapes, the club makes two groups: those with all sides equal, the squares, and those without, the rectangles and the kite. A table with rows and columns, or two overlapping circles called a Venn diagram, shows sorting by two properties clearly.

The questions for this section are with you now: sorting polygons by number of sides and comparing shapes.

## Subtopic: Drawing Shapes on Grid Paper

Grid paper has lines that make small squares. Drawing along the lines, or from dot to dot where lines cross, gives straight sides and exact lengths.

To draw a rectangle 5 squares long and 3 squares high: start at a corner where two lines cross, go 5 squares to the right, then 3 squares up, then 5 squares to the left, then 3 squares down to the start. The opposite sides are equal: 5 and 5, 3 and 3. A square is drawn the same way with all four sides the same, such as 4, 4, 4 and 4.

For other polygons, plan the corners first. A triangle needs only 3 corners: mark them, then join them with a ruler. A house-shaped pentagon is a square with a triangle on top: draw a 4 by 4 square, mark a point 2 squares above the middle of the top side, and join it to the two top corners, then rub out the top side of the square. Count the outside sides: 5. A hexagon can be drawn by marking 6 corners and joining them in order.

Always count the sides of your finished drawing and check that it is closed, with no gaps where the lines should meet.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: putting the semicircle with the polygons because it has a straight side; it also has a curved side, so it belongs with both. Two: putting a big triangle and a small triangle in different groups; size does not change the number of sides. Three: counting lines instead of squares, starting with the line you are on as one, so a side meant to be 5 squares long is only 4. Four: leaving the top of the square inside the house shape and calling it a pentagon; the outline has 5 sides, and the extra line is not part of it.

The questions for this section are with you now: drawing rectangles, squares, triangles and other polygons on grid paper.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the craft club's mobile in Gqeberha — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Straight or Curved

Only straight sides: triangle, square, hexagon.

Only curved: circle, oval.

Both: semicircle, door shape.

Keep the three groups in mind and try the questions for this part: kinds of sides.

## Subtopic: Count and Sort

3 sides together, 4 sides together, 5, 6.

Size and colour do not matter. Count only the sides.

A hexagon has 3 more sides than a triangle.

Hold the count-only-the-sides idea in mind and try this part's questions: sorting by sides.

## Subtopic: Draw on the Grid

Rectangle: 5 right, 3 up, 5 left, 3 down. Count squares, not lines.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Describe a shape by the kind of sides it has, straight, curved or both, and by how many sides it has. Sort by one property at a time, and ignore size, colour and position. Compare shapes by saying what is the same and what is different. On grid paper, follow the lines, count the squares for each side, and plan the corners before you join them. Check that your drawing is closed and count its sides.

Hold the count-the-squares habit in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: drawing on grid paper.
