# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers investigating and extending geometric patterns, looking for relationships or rules, in sequences not limited to a constant difference or ratio, and describing the rules in your own words. The anchor is a Grade 5 class in Mbombela building a maths display: squares made of matchsticks in a row, triangles of dots, squares of tiles, and a sheet of paper folded again and again. The governing idea is that every geometric pattern has a rule you can find by counting and looking at how each shape grows, and that a rule using the shape number lets you jump to any shape without drawing all the ones before. Extending geometric patterns, finding and describing their rules, and finding a far-off term are examinable.

## Subtopic: Patterns That Grow by the Same Amount

The class makes squares in a row with matchsticks. Shape 1 is one square: 4 matches. Shape 2 is two squares side by side: 7 matches. Shape 3: 10 matches. Shape 4: 13 matches. Each new square adds only 3 matches, not 4, because it shares one side with the square before it.

A table makes the pattern clear. Write the shape numbers 1, 2, 3, 4 in the top row and the matches 4, 7, 10, 13 in the bottom row. The bottom row goes up by 3 every time, so the next shapes need 16, 19 and 22 matches. A pattern that grows by the same amount every time has a constant difference.

There is a second way to see the rule. Shape 3 has 3 squares, and each square adds 3 matches, which is 9, plus 1 extra match for the very first side on the left. 9 plus 1 is 10. So the rule is: shape number times 3, plus 1. Check with shape 4: 4 times 3 is 12, plus 1 is 13. Correct.

That rule lets you jump ahead. Shape 10 needs 10 times 3 plus 1, which is 31 matches. You do not have to build shapes 5 to 9 first. Saying the rule in your own words is part of the work: each new square adds 3 matches, and the first square needs one extra.

The questions for this section are with you now: extending a pattern with a constant difference, making a table, and using a rule to find a later shape.

## Subtopic: Patterns That Grow Faster

Not every pattern grows by the same amount. The class arranges dots in triangles: 1 dot, then 3 dots, then 6, then 10. The differences are 2, 3 and 4. Each new triangle adds one more row than the last, so the next differences are 5 and 6, and the next triangles have 15 and 21 dots.

Square tiles make another kind of pattern. A 1 by 1 square uses 1 tile, a 2 by 2 square uses 4 tiles, a 3 by 3 square uses 9 and a 4 by 4 square uses 16. The rule is shape number times itself. Shape 5 uses 5 times 5, which is 25 tiles. The differences, 3, 5, 7 and 9, grow by 2 each time.

Folding paper makes a doubling pattern. One sheet has 1 layer. Fold it once: 2 layers. Fold again: 4 layers. Again: 8, then 16. Each shape is double the one before. A pattern that multiplies by the same number each time has a constant ratio.

So there are three kinds of growth here: adding the same amount, adding an amount that grows, and multiplying by the same number. To find the kind, write the numbers in a row and look at the differences between neighbours. If the differences are all the same, add that difference. If they change, look for a pattern in the differences.

The questions for this section are with you now: dot triangles, square tiles, doubling patterns, and looking at differences.

## Subtopic: Describing and Using Rules

A rule can be described in two ways. A next-shape rule says how to get from one shape to the next: add 3 matches. A shape-number rule says how to get any shape from its number: shape number times 3, plus 1. The next-shape rule is easy to find, but the shape-number rule is better for jumping ahead.

For shape 20 of the matchstick pattern, the shape-number rule gives 20 times 3, plus 1, which is 61 matches. Doubling shape 10, which has 31 matches, would give 62, which is wrong, because it counts the extra first side twice. Always use the rule, not a shortcut that only seems right.

For the dot triangles, triangle 10 has 1 plus 2 plus 3 and so on up to 10 dots. Pair the numbers from the ends: 1 and 10, 2 and 9, 3 and 8, 4 and 7, 5 and 6. Each pair makes 11, and there are 5 pairs, so triangle 10 has 55 dots.

You can create your own pattern too. Start with a shape, decide how each new shape grows, and then write the rule in words, such as each new house shape adds 5 matches. Ask a friend to find shape 10 from your rule. Inventing patterns is one of the best ways to understand them.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: assuming every pattern grows by the same amount, so the triangles 1, 3, 6, 10 continue with 14; the differences grow, so the next is 15. Two: counting 4 matches for every square, so shape 10 needs 40; shared sides are counted once, so it is 31. Three: doubling shape 10 to find shape 20 and getting 62; use the rule to get 61. Four: continuing 1, 4, 9, 16 with 20 by adding 4; the next square is 5 times 5, which is 25.

The questions for this section are with you now: next-shape and shape-number rules, jumping to a far-off shape, and describing a rule in your own words.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the matchsticks, the dots and the folded paper — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Add the Same Each Time

Count the matches: 4, 7, 10, 13. Each new square adds 3, because it shares a side.

Next shapes: 16, 19, 22.

Rule: shape number times 3, plus 1. Shape 10 has 31 matches.

Keep the add-3 pattern in mind and try the questions for this part: same-amount patterns.

## Subtopic: Faster and Faster

Triangles of dots: 1, 3, 6, 10. Add 2, then 3, then 4. Next add 5: 15.

Squares of tiles: 1, 4, 9, 16. Next is 5 times 5: 25.

Folded paper: 1, 2, 4, 8. Double each time: 16.

Hold the look-at-the-differences habit in mind and try this part's questions: faster patterns.

## Subtopic: Say the Rule

Say how each shape grows: add 3 matches.

Say how to get any shape from its number: times 3, plus 1. Shape 20 has 61 matches.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Count each shape and write the numbers in a table. Look at the differences: the same difference means add that amount; growing differences mean the pattern grows faster; multiplying by the same number means a constant ratio. A next-shape rule says how to get from one shape to the next. A shape-number rule lets you jump to any shape. Describe the rule in your own words and check it on a shape you already know.

Hold the shape-number rule in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: describing and using rules.
