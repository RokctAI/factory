### subtopic_1 — Flow Diagrams: Input, Rule, Output
**Assistant:** Why must I use the opposite operations in the opposite order when I work backwards?

**Tutor:** Because you are undoing what the flow diagram did, and the last thing done has to be undone first. Think about getting dressed: you put on socks and then shoes, so to get undressed you take off the shoes first and then the socks. The table rule does times 4 first and plus 2 second. So to go back from 30 chairs, you first undo the plus 2 by taking away 2, which gives 28, and then you undo the times 4 by dividing by 4, which gives 7. If you divide first, you get 30 divided by 4, which is 7 with 2 left over, and tables do not come in pieces, so something has gone wrong. The opposite of plus is minus, and the opposite of times is divided by, because each one cancels the other: add 2 and then take away 2, and you are back where you started. Always check your answer by putting it through the flow diagram forwards. 7 times 4 is 28, and 28 plus 2 is 30, so 7 tables is right. That forward check takes only a few seconds and catches most mistakes.

### subtopic_2 — Finding Inputs and Rules
**Assistant:** How do I find the rule when I only have a table of inputs and outputs?

**Tutor:** Look at how the outputs change when the input goes up by 1, and then fix the start. Take the second kind of table: input 1 gives 5, input 2 gives 8, input 3 gives 11. Each time the input goes up by 1, the output goes up by 3. That jump of 3 tells you the rule begins with times 3. Now try times 3 on input 1: you get 3, but the real output is 5. The gap is 2, so the rule needs plus 2 as well. The rule is times 3, then plus 2. Now test it on every pair: input 2 gives 6 plus 2, which is 8, and input 3 gives 9 plus 2, which is 11. Every pair works, so you can trust it. If the inputs jump by more than 1, such as inputs 2, 4 and 6, divide the output jump by the input jump first. If one pair does not work, go back and look again, because a rule must fit every pair, not just one. Sometimes the rule is just one step, such as times 5 or plus 7, so do not force a second step if the first one already fits.

### subtopic_3 — One Rule, Three Ways
**Assistant:** How can two rules that look different be equivalent?

**Tutor:** Two rules are equivalent when they give the same output for every input, even if their steps are written differently. Times 4 then plus 2 looks different from double, double again, then plus 2. But doubling a number and doubling it again is the same as multiplying it by 4, because 2 times 2 is 4. Test with input 3: times 4 gives 12, and plus 2 gives 14. Double 3 is 6, double 6 is 12, plus 2 is 14. The same answer. Try input 10: both give 42. A number sentence and a sentence in words can also be equivalent to a flow diagram. Number of tables times 4 plus 2 equals number of chairs says exactly the same as the flow diagram with a times 4 box and then a plus 2 box. On the other hand, rules that use the same numbers can be different. Plus 2 then times 4 uses the same numbers, but for input 3 it gives 5 times 4, which is 20, not 14. So you cannot tell just by looking at the numbers in a rule. Put a few inputs through both and compare. If even one output is different, the rules are not equivalent.
