### subtopic_1 — Whole Numbers and the Four Operations
**Assistant:** There is so much in Terms 1 and 2. What should I revise first?

**Tutor:** Start with the skills that other skills depend on, because fixing those fixes several topics at once. The times tables come first. Multiplication, long division, fractions of amounts, equivalent fractions and the rules in flow diagrams all need them, and a shaky table slows every one of those down. Second, place value, because column addition, subtraction, rounding and long division all use it. Third, the checking habits: estimate before you calculate, and check with the opposite operation afterwards. After that, go through the topics in order and do two or three problems from each, not twenty from one. For fractions, practise drawing a quick picture, because a picture shows at once whether an answer makes sense. For patterns, practise finding the jump and testing a rule on every number. For shapes, practise counting sides and folding in your mind. Keep a page of the mistakes you made, with the correct method next to each, and read it the night before the test. Spend your time on the topics that feel uncomfortable, because that is where the extra marks are.

### subtopic_2 — Common Fractions
**Assistant:** How do I decide what to do with a remainder in a division problem?

**Tutor:** Read the question again and ask what the remainder stands for in the story. When 350 bottles are packed in crates of 24, the division gives 14 remainder 14: 14 full crates and 14 bottles left over. If the question asks how many crates are needed to pack all the bottles, the 14 left-over bottles still need a crate, so the answer is 15. If the question asks how many full crates can be packed, the answer is 14. If the question asks how many bottles are left over, the answer is the remainder, 14. And if the thing being shared can be cut, like oranges, the remainder can become a fraction: 7 oranges shared between 2 learners is 3 remainder 1, and the 1 left-over orange is cut in half, so each learner gets 3 and 1/2. So the same calculation can give different answers depending on the question. Always write your answer as a sentence that answers the question, such as 15 crates are needed. That sentence forces you to think about whether your answer makes sense in the story.

### subtopic_3 — Patterns, Shapes and Symmetry
**Assistant:** How can I check a fraction answer quickly?

**Tutor:** Use a picture, an estimate or the opposite operation. For 3/4 plus 2/4, draw two oranges cut into quarters and shade 3 quarters and then 2 more. You will see 5 quarters, which is one whole orange and 1 quarter of another, 1 and 1/4. If you had written 5/8, the picture would show at once that something is wrong, because 5/8 is less than one whole, but you added more than half to three quarters. For subtraction, add back: if 2 and 1/4 minus 3/4 is 1 and 2/4, then 1 and 2/4 plus 3/4 must be 2 and 1/4. It is, because 2/4 plus 3/4 is 5/4, which is 1 and 1/4, and 1 plus 1 and 1/4 is 2 and 1/4. For fractions of amounts, check that the answer is smaller than the whole amount and that the parts add up: 3/4 of 24 is 18, and the other 1/4 is 6, and 18 plus 6 is 24. For equivalent fractions, check that you multiplied or divided the top and bottom by the same number. These checks take only a few seconds and catch most mistakes.
