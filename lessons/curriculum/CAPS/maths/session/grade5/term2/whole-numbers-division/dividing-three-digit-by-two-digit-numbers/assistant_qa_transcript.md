### subtopic_1 — Dividing by Tens and by One Digit
**Assistant:** Why does 480 divided by 60 give a smaller answer than 480 divided by 6?

**Tutor:** Because dividing asks how many groups fit, and when each group is bigger, fewer groups fit. Think of 480 apples. If you pack them into bags of 6, you need a lot of bags: 80 of them. If you pack them into crates of 60, each crate holds ten times as many apples, so you need only a tenth as many crates: 8. The amount being shared has not changed; only the size of each group has. This is the opposite of what happens in multiplication, where a bigger number gives a bigger answer, and it often surprises learners at first. A good habit is to ask whether your answer should be bigger or smaller before you work it out. Dividing by a big number should give a fairly small answer. Dividing by a small number should give a bigger answer. If you divide 480 by 60 and get 80, the habit will warn you, because 80 crates of 60 would be 4 800 apples, not 480. The multiply-back check catches the same mistake: 80 times 60 is 4 800, which does not match. The same idea explains why dividing by 1 changes nothing and why dividing a number by itself always gives 1: one group of the whole amount.

### subtopic_2 — Dividing by Taking Away Chunks
**Assistant:** How do I choose the size of the chunks?

**Tutor:** Choose the biggest easy chunk that fits, and easy usually means 10, 20, 30 or another multiple of 10 times the divisor, because those are quick to work out. For 864 divided by 24, start by asking how many 24s fit in 864, roughly. 10 times 24 is 240, which is far less than 864. 20 times 24 is 480, still less. 30 times 24 is 720, still less than 864. 40 times 24 would be 960, which is too much. So 30 is the biggest multiple of 10 that fits, and it is a great first chunk. After taking it away, 144 is left, which is small, so the second chunk will be a single-digit number of groups: 6. If you are unsure, start with 10 groups. It is always safe, because 10 times any two-digit number is easy to find, and you can take away 10 groups several times. It just takes a few more steps. As you get more confident, you will start with bigger chunks and finish in two steps. There is no wrong chunk, as long as it is not bigger than what is left. If you take away too much and get a negative amount, step back and use a smaller chunk.

### subtopic_3 — Building a Table and Checking
**Assistant:** What does the remainder actually mean?

**Tutor:** The remainder is the amount left over after you have made as many full groups as possible. In 500 divided by 32, you can fill 15 buses of 32 learners, which uses 480 learners, and 20 learners are left without a full bus. Those 20 are the remainder. They are real: they are learners, or apples, or rands, that did not fit into a complete group. That is why the remainder must be smaller than the divisor. If 32 or more were left, they would fill another full bus, and you would not have finished dividing. What you do with the remainder depends on the story, and you will practise that in the problem-solving lesson. Sometimes you need an extra group, like an extra bus for the 20 learners, so the answer becomes 16 buses. Sometimes the leftover is simply left over, like apples that do not fill a box. Sometimes it can be shared further, like money that can be split into cents. In a plain calculation, without a story, write the answer as 15 remainder 20, and check it by multiplying and adding: 15 times 32 plus 20 is 500.
