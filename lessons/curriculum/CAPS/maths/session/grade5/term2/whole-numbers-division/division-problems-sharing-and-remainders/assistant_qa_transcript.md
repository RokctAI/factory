### subtopic_1 — Sharing and Grouping Problems
**Assistant:** How do I know whether a division problem is about sharing or grouping?

**Tutor:** Ask what you already know about the groups. If you know how many groups there are and need to find how much goes in each, it is sharing. If you know how much goes in each group and need to find how many groups there are, it is grouping. R675 for 15 tickets is sharing: there are 15 tickets, and we find what each one costs. 350 rolls in packs of 12 is grouping: each pack has 12, and we find how many packs. The calculation is the same kind, a division, and the numbers are put in the same order, the total divided by the known number. So why does the difference matter? Mostly for the answer sentence and for the remainder. In sharing, the answer is an amount per group, like R45 per ticket, and a remainder means an amount that could not be shared out evenly. In grouping, the answer is a number of groups, like 29 packs, and a remainder means things that did not make a full group. Thinking about which kind you have helps you write a sensible answer with the right unit and decide what the remainder means. A quick sketch helps too: draw the groups as circles and ask whether you know how many circles or how much goes in each.

### subtopic_2 — What to Do with the Remainder
**Assistant:** Why is the answer 16 buses when the division says 15 remainder 20?

**Tutor:** Because the division answers a slightly different question from the one the story asks. 500 divided by 32 tells you how many full buses the learners can fill: 15 full buses, with 20 learners not yet seated. But the school's real question is how many buses must be booked so that every learner can go. Those 20 learners are real children who need seats, so a 16th bus has to come, even though it will not be full. If the school booked only 15 buses, 20 learners would be left standing at the gate. This is why you must always go back to the story after dividing. The calculation gives you the facts: 15 groups and 20 over. The story tells you what to do with those facts. In a different story with the same numbers, the answer would change. If 500 eggs were packed in trays of 32 and the question asked how many full trays there are, the answer would be 15, because a part tray is not a full tray. If it asked how many eggs are left over, the answer would be 20. Same division, three different answers, all correct for their questions.

### subtopic_3 — Rates, Ratios and Money with Division
**Assistant:** How do I share an amount in a ratio?

**Tutor:** First add the parts of the ratio to find how many equal parts the amount must be split into, then divide to find the size of one part, then multiply for each person. With 72 sweets in the ratio 1 to 2, the ratio says that for every 1 sweet Naledi gets, Sipho gets 2. Every time they share out 3 sweets, Naledi gets 1 and Sipho gets 2. So the sweets are really split into 3 equal parts. 72 divided by 3 is 24, so each part is 24 sweets. Naledi has 1 part, which is 24 sweets, and Sipho has 2 parts, which is 48 sweets. Check in two ways: the shares should add up to the total, and 24 plus 48 is 72; and they should still be in the ratio, and 48 is indeed twice 24. The most common mistake is to divide by 2 because there are two people, giving 36 each. That would be a ratio of 1 to 1, an equal share, not 1 to 2. Another mistake is to divide by the bigger part only. Always add the parts first. For a ratio of 2 to 3, there would be 5 parts; for 3 to 5, there would be 8.
