### subtopic_1 — Estimating Before You Divide
**Assistant:** Why should I estimate if I am going to work out the exact answer anyway?

**Tutor:** Because the estimate tells you what kind of answer to expect, and that protects you from the mistakes that are hardest to spot. Division mistakes often change the size of the answer completely: a digit in the wrong place turns 42 into 420, or a missed group turns 42 into 4 or 24. When you work carefully through the steps, those mistakes can feel completely normal. An estimate of about 40, made in a few seconds at the start, makes 420 look silly at once. The estimate also helps you get started. If you know the answer is about 40, you can begin building up from 18 times 40, which is 720, and you only have a small amount left to deal with. Without an estimate, you might start with 18 times 10 and take many more steps. Finally, in real life an estimate is often all you need. A sports organiser who needs to know roughly how many group leaders to find can work with about 40 groups straight away and fill in the exact number later. So estimating is not extra work on top of the real work; it is part of working well.

### subtopic_2 — Breaking Down and Building Up
**Assistant:** Why can I split the number being divided but not the number I divide by?

**Tutor:** Because the two numbers do different jobs in a division. The number being divided is the amount of stuff, and you can share out stuff in parts. If R936 is shared among 12 classes, you can share out R600 first, giving each class R50, and then share out the other R336, giving each class R28 more. Each class ends up with R78, the same as if you had shared the whole R936 at once. Splitting the amount just means sharing it in two rounds. The divisor is the number of shares, or the size of each group, and splitting it changes the whole meaning of the division. If you share R480 among 10 people and then share R480 again among 2 more people, you have handed out R480 twice, to two separate sets of people, and each person in the second set gets R240. That is not what sharing R480 among 12 people means; each of the 12 should get R40. So the answer 288, from 48 plus 240, is far too big. A quick way to remember: you may break up the cake, but you may not break up the people.

### subtopic_3 — Halving, Doubling and the Inverse Check
**Assistant:** Why does halving both numbers keep the answer the same?

**Tutor:** Because the answer to a division depends on how the two numbers compare, and halving both keeps that comparison exactly the same. Think of R936 shared among 12 classes. Now imagine half the money, R468, shared among half the classes, 6 of them. Each class still gets the same amount, R78, because there is half as much money but also half as many classes to share it. In grouping language, 936 divided by 12 asks how many 12s fit into 936. Halving both asks how many 6s fit into 468. Every 12 in 936 matches exactly one 6 in 468, so the number of groups is the same. The same reasoning works for doubling both, or for multiplying or dividing both by any number. This is very different from multiplication, where you double one number and halve the other. In division you change both numbers in the same direction. If you change only one, the answer changes: halving only the divisor doubles the answer, which is the mistake in 936 divided by 6 equals 156. A quick check with small numbers you know always settles it: 12 divided by 4 is 3, and halving both gives 6 divided by 2, which is also 3.
