# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers solving problems with whole numbers in financial and measurement contexts, ratio and rate, and grouping and equal sharing with remainders. The anchor is a community soup kitchen in Khayelitsha that packs 350 bread rolls into packs of 12, a school trip for 500 learners in buses with 32 seats, 15 concert tickets that cost R675 altogether, and 72 sweets shared between Naledi and Sipho. The governing idea is that division answers both sharing questions and grouping questions, and that the story, not the calculation, decides what happens to the remainder. Solving sharing and grouping problems, interpreting remainders, and using division with money, measurement, rate and ratio are examinable.

## Subtopic: Sharing and Grouping Problems

Division solves two kinds of problem. In sharing, the number of groups is known and we find how much goes in each. 15 concert tickets cost R675 altogether. How much is one ticket? Share R675 into 15 equal parts: 675 divided by 15 is 45, because 15 times 45 is 675. Each ticket costs R45.

In grouping, the size of each group is known and we find how many groups there are. The soup kitchen packs 350 bread rolls into packs of 12. How many packs? 12 times 29 is 348, and 12 times 30 is 360, which is too many. So 350 divided by 12 is 29 remainder 2.

Measurement problems are often grouping problems. A roll of ribbon 900 cm long is cut into pieces of 35 cm for prizes. How many pieces? 35 times 25 is 875, and 35 times 26 is 910, which is too long. So there are 25 pieces, with 900 minus 875, which is 25 cm, left over.

Both kinds use the same calculation, and both are checked the same way: multiply back and add the remainder. 29 times 12 plus 2 is 350. 25 times 35 plus 25 is 900. Knowing which kind of problem you have helps you write the answer in the right words: R45 per ticket, 29 packs, 25 pieces.

The questions for this section are with you now: sharing problems, grouping problems, and measurement problems with leftovers.

## Subtopic: What to Do with the Remainder

The remainder means different things in different stories, so always read the question again after dividing. 500 learners are going on a trip in buses with 32 seats. 500 divided by 32 is 15 remainder 20. But 15 buses would leave 20 learners behind, and every learner must go. So round up: 16 buses are needed.

Sometimes you round down. The soup kitchen has 350 rolls and packs of 12. 350 divided by 12 is 29 remainder 2. Only full packs can be handed out, and 2 rolls cannot make a pack. So there are 29 full packs, not 30.

Sometimes the remainder is the answer. How many rolls are left over after packing? The remainder, 2, answers that question directly. A different question about the same division needs a different answer.

Sometimes the remainder can be shared further. R100 shared equally among 8 children: 100 divided by 8 is 12 remainder 4. Each child gets R12, and the R4 left over can be changed into smaller coins and shared, giving each child another 50 cents. Money, unlike buses, can be split into smaller parts.

The questions for this section are with you now: rounding up, rounding down, using the remainder as the answer, and sharing a remainder.

## Subtopic: Rates, Ratios and Money with Division

Division finds the price of one item, a kind of rate. 24 pens cost R192. One pen costs 192 divided by 24, which is R8. To compare packs, find the price of one in each. 12 juice boxes for R96 is R8 each, and 15 for R105 is R7 each, so the pack of 15 is cheaper per box.

Rates also work with distance. A car travels 12 km on 1 litre of petrol. A trip of 540 km needs 540 divided by 12 litres. 12 times 45 is 540, so the trip needs 45 litres.

To share in a ratio, first find the total number of parts. 72 sweets are shared between Naledi and Sipho in the ratio 1 to 2. The ratio has 1 plus 2, which is 3 parts. 72 divided by 3 is 24, so one part is 24 sweets. Naledi gets 1 part, 24 sweets, and Sipho gets 2 parts, 48 sweets. Check: 24 plus 48 is 72, and 48 is twice 24.

In every problem, finish with an answer sentence and a check. The car needs 45 litres; check: 45 times 12 is 540. Each pen costs R8; check: 24 times 8 is 192.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: ordering 15 buses for 500 learners because the division gave 15; 20 learners would be left behind, so 16 buses are needed. Two: saying 30 packs of rolls can be made by rounding up; only 29 full packs fit into 350 rolls. Three: dividing the wrong way round, 15 divided by 675, to find the price of a ticket; the total amount is divided by the number of tickets. Four: sharing 72 sweets in the ratio 1 to 2 as 36 and 36; the ratio has 3 parts, so the shares are 24 and 48.

The questions for this section are with you now: price for one item, rates with distance, and sharing in a ratio.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the bread rolls, the buses and the sweets — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Share or Group

Sharing: you know how many groups. R675 for 15 tickets: each ticket is R45.

Grouping: you know the size of each group. 350 rolls in packs of 12: 29 packs and 2 rolls over.

Ribbon of 900 cm in 35 cm pieces: 25 pieces and 25 cm left.

Keep the two kinds in mind and try the questions for this part: sharing and grouping.

## Subtopic: Up, Down or Left Over

500 learners, 32 seats a bus: 15 remainder 20. Everyone needs a seat, so round up to 16 buses.

350 rolls, packs of 12: 29 remainder 2. Only full packs count, so round down to 29.

How many rolls left over? The remainder is the answer: 2.

Hold the read-again habit in mind and try this part's questions: remainders.

## Subtopic: Price for One

24 pens for R192: divide to find one pen. R8.

12 km on 1 litre, 540 km to go: 540 divided by 12 is 45 litres.

Ratio 1 to 2 for 72 sweets: 3 parts, so one part is 24. Naledi 24, Sipho 48.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Sharing finds the size of each group; grouping finds the number of groups. After dividing, read the question again: round up when everyone must fit, round down when only full groups count, use the remainder when it asks what is left, and share it further when it can be split. Divide to find the price of one item or a rate. To share in a ratio, add the parts and divide by the total.

Hold the price-for-one idea in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: rate and ratio.
