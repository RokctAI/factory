# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers techniques for dividing and for checking division: estimation, building up and breaking down numbers, and multiplication and division as inverse operations. The anchor is a sports day near Brits in North West, where 756 learners are split into groups of 18, R936 of prize money is shared equally among 12 classes, and 945 water bottles are packed 15 to a box. The governing idea is that a division can be made easier by estimating its size, by splitting the number being divided into friendly parts, by building up with multiples of the divisor, or by halving or doubling both numbers, and that multiplying back checks every answer. Using any two techniques to perform and check division is examinable.

## Subtopic: Estimating Before You Divide

Estimate first, to know roughly what the answer should be. 756 learners in groups of 18: round 756 to 800 and 18 to 20. 800 divided by 20 is 40, because 20 times 40 is 800. So there will be about 40 groups. An answer of 4 or 420 would be clearly wrong.

Choose numbers that divide easily, called compatible numbers. For R936 shared among 12 classes, rounding to 900 and 10 gives 90, but 960 and 12 are better, because 12 times 8 is 96, so 960 divided by 12 is 80. Each class gets about R80. The closer the friendly numbers are to the real ones, the better the estimate.

An estimate also tells you how many digits the answer has. A three-digit number divided by a two-digit number always gives a whole-number answer with one or two digits, because even 999 divided by 10 is less than 100. If you get a three-digit answer, something has gone wrong.

Estimates are a guide, not the final answer. When the question asks for the exact number of groups, use the estimate as a starting point and then work it out exactly.

The questions for this section are with you now: estimating with friendly numbers, choosing compatible numbers, and the size of the answer.

## Subtopic: Breaking Down and Building Up

Breaking down splits the number being divided into parts that the divisor goes into easily. 936 divided by 12: split 936 into 600 and 336. 600 divided by 12 is 50, because 12 times 5 is 60. 336 divided by 12 is 28, because 12 times 28 is 336. So 936 divided by 12 is 50 plus 28, which is 78. Each class gets R78.

Another way to break down is to go to a friendly number above and take away. 960 divided by 12 is 80. 936 is 24 less than 960, and 24 is 2 twelves, so 936 divided by 12 is 80 minus 2, which is 78. This is rounding and compensating for division.

Building up uses multiplication to climb towards the number. 756 divided by 18: 18 times 40 is 720. 756 is 36 more than 720, and 36 is 2 eighteens. So 18 times 42 is 756, and the answer is 42 groups. The estimate of 40 was close.

Always split the number being divided, never the divisor. 480 divided by 12 is 40. It is not 480 divided by 10 plus 480 divided by 2, which would be 48 plus 240, which is 288. Sharing among 12 people is not the same as sharing among 10 people and then also among 2 more.

The questions for this section are with you now: splitting the number being divided, rounding and compensating, and building up with multiples of the divisor.

## Subtopic: Halving, Doubling and the Inverse Check

Halving both numbers keeps the answer the same. 936 divided by 12: halve both to get 468 divided by 6. 420 divided by 6 is 70 and 48 divided by 6 is 8, so the answer is 78. Half as many things shared among half as many classes gives each class the same amount.

Doubling both numbers also keeps the answer the same, and it is handy when the divisor ends in 5. 945 water bottles packed 15 to a box: double both to get 1 890 divided by 30. 189 tens divided by 3 tens is 63, so there are 63 boxes.

Multiplication checks every division, because it is the inverse. If 756 divided by 18 is 42, then 42 times 18 must be 756: 40 times 18 is 720 and 2 times 18 is 36, which makes 756. If 936 divided by 12 is 78, then 78 times 12 is 780 plus 156, which is 936. Both are correct.

CAPS asks for two techniques for each calculation. A strong pair for division is an estimate before you start and a multiplication check at the end, with breaking down, building up, or halving and doubling in between.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: halving only one number, so 936 divided by 12 becomes 936 divided by 6, which is 156; halve both numbers to keep the answer 78. Two: splitting the divisor, so 480 divided by 12 becomes 48 plus 240; split only the number being divided. Three: building up to 720 and giving 40 as the answer; the last 36 is 2 more groups, so the answer is 42. Four: checking a division by dividing again in the same way; check by multiplying back.

The questions for this section are with you now: halving and doubling both numbers, and checking by multiplication.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the sports day groups, the prize money and the water bottles — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Guess the Size First

756 learners in groups of 18. Round to friendly numbers: 800 and 20. 800 divided by 20 is 40.

So the answer is about 40. 42 makes sense. 420 does not.

For R936 among 12 classes, use 960 divided by 12, which is 80.

Keep the guess in mind and try the questions for this part: estimating.

## Subtopic: Split It into Easy Parts

Split the number being divided: 936 splits into 600 and 336. 600 divided by 12 is 50. 336 divided by 12 is 28. Together 78.

Or build up: 18 times 40 is 720. Two more 18s make 756. So 42.

Never split the number you divide by.

Hold the easy parts in mind and try this part's questions: breaking down and building up.

## Subtopic: Change It, Then Check It

Halve both: 936 divided by 12 is the same as 468 divided by 6. That is 78.

Double both: 945 divided by 15 is the same as 1 890 divided by 30. That is 63.

Multiply back to check: 78 times 12 is 936.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Estimate first with friendly numbers to know the size of the answer. Break down the number being divided into easy parts, or build up with multiples of the divisor. Halve both numbers or double both numbers to make a friendlier division. Never split the divisor. Check every answer by multiplying back.

Hold the multiply-back check in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: halving, doubling and checking.
