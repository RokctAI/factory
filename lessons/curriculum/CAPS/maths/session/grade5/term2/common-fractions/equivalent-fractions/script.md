# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers recognising and using equivalent forms of common fractions, with halves, quarters and eighths; thirds, sixths and twelfths; and fifths and tenths. The anchor is a birthday cake in George cut into halves, then quarters, then eighths, a box of 12 eggs, and a fraction wall with strips for halves to twelfths. The governing idea is that cutting every piece into the same number of smaller pieces changes the name of a fraction but not its size, so multiplying or dividing the top and bottom numbers by the same number gives an equivalent fraction. Recognising equivalent fractions, making them by multiplying and simplifying them by dividing, and using them to compare fractions are examinable.

## Subtopic: Same Amount, Different Names

A birthday cake is cut in half, and Zanele takes one half. Then her mother cuts each half into 2, so the cake is in quarters. Zanele's share is now 2 quarters. Cut each quarter into 2, and the cake is in eighths. Her share is 4 eighths. The amount of cake on her plate never changed, so 1/2, 2/4 and 4/8 are equal. Fractions that name the same amount are called equivalent fractions.

A fraction wall shows this at a glance. The strip for halves, the strip for quarters and the strip for eighths are lined up under the same whole. The end of 1/2 lines up exactly with the end of 2/4 and with the end of 4/8. In the same way, 1/3 lines up with 2/6 and with 4/12, and 1/5 lines up with 2/10.

The egg box shows twelfths. Half the box is 6 eggs, so 6/12 equals 1/2. A third of the box is 4 eggs, so 4/12 equals 1/3. A quarter of the box is 3 eggs, so 3/12 equals 1/4.

There is a pattern in every pair. In 1/2 and 4/8, both numbers have been multiplied by 4. In 1/3 and 4/12, both have been multiplied by 4. The pieces are 4 times smaller, so you need 4 times as many of them.

The questions for this section are with you now: recognising equivalent fractions on a fraction wall, with a cake and with an egg box.

## Subtopic: Making and Simplifying Equivalent Fractions

To make an equivalent fraction, multiply the top and the bottom by the same number. 3/4: multiply both by 2 to get 6/8, or both by 3 to get 9/12. Cutting every quarter into 3 pieces makes twelfths, and 3 quarters become 9 twelfths. 2/3: multiply both by 4 to get 8/12. 3/5: multiply both by 2 to get 6/10.

Simplifying goes the other way: divide the top and the bottom by the same number. 6/12: both 6 and 12 can be divided by 6, giving 1/2. 8/12: both can be divided by 4, giving 2/3. 4/10: both can be divided by 2, giving 2/5. A fraction is in its simplest form when no number bigger than 1 divides both the top and the bottom.

Only multiplying or dividing works. Adding the same number to the top and bottom does not keep the amount the same. 1/2 with 1 added to both becomes 2/3, but 2/3 is more than 1/2. Multiplying only the top changes the fraction too: 3/4 with only the top doubled becomes 6/4, which is more than a whole.

To find a missing number, see what the bottom was multiplied by, and do the same to the top. 3/4 equals how many twelfths? 4 times 3 is 12, so the top is 3 times 3, which is 9. 9/12.

The questions for this section are with you now: making equivalent fractions by multiplying, simplifying by dividing, and finding missing numbers.

## Subtopic: Using Equivalent Fractions

Equivalent fractions make comparing easier. Which is bigger, 2/3 or 3/4? Change both to twelfths. 2/3 is 8/12 and 3/4 is 9/12. Now the bottom numbers are the same, so compare the tops: 9 is more than 8, so 3/4 is bigger.

They also help with answers. If 6 of the 12 eggs are used, 6/12 of the box is used, which in simplest form is 1/2. If a pizza cut into 8 slices has 6 slices eaten, 6/8 of it is eaten, which is 3/4. In the last lesson, 10 cakes shared among 4 gave 2 and 2/4 each, and 2/4 simplifies to 1/2, so each person gets 2 and 1/2 cakes.

Comparing with a known fraction works well. Is 5/12 more or less than 1/3? 1/3 is 4/12, so 5/12 is a little more than a third. Is 4/6 equal to 1/2? Half of 6 is 3, so 1/2 is 3/6, and 4/6 is more than a half.

Being able to swap between equivalent names is a skill you will use for fractions for the rest of your schooling, especially when you add fractions with different bottom numbers in Grade 6.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: adding the same number to top and bottom, so 1/2 becomes 2/3; multiply or divide instead. Two: multiplying only the top, so 3/4 becomes 6/4; do the same to the bottom to get 6/8. Three: thinking 4/8 is bigger than 1/2 because its numbers are bigger; they are the same amount. Four: dividing the top and bottom by different numbers, so 6/12 becomes 3/4; divide both by 6 to get 1/2.

The questions for this section are with you now: comparing with equivalent fractions, simplifying answers, and comparing with a known fraction.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the birthday cake, the egg box and the fraction wall — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Cut the Pieces Smaller

Half a cake. Cut each half in two: 2 quarters. Cut again: 4 eighths. Same cake on the plate.

So 1/2, 2/4 and 4/8 are equal.

Half the egg box is 6/12. A third is 4/12.

Keep the cake in mind and try the questions for this part: equivalent fractions.

## Subtopic: Same Number Top and Bottom

Multiply the top and the bottom by the same number. 3/5 is 6/10. 3/4 is 9/12.

Divide the top and the bottom by the same number to simplify. 4/10 is 2/5. 6/12 is 1/2.

Never add. 1/2 is not 2/3.

Hold the same-number rule in mind and try this part's questions: making and simplifying.

## Subtopic: Same Bottom, Then Compare

Change to the same bottom number. 2/3 is 8/12 and 3/4 is 9/12. 3/4 is bigger.

1/3 is 4/12, so 5/12 is a bit more than a third.

6/8 of a pizza is 3/4 of the pizza.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Equivalent fractions name the same amount with different-sized pieces. Make them by multiplying the top and bottom by the same number, and simplify them by dividing the top and bottom by the same number. Never add the same number to both. To compare fractions with different bottom numbers, change them to equivalent fractions with the same bottom number and compare the tops.

Hold the same-bottom trick in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: using equivalent fractions.
