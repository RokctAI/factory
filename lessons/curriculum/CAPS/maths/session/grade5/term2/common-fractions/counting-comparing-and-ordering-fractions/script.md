# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers counting forwards and backwards in fractions, and comparing and ordering common fractions to at least twelfths. The anchor is an egg box that holds 12 eggs, a slab of chocolate with 8 blocks, and a 3 km fun run in Polokwane with a marker every quarter of a kilometre. The governing idea is that a fraction names equal pieces of a whole, that the bottom number tells you how many pieces the whole is cut into and the top number how many pieces you have, and that more pieces means smaller pieces. Counting in fractions past whole numbers, comparing fractions with the same bottom number or the same top number, and ordering fractions and mixed numbers on a number line are examinable.

## Subtopic: Counting in Fractions

In a fraction, the bottom number, the denominator, says how many equal pieces make a whole, and the top number, the numerator, says how many pieces we are counting. Each egg is one twelfth of the egg box, written 1/12. Five eggs are 5/12 of the box.

Counting in fractions means adding one piece at a time. The fun run has a marker every quarter of a kilometre: 1/4, 2/4, 3/4, and then 4/4, which is 1 whole kilometre. Keep counting: 1 and 1/4, 1 and 2/4, 1 and 3/4, 2, 2 and 1/4, and so on to 3. A whole number with a fraction, like 1 and 3/4, is called a mixed number.

You can count backwards too. Counting back in thirds from 3: 3, 2 and 2/3, 2 and 1/3, 2, 1 and 2/3, 1 and 1/3, 1, 2/3, 1/3, 0. Each step takes away one third. Notice that the bottom number never changes while you count; only the number of pieces changes.

Counting in twelfths with the egg box: 9/12, 10/12, 11/12, 12/12. Twelve twelfths fill the box, so 12/12 is 1 whole. The next count is 1 and 1/12, the start of a second box. Whenever the top number reaches the bottom number, you have made one more whole.

The questions for this section are with you now: counting forwards and backwards in fractions, and counting past a whole number.

## Subtopic: Comparing Fractions

When fractions have the same bottom number, the pieces are the same size, so the one with more pieces is bigger. On a slab of 8 blocks, 5/8 is more chocolate than 3/8. Compare the top numbers: 5 is more than 3, so 5/8 is greater than 3/8.

When fractions have the same top number, look at the size of the pieces. A slab cut into 3 pieces has bigger pieces than a slab cut into 5 pieces. So 1/3 is greater than 1/5, even though 3 is less than 5. The bigger the bottom number, the smaller each piece. In the same way, 3/4 is greater than 3/10, because quarters are bigger than tenths.

A half is a useful landmark. Half of the egg box is 6 eggs, which is 6/12. So 5/12 is less than a half and 7/12 is more than a half. A fraction is exactly a half when its top number is half of its bottom number, like 3/6, 4/8 or 6/12. Comparing each fraction with a half can settle many comparisons quickly.

Fractions can only be compared fairly when they are fractions of the same whole. Half of a large pizza is more food than half of a small pizza, even though both are halves. In mathematics questions, fractions are of the same whole unless the question says otherwise.

The questions for this section are with you now: comparing fractions with the same bottom number, with the same top number, and with a half.

## Subtopic: Ordering Fractions on a Number Line

Ordering fractions means arranging them from smallest to biggest, or biggest to smallest. Unit fractions, with a top number of 1, are ordered by their bottom numbers in reverse: 1/12, 1/6, 1/3, 1/2 from smallest to biggest. More pieces means smaller pieces, so 1/12 is the smallest.

Fractions with the same bottom number are ordered by their top numbers: 1/8, 5/8, 7/8 from smallest to biggest. For mixed numbers, compare the whole numbers first, then the fractions: 1 and 1/4, 1 and 3/4, 2 and 1/4. 2 and 1/4 is the biggest because it has 2 wholes.

A number line shows fractions in their places. Draw a line from 0 to 1 and mark it in quarters: 1/4, 2/4, 3/4. 3/4 sits between 1/2 and 1, closer to 1. On the fun-run line from 0 to 3 km, 2 and 1/4 km sits just past the 2 km mark. The further right a fraction sits, the bigger it is.

A fraction wall, with strips for halves, thirds, quarters, sixths, eighths and twelfths lined up under one whole, helps with harder comparisons. On the wall, 2/3 lines up with 8/12 and 3/4 lines up with 9/12, so 3/4 is a little bigger than 2/3.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: saying 1/8 is bigger than 1/4 because 8 is bigger than 4; more pieces means smaller pieces, so 1/4 is bigger. Two: counting in thirds and writing 1/3, 2/3, 3/3, 1/4, as if the bottom number changed; the bottom number stays the same, and after 3/3 comes 1 and 1/3. Three: comparing half of a small pizza with half of a big pizza and calling them equal; fractions compare fairly only for the same whole. Four: ordering by top numbers only and saying 3/12 is bigger than 2/3; 3/12 is a quarter, which is much smaller than 2/3.

The questions for this section are with you now: ordering unit fractions, ordering mixed numbers, and placing fractions on a number line.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the egg box, the chocolate slab and the fun run — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: One Piece at a Time

Count in quarters: 1/4, 2/4, 3/4, 4/4. 4/4 is one whole kilometre.

Keep going: 1 and 1/4, 1 and 2/4. The bottom number stays the same.

Back in thirds from 3: 3, 2 and 2/3, 2 and 1/3, 2.

Keep the one-piece step in mind and try the questions for this part: counting in fractions.

## Subtopic: Which Piece Is Bigger

Same bottom number? Look at the top. 5/8 beats 3/8.

Same top number? More pieces means smaller pieces. 1/3 beats 1/5.

Half the egg box is 6/12. 5/12 is less than a half. 7/12 is more.

Hold the more-pieces-smaller-pieces rule in mind and try this part's questions: comparing fractions.

## Subtopic: Line Them Up

Smallest to biggest: 1/12, 1/6, 1/3, 1/2.

Mixed numbers: look at the wholes first. 1 and 3/4 comes before 2 and 1/4.

On a number line, bigger fractions sit further to the right.

Pocket summary of the lesson. The bottom number says how many equal pieces make a whole; the top number says how many pieces you have. Count in fractions one piece at a time, and when the top reaches the bottom you have one more whole. With the same bottom number, compare the tops. With the same top number, the smaller bottom number gives bigger pieces. Compare with a half, and order mixed numbers by their wholes first.

Hold the number line in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: ordering fractions.
