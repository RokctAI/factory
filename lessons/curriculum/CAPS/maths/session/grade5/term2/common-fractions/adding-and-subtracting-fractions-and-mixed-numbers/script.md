# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers addition and subtraction of common fractions with the same denominators, and of mixed numbers. The anchor is a bakery in Paarl measuring flour in quarter cups, a pizza cut into 12 equal slices, and a family hiking in the Drakensberg who walk 2 and 3/8 km before lunch and 1 and 2/8 km after. The governing idea is that fractions with the same bottom number are pieces of the same size, so adding or subtracting them means adding or subtracting the top numbers while the bottom number stays the same, and that wholes can be swapped for pieces and pieces for wholes. Adding and subtracting fractions with the same denominator, writing answers bigger than 1 as mixed numbers, and adding and subtracting mixed numbers are examinable.

## Subtopic: Adding Fractions with the Same Bottom Number

When fractions have the same bottom number, the pieces are the same size, so adding them is just counting pieces. The baker uses 3/8 of a bag of flour in the morning and 2/8 in the afternoon. 3 eighths plus 2 eighths is 5 eighths: 3/8 plus 2/8 equals 5/8. Add the top numbers; keep the bottom number.

The bottom number does not change because the size of the pieces does not change. Eighths plus eighths are still eighths, in the same way that 3 apples plus 2 apples are 5 apples, not 5 double apples. Writing 5/16 would mean the bag had suddenly been cut into 16 pieces, which did not happen.

Sometimes the answer is more than a whole. A recipe uses 3/4 cup of milk for the dough and 3/4 cup for the glaze. 3/4 plus 3/4 is 6/4. Four quarters make 1 whole cup, and there are 2 quarters more, so 6/4 is 1 and 2/4 cups. When the top number is bigger than the bottom, take out the wholes.

Twelfths work the same way. Lerato eats 5/12 of the pizza and her brother eats 4/12. Together they eat 9/12. Three fractions can be added at once: 1/10 plus 4/10 plus 5/10 is 10/10, which is exactly 1 whole.

The questions for this section are with you now: adding fractions with the same bottom number, and answers that are a whole or more.

## Subtopic: Subtracting Fractions with the Same Bottom Number

Subtracting works the same way: take away the top numbers and keep the bottom number. The baker had 7/8 of a bag and used 3/8. 7/8 minus 3/8 is 4/8. The pieces are still eighths; there are just fewer of them.

To take a fraction away from a whole, write the whole as a fraction with the same bottom number. The pizza has 12 slices, so the whole pizza is 12/12. If 5/12 is eaten, the part left is 12/12 minus 5/12, which is 7/12. In the same way, 1 minus 3/8 is 8/8 minus 3/8, which is 5/8.

A whole and a part make the same whole again, so subtraction can be checked by adding. If 7/12 is left and 5/12 was eaten, then 7/12 plus 5/12 must be 12/12, the whole pizza. It is, so the answer is right.

Word problems often ask what is left or how much more. A jug holds 9/10 of a litre of juice and 4/10 of a litre is poured out: 9/10 minus 4/10 leaves 5/10 of a litre. Sipho read 7/12 of his book and Lerato read 4/12 of the same book: Sipho read 3/12 of the book more.

The questions for this section are with you now: subtracting fractions with the same bottom number, taking a fraction from a whole, and checking by adding.

## Subtopic: Adding and Subtracting Mixed Numbers

A mixed number has wholes and a fraction. To add mixed numbers, add the wholes and add the fractions. The hikers walk 2 and 3/8 km before lunch and 1 and 2/8 km after. Wholes: 2 plus 1 is 3. Fractions: 3/8 plus 2/8 is 5/8. They walk 3 and 5/8 km altogether.

If the fractions add up to more than a whole, carry the whole. 1 and 3/4 cups plus 2 and 2/4 cups: wholes 1 plus 2 is 3, fractions 3/4 plus 2/4 is 5/4. 5/4 is 1 and 1/4, so the total is 3 plus 1 and 1/4, which is 4 and 1/4 cups.

To subtract mixed numbers, subtract the wholes and subtract the fractions. 3 and 5/6 minus 1 and 2/6: wholes 3 minus 1 is 2, fractions 5/6 minus 2/6 is 3/6. The answer is 2 and 3/6. If the first fraction is too small, break one whole into pieces. 3 and 1/4 minus 1 and 3/4: 1/4 is less than 3/4, so change 3 and 1/4 into 2 and 5/4, swapping one whole for 4 quarters. Then 2 minus 1 is 1, and 5/4 minus 3/4 is 2/4. The answer is 1 and 2/4.

This is the same as exchanging in column subtraction. One whole is swapped for pieces, just as one ten is swapped for ten units. Check by adding: 1 and 2/4 plus 1 and 3/4 is 2 and 5/4, which is 3 and 1/4.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: adding the bottom numbers, so 3/8 plus 2/8 becomes 5/16; the pieces stay eighths, so it is 5/8. Two: taking 5/12 from 1 and writing 4/12; write the whole as 12/12 to get 7/12. Three: subtracting the smaller fraction from the bigger, so 3 and 1/4 minus 1 and 3/4 becomes 2 and 2/4; break a whole into quarters first to get 1 and 2/4. Four: leaving an answer as 3 and 5/4; 5/4 is more than a whole, so carry it to get 4 and 1/4.

The questions for this section are with you now: adding mixed numbers, carrying a whole, and subtracting mixed numbers by breaking a whole.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the flour, the pizza and the hike — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Count the Same Pieces

Same bottom number means same-size pieces. Add the tops. Keep the bottom.

3/8 plus 2/8 is 5/8. Not 5/16: the bag is still in eighths.

3/4 plus 3/4 is 6/4. Four quarters make 1 whole, so 6/4 is 1 and 2/4.

Keep the same pieces in mind and try the questions for this part: adding fractions.

## Subtopic: Take Some Pieces Away

Take away the tops. Keep the bottom. 7/8 minus 3/8 is 4/8.

A whole pizza is 12/12. Eat 5/12 and 7/12 is left.

Check: 7/12 plus 5/12 makes the whole pizza again.

Hold the whole-as-pieces idea in mind and try this part's questions: subtracting fractions.

## Subtopic: Wholes and Pieces Together

Add wholes with wholes and pieces with pieces. 2 and 3/8 plus 1 and 2/8 is 3 and 5/8 km.

Too many pieces? Make a whole. 3 and 5/4 is 4 and 1/4.

Too few pieces to take away? Break a whole. 3 and 1/4 becomes 2 and 5/4. Take away 1 and 3/4: 1 and 2/4.

Pocket summary of the lesson. With the same bottom number, add or subtract the top numbers and keep the bottom number. A whole is the same as the bottom number over itself, like 12/12. When an answer has more pieces than make a whole, take out the whole. For mixed numbers, work with wholes and pieces separately, carrying a whole when the pieces add up past one and breaking a whole when there are too few pieces to subtract.

Hold the swap between wholes and pieces in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: mixed numbers.
