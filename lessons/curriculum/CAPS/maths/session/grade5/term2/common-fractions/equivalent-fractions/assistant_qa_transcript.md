### subtopic_1 — Same Amount, Different Names
**Assistant:** How can 1/2 and 4/8 be equal when the numbers are different?

**Tutor:** Because the numbers describe pieces, and the pieces are different sizes. 1/2 is one big piece: the whole cut into 2. 4/8 is four small pieces: the whole cut into 8. Each eighth is a quarter of the size of a half, so you need 4 of them to cover the same amount. Put the four small pieces together and they fit exactly over the one big piece. It is like money: one R10 note and five R2 coins are different things to hold, but they are worth exactly the same. The amount is what matters, not how many pieces you have. That is why the word equivalent is used: it means equal in value. The numbers in the fraction tell you two things together, the size of each piece and how many pieces you have, and those two can change together without changing the amount. If the pieces become 4 times smaller, you need 4 times as many. On a fraction wall you can see it immediately, because the strips line up. So when you see 4/8, you can always ask whether it has a simpler name. Here the simpler name is 1/2, which is usually easier to picture and to work with.

### subtopic_2 — Making and Simplifying Equivalent Fractions
**Assistant:** Why can I multiply the top and bottom by the same number but not add the same number to both?

**Tutor:** Because multiplying the top and bottom by the same number matches what really happens when you cut pieces smaller, and adding does not. If every piece of a cake is cut into 3, the number of pieces in the whole triples, so the bottom number is multiplied by 3, and the number of pieces on your plate also triples, so the top number is multiplied by 3. The amount of cake is unchanged. That is why 3/4 equals 9/12. Adding has no such picture. If you add 1 to the top and bottom of 1/2, you get 2/3. That would mean the cake was cut into 3 instead of 2, and you took 2 pieces instead of 1. You now have two thirds of the cake, which is more than half; the amount has changed. A quick test with something you know shows the problem: 2/2 is a whole, but adding 1 to both gives 3/3, which is also a whole, so that one happens to work; yet 1/2 becoming 2/3 does not. A rule that only works sometimes cannot be trusted. Multiplying or dividing both numbers by the same number always works, for every fraction, every time.

### subtopic_3 — Using Equivalent Fractions
**Assistant:** Why do we simplify fractions if the bigger form is also correct?

**Tutor:** Because the simplest form is the easiest to understand and compare, and it is the form most people expect. If you tell a friend you ate 6/12 of a pizza, they have to stop and think, but if you say you ate half, they understand at once. Simple forms are easier to picture, easier to compare and easier to calculate with. Is 6/12 more than 2/5? That is hard to say. Is 1/2 more than 2/5? Much easier, because 2/5 is a bit less than 2 and a half fifths. In tests, an answer of 6/12 is usually marked correct, but a question that asks for the simplest form expects 1/2, so read the instructions. Simplifying is also a good check of your understanding: to simplify 8/12, you need to find a number that divides both 8 and 12, which is 4, and that uses your knowledge of factors from Term 1. The biggest such number, the highest common factor, simplifies the fraction in one step. You do not have to find it straight away, though. You can divide by 2 first, getting 4/6, and then by 2 again, getting 2/3. Keep going until nothing bigger than 1 divides both numbers.
