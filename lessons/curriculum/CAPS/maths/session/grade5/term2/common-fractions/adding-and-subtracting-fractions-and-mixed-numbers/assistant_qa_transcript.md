### subtopic_1 — Adding Fractions with the Same Bottom Number
**Assistant:** Why do we not add the bottom numbers as well?

**Tutor:** Because the bottom number is not an amount; it is the name of the size of the pieces. 3/8 means 3 pieces, each one eighth of the whole. When you add 2 more eighths, you have 5 pieces, and each piece is still one eighth. The size of the pieces has not changed, so the name has not changed. It works exactly like adding things with names: 3 rand plus 2 rand is 5 rand, not 5 double rand. 3 eighths plus 2 eighths is 5 eighths. If you added the bottom numbers and wrote 5/16, you would be saying that the pieces are now sixteenths, half the size of eighths, which would make the answer smaller than either of the amounts you started with. That cannot be right, because adding should give more, not less. A quick picture always settles it: draw a bar cut into 8 equal parts, shade 3, then shade 2 more. You will see 5 shaded parts out of 8, not out of 16. The bar did not grow more cuts just because you added. Later, when you add fractions with different bottom numbers, like halves and quarters, you will first change them into pieces of the same size, and then add the tops in exactly the same way.

### subtopic_2 — Subtracting Fractions with the Same Bottom Number
**Assistant:** How do I take a fraction away from a whole number like 1 or 3?

**Tutor:** Write the whole number as pieces of the same size as the fraction you are taking away, and then subtract the tops. A whole pizza cut into 12 slices is 12 twelfths, or 12/12. If 5/12 is eaten, think of it as 12 slices take away 5 slices, which leaves 7 slices, so 7/12 is left. For 1 minus 3/8, the whole is 8/8, and 8/8 minus 3/8 is 5/8. With bigger whole numbers, you only need to break up one of the wholes. 3 minus 3/8 is the same as 2 and 8/8 minus 3/8, which is 2 and 5/8. You keep two of the wholes as they are and break only one into eighths. A useful check is to add your answer back to the fraction you took away: 2 and 5/8 plus 3/8 is 2 and 8/8, which is 3. A common slip is to subtract the top number from the whole number, as in 1 minus 5/12 giving 4/12. That treats the whole as if it were 1 slice instead of 12 slices. Always ask how many pieces make the whole before you subtract.

### subtopic_3 — Adding and Subtracting Mixed Numbers
**Assistant:** Why do I sometimes need to break up a whole when subtracting mixed numbers?

**Tutor:** Because sometimes there are not enough pieces in the first fraction to take away the pieces in the second, just as sometimes there are not enough units to subtract in column subtraction. In 3 and 1/4 minus 1 and 3/4, you need to take away 3 quarters, but you only have 1 quarter. You do have 3 wholes, though, and a whole can be swapped for 4 quarters without changing the amount. So 3 and 1/4 becomes 2 and 5/4: two wholes, plus the 4 quarters from the broken whole, plus the 1 quarter you already had. Now you have enough quarters. 5/4 minus 3/4 is 2/4, and 2 minus 1 is 1, so the answer is 1 and 2/4. Think of a baker with 3 full bags of flour and a quarter of a bag. To use 1 and 3/4 bags, the baker opens one full bag. The trap is to subtract the smaller fraction from the bigger one, getting 2 and 2/4. That answer is too big, and a check by adding shows it: 2 and 2/4 plus 1 and 3/4 is 4 and 1/4, not 3 and 1/4. Breaking a whole is the fraction version of exchanging a ten for ten units.
