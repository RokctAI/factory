### subtopic_1 — Unit Fractions of Amounts
**Assistant:** Why do I divide by the bottom number to find a fraction of an amount?

**Tutor:** Because the bottom number tells you how many equal parts the whole is cut into, and dividing is how you cut an amount into equal parts. A quarter of something means one of 4 equal parts. To find out how big each of those parts is when the whole is 36 learners, you share the 36 learners into 4 equal groups, and that is exactly what 36 divided by 4 does. Each group has 9, so one quarter of 36 is 9. If the fraction were a twelfth, you would share into 12 groups, and each group would be smaller: one twelfth of 36 is 3. That matches what you know about fractions: the bigger the bottom number, the smaller each piece. The top number then tells you how many of those equal parts you want. For 3/4, you want 3 of the 4 groups, so 3 times 9, which is 27. You can always check with a picture: draw the amount as dots, draw as many circles as the bottom number, share the dots out, and count the dots in as many circles as the top number. For big amounts the picture would take too long, but the division and multiplication do the same job in two quick steps.

### subtopic_2 — Other Fractions of Amounts
**Assistant:** Why should 3/4 of 36 always be less than 36?

**Tutor:** Because 3/4 is less than one whole, and taking less than the whole of something must leave you with less than you started with. Three quarters of the class is most of the class, but not all of it; one quarter of the learners is missing. So the answer must be somewhere between 0 and 36, and since 3/4 is more than a half, it must be more than 18, half of 36. Our answer, 27, sits nicely between 18 and 36, so it makes sense. This gives you a powerful quick check. If you work out 3/4 of 36 and get 48, you know straight away that something has gone wrong, because 48 is more than the whole class. In fact, 48 comes from dividing by 3 and multiplying by 4, which is the operations the wrong way around. The same check works for any fraction less than 1. A fraction equal to 1, like 4/4, gives exactly the whole amount, and a fraction bigger than 1, like 5/4, gives more than the amount. So before calculating, ask whether the fraction is less than, equal to, or more than one whole, and you will know roughly what kind of answer to expect.

### subtopic_3 — Fraction Problems: The Part and the Rest
**Assistant:** How do I know whether the question wants the part or what is left?

**Tutor:** Read the last sentence of the question very carefully, because the same numbers can lead to two different answers, and both will look reasonable. If 3/4 of 36 learners walk to school, a question asking how many walk wants 27. A question asking how many do not walk wants the rest, 9. With the jacket, a question asking how much is taken off wants R60, the discount, while a question asking how much you pay wants R180, the new price. Underline the words that tell you which part is wanted, such as walk, do not walk, left, remaining, off, discount, new price or pay. Then, after you work out the fraction, ask yourself whether that is the part the question is about. If it is not, you need one more step: subtract it from the whole, or use the fraction that is left over. For the rest, you can either subtract, 36 minus 27 is 9, or use the remaining fraction, 1/4 of 36 is 9. Doing it both ways is a great check, because the two answers must match. Finally, check that the part and the rest add back to the whole: 27 plus 9 is 36, and R60 plus R180 is R240.
