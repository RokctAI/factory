# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers determining input values, output values and rules for numeric patterns using flow diagrams and tables, and determining the equivalence of rules given in words, in a flow diagram, in a table and by a number sentence. The anchor is a vetkoek stall at a primary school in East London, where each vetkoek costs R6 and orders delivered to a classroom cost R5 extra. The governing idea is that one rule can be shown in four ways, and a table is simply a neat record of many trips through a flow diagram. Finding outputs, finding inputs, finding rules from tables, and matching the four kinds of description are examinable.

## Subtopic: Flow Diagrams with Many Inputs

At the stall, each vetkoek costs R6. A flow diagram for the price has one operation box: times 6. Instead of putting in one number at a time, you can list several inputs on the left, such as 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5, and the matching outputs on the right: 6, 12, 18, 24 and 30. Each input is joined to its own output.

Orders delivered to a classroom cost R5 extra. Now the flow diagram has two boxes: times 6, then plus 5. An order of 3 vetkoek gives 18 after the first box and 23 after the second, so it costs R23. An order of 8 gives 48, then 53, so it costs R53.

Some flow diagrams have missing inputs. A teacher pays R47 for a delivered order. To find how many vetkoek she ordered, work backwards with the opposite operations in the opposite order: 47 minus 5 is 42, and 42 divided by 6 is 7. She ordered 7 vetkoek. Check forwards: 7 times 6 is 42, and 42 plus 5 is 47.

A flow diagram can also have a missing rule. If inputs 2, 3 and 4 give outputs 14, 21 and 28, every output is 7 times its input, so the missing box is times 7.

The questions for this section are with you now: finding outputs, missing inputs and missing rules in flow diagrams.

## Subtopic: Input and Output Tables

A table shows the same information as a flow diagram, but in rows. The top row holds the inputs and the bottom row holds the outputs. For delivered orders, the top row could be 1, 2, 3, 4 and 10, and the bottom row R11, R17, R23, R29 and R65.

To fill a gap in a table, use the rule on that input. For an input of 6: 6 times 6 is 36, and 36 plus 5 is 41, so the gap is R41. Be careful when the inputs do not go up by 1. The jump from 4 to 10 is not one step, so you cannot just add R6 to R29. Use the rule: 10 times 6 is 60, and 60 plus 5 is 65.

To find a rule from a table, compare how much the outputs grow with how much the inputs grow. A learner records: inputs 2, 4, 6, 8 and outputs 7, 13, 19, 25. The inputs go up by 2 and the outputs go up by 6. So each 1 more in the input adds 3 to the output, and the rule starts with times 3. Test input 2: 2 times 3 is 6, but the output is 7, so add 1. The rule is times 3, then plus 1. Check input 8: 8 times 3 is 24, and 24 plus 1 is 25. It works.

Tables are useful for spotting patterns quickly. In the vetkoek table, every output ends in 1, 7, 3, 9, 5 and then repeats, because adding 6 each time changes the last digit in a cycle.

The questions for this section are with you now: completing tables and finding rules from tables.

## Subtopic: Words, Diagrams, Tables and Number Sentences

The delivery rule can be shown in four ways. In words: multiply the number of vetkoek by 6 and then add 5 rand. As a flow diagram: input, times 6 box, plus 5 box, output. As a table: 1, 2, 3 on top and 11, 17, 23 below. As a number sentence: number of vetkoek times 6 plus 5 equals cost in rand, so for 4 vetkoek, 4 times 6 plus 5 equals 29.

These four descriptions are equivalent because every one gives the same output for the same input. To check whether a table matches a rule, test each column. The table 1, 2, 3 above 11, 17, 23 matches times 6 then plus 5. The table 1, 2, 3 above 11, 16, 21 does not, because input 2 should give 17.

A number sentence can also hide the input. If 6 times a number plus 5 equals 35, then the number is 5, because 35 minus 5 is 30 and 30 divided by 6 is 5. This is the same as working backwards through the flow diagram.

When you write a number sentence, put the steps in the same order as the flow diagram, and say what each number stands for.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: adding R6 to R29 to fill the column for 10 vetkoek, getting R35 instead of R65. Two: forgetting the delivery box, so 3 delivered vetkoek cost R18 instead of R23. Three: using the output jump without dividing by the input jump, so the table 2, 4, 6 above 7, 13, 19 is called times 6. Four: dividing first when working backwards, so 47 divided by 6 gives a remainder and no sensible answer.

The questions for this section are with you now: matching words, flow diagrams, tables and number sentences.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the vetkoek stall in East London — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Many Numbers Through One Machine

R6 each: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 vetkoek cost R6, R12, R18, R24, R30.

Delivered: times 6, then plus 5. 3 vetkoek cost R23.

Backwards: R47 minus 5 is 42, and 42 divided by 6 is 7 vetkoek.

Keep the two-box machine in mind and try the questions for this part: flow diagrams.

## Subtopic: Read the Table

Top row: vetkoek. Bottom row: cost. 1, 2, 3, 4 give R11, R17, R23, R29.

For 10 vetkoek, use the rule, not the jump: 10 times 6 is 60, plus 5 is 65.

Inputs go up 2, outputs go up 6: so times 3, then fix the start.

Hold the use-the-rule idea in mind and try this part's questions: tables.

## Subtopic: Four Ways, One Rule

Words, flow diagram, table, number sentence. 4 times 6 plus 5 equals 29.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A flow diagram takes inputs through the boxes in order to give outputs. A table is a neat list of inputs and their outputs. To fill a gap, use the rule on that input, especially when the inputs jump. To find a rule, compare the output jump with the input jump, then fix the start, and test every column. To find an input, work backwards with opposite operations in the opposite order. Words, flow diagrams, tables and number sentences are equivalent when they give the same output for every input.

Hold the four-ways idea in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: matching descriptions.
