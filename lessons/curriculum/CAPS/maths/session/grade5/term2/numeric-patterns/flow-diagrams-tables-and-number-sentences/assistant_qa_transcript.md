### subtopic_1 — Flow Diagrams with Many Inputs
**Assistant:** Why can I not just add the same amount to get the next number in a table?

**Tutor:** You can only do that when the inputs go up by 1 each time. In the vetkoek table, the inputs 1, 2, 3, 4 go up by 1 and the outputs go up by 6, because each extra vetkoek costs R6. But tables often skip inputs to save space. If the next input after 4 is 10, that is 6 more vetkoek, not 1 more. Adding R6 to R29 would give R35, which is the price of 5 delivered vetkoek, not 10. The safe way is always to put the new input through the rule: 10 times 6 is 60, and 60 plus 5 is 65, so 10 delivered vetkoek cost R65. You can also use the jump if you are careful: the input jumped by 6, so the output jumps by 6 times 6, which is 36, and R29 plus R36 is R65. Both ways give the same answer. Before filling any gap, look at the top row and ask how far the input has moved. A quick glance at the inputs saves many mistakes in tables.

### subtopic_2 — Input and Output Tables
**Assistant:** How do I find a rule from a table when the inputs go up by 2?

**Tutor:** Find how much the output grows for each 1 the input grows, and then fix the start. In the table with inputs 2, 4, 6, 8 and outputs 7, 13, 19, 25, the inputs go up by 2 and the outputs go up by 6. Six shared over the 2 steps of the input is 3 for each step, so the rule begins with times 3. Now test the first column: 2 times 3 is 6, but the output is 7. The difference is 1, so the rule is times 3, then plus 1. Test every other column too: 4 times 3 plus 1 equals 13, 6 times 3 plus 1 equals 19, and 8 times 3 plus 1 equals 25. All of them work, so the rule is correct. A common slip is to see the output jump of 6 and say the rule is times 6. Test it: 2 times 6 is 12, already bigger than 7, so times 6 cannot be right. The test on every column is what makes you sure. If the inputs go up by 5, divide the output jump by 5 in the same way.

### subtopic_3 — Words, Diagrams, Tables and Number Sentences
**Assistant:** What does it mean that a number sentence and a table are equivalent?

**Tutor:** It means they describe exactly the same rule, so they give the same output for every input. The number sentence number of vetkoek times 6 plus 5 equals cost in rand is a short way of writing the whole rule. The table lists some examples of that rule: 1 gives 11, 2 gives 17, 3 gives 23. To check that they are equivalent, put each input from the table into the number sentence. 1 times 6 plus 5 equals 11, 2 times 6 plus 5 equals 17, and 3 times 6 plus 5 equals 23. Every column matches, so they are equivalent. The flow diagram and the words say the same thing again: times 6, then add 5. Each kind of description is useful for a different job. Words are easy to say aloud. A flow diagram shows the order of the steps clearly. A table shows many answers at once and helps you see the pattern. A number sentence is short and is the start of the algebra you will learn later. Being able to move between them means you really understand the rule.
