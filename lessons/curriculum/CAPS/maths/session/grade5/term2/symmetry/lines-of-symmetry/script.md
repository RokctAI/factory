# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers recognising, drawing and describing lines of symmetry in 2D shapes. The anchor is a Grade 5 class in Soweto preparing for Youth Day: they cut paper shapes for a display, design posters with bold letters and finish half-drawn patterns on grid paper. The governing idea is that a line of symmetry splits a shape into two halves that match exactly when folded along the line, so that each half is the mirror image of the other. Finding lines of symmetry, counting how many a shape has, and completing symmetrical drawings are examinable.

## Subtopic: What a Line of Symmetry Is

A learner folds a sheet of paper in half, cuts a curve along the fold side, and opens it out. The cut-out is a heart shape, and the fold line runs straight down its middle. The two halves match exactly. That fold line is a line of symmetry.

A line of symmetry is a line that divides a shape into two parts that fit exactly on top of each other when the shape is folded along the line. Each half is a mirror image of the other: if you stand a small mirror on the line, the half you can see plus its reflection makes the whole shape.

The fold test is the surest way to check. Cut out the shape, fold it along the line you are testing, and look. If every edge lines up, the line is a line of symmetry. If any part sticks out, it is not.

A line of symmetry can run up and down, across, or on a slant. A square has lines in all three directions. A line that only cuts a shape into two parts of the same size is not always a line of symmetry: the two parts must also be mirror images.

A mirror helps when you cannot fold. A learner stands a small mirror upright on the line down the middle of a poster letter A. Half the letter and its reflection show the whole A, so the line is a line of symmetry. On the letter F, no position of the mirror shows the whole letter.

The questions for this section are with you now: recognising lines of symmetry with the fold test and the mirror test.

## Subtopic: How Many Lines?

Shapes can have no lines of symmetry, one, or many. The class tests their cut-outs and records the results.

A square has 4 lines of symmetry: one up and down, one across, and two along the diagonals from corner to corner. A rectangle that is not a square has only 2: one up and down and one across. Its diagonals are not lines of symmetry. If you fold a rectangle along a diagonal, the corners do not meet, because the long and short sides do not match.

A triangle with three equal sides has 3 lines of symmetry, each from a corner to the middle of the opposite side. A triangle with exactly two equal sides has 1 line. A triangle with no equal sides has none. A regular pentagon has 5 lines and a regular hexagon has 6, so a regular polygon has as many lines of symmetry as it has sides.

A circle has more lines of symmetry than you could ever draw: any straight line through its centre is a line of symmetry. A kite has 1 line, through the corners where the equal sides meet.

Find the number of lines by testing every direction. For a square, test up and down, across and both diagonals: all 4 work. For a rectangle, test the same 4: only up and down and across work. Draw each line you find on a sketch of the shape.

The questions for this section are with you now: counting lines of symmetry in squares, rectangles, triangles, regular polygons and circles.

## Subtopic: Drawing Symmetrical Shapes

For the posters, the class looks at capital letters. A has 1 line, up and down. B, C, D and E have 1 line, across. H and X have 2 lines, one up and down and one across. F, G, J, N, S and Z have none.

To complete a symmetrical drawing on grid paper, use the line of symmetry as a mirror. Each corner on one side has a partner on the other side, the same number of squares away from the line, directly across from it. If a corner is 3 squares to the left of the line, its partner is 3 squares to the right on the same row. Mark all the partner corners first, then join them in the same order as the first half.

A corner that sits on the line of symmetry is its own partner and stays where it is. When the drawing is finished, check it with the fold test or a mirror.

To describe the symmetry of a shape, say how many lines it has and where they are, for example: a rectangle has 2 lines of symmetry, one up and down through the middle and one across through the middle.

Some poster designs have two lines of symmetry, one up and down and one across. Such a design can be completed from just one quarter: reflect the quarter across one line to make a half, then reflect the half across the other line to make the whole.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: saying a rectangle's diagonals are lines of symmetry; folding shows the corners do not meet. Two: saying the letter S has a line of symmetry because both halves look alike; the halves are turned, not mirrored. Three: drawing a partner corner 3 squares from the line when the first corner is 4 squares away; count the squares from the line. Four: saying a circle has 1 or 2 lines; every line through the centre is a line of symmetry.

The questions for this section are with you now: letters, completing symmetrical shapes on grid paper, and describing symmetry.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the Youth Day cut-outs and posters — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Fold and Match

Fold the shape along a line. If the two halves match exactly, that line is a line of symmetry.

Each half is the mirror image of the other.

Keep the fold test in mind and try the questions for this part: finding a line of symmetry.

## Subtopic: Count the Lines

Square: 4. Rectangle: 2, not the diagonals. Equal-sided triangle: 3.

Regular hexagon: 6. Circle: more than you can draw.

Hold the fold test for each line in mind and try this part's questions: how many lines.

## Subtopic: Mirror on the Grid

Each corner has a partner, the same number of squares from the line on the other side.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A line of symmetry divides a shape into two halves that match exactly when folded, like mirror images. Test a line by folding or with a mirror. A square has 4 lines, a rectangle 2, a triangle with three equal sides 3, a regular polygon as many as its sides, and a circle more than you can draw. Some shapes and letters have none. To complete a symmetrical drawing, put each partner corner the same number of squares from the line on the other side, then join the corners in order.

Hold the same-distance-from-the-line rule in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: drawing symmetrical shapes.
