### subtopic_1 — What a Line of Symmetry Is
**Assistant:** Why are the diagonals of a rectangle not lines of symmetry?

**Tutor:** Because when you fold a rectangle along a diagonal, the two halves do not land exactly on top of each other. Cut out a rectangle, such as one 8 centimetres long and 4 centimetres wide, and fold it so that one corner goes to the opposite corner along the diagonal. You will see two pointed flaps sticking out past the edges. The diagonal does cut the rectangle into two triangles of the same size, so it is easy to think it must be a line of symmetry. But a line of symmetry needs more than two equal parts. The parts must be mirror images, so that folding puts every edge on top of a matching edge. In a rectangle, folding along a diagonal would have to put a long side on top of a short side, and they cannot match. In a square, all four sides are the same length, so folding along a diagonal puts a side on top of an equal side, and the halves match perfectly. That is why a square has 4 lines of symmetry but a rectangle has only 2. Whenever you are unsure, the fold test gives the true answer.

### subtopic_2 — How Many Lines?
**Assistant:** How do I complete a symmetrical shape on grid paper?

**Tutor:** Treat the line of symmetry as a mirror and find a partner for every corner. Start with one corner of the half that is drawn. Count how many squares it is from the line of symmetry, going straight across to the line. Then count the same number of squares straight across on the other side, and mark a dot. That dot is the partner corner. For example, if the line goes up and down and a corner is 3 squares to its left, the partner is 3 squares to its right, on the same row. Do this for every corner. Corners that are on the line already are their own partners. Then join the new dots in the same order as the corners on the first side, using a ruler. The finished shape should look like the first half seen in a mirror. Check by counting a few distances again, or by folding the paper along the line and holding it up to the light: the two halves should line up. A common slip is to copy the first half without flipping it, so that it points the same way instead of the mirror way. Counting from the line for each corner prevents that.

### subtopic_3 — Drawing Symmetrical Shapes
**Assistant:** Does every shape have a line of symmetry?

**Tutor:** No. Many shapes have none at all. A triangle with three sides of different lengths has no line of symmetry, because however you fold it, the halves never match. A parallelogram that is not a rectangle has none either. Some capital letters, such as F, G, J, N, S and Z, have none. The letter S is a good example to think about: its top half and bottom half look alike, but one is turned around, not mirrored, so folding does not make them match. Other shapes have many lines. A square has 4, a regular hexagon has 6, and a circle has more than you could ever draw, because every straight line through the centre works. So the number of lines of symmetry can be 0, 1, 2, 3, 4 or more, depending on the shape. When you describe a shape, give the number and say where the lines are. If you find none, try every direction before you decide, including the slanted lines from corner to corner, because those are easy to miss.
