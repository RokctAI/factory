### subtopic_1 — Reading and Writing Six-Digit Numbers
**Assistant:** Why do we write big numbers with a space and not a comma like I saw in a book?

**Tutor:** Both ways are trying to do the same job, which is to make a long number easy to read by breaking it into groups of three digits. Different countries made different choices. In South Africa, and in many other countries, the comma is used as the decimal comma, the mark that separates whole numbers from parts of a whole, like R12,50. If we also used a comma to group the thousands, a number like 245,318 could be read as two hundred and forty-five thousand three hundred and eighteen, or as two hundred and forty-five and a bit. That is a dangerous mix-up with money. So our textbooks, newspapers and tests use a space instead: 245 318. Some books from other countries, and some calculators and computers, use a comma for grouping, and you may also see numbers written with no gap at all. When you read those, count the digits from the right in threes to find the groups. When you write, always use the space, and always group from the right. The group on the right must have exactly three digits, so 2 453 18 is wrong and 245 318 is right. The groups also match the way we speak: the left group is said with the word thousand, and later, when you meet seven-digit numbers, a third group will be said with the word million.

### subtopic_2 — Comparing Six-Digit Numbers
**Assistant:** If a number starts with a 9, is it always bigger than one that starts with a 1?

**Tutor:** Only when the two numbers have the same number of digits, and that is exactly why the first step is to count the digits. 99 999 starts with a 9 and 100 000 starts with a 1, but 100 000 is bigger, because it has six digits and 99 999 has only five. The first digit of a six-digit number is worth hundreds of thousands, while the first digit of a five-digit number is worth only tens of thousands, so the two first digits are not doing the same job and cannot be compared directly. Think of it like money: one R100 note is worth more than nine R10 notes, even though nine is a bigger number than one. Once you know the numbers have the same length, then yes, a bigger first digit settles it, and you can stop. 254 138 is bigger than 189 950 straight away because 2 hundred thousands beats 1 hundred thousand. If the first digits match, you move one step right and compare again. A good habit is to write the numbers one under the other, lined up on the right, so that matching places sit in the same column. Then any difference in length shows up at once on the left.

### subtopic_3 — Ordering and Representing on a Number Line
**Assistant:** Why do I need a number line if I can just compare the digits?

**Tutor:** Comparing digits tells you which number is bigger, but a number line also tells you by how much, and that is often what matters in real life. 245 318 and 245 381 are in a different order from 254 138, but on a number line you can see that the first two are nearly the same, only 63 apart, while 254 138 is roughly 9 000 further along. If those were visitors to game reserves, the first two reserves had almost the same year, and the third had a much busier one. The digit rule cannot show you that picture. A number line is also how you find numbers in between, round numbers, and estimate. Rounding, which comes next in this topic, is really a question about which marked point on a number line a number is closest to. Number lines also help with counting on and back: 1 000 more than 245 318 is one jump of 1 000 along the line to 246 318, and 1 less than 300 000 is one tiny step back to 299 999. When you draw a number line, choose the marks to suit your numbers. For numbers around 245 000, marks every 1 000 work well; marks every 1 would need a line hundreds of kilometres long.
