### subtopic_1 — Rounding to the Nearest 5 and 10
**Assistant:** Why do we need to round to the nearest 5? Isn't the nearest 10 good enough?

**Tutor:** Sometimes the nearest 10 is good enough, but sometimes it is too rough, and the nearest 5 gives a closer answer that is still easy to work with. Think about where fives appear in daily life. Clock faces are marked in five-minute steps, so people say a bus leaves at about twenty-five past, not at about twenty past or half past. Prices are often set at amounts ending in 5 or 0, like R45 or R95, because they are easy to pay with R5 coins and R10 notes. Rulers and measuring jugs often have a longer mark at every 5. In each case the nearest 5 is half as rough as the nearest 10: rounding to the nearest 10 can move a number by up to 5, but rounding to the nearest 5 never moves it by more than 2. So 21 367 becomes 21 365, only 2 away, instead of 21 370, 3 away. There is another good reason. Multiples of 5 are easy to calculate with, because they are halfway between multiples of 10. Adding R45 and R35 in your head is easy, and doubling R65 is easy. When the question asks for the nearest 5, choose the closest number ending in 0 or 5. When it asks for the nearest 10, choose the closest number ending in 0. Read the question every time, because both kinds will appear.

### subtopic_2 — Rounding to the Nearest 100 and 1 000
**Assistant:** When I round 39 960 to the nearest 100, why do so many digits change at once?

**Tutor:** Because the rounding fills up more than one place at the same time. The tens digit is 6, so you round up to the next hundred. The number of hundreds in 39 960 is 399, and one more hundred makes 400 hundreds. But 400 hundreds is 40 thousands, and 40 thousands is 4 ten thousands. So the hundreds place, the thousands place and the ten thousands place all change together, and the answer is 40 000. A car's distance meter shows the same thing. When it reads 39 999 kilometres and the car drives one more kilometre, every digit rolls over at once to 40 000. Nothing strange has happened; each place reached ten and passed one to the place on its left. A good way to avoid mistakes is to find the two hundreds that the number sits between before you decide. 39 960 sits between 39 900 and 40 000. It is 60 away from 39 900 and only 40 away from 40 000, so it is closer to 40 000. Writing the two neighbours first also stops the common mistake of writing something like 39 1000, which is not a proper number at all. Every rounded answer must be a multiple of the place you are rounding to, so to the nearest 100 it must end in two zeros.

### subtopic_3 — Rounding to Estimate
**Assistant:** When I estimate, how do I know how much to round?

**Tutor:** Ask yourself what the estimate is for. If you only want to check that an exact answer is sensible, round so that each number has one or two digits that are not zero, because then the mental work is easy. 21 367 plus 18 842 becomes 21 000 plus 19 000, which is 40 000, and you can see at once that 40 209 is reasonable and 4 020 is not. If you need an estimate that is closer to the truth, for example to decide whether R11 000 in your purse is enough for a fridge and a stove, round more finely. To the nearest R1 000 the fridge and stove are about R11 000, which might make you think the money is just enough. To the nearest R100 they are about R11 100, which shows you would be short. The exact total, R11 143, confirms it. So rough rounding is quick but can hide a problem, and finer rounding is slower but safer. For multiplication, round each number to something easy to multiply: 32 times R185 becomes 30 times R200. Remember that rounding one number up and the other down can make errors cancel, while rounding both up makes the estimate a little too big. That is fine, as long as you know the estimate is only a guide.
