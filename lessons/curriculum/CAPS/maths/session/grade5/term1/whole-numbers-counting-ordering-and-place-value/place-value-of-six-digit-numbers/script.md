# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the place value of digits in whole numbers to at least six digits. The anchor is 136 482 learners writing a provincial test in KwaZulu-Natal and a house in Gqeberha priced at R765 430. The governing idea is that each place is worth ten times the place to its right, so the value of a digit depends on where it sits. Naming the place and value of a digit, writing numbers in expanded notation, building numbers from their parts, and making the biggest and smallest numbers from digit cards are examinable.

## Subtopic: Six Places and Their Values

A six-digit number has six places. Starting from the right they are units, tens, hundreds, thousands, ten thousands and hundred thousands. Each place is worth ten times the place to its right: 10 units make 1 ten, 10 tens make 1 hundred, 10 hundreds make 1 thousand, 10 thousands make 1 ten thousand and 10 ten thousands make 1 hundred thousand.

Look at 136 482. The 1 is in the hundred thousands place, the 3 in the ten thousands, the 6 in the thousands, the 4 in the hundreds, the 8 in the tens and the 2 in the units. The digit 3 looks small, but its value is 30 000, because it sits in the ten thousands place.

The same digit can have a different value in a different number. In R765 430 the 6 sits in the ten thousands place, so it is worth R60 000. In 136 482 the 6 sits in the thousands place, so it is worth only 6 000. One place further left means ten times as much. The seat, not the digit, decides the value.

Zero holds a place even though it is worth nothing. R765 430 has 0 units. If we left the 0 out we would write 76 543, which is about ten times smaller. Every zero inside or at the end of a number keeps the other digits in their correct seats.

The questions for this section are with you now: naming the six places, finding the value of a digit, and the job of zero.

## Subtopic: Expanded Notation and Building Numbers

Expanded notation writes a number as the sum of the values of its digits. 136 482 is 100 000 plus 30 000 plus 6 000 plus 400 plus 80 plus 2. R765 430 is 700 000 plus 60 000 plus 5 000 plus 400 plus 30. There is no part for the 0, because zero units adds nothing.

Building a number from its parts is the same job in reverse, and the parts can come in any order. 4 000 plus 300 000 plus 9 plus 50: put each part in its seat. 3 hundred thousands, 0 ten thousands, 4 thousands, 0 hundreds, 5 tens, 9 units. The number is 304 059. The two zeros are essential; without them you would write 3 459, which is a completely different number.

There is a difference between the thousands digit and the number of thousands. The thousands digit of 765 430 is 5, but the number contains 765 whole thousands. If the house price were paid in R1 000 notes, it would take 765 notes, with R430 still to pay in smaller money. In the same way 136 482 contains 1 364 whole hundreds.

Numbers given in words can be written in expanded notation straight away. Two hundred and eight thousand and seventy is 200 000 plus 8 000 plus 70, which is 208 070. Writing the parts first stops you from losing a zero.

The questions for this section are with you now: writing numbers in expanded notation, building numbers from parts in any order, and counting whole thousands.

## Subtopic: Changing Digits and Digit Cards

Adding or subtracting a place value changes just one digit, unless a place rolls over. 136 482 plus 10 000 is 146 482: only the ten thousands digit changes. 136 482 plus 100 is 136 582. 136 482 minus 1 000 is 135 482. But 136 482 plus 4 000 is 140 482, because 6 thousands and 4 thousands make 10 thousands, which is one more ten thousand.

To change one digit on purpose, add or subtract the difference in that place. To turn 765 430 into 795 430, the ten thousands digit goes from 6 to 9, which is 3 ten thousands more, so add 30 000.

Digit cards test place value. With the cards 4, 0, 7, 2, 9 and 5, the biggest number puts the biggest digit in the place worth the most: 975 420. The smallest number puts the smallest digit first, but a six-digit number cannot start with 0, or it would only have five digits. So 2 goes first, then 0, then the rest in order: 204 579.

A place value chart, or an abacus with six rods, shows all of this. Each column can hold the digits 0 to 9. When a column reaches ten, the ten are exchanged for one in the next column to the left. That exchange is exactly what happens when 136 482 plus 4 000 rolls over to 140 482.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: saying the 3 in 136 482 is worth 3; it sits in the ten thousands place, so it is worth 30 000. Two: building 300 000 plus 4 000 plus 50 plus 9 as 34 059; keep the empty places and write 304 059. Three: making the smallest number from the cards as 024 579; a six-digit number cannot start with 0, so it is 204 579. Four: saying 765 430 has only 5 thousands; the thousands digit is 5, but there are 765 whole thousands.

The questions for this section are with you now: adding place values, changing one digit, and the biggest and smallest numbers from digit cards.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the test writers and the house in Gqeberha — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Every Digit Has a Seat

From the right the seats are units, tens, hundreds, thousands, ten thousands and hundred thousands. Each seat is worth ten times the seat on its right.

In 136 482 the 3 sits in the ten thousands seat, so it is worth 30 000.

The 6 in R765 430 is worth R60 000. The 6 in 136 482 is worth 6 000. Same digit, different seat, different value.

Keep the six seats in mind and try the questions for this part: place and value.

## Subtopic: Break It Up, Build It Back

Break it up: 136 482 is 100 000 plus 30 000 plus 6 000 plus 400 plus 80 plus 2.

Build it back by putting each part in its seat. 300 000 plus 4 000 plus 50 plus 9 is 304 059. Empty seats get zeros.

R765 430 in R1 000 notes is 765 notes, with R430 left over.

Hold the break-up habit in mind and try this part's questions: expanded notation.

## Subtopic: Swap a Digit, Make a Number

Add 10 000 and only the ten thousands digit grows: 136 482 becomes 146 482.

Cards 4, 0, 7, 2, 9, 5. Biggest number: 975 420. Smallest number: 204 579, because 0 cannot go first.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A six-digit number has six seats: units, tens, hundreds, thousands, ten thousands and hundred thousands, each worth ten times the one on its right. The seat decides the value of a digit. Expanded notation breaks a number into the values of its digits, and zeros keep the empty seats. Biggest numbers put big digits on the left; smallest numbers put small digits on the left, but never 0 first.

Hold the digit cards in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: changing digits and making numbers.
