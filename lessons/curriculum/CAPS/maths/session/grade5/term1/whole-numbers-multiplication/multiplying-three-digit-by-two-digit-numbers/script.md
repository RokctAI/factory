# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers multiplication of whole three-digit numbers by two-digit numbers. The anchor is a bakery in Soweto that bakes 345 loaves a day, a school that buys 24 boxes of 144 pencils, and 126 learners who each pay R35 for a school trip. The governing idea is that both numbers are split by place value, every part of one is multiplied by every part of the other, and the partial products are added, whether we show this in a grid or in columns. Multiplying by multiples of 10, multiplying three-digit numbers by two-digit numbers with the grid method and with long multiplication, and estimating to check are examinable.

## Subtopic: Multiplying by Tens and by One Digit

Two smaller skills make big multiplications possible. The first is multiplying a three-digit number by a one-digit number. The bakery bakes 345 loaves a day. In 8 days that is 345 times 8. Split 345 into 300, 40 and 5. 300 times 8 is 2 400, 40 times 8 is 320 and 5 times 8 is 40. Add the parts: 2 400 plus 320 plus 40 is 2 760 loaves.

The second skill is multiplying by 10 and by multiples of 10. Multiplying by 10 makes every digit worth ten times as much, so each digit moves one place to the left and a 0 fills the units: 345 times 10 is 3 450. To multiply by 20, multiply by 2 and then by 10: 345 times 2 is 690, and 690 times 10 is 6 900. So in 20 days the bakery bakes 6 900 loaves.

Basic facts grow with place value. 7 times 6 is 42, so 70 times 6 is 420, and 70 times 60 is 4 200. 126 times 3 is 378, so 126 times 30 is 3 780. Count the zeros in the numbers you multiply and make sure the answer has at least that many.

These two skills are all you need for long multiplication. A two-digit number is a number of tens plus a number of units, so multiplying by 28 is multiplying by 20 and by 8 and adding the two results.

The questions for this section are with you now: three-digit by one-digit multiplication, multiplying by 10 and multiples of 10, and growing basic facts.

## Subtopic: The Grid Method

The grid method shows every part of the multiplication. For 345 times 28, split 345 into 300, 40 and 5, and split 28 into 20 and 8. Draw a grid with three columns and two rows, and multiply each part by each part. The top row is 300 times 20, 40 times 20 and 5 times 20: 6 000, 800 and 100. The bottom row is 300 times 8, 40 times 8 and 5 times 8: 2 400, 320 and 40.

Add all six products: 6 000 plus 800 plus 100 is 6 900, and 2 400 plus 320 plus 40 is 2 760. 6 900 plus 2 760 is 9 660. In 28 days the bakery bakes 9 660 loaves. Notice that the two rows are exactly the two skills from the first section: 345 times 20 and 345 times 8.

126 learners each pay R35. Split 126 into 100, 20 and 6, and 35 into 30 and 5. The products are 3 000, 600 and 180 in the first row, and 500, 100 and 30 in the second row. 3 780 plus 630 is 4 410. The teacher collects R4 410.

The grid works because of the distributive property: every part of one number must meet every part of the other. A three-digit number has three parts and a two-digit number has two, so there are always three times two, which is six, products.

The questions for this section are with you now: splitting both numbers, filling the six boxes of the grid, and adding the partial products.

## Subtopic: Long Multiplication in Columns

Long multiplication is a shorter way of writing the grid. The school buys 24 boxes of 144 pencils. Write 144 above 24, lined up on the right. First row: 144 times 4. 4 times 4 is 16, write 6 carry 1; 4 times 4 is 16, and 16 plus the carried 1 is 17, write 7 carry 1; 1 times 4 is 4, and 4 plus the carried 1 is 5. The first row is 576.

Second row: 144 times 20. Because we are multiplying by 2 tens, write a 0 in the units place first. Then 144 times 2: 4 times 2 is 8, 4 times 2 is 8, 1 times 2 is 2. The second row is 2 880. Add the rows: 576 plus 2 880 is 3 456. The school receives 3 456 pencils.

The same layout works for 345 times 28. First row: 345 times 8 is 2 760. Second row: write 0, then 345 times 2 is 690, giving 6 900. Add: 9 660, the same answer as the grid. Every row in long multiplication is one row of the grid, already added up.

Estimate to check. Round 345 to 300 and 28 to 30: 300 times 30 is 9 000. The exact answer, 9 660, is close. For 144 times 24, round to 140 times 25, which is 3 500, close to 3 456. An estimate catches the biggest slips at once.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: writing 345 times 20 as 690; multiplying by 20 is multiplying by 2 and then by 10, so the answer is 6 900. Two: in the grid, multiplying only some parts, such as 300 times 20 plus 45 times 8, which gives 6 360; every part must multiply every part, giving six products and 9 660. Three: leaving out the 0 at the start of the second row, so 345 times 28 becomes 2 760 plus 690, which is 3 450; the second row is tens and needs the 0. Four: skipping the estimate, so 3 450 goes unnoticed; 300 times 30 is 9 000, which shows that 3 450 is far too small.

The questions for this section are with you now: the two rows of long multiplication, the placeholder zero, and estimating to check.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the bakery loaves, the pencil boxes and the trip money — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Times Ten, Times One Digit

Times 10: every digit moves one place left. 345 times 10 is 3 450.

Times 20: times 2, then times 10. 345 times 2 is 690, so 345 times 20 is 6 900.

Times 8: split 345 into 300, 40 and 5. 2 400 plus 320 plus 40 is 2 760.

Keep the two skills in mind and try the questions for this part: times tens and times one digit.

## Subtopic: Six Boxes

Split 345 into 300, 40, 5. Split 28 into 20, 8. That makes six boxes.

Fill every box: 6 000, 800, 100, 2 400, 320, 40. Add them all: 9 660.

126 times 35: 3 000, 600, 180, 500, 100, 30. Total R4 410.

Hold the six boxes in mind and try this part's questions: the grid method.

## Subtopic: Two Rows, Then Add

Row one: times the units. 144 times 4 is 576.

Row two: put a 0 first, then times the tens digit. 144 times 2 tens is 2 880.

Add the rows: 3 456 pencils.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Multiply by 10 by moving digits one place left; multiply by 20 by doubling and then multiplying by 10. Split both numbers by place value and multiply every part by every part: three parts times two parts makes six products. Long multiplication puts the grid into two rows: units first, then tens with a 0. Add the rows and estimate to check.

Hold the two rows in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: long multiplication.
