# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers solving problems with whole numbers in financial and measurement contexts, and problems involving ratio and rate, using multiplication. The anchor is a building project at a school in Tzaneen: 36 bags of cement at R95 a bag, concrete mixed 1 bucket of cement to 3 buckets of sand, tomatoes for the builders' lunch at R45 per kilogram, and a delivery truck that travels at 80 kilometres per hour. The governing idea is that equal groups are found by multiplying, that a ratio compares amounts of the same kind and is scaled by multiplying both parts, and that a rate compares amounts of different kinds, with per meaning for each. Solving money and measurement problems by multiplication, scaling ratios, and calculating with rates are examinable.

## Subtopic: Money and Measurement Problems

When a story has equal groups, multiply. The builder needs 36 bags of cement at R95 a bag. 36 times 95: round R95 up to R100. 36 times 100 is 3 600. That is R5 too much for every bag, and 36 times 5 is 180. 3 600 minus 180 is 3 420. The cement costs R3 420.

The school orders 125 textbooks at R148 each. 148 times 100 is 14 800 and 148 times 25 is 3 700, so 148 times 125 is 18 500. The textbooks cost R18 500. Splitting 125 into 100 and 25 makes the calculation friendly, because multiplying by 25 is a quarter of multiplying by 100.

Measurement works the same way. A fence is made of 24 panels, each 175 cm long. 175 times 20 is 3 500 and 175 times 4 is 700, so the fence is 4 200 cm long. The answer has the same unit as each panel: centimetres. Later in the year you will see that 4 200 cm is 42 metres.

Words like each, every, per and a bag tell you that every group is the same size. Write the number sentence first, 36 times 95 equals box, then choose a technique, and finish with an estimate: 40 times R100 is R4 000, so R3 420 is sensible.

The questions for this section are with you now: money totals, measurement totals, and writing the multiplication sentence.

## Subtopic: Ratio: Comparing the Same Kind

A ratio compares two amounts of the same kind. Concrete is mixed 1 bucket of cement to 3 buckets of sand. Both amounts are buckets, so this is a ratio, written 1 to 3. It means that for every bucket of cement there are 3 buckets of sand, however big the batch is.

To make a bigger batch, multiply both parts by the same number. For 12 buckets of cement, multiply both parts by 12: 12 buckets of cement to 36 buckets of sand. The mix is still 1 to 3, just bigger. Altogether that is 12 plus 36, which is 48 buckets of concrete.

Juice for the builders is mixed 1 part concentrate to 4 parts water. For 5 litres of concentrate, multiply both parts by 5: 5 litres of concentrate to 20 litres of water, making 25 litres of juice. The taste is the same as a small glass mixed 1 to 4.

A ratio can also describe a group. In a class of 35, the ratio of boys to girls is 2 to 3. That means every group of 5 learners has 2 boys and 3 girls. 35 is 7 groups of 5, so there are 7 times 2, which is 14 boys, and 7 times 3, which is 21 girls. Check: 14 plus 21 is 35.

The questions for this section are with you now: reading a ratio, scaling both parts, and sharing a group in a ratio.

## Subtopic: Rate: Comparing Different Kinds

A rate compares two amounts of different kinds, such as rands and kilograms, or kilometres and hours. Tomatoes cost R45 per kilogram. Per means for each, so every kilogram costs R45. 12 kg cost 12 times 45, which is 540. The tomatoes cost R540.

The delivery truck travels at 80 kilometres per hour. In 3 hours it travels 3 times 80, which is 240 km. A builder pays a taxi fare of R18 per trip and makes 26 trips a month, so the fares cost 26 times 18, which is R468. A dripping tap loses 15 ml per minute, so in an hour of 60 minutes it loses 60 times 15, which is 900 ml.

Rates help you compare prices. Cool drinks come 4 cans for R48 or 6 cans for R66. Find the price of one can: R48 shared by 4 is R12, and R66 shared by 6 is R11. The 6-pack is cheaper per can, even though it costs more altogether.

The unit of the answer comes from the rate. Rands per kilogram times kilograms gives rands. Kilometres per hour times hours gives kilometres. Writing the units in the number sentence helps you check that the answer is the right kind of amount.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: adding instead of multiplying in a ratio, so 1 to 3 with 12 buckets of cement becomes 12 to 14; multiply both parts by 12 to get 12 to 36. Two: reading boys to girls 2 to 3 as 2 out of every 3 learners being boys; 2 to 3 means 2 boys in every 5 learners. Three: giving the cost of 12 kg of tomatoes as 540 kg; rand per kilogram times kilograms gives rand, so the answer is R540. Four: choosing the 4-pack because R48 is less than R66; compare the price of one can.

The questions for this section are with you now: calculating with rates, comparing prices with a rate for one item, and getting the units right.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the cement, the concrete mix, the tomatoes and the truck — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: How Much Altogether

Equal groups mean multiply. 36 bags at R95 each is 36 times 95.

Round R95 to R100: 3 600. Take off R5 for each bag, 180. R3 420.

24 panels of 175 cm make 4 200 cm of fence.

Keep the equal groups in mind and try the questions for this part: money and measurement totals.

## Subtopic: Parts of a Mix

1 bucket of cement to 3 buckets of sand. Same kind, so it is a ratio.

Bigger batch? Multiply both parts. 12 cement to 36 sand. 48 buckets altogether.

2 boys to 3 girls means 2 boys in every 5 learners. In 35 learners: 14 boys and 21 girls.

Hold the multiply-both-parts rule in mind and try this part's questions: ratio.

## Subtopic: Per Means For Each

R45 per kilogram means R45 for each kilogram. 12 kg cost R540.

80 km per hour for 3 hours is 240 km.

Compare prices for one item: 4 for R48 is R12 a can, 6 for R66 is R11 a can. The 6-pack wins.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Equal groups mean multiply, and an estimate checks the total. A ratio compares amounts of the same kind; scale it by multiplying both parts by the same number. A rate compares amounts of different kinds; per means for each, so multiply by the number of units. Compare prices by finding the cost of one item, and give every answer its unit.

Hold the for-each idea in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: rates.
