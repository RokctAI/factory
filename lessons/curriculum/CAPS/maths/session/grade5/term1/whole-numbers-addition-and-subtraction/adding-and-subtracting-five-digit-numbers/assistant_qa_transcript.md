### subtopic_1 — Adding in Columns with Carrying
**Assistant:** Why do we start adding from the units and not from the left, the way we read?

**Tutor:** Because carrying only ever moves to the left, so starting on the right means every carry arrives before you need it. When you add the units, 6 plus 7 makes 13, and the extra ten has to go into the tens column. If you had already finished the tens column, you would have to go back and change it, and with five columns that could mean changing several answers you had already written. Starting from the units avoids all that rubbing out. It is possible to add from the left, and some people do it in their heads, by adding the biggest parts first: 20 000 plus 10 000, then 4 000 plus 8 000, and so on, and then putting the parts together. That is a good method for estimating and for mental work, and you will use it when you build up and break down numbers. But on paper, with long numbers, the column method from the right is the most reliable, because each step is small and nothing needs to be corrected later. Subtraction works the same way: exchanges always take from the column on the left, so starting on the right means you exchange only when you need to, one column at a time.

### subtopic_2 — Subtracting in Columns with Exchanging
**Assistant:** Why is exchanging allowed? It feels like taking from the column next door.

**Tutor:** It is allowed because exchanging does not change the number at all; it only changes how the number is written. 24 586 is 2 ten thousands, 4 thousands, 5 hundreds, 8 tens and 6 units. When we exchange 1 ten for 10 units, it becomes 2 ten thousands, 4 thousands, 5 hundreds, 7 tens and 16 units. Add those up and you still get 24 586. Nothing has been lost or borrowed; one ten has been broken into ten units, the way a R10 note can be swapped for ten R1 coins without making you richer or poorer. That is why some teachers prefer the word exchange to the older word borrow. Borrowing sounds as if something must be paid back, and nothing has to be paid back here. The reason we exchange is simply that we need more units in the units column to take 7 away. Once you see exchanging as changing notes for coins, the steps make sense: the column on the left goes down by 1, and the column you are working in goes up by 10. The same thing happens in every column, because each place is worth ten times the place on its right.

### subtopic_3 — Subtracting Across Zeros and Adding Many Numbers
**Assistant:** Is there a faster way to work out 50 000 minus 23 476 than all that exchanging?

**Tutor:** Yes, and there are two good ones. The first is the 49 999 shortcut. Exchanging across all those zeros is slow and easy to get wrong, but 49 999 minus 23 476 needs no exchanging at all, because every top digit is 9 or bigger than the digit below it. Work it column by column: 9 minus 6 is 3, 9 minus 7 is 2, 9 minus 4 is 5, 9 minus 3 is 6 and 4 minus 2 is 2, giving 26 523. Since 49 999 is 1 less than 50 000, the real answer is 1 more: 26 524. The second way is to count up from the smaller number, like a shopkeeper giving change. Start at 23 476. Add 24 to reach 23 500. Add 500 to reach 24 000. Add 26 000 to reach 50 000. The jumps are 24, 500 and 26 000, which add up to 26 524. Both methods give the same answer as the long exchange, and both are less likely to go wrong. Whichever method you use, finish with a check: 23 476 plus 26 524 should give 50 000, and it does.
