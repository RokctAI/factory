### subtopic_1 — Money Problems
**Assistant:** What is the difference between profit and the money the shop takes in?

**Tutor:** The money taken in is everything customers pay, but not all of it belongs to the shop owner to keep, because some of it has to cover what the stock cost. The spaza shop took in R15 230 when it sold its stock. But the owner had already paid R12 650 for that stock. So only the part left over after replacing the cost is truly earned, and that is the profit: R15 230 minus R12 650, which is R2 580. Many new business owners get into trouble because they look at all the money in the till and think it is theirs to spend. If the owner spent the whole R15 230, there would be no money to buy the next lot of stock, and the shop would have nothing to sell next month. In real businesses there are other costs as well, like electricity, rent for the shop and transport to fetch the stock, and these are also taken away before the real profit is known. If the costs are bigger than the money taken in, the business makes a loss instead of a profit. In Grade 5 we keep it simple, with one cost price and one selling price, but the idea is the same one that every business uses: profit is what is left after the costs are paid.

### subtopic_2 — Measurement Problems
**Assistant:** Why does the unit matter if the number is right?

**Tutor:** Because a number without a unit does not tell anyone how much of what. The answer 1 445 could mean 1 445 kilometres, 1 445 metres, 1 445 rands or 1 445 litres, and those are completely different amounts. A trip of 1 445 metres is a short walk; a trip of 1 445 kilometres is a long day of driving across the country. If a truck driver were told the load limit was 15 000 without a unit, he would not know whether that meant grams, which is a few bags of flour, or kilograms, which is a full truck. Units also protect you from adding things that do not belong together. 3 kilometres and 250 metres cannot be added as 3 plus 250, because a kilometre is a thousand times longer than a metre; the answer, 253, would be meaningless. Before you add or subtract measurements, check that they are in the same unit. In Term 3 you will learn to convert one unit into another, so that you can then add them properly. In tests, a correct number with no unit or the wrong unit often loses marks, because the answer is not complete. Getting into the habit of writing the unit every time is one of the easiest ways to make sure your answers are right.

### subtopic_3 — Multi-Step Problems and Sensible Answers
**Assistant:** How do I know if a problem needs two steps?

**Tutor:** Look at the question at the end and ask whether you can answer it straight away with the numbers given. If you can, it is one step. If one of the numbers you need is missing and has to be worked out first, it is two steps. In the budget problem, the question is how much is left after rent, food and transport. To answer it, you need the income and the total spending. The income is given, R18 500, but the total spending is not; only the three separate amounts are. So the first step is to find the total spending, R13 595, and the second step is to subtract it from the income. A helpful habit is to write what you need, then what you have. Need: income and total spending. Have: income and three separate costs. The gap tells you the first step. Words like altogether and then left in the same problem often signal two steps, as does a problem that mentions several things happening one after another, like two weeks of water use. Once you finish, check that your final answer actually answers the question that was asked. Many learners correctly find R13 595 and stop there, but the question asked what was left, not what was spent.
