### subtopic_1 — From Story to Number Sentence
**Assistant:** Why should I write a number sentence when I can just work out the answer?

**Tutor:** Because the number sentence is where most mistakes are caught before they happen. When a problem is short and easy, you may be able to jump straight to the answer, and that is fine. But as problems get longer, with bigger numbers and more than one step, it becomes easy to add when you should subtract, or to use the wrong number. Writing the sentence forces you to decide what the story is doing before you start calculating, and it shows that decision on paper, where you and your teacher can check it. If your answer is wrong but your sentence is right, you know the mistake is in the calculation, and you can find it quickly. If your sentence is wrong, no amount of careful calculating will save the answer. There is another reason. In tests, marks are often given for the method as well as the answer, and the number sentence is the clearest way to show your method. Later in school, number sentences grow into equations with letters, like n plus 250 equals 1 750, and those equations are how scientists, engineers and bookkeepers describe real situations. Learning to write the story in numbers now is practice for a skill you will use for many years.

### subtopic_2 — Where the Box Goes
**Assistant:** Can a story have more than one correct number sentence?

**Tutor:** Yes, and that is perfectly fine, as long as each sentence tells the same story and gives the same answer. Take Lerato, who had some money, saved R250 more and now has R1 750. Box plus 250 equals 1 750 follows the story in the order it happened: something, plus R250, gives R1 750. 1 750 minus 250 equals box also fits, because it undoes the saving to get back to the start. Both have the answer R1 500. The first sentence describes the problem and the second shows how to solve it, so you could even write both, one after the other. Addition and multiplication give you more freedom, because the order of the numbers can be swapped: 1 245 plus 1 378 and 1 378 plus 1 245 give the same total, and 24 times 15 and 15 times 24 give the same product. Subtraction and division are not like that. 1 378 minus 1 245 is not the same as 1 245 minus 1 378, and 432 divided by 18 is not the same as 18 divided by 432. So when you write a sentence with minus or divided by, make sure the numbers are in the order the story needs. If your teacher's sentence looks different from yours, read both back as stories. If they tell the same story, both are correct.

### subtopic_3 — Two-Step Sentences and Brackets
**Assistant:** Why do we need brackets? Can't I just work from left to right?

**Tutor:** Working from left to right gives the right answer in some sentences but not in others, so brackets are there to remove all doubt. Look at 30 plus 2 times 45, which describes one R30 ticket and two R45 tickets. If you work strictly from left to right, you get 32 times 45, which is 1 440, a silly answer for three tickets. Mathematicians agreed long ago that multiplication and division are done before addition and subtraction, which gives 30 plus 90, or 120, the correct cost. You will learn that rule properly in later grades. But brackets make the order obvious without anyone needing to remember a rule: 30 plus (2 times 45) can only mean one thing. Brackets also let you show an order that the rule would not give. 2 times (45 plus 30) means two people each buying one ticket of each kind, which is 150, and without the brackets it would be read differently. So use brackets around the step that comes first in the story. In Lerato's problem, the savings must be worked out before the bicycle is paid for, so (12 times 250) minus 1 200 shows the order clearly. It never hurts to add brackets to make your meaning clear, even when the rule would give the same answer.
