### subtopic_1 — Solving by Inspection
**Assistant:** Is trial and improvement just guessing? My teacher says guessing is bad.

**Tutor:** Wild guessing is not helpful, but trial and improvement is something different: it is guessing with a plan, and every try teaches you something. When you try 20 in 23 times box equals 552 and get 460, you learn that 20 is too small. That rules out every number from 20 downwards, so your next try must be bigger. When you try 25 and get 575, you learn that 25 is too big, so the answer is trapped between 20 and 25. Each try makes the trap smaller, until only one number is left. That is very different from writing down a random number and hoping. Good trial and improvement also starts with a sensible first try. 23 is close to 20, and 20 times 25 is 500, so the box is likely to be in the twenties; starting at 20 is sensible, while starting at 3 or 300 wastes time. Scientists and engineers use this same idea, called improving an estimate step by step, to solve problems that have no neat formula, and computers do it millions of times a second. Your teacher is right that a wild guess is not a method. But a careful try, followed by a check and a better try, is a real mathematical method, and it is one CAPS expects you to use.

### subtopic_2 — Trial and Improvement
**Assistant:** Why should I check by substitution if I'm already sure my answer is right?

**Tutor:** Because being sure and being right are not the same thing, and the check costs only a few seconds. Most mistakes in solving number sentences are not about understanding; they are small slips, like subtracting when you should add or forgetting to carry a 1. Those slips feel exactly like correct work while you are doing them, so you cannot feel when you have made one. Substitution catches them, because it tests the answer against the original sentence instead of against your own working. If you solved box minus 368 equals 1 250 by subtracting and got 882, your working might look neat and confident. But put 882 back in: 882 minus 368 is 514, and 514 is not 1 250. The sentence is false, and the mistake is caught. There is a second benefit. Substitution uses a different calculation from the one you used to solve, often the inverse. If both calculations agree, it is very unlikely that you made the same slip twice. That is why accountants check their totals by adding in a different order, and why builders measure twice before they cut. In a test, a quick substitution check on every answer is one of the cheapest ways to protect your marks.

### subtopic_3 — Checking by Substitution and Inverses
**Assistant:** In box minus 368 equals 1 250, why do I add to find the box when the sentence says minus?

**Tutor:** Because you are going backwards through the story, and going backwards means undoing what was done. Read the sentence as a story: there was some number of rands in a purse, R368 was spent, and R1 250 was left. To find out what was in the purse at the start, you have to put the R368 back, and putting back is adding. 1 250 plus 368 is 1 618, so the purse started with R1 618. The sentence says minus because it tells the story forwards, in the order things happened. Solving it asks you to run the story in reverse, and the reverse of taking away is adding back. The same thinking works for every operation. Box plus 1 875 equals 4 300: going backwards, take the 1 875 away. Box times 30 equals 840: going backwards, share the 840 into groups of 30 by dividing. Box divided by 12 equals 15: going backwards, put the 12 equal groups of 15 back together by multiplying. If you are ever unsure which way to go, try both and substitute. Only one of them will make the sentence true, and substitution will tell you which.
