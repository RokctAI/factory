### subtopic_1 — Numbers: Place Value, Ordering and Rounding
**Assistant:** There is so much in Term 1. What should I revise first?

**Tutor:** Start with the skills that every other skill depends on, because fixing those fixes several topics at once. Place value comes first. If you can say what each digit in 148 365 is worth, then column addition, column subtraction, rounding and long multiplication all make sense, because each of them is place value in action. Second, the multiplication tables, because three-digit by two-digit multiplication, factors, multiples, ratio, rate and the division you will meet in Term 2 all lean on them, and a shaky table slows every one of those down. Third, the checking habits, estimation and inverse operations, because they turn an unsure answer into a sure one and cost almost no time. Once those three are solid, go through the topics in order and do two or three problems from each, not twenty from one. For each problem, write the number sentence, estimate, calculate and check, so that you practise the routine as well as the content. Keep a page of the mistakes you made and the correct method next to each, and read it the night before the test. Do not spend long on the things you already find easy. The marks you can still gain are in the topics that feel uncomfortable, which is exactly where most learners avoid spending time.

### subtopic_2 — Number Sentences, Adding and Subtracting
**Assistant:** Which checking method should I use for which kind of calculation?

**Tutor:** Use an estimate for every calculation, because it is quick and catches the big mistakes, and then add an inverse check whenever you can. For addition, estimate by rounding each number, and check exactly by subtracting one number from the total: if 12 476 plus 9 858 is 22 334, then 22 334 minus 9 858 must give 12 476. For subtraction, the best check is adding back: the answer plus the number you took away must give the number you started with, so 5 475 plus 148 365 must be 153 840. For multiplication, estimate by rounding each number to something easy, like 25 times 130 for 24 times 135, and check exactly by dividing the product by one of the numbers, if the division is easy enough. For number sentences, the check is always substitution: put your answer into the box and see if the sentence is true. For rounding, check that your answer is a multiple of the place you rounded to, and that it is the closer of the two neighbours. For ratio, check that the new numbers still compare in the same way: 4 to 20 is the same as 1 to 5, because both parts were multiplied by 4. Using a different method to check is what makes the check powerful.

### subtopic_3 — Multiplying, Factors, Ratio and Rate
**Assistant:** I keep mixing up ratio and rate. How can I tell them apart quickly?

**Tutor:** Look at the units of the two amounts being compared. If they are the same kind of thing, it is a ratio. If they are different kinds of things, it is a rate. Lemonade mixed 1 part syrup to 5 parts water compares litres with litres, so it is a ratio. Boys to girls compares learners with learners, so it is a ratio. R25 per boerewors roll compares rands with rolls, so it is a rate. 80 kilometres per hour compares kilometres with hours, so it is a rate. The word per is a strong clue for a rate, because it links two different units: rand per kilogram, kilometres per hour, millilitres per minute. Ratios are usually said with the word to: 1 to 5, 2 to 3. Both are worked out in a similar way, by multiplying, but what you multiply is different. For a ratio, you multiply both parts by the same number to make a bigger batch that tastes or looks the same. For a rate, you multiply the rate by the number of units: R25 per roll times 148 rolls. Once you have checked the units, the method follows, and the units also tell you what the answer should be measured in.
