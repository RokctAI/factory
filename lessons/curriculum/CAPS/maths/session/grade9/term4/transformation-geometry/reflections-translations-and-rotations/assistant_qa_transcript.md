### subtopic_1 — Reflection in the Axes
**Assistant:** Why does reflecting in the x-axis change the y co-ordinate and not the x co-ordinate?

**Tutor:** Because the mirror is horizontal, so the reflection moves points vertically, and vertical position is measured by y. Picture the point 1 semicolon 2. Its x co-ordinate, 1, says how far it is to the right of the y-axis; its y co-ordinate, 2, says how far it is above the x-axis. When the x-axis is the mirror, the image must be directly opposite the point across that mirror, the same distance away on the other side, so the line joining the point to its image is vertical and crosses the axis at right angles. Moving vertically does not change how far right the point is, so x stays 1. The distance from the mirror stays 2, but the side changes from above to below, so y becomes negative 2. The image is 1 semicolon negative 2. The same reasoning with the y-axis as the mirror moves points horizontally, so y is kept and x changes sign. That is why the memory aid works: the axis acting as the mirror keeps its own co-ordinate. Students who learn the rule without the picture often swap the two, changing x when reflecting in the x-axis, because the name of the axis seems to point at the co-ordinate that changes. If you are ever unsure, plot the point and its intended image and ask whether the mirror sits exactly halfway between them, with the joining line at right angles to it. If it does not, the rule has been applied to the wrong co-ordinate.

### subtopic_2 — Reflection in the Line y = x
**Assistant:** Why does reflecting in y equals x simply swap the two co-ordinates?

**Tutor:** Because the line y equals x is the line of balance between the two axes, and reflecting in it exchanges the roles of x and y. Every point on the line has equal co-ordinates, and the line makes 45 degrees with both axes, so it acts like a hinge between them: folding the plane along it lays the positive x-axis exactly onto the positive y-axis and the negative x-axis onto the negative y-axis. A point's distance to the right of the y-axis becomes, after folding, its distance above the x-axis, and the other way round. That is precisely what swapping the co-ordinates does. You can confirm it with any point. Take 1 semicolon 2 and its swap 2 semicolon 1. The midpoint of the two is 1.5 semicolon 1.5, which is on the line y equals x, and the segment between them runs 1 right and 1 down, at right angles to the mirror line, which runs 1 right and 1 up. The mirror is therefore the perpendicular bisector of the segment, which is the defining property of a reflection. The swap also explains why horizontal lines become vertical: a horizontal segment has a constant y, and after swapping it has a constant x. A warning follows from this. The rotation through 90 degrees also swaps the co-ordinates, but it changes a sign as well. Swap only, and you have reflected in y equals x; swap and change a sign, and you have rotated.

### subtopic_3 — Translations
**Assistant:** Why does moving left mean subtracting from x, when the shape still looks the same?

**Tutor:** Because x measures horizontal position, increasing to the right, so any move to the left reduces it, and the shape looking the same is exactly what a translation promises. The co-ordinate plane is built from number lines: along the x-axis the numbers increase from left to right, and along the y-axis they increase from bottom to top. A point at x equals 1 that moves 3 units left arrives at x equals negative 2, which is 1 minus 3. Moving down 2 from y equals 2 arrives at y equals 0, which is 2 minus 2. So the translation 3 left and 2 down is x semicolon y becomes x minus 3 semicolon y minus 2, and it is applied to every vertex the same way. The shape looks the same because the same subtraction is applied to every point: the differences between points, which determine lengths and directions, do not change. The distance from A to B was 3 along the x-axis before, from 1 to 4, and it is 3 after, from negative 2 to 1. That is why a translation preserves lengths, angles, orientation and the direction of every side. The common error, writing x plus 3 for a move to the left, comes from reading the size of the shift and forgetting its direction. A quick check is to translate one vertex and look at the plot: if the image has moved right when the question said left, the sign is wrong. In words, right and up add, left and down subtract.

### subtopic_4 — Rotations About a Point and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** How can I remember which 90 degree rule is clockwise and which is anticlockwise?

**Tutor:** Do not rely on memory alone; check with the quadrant, because it is quick and it never fails. Both 90 degree rules swap the co-ordinates and make one of them negative; the only difference is which one. Take a simple test point such as 1 semicolon 2 in the first quadrant, up and to the right. A quarter turn anticlockwise about the origin swings it up and over to the left, into the second quadrant, where x is negative and y is positive. So the image must have a negative first co-ordinate: negative 2 semicolon 1, and the rule is x semicolon y becomes negative y semicolon x. A quarter turn clockwise swings it down to the right, into the fourth quadrant, where x is positive and y is negative, so the image is 2 semicolon negative 1, and the rule is x semicolon y becomes y semicolon negative x. You can confirm either answer by checking distance and angle. The distance from the origin to 1 semicolon 2 and to negative 2 semicolon 1 is the same, the square root of 5, as a rotation requires. And the two lines from the origin are perpendicular: one rises 2 for every 1 across, the other falls 1 for every 2 across, and gradients of 2 and negative one half multiply to negative 1. Half a turn needs no memory at all, because it reverses both directions: both signs change. With the quadrant check in hand, the rules rebuild themselves whenever you need them.
