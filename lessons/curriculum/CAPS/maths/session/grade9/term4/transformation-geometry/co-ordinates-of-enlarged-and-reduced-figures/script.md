# Part 1 — Expert

The last lesson measured what an enlargement does to perimeter and area. Today we put the enlargement on the co-ordinate plane. The CAPS instruction is to investigate the co-ordinates of vertices of figures enlarged or reduced by a given scale factor, and the investigation leads to one clean rule: an enlargement or reduction with centre at the origin and scale factor k sends the point x semicolon y to k x semicolon k y. We discover the rule from examples, apply it to reductions with fractional scale factors, see why it works by drawing rays from the origin, use it to check whether a given image is an enlargement at all, and connect it back to area, finishing with the error museum.

## Subtopic: Multiplying Co-ordinates by k

Plot triangle ABC with A at 1 semicolon 1, B at 3 semicolon 1 and C at 1 semicolon 2. Now plot a second triangle with vertices 2 semicolon 2, 6 semicolon 2 and 2 semicolon 4. Measure: AB was 2 units and the new side is 4 units; AC was 1 unit and the new side is 2 units; the angle at A is still a right angle. The second triangle is an enlargement of the first by scale factor 2, and every co-ordinate has been multiplied by 2.

Try scale factor 3. Multiplying every co-ordinate by 3 gives 3 semicolon 3, 9 semicolon 3 and 3 semicolon 6. The sides are now 6 units and 3 units, three times the original, and the shape is unchanged. The pattern is the rule: an enlargement with centre the origin and scale factor k is x semicolon y becomes k x semicolon k y.

Both co-ordinates must be multiplied, and by the same number. Multiplying only the x co-ordinates by 2 gives 2 semicolon 1, 6 semicolon 1 and 2 semicolon 2: the horizontal side doubles but the vertical side stays 1, so the triangle is stretched, not enlarged. That is a different transformation, and it does not produce a similar figure.

It helps to record the working in a table with three columns: the original vertex, the multiplication, and the image vertex. Writing A at 1 semicolon 1 becomes 2 times 1 semicolon 2 times 1, which is 2 semicolon 2, for every vertex makes each step visible and each mark easy to award.

Negative co-ordinates obey the same rule. The point negative 2 semicolon 4, enlarged by scale factor 3 about the origin, becomes negative 6 semicolon 12. The sign stays, because a positive scale factor does not change direction; it only changes distance from the origin.

The rule also works backwards. If the point 4 semicolon negative 2 has image 10 semicolon negative 5, then 10 divided by 4 is 2.5, and negative 5 divided by negative 2 is 2.5. The two divisions agree, so this is an enlargement about the origin with scale factor 2.5. The questions for this section are with you now: applying x semicolon y becomes k x semicolon k y, why both co-ordinates must be multiplied, negative co-ordinates, and finding k from a point and its image.

## Subtopic: Reductions and Fractional Scale Factors

A reduction uses a scale factor between 0 and 1, and the same rule applies: multiply each co-ordinate by k. Triangle PQR has P at 4 semicolon 6, Q at 8 semicolon 2 and R at 2 semicolon 2. Reduce it by scale factor one half: P dash is 2 semicolon 3, Q dash is 4 semicolon 1 and R dash is 1 semicolon 1. Every co-ordinate has been halved, and the image is a smaller copy pulled towards the origin. The side QR, which ran from 2 to 8 along the line y equals 2, a length of 6, becomes the side from 1 to 4 along y equals 1, a length of 3.

Scale factor one quarter takes 8 semicolon 12 to 2 semicolon 3. Scale factor three quarters takes 8 semicolon 12 to 6 semicolon 9, since three quarters of 8 is 6 and three quarters of 12 is 9. Scale factor 0.4 takes 5 semicolon negative 10 to 2 semicolon negative 4. Multiplying by a fraction is the same as dividing by its reciprocal: a scale factor of one third means dividing every co-ordinate by 3.

A reduction can be undone by an enlargement. Halving every co-ordinate and then doubling every co-ordinate returns each point to where it started, so the reduction with k equal to one half and the enlargement with k equal to 2 are inverse transformations. In general, the inverse of the scale factor k is the scale factor 1 over k. This is useful when a question gives the image and asks for the original: if A dash is 3 semicolon negative 6 after an enlargement by scale factor 3, then A is 1 semicolon negative 2.

A practical example is a school badge. A design team draws the badge on a large grid with corners at 0 semicolon 0, 40 semicolon 0, 40 semicolon 48 and 0 semicolon 48, measured in millimetres, so that the detail is easy to draw. For embroidery on a blazer pocket the badge must be reduced by scale factor one eighth. Every co-ordinate is divided by 8, giving corners at 0 semicolon 0, 5 semicolon 0, 5 semicolon 6 and 0 semicolon 6: a badge 5 millimetres wide would be far too small, so the team checks the units and realises the grid was in centimetres, giving a badge 5 centimetres by 6 centimetres. Reading the scale factor correctly matters, but so does reading the units.

Fractional scale factors sometimes produce fractional co-ordinates. Reducing 3 semicolon 5 by one half gives 1.5 semicolon 2.5, which is a perfectly good point, halfway between grid lines. When plotting, mark it carefully rather than rounding it to a grid point, or the image will no longer be similar to the original. The questions for this section are ready: reductions by one half and by fractions, the inverse scale factor, and finding the original from an image.

## Subtopic: Rays from the Origin and Checking an Enlargement

Why does multiplying co-ordinates by k enlarge the figure? Draw a ray from the origin through the point 3 semicolon 4. Its distance from the origin is 5 units, by the theorem of Pythagoras, since 3 squared plus 4 squared is 25. The point 6 semicolon 8 lies on the same ray, because 6 to 8 is in the same ratio as 3 to 4, and its distance from the origin is 10 units, twice as far. So the rule x semicolon y becomes k x semicolon k y moves every point along its own ray from the origin to k times its original distance. The origin is the centre of enlargement, the one point that does not move.

This gives a geometric construction. To enlarge a figure by scale factor 2 about the origin without co-ordinates, draw a ray from the origin through each vertex, measure the distance from the origin to the vertex, and mark the image at twice that distance along the same ray. To reduce by one half, mark it at half the distance. The rays from corresponding vertices all meet at the centre, which is how you recognise an enlargement on a diagram.

It also gives a test for whether an image is an enlargement about the origin. Divide each image co-ordinate by the original co-ordinate; every quotient, for every vertex, must be the same number k. The point 2 semicolon 3 with image 4 semicolon 9 fails: 4 divided by 2 is 2, but 9 divided by 3 is 3. The point has been stretched by different amounts in the two directions, and it does not lie on the ray through 2 semicolon 3. A triangle whose vertices give the same quotient at two vertices and a different one at the third is also not an enlargement.

Sides parallel to their originals are another sign. In an enlargement about the origin, every side of the image is parallel to the corresponding side of the original. The side from 1 semicolon 1 to 3 semicolon 1 is horizontal, and its image from 2 semicolon 2 to 6 semicolon 2 is horizontal too. The questions for this section are ready: points moving along rays, the construction with rays, the quotient test, and parallel sides.

## Subtopic: Area of the Image and the Error Museum

Co-ordinates let us confirm the area rule from the previous lesson. The rectangle with vertices 1 semicolon 1, 4 semicolon 1, 4 semicolon 3 and 1 semicolon 3 is 3 units by 2 units, area 6 square units. Enlarged by scale factor 2, its vertices become 2 semicolon 2, 8 semicolon 2, 8 semicolon 6 and 2 semicolon 6, a rectangle 6 units by 4 units with area 24 square units. The area has been multiplied by 4, which is 2 squared. The perimeter went from 10 units to 20 units, multiplied by 2.

The right triangle ABC from earlier, with A at 1 semicolon 1, B at 3 semicolon 1 and C at 1 semicolon 2, has legs 2 and 1 and area 1 square unit. Enlarged by scale factor 3, its legs become 6 and 3, and its area is half of 6 times 3, which is 9 square units, 3 squared times the original. Reduced by one half, triangle PQR with base 6 and height 4, area 12, becomes a triangle with base 3 and height 2, area 3, one quarter of 12.

Two enlargements about the origin combine by multiplying their scale factors. Enlarging by 2 and then by 1.5 sends x semicolon y to 2 x semicolon 2 y and then to 3 x semicolon 3 y, a single enlargement by scale factor 3. Enlarging by 4 and then reducing by one half is a single enlargement by scale factor 2. Unlike the reflection and translation of the previous lessons, enlargements about the same centre can be done in either order.

A digital image works in exactly this way. A picture on a phone has pixels at co-ordinates, and enlarging it to 150 percent multiplies every co-ordinate by 1.5. The width and the height each grow by a factor of 1.5, so the picture covers 2.25 times the area of the screen.

The error museum, five exhibits. Exhibit one: adding k instead of multiplying, so that scale factor 2 sends 3 semicolon 1 to 5 semicolon 3, which is a translation. Exhibit two: multiplying only one co-ordinate, which stretches the figure instead of enlarging it. Exhibit three: using the rule when the centre of enlargement is not the origin; x semicolon y becomes k x semicolon k y works only for the origin as centre. Exhibit four: giving the image area as k times the original area. Exhibit five: accepting a mapping as an enlargement without checking that the quotient is the same for both co-ordinates of every vertex.

Layout for marks: state the rule with the scale factor, write the image of every vertex with its working, plot the image, label it with dashes, and where asked state the effect on lengths, perimeter and area. The questions for this section are with you now: areas from co-ordinates, the digital image, and the five exhibits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through multiplying both co-ordinates, halving to bring a shape home, and rays from the origin — plain words, same rules.

## Subtopic: Multiply Both Co-ordinates

To make a shape on the grid bigger, with the origin as the centre, multiply both numbers of every corner by the scale factor. The rule is x semicolon y becomes k x semicolon k y.

Triangle with corners 1 semicolon 1, 3 semicolon 1 and 1 semicolon 2. Times 2: 2 semicolon 2, 6 semicolon 2 and 2 semicolon 4. Times 3: 3 semicolon 3, 9 semicolon 3 and 3 semicolon 6. Each time, the sides grow by the same scale factor and the shape stays the same.

Both numbers, same multiplier. Multiply only the first number and the shape gets stretched sideways, which is not the same shape. Negative numbers keep their sign: negative 2 semicolon 4 times 3 is negative 6 semicolon 12.

A school logo drawn big on a grid can be shrunk for a badge by multiplying every corner by a small scale factor, and grown for a banner by multiplying by a big one. Same logo, different size.

To find k, divide the new numbers by the old ones. 4 semicolon negative 2 going to 10 semicolon negative 5: both divisions give 2.5, so k is 2.5. Your questions on this section are ready: multiplying both numbers, the stretched mistake, and finding k by dividing.

## Subtopic: Halving Brings It Home

A scale factor smaller than 1 shrinks the shape and pulls it towards the origin. Halve every number: 4 semicolon 6 becomes 2 semicolon 3, 8 semicolon 2 becomes 4 semicolon 1, and 2 semicolon 2 becomes 1 semicolon 1.

Other fractions work the same. A quarter of 8 semicolon 12 is 2 semicolon 3. Three quarters of it is 6 semicolon 9. A third means divide by 3. Halving 3 semicolon 5 gives 1.5 semicolon 2.5, which sits between grid lines; plot it there and do not round it.

Doubling undoes halving, and tripling undoes taking a third. So if a shape was enlarged by 3 and a corner landed on 3 semicolon negative 6, divide by 3 to get back to the start: 1 semicolon negative 2.

Shrinking the carpet works like last lesson: half the sides means a quarter of the area. The questions for this section: halving, other fractions, undoing a scale factor, and points between grid lines.

## Subtopic: Rays from the Origin

Draw a line from the origin through a corner, and keep going. That is a ray. Enlarging by 2 slides the corner along its ray until it is twice as far from the origin. 3 semicolon 4 is 5 away from the origin; 6 semicolon 8 is on the same ray, 10 away.

So rays tell you whether a picture shows a real enlargement. Join each corner to its image and extend the lines: they should all meet at the centre. Sides of the image are parallel to the matching sides of the original.

Numbers tell you too. 2 semicolon 3 going to 4 semicolon 9 is not an enlargement, because the first number doubled but the second number tripled. Both must use the same multiplier.

Watch for the usual traps: adding k instead of multiplying, multiplying only one number, using the rule when the centre is not the origin, area times k instead of k squared, and skipping the check. Multiply both; halve to shrink; rays from the origin. The final questions of the lesson are with you now: rays, checking an image, and the traps.
