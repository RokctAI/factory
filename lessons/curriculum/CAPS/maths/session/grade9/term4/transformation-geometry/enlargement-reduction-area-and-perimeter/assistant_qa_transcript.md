### subtopic_1 — Same Shape, Different Size
**Assistant:** Why does adding the same amount to every side not give a similar figure?

**Tutor:** Because similarity is about ratios, and adding a constant changes the ratios between the sides, while multiplying keeps them. Take the rectangle 4 centimetres by 3 centimetres. Its long side is one and a third times its short side, and that ratio is what gives the rectangle its particular shape. Multiply both sides by 2 and you get 8 by 6, and 8 is still one and a third times 6, so the shape is the same, only bigger. Add 2 to both sides instead and you get 6 by 5, and 6 is only 1.2 times 5. The rectangle has become squarer. If you kept adding, say 100 to each side, you would get 104 by 103, which is almost a square, and nobody would call that the same shape as the original. Adding affects short sides relatively more than long sides, because 2 centimetres is two thirds of a 3 centimetre side but only half of a 4 centimetre side. Multiplying affects every side by the same proportion, which is precisely what keeps the angles and the shape intact. This is why the test for similarity divides: every image side divided by its original side must give the same scale factor. You see the effect in everyday life when someone resizes a photo on a phone by dragging only one edge: the faces become stretched or squashed because the two dimensions were not scaled by the same factor. Dragging the corner multiplies both together and the picture stays true.

### subtopic_2 — Perimeter Multiplies by k
**Assistant:** Is the perimeter rule true for shapes with curved edges, like circles?

**Tutor:** Yes, and the circle is a good way to see that the rule does not depend on the figure being a polygon. The circumference of a circle is 2 times pi times the radius. If the radius is multiplied by a scale factor k, the new circumference is 2 times pi times k times the radius, and by rearranging the multiplication that is k times the original circumference. So a circle of radius 3 centimetres, with circumference about 18.85 centimetres, enlarged by scale factor 2, becomes a circle of radius 6 centimetres with circumference about 37.70 centimetres, exactly twice as much. For an irregular curved shape, think of approximating its boundary by many tiny straight pieces. Each piece is multiplied by k under the enlargement, so their total is multiplied by k, and as the pieces become smaller and smaller the total approaches the true length of the curve, which is therefore also multiplied by k. The general principle is that any length measured in the figure, whether a side, a diagonal, a height, a radius, a curved boundary or the distance between two marked points, is multiplied by the same scale factor k. That is what an enlargement means. Perimeter is just one particular length, so it obeys the rule automatically. A practical example is a circular running track drawn on a plan: if the plan is at scale factor one five-hundredth, then the real lane length is 500 times the length measured around the curve on paper.

### subtopic_3 — Area Multiplies by k Squared
**Assistant:** How do I find the scale factor when I am only told the two areas?

**Tutor:** Divide the image area by the original area to get k squared, and then take the square root to get k. The key is to remember which quantity you have found at each step. Suppose two similar triangles have areas 20 square centimetres and 180 square centimetres. The area ratio is 180 divided by 20, which is 9. That 9 is not the scale factor; it is the scale factor squared, because area is multiplied by k squared. So k is the square root of 9, which is 3, and every side of the larger triangle is 3 times the corresponding side of the smaller. If a side of the small triangle is 5 centimetres, the matching side of the large one is 15 centimetres, not 45. Reductions work the same way. If the areas go from 50 square centimetres to 8 square centimetres, the ratio is 0.16, and the square root of 0.16 is 0.4, so the sides are reduced to four tenths of their length. When the ratio is not a perfect square, use a calculator and round sensibly: an area ratio of 2 gives a scale factor of about 1.41. This explains a fact about paper sizes: an A3 sheet has twice the area of an A4 sheet, so its sides are about 1.41 times as long, which is why an A4 page enlarges neatly onto A3 on a photocopier at about 141 percent. Always check by squaring your k: it must give back the area ratio.

### subtopic_4 — Proportion Problems and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Why does doubling a pizza's diameter give so much more than double the pizza?

**Tutor:** Because the amount of pizza is an area, so it is multiplied by the square of the scale factor, and 2 squared is 4. The diameter is a length. Doubling it is an enlargement with scale factor 2, so the circumference, the crust around the edge, doubles, but the area of the top, where the cheese and toppings sit, becomes 4 times as large. A 20 centimetre pizza has area pi times 10 squared, about 314 square centimetres; a 40 centimetre pizza has area pi times 20 squared, about 1257 square centimetres, four times as much. Even a smaller change makes a large difference: going from 20 centimetres to 30 centimetres is a scale factor of 1.5, and the area is multiplied by 2.25, more than double. The same reasoning explains why an enlarged mural needs so much paint, why carpeting a room twice as long and twice as wide costs four times as much, and why a map's areas shrink so dramatically. Shops often price larger sizes as if the amount grew in proportion to the diameter, so comparing the price per square centimetre can reveal which size is the better buy. The habit to build is to ask, for every quantity in a scaling problem, whether it is a length, scaling by k, or an area, scaling by k squared. Most errors in this topic come from skipping that one question and using k for everything.
