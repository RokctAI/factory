# Part 1 — Expert

Last lesson applied transformations: given a rule, find the image. Today runs the process backwards. The CAPS instruction is to identify the transformation of a point given the co-ordinates of its image, and the skill is detective work: compare the original co-ordinates with the image co-ordinates, look for a sign change, a swap, or a constant shift, and name the transformation that produces exactly that pattern. We build a table of the seven patterns from reflections, rotations and translations, meet the cases where a single point is ambiguous and a second point is needed to decide, describe each transformation fully, with its mirror line, its centre and angle and direction, or its shift, and finish with combined transformations, where two moves in a row can be equivalent to a single one, and where the order of the moves can change the result.

## Subtopic: Reading the Rule from a Point and Its Image

Each transformation of Grade 9 leaves a recognisable fingerprint on the co-ordinates, and identifying it means comparing the image with the original position by position. Use the point 3 semicolon 5 as the original and look at what each transformation produces.

If the image is 3 semicolon negative 5, the first co-ordinate is unchanged and the second has changed sign: reflection in the x-axis. If the image is negative 3 semicolon 5, the first has changed sign and the second is unchanged: reflection in the y-axis. If the image is 5 semicolon 3, the co-ordinates have swapped with no sign change: reflection in the line y equals x. If the image is negative 3 semicolon negative 5, both have changed sign without swapping: rotation through 180 degrees about the origin. If the image is negative 5 semicolon 3, the co-ordinates have swapped and the new first co-ordinate is negative: rotation through 90 degrees anticlockwise about the origin. If the image is 5 semicolon negative 3, they have swapped and the new second co-ordinate is negative: rotation through 90 degrees clockwise about the origin.

The seventh pattern is the translation, in which neither a swap nor a simple sign change explains the image, but the same amount has been added to each co-ordinate. If 3 semicolon 5 maps to 7 semicolon 2, then x has increased by 4 and y has decreased by 3: a translation 4 units right and 3 units down, with rule x semicolon y becomes x plus 4 semicolon y minus 3. The shift is always found as image minus original: 7 minus 3 is 4, and 2 minus 5 is negative 3.

So the identification procedure has three questions in order. Have the co-ordinates swapped? Which signs have changed? If neither explains it, what constant has been added to each co-ordinate? The answers point to exactly one row of the table in most cases.

A useful supporting check is the quadrant. A point in the first quadrant whose image lies in the second has moved to the left side of the y-axis, which a reflection in the y-axis or a quarter turn anticlockwise could achieve, but a reflection in the x-axis could not. The questions for this section are with you now: the six fingerprints of reflections and rotations, the translation found as image minus original, and the three questions in order.

## Subtopic: When One Point Is Not Enough

Some points are special, and for them two different transformations produce the same image. A single point and its image then cannot decide between them, and a second point is needed.

The point 2 semicolon negative 2 maps to negative 2 semicolon 2. Reflection in y equals x swaps the co-ordinates, giving negative 2 semicolon 2; rotation through 180 degrees changes both signs, also giving negative 2 semicolon 2. Both fit, and so does a translation 4 left and 4 up. The point 0 semicolon 4 maps to 0 semicolon negative 4: reflection in the x-axis fits, and so does rotation through 180 degrees, because negative 0 is 0. The point 4 semicolon 0 maps to 0 semicolon 4: reflection in y equals x fits, and so does rotation through 90 degrees anticlockwise. The point 3 semicolon 3 maps to negative 3 semicolon 3: reflection in the y-axis and rotation through 90 degrees anticlockwise both fit.

The trouble comes from points on the axes or on the lines y equals x and y equals negative x, where a co-ordinate is zero or the two co-ordinates are equal in size. A point whose two co-ordinates are non-zero and of different sizes, such as 3 semicolon 5, is never ambiguous between the reflections and rotations, because each of the six fingerprints gives a different image.

The remedy is a second point. Suppose a figure has A at 2 semicolon negative 2 and B at 4 semicolon 1, with images A dash at negative 2 semicolon 2 and B dash at 1 semicolon 4. The A pair allows reflection in y equals x, rotation through 180 degrees, or a translation. Test each on B. Reflection in y equals x takes 4 semicolon 1 to 1 semicolon 4, which matches. Rotation through 180 degrees takes it to negative 4 semicolon negative 1, which does not. The translation 4 left and 4 up takes it to 0 semicolon 5, which does not. The transformation is reflection in the line y equals x, and only that.

Orientation gives a second test. If the original figure reads anticlockwise and the image reads clockwise, the transformation is a reflection; if the orientation is unchanged, it is a rotation or a translation. And if the segments joining every point to its image are all the same length and direction, it is a translation. Your questions on this section are ready: the four ambiguous examples, testing candidates on a second point, and orientation as a deciding test.

## Subtopic: Describing the Transformation Fully

Identifying the transformation is half the answer; describing it fully is the other half, and marks are allocated to each detail. A reflection needs its mirror line: reflection in the x-axis, the y-axis or the line y equals x. A translation needs its distance and direction in both co-ordinates, or its rule: 3 units right and 4 units down, x semicolon y becomes x plus 3 semicolon y minus 4. A rotation needs three things: the centre, the angle and the direction. Rotation through 90 degrees clockwise about the origin is complete; rotation through 90 degrees is not. A rotation through 180 degrees needs no direction, since half a turn either way ends in the same place, but it still needs its centre.

Work through a full example. Triangle PQR has P at 1 semicolon 1, Q at 3 semicolon 1 and R at 3 semicolon 4, and its image has P dash at negative 1 semicolon 1, Q dash at negative 1 semicolon 3 and R dash at negative 4 semicolon 3. P alone allows reflection in the y-axis or a quarter turn anticlockwise. Test Q: reflection in the y-axis would give negative 3 semicolon 1, but the image is negative 1 semicolon 3, which is the swap with the new first co-ordinate negative. Test R: negative 4 semicolon 3, the same pattern. The full description is a rotation through 90 degrees anticlockwise about the origin, with rule x semicolon y becomes negative y semicolon x, and it checks on every vertex.

For a translation, the rule found from one point applies to all. A at negative 2 semicolon 3 maps to A dash at 1 semicolon negative 1, so the shift is 1 minus negative 2, which is 3, and negative 1 minus 3, which is negative 4: 3 right and 4 down. Then B at 0 semicolon 5 must map to 3 semicolon 1, which can be checked against the given image.

The centre of a 180 degree rotation that is not the origin can be found as the midpoint of any point and its image, because the centre lies halfway along the segment joining them. P at 5 semicolon 3 and its image negative 1 semicolon negative 1 have midpoint 2 semicolon 1, so the transformation is a rotation through 180 degrees about 2 semicolon 1. The questions for this section are ready: the three details of a rotation, the full description of the PQR rotation, the translation rule from one point, and the midpoint as the centre of a half turn.

## Subtopic: Combined Transformations and the Error Museum

A point can be transformed twice in a row, and the combined effect is often a single familiar transformation. Reflect 2 semicolon 5 in the x-axis to get 2 semicolon negative 5, then reflect that in the y-axis to get negative 2 semicolon negative 5. Both signs have changed: the two reflections together are a rotation through 180 degrees about the origin. In rules, x semicolon y becomes x semicolon negative y, which becomes negative x semicolon negative y.

Reflect 2 semicolon 5 in y equals x to get 5 semicolon 2, then in the x-axis to get 5 semicolon negative 2. The combined rule is x semicolon y becomes y semicolon negative x, which is rotation through 90 degrees clockwise. Two reflections in lines through the origin always combine into a rotation, which is why orientation, reversed twice, comes back to the original.

Order can matter. Take 3 semicolon 1. Reflect in the y-axis, then translate 2 right: negative 3 semicolon 1, then negative 1 semicolon 1. Now translate 2 right first, then reflect in the y-axis: 5 semicolon 1, then negative 5 semicolon 1. The results differ, so a combined transformation must be carried out in the order stated. Two translations, or two rotations about the same centre, can be done in either order, but a reflection and a translation generally cannot be swapped.

The error museum, five exhibits. Exhibit one: deciding from a single ambiguous point, naming reflection in y equals x for 2 semicolon negative 2 to negative 2 semicolon 2 without checking another vertex. Exhibit two: an incomplete description, rotation through 90 degrees with no centre or direction, or reflection with no mirror line. Exhibit three: confusing reflection in y equals x with a quarter turn, overlooking the sign change that only the rotation makes. Exhibit four: finding a translation as original minus image, which reverses the direction. Exhibit five: carrying out combined transformations in the wrong order.

Layout for marks: write the original and image co-ordinates side by side for every vertex, state the pattern, swap, sign change or shift, name the transformation with every detail, give the rule in arrow notation, and confirm it on all the vertices. The questions for this section are with you now: two reflections making a rotation, order mattering, and the five exhibits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through spotting the sign change and the swap, checking a second point, and two moves in a row — plain words, same rules.

## Subtopic: Spot the Sign Change and the Swap

You are given a point and where it ended up, and you have to say what happened. Line the two up and ask two questions: did the numbers swap places, and which signs flipped?

Start with 3 semicolon 5. Ended at 3 semicolon negative 5: no swap, second sign flipped, so it was reflected in the x-axis. Ended at negative 3 semicolon 5: no swap, first sign flipped, reflected in the y-axis. Ended at 5 semicolon 3: swap, no sign flipped, reflected in the slanted line y equals x. Ended at negative 3 semicolon negative 5: no swap, both signs flipped, half a turn about the origin.

The quarter turns swap and flip one sign. Ended at negative 5 semicolon 3: swap and the new first number is negative, a quarter turn anticlockwise. Ended at 5 semicolon negative 3: swap and the new second number is negative, a quarter turn clockwise.

If nothing swapped and no sign flipped in a neat way, look for a slide. 3 semicolon 5 to 7 semicolon 2: the first number went up 4 and the second went down 3, so it slid 4 right and 3 down. Always work out the slide as where it ended take away where it started. Your questions on this section are ready: the swap question, the sign question, the two quarter turns, and the slide.

## Subtopic: Check a Second Point

Sometimes one point fools you. 2 semicolon negative 2 ending at negative 2 semicolon 2 could be a swap, a reflection in y equals x, or both signs flipping, a half turn. The numbers are the same size, so you cannot tell which. Points on the axes are tricky too: 0 semicolon 4 ending at 0 semicolon negative 4 could be a reflection in the x-axis or a half turn, because a zero does not care about its sign.

So check a second point. If the shape also has B at 4 semicolon 1, and B ends up at 1 semicolon 4, that is a swap with no sign change, which only the y equals x mirror does. Half a turn would have sent B to negative 4 semicolon negative 1. Case closed.

A second clue is the direction the corners read. Mirrors flip the order, so corners that went anticlockwise come back clockwise. Slides and turns keep the order. And if every point moved by exactly the same slide, it was a translation.

When you name the move, give every detail. A mirror needs its line. A slide needs how far right or left and how far up or down. A turn needs its pin, how far and which way: a quarter turn clockwise about the origin. The questions for this section: the tricky points, testing with a second point, the flipped order, and naming every detail.

## Subtopic: Two Moves in a Row

Do two moves one after the other and they often add up to one move you already know. Mirror 2 semicolon 5 in the x-axis: 2 semicolon negative 5. Then mirror in the y-axis: negative 2 semicolon negative 5. Both signs flipped, so two mirrors made a half turn.

Mirror 2 semicolon 5 in y equals x: 5 semicolon 2. Then in the x-axis: 5 semicolon negative 2. Swapped, second number negative: a quarter turn clockwise. Two flips undo the flipping, which is why two mirrors make a turn.

But the order of moves can matter. Take 3 semicolon 1. Mirror in the y-axis then slide 2 right: negative 3 semicolon 1, then negative 1 semicolon 1. Slide 2 right first, then mirror: 5 semicolon 1, then negative 5 semicolon 1. Different answers, so always do the moves in the order you are told.

Watch for the usual traps: deciding from one tricky point, leaving out the pin or the direction of a turn, mixing up the swap mirror with a quarter turn, working out a slide backwards, and doing two moves in the wrong order. Swap and signs; second point; two moves in order. The final questions of the lesson are with you now: two mirrors making a turn, order mattering, and the traps.
