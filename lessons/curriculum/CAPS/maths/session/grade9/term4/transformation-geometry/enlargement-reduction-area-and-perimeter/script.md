# Part 1 — Expert

The last two lessons moved figures without changing their size. Today the size changes. The CAPS instruction is to use proportion to describe the effect of enlargement or reduction on area and perimeter, and the central result is short enough to memorise and important enough to understand: when every length of a figure is multiplied by a scale factor k, the perimeter is multiplied by k and the area is multiplied by k squared. We define enlargement and reduction through the scale factor and similar figures, prove the perimeter rule, prove the area rule, and then use both in proportion problems from printing, building and maps, finishing with the error museum.

## Subtopic: Same Shape, Different Size

An enlargement or a reduction changes the size of a figure while keeping its shape. Every length is multiplied by the same number, the scale factor k, and every angle stays exactly as it was. The original and the image are similar figures: corresponding angles are equal and corresponding sides are in proportion.

When k is greater than 1 the image is bigger, an enlargement. When k is between 0 and 1 the image is smaller, a reduction. When k equals 1 the image is the same size, which is congruence. A rectangle 4 centimetres long and 3 centimetres wide, enlarged by scale factor 2, becomes 8 centimetres by 6 centimetres. A triangle with sides 6, 8 and 10 centimetres, reduced by scale factor one half, becomes a triangle with sides 3, 4 and 5 centimetres.

The scale factor is found by dividing an image length by the corresponding original length. If a side of 5 centimetres becomes 12.5 centimetres, the scale factor is 12.5 divided by 5, which is 2.5. If a side of 12 centimetres becomes 4 centimetres, the scale factor is 4 divided by 12, which is one third, a reduction. The scale factor must be the same for every pair of corresponding sides, and that gives the test for similarity. A rectangle 4 by 3 and a rectangle 8 by 5 are not similar: 8 divided by 4 is 2, but 5 divided by 3 is not 2.

That test rules out a common false idea of enlargement, which is adding the same amount to every side. Adding 2 centimetres to each side of the 4 by 3 rectangle gives 6 by 5, and 6 divided by 4 is 1.5 while 5 divided by 3 is about 1.67. The shape has changed: the long side is now relatively shorter. Enlargement multiplies; it never adds.

A photograph printed at 10 centimetres by 15 centimetres and enlarged to 20 by 30 keeps its proportions, because both sides doubled. Cropped to 20 by 25 it would not. The questions for this section are with you now: the meaning of k greater or less than 1, finding the scale factor by division, and the multiplying test for similarity.

## Subtopic: Perimeter Multiplies by k

Perimeter is a length, the total length of the boundary, so it behaves exactly like a side. The rectangle 4 by 3 has perimeter 4 plus 3 plus 4 plus 3, which is 14 centimetres. Its enlargement by scale factor 2, 8 by 6, has perimeter 8 plus 6 plus 8 plus 6, which is 28 centimetres, and 28 is 2 times 14.

The reason is the distributive law. If the sides of a figure are a, b, c and so on, its perimeter is a plus b plus c and so on. After enlargement by k, the sides are k a, k b, k c, and the perimeter is k a plus k b plus k c, which equals k times the bracket of a plus b plus c. Every term carries the same factor k, so the sum carries it too. This works for every polygon, and for circles: a circle of radius r has circumference 2 pi r, and a circle of radius k r has circumference 2 pi k r, which is k times as much.

Try a triangle with sides 3, 4 and 5 centimetres, perimeter 12 centimetres. Enlarged by scale factor 3, its sides are 9, 12 and 15 centimetres, and its perimeter is 36 centimetres, which is 3 times 12. Reductions follow the same rule. A rectangular vegetable bed 12 metres by 9 metres has perimeter 42 metres. A plan of it drawn at scale factor one third shows a rectangle 4 by 3 with perimeter 14, which is one third of 42.

The rule runs backwards too. If two similar figures have perimeters 20 centimetres and 50 centimetres, then the scale factor from the first to the second is 50 divided by 20, which is 2.5, and every side of the second is 2.5 times the corresponding side of the first. If one side of the first is 6 centimetres, the corresponding side of the second is 15 centimetres.

In practical terms, anything measured along an edge scales by k: fencing around a field, skirting board around a room, edging around a tablecloth, piping around a cake. A school that doubles the length and the width of its netball practice area needs exactly twice as much fencing. The questions for this section are ready: perimeter times k, the distributive reason, perimeters of reductions, and finding k from two perimeters.

## Subtopic: Area Multiplies by k Squared

Area behaves differently, because area involves two lengths multiplied together. The 4 by 3 rectangle has area 12 square centimetres. The 8 by 6 rectangle has area 48 square centimetres, which is 4 times 12, not 2 times 12. Doubling the length and doubling the width doubles the area twice.

The proof for a rectangle with length l and width b: its area is l times b. After enlargement by k the sides are k l and k b, and the area is k l times k b, which is k squared times l b. The factor k appears once from each dimension. The same argument works for a triangle, half of k times base times k times height, which is k squared times the original area, and for a circle, pi times k r squared, which is k squared times pi r squared. Since every polygon can be cut into triangles, the rule holds for all of them.

You can also see it on squared paper. Draw a 1 by 1 square and enlarge it by scale factor 3. The image is 3 by 3, and nine copies of the original square fit inside it. Enlarge by scale factor 2 and four copies fit. So k equals 2 gives area times 4, and k equals 3 gives area times 9.

The 3, 4, 5 right triangle has area half of 3 times 4, which is 6 square centimetres. Enlarged by scale factor 3, its legs are 9 and 12, and its area is half of 9 times 12, which is 54 square centimetres, and 54 is 9 times 6. For a reduction by scale factor one half, the area is multiplied by one quarter: a 12 by 8 poster of area 96 square centimetres reduces to 6 by 4, area 24 square centimetres.

Running the rule backwards needs a square root. Two similar figures have areas 20 and 180 square centimetres. The area ratio is 180 divided by 20, which is 9, so k squared is 9 and k is 3. The lengths have tripled, not multiplied by 9. If the areas are 50 and 8, the ratio is 0.16, so k is 0.4, a reduction.

The units give a reliable reminder. Perimeter is measured in centimetres or metres, a single length unit, so it picks up a single factor of k. Area is measured in square centimetres or square metres, a length unit times a length unit, so it picks up k twice. Whenever a quantity is measured in squared units, its scale factor is squared too. Later, volume, measured in cubic units, will pick up k three times, but in this topic only lengths and areas are needed.

Maps show how large k squared can be. On a map with scale 1 to 50 000, 1 centimetre stands for 50 000 centimetres, which is half a kilometre. A square centimetre on the map stands for a square half a kilometre on each side, which is a quarter of a square kilometre, or 2.5 billion square centimetres: 50 000 squared. The questions for this section are with you now: area times k squared, the proof with length and width, the squared-paper picture, and finding k from two areas with a square root.

## Subtopic: Proportion Problems and the Error Museum

Every problem in this topic is a proportion problem with one decision at its heart: is the quantity a length or an area? Lengths scale by k; areas scale by k squared.

A school mural is designed on a sheet 30 centimetres by 20 centimetres and painted on a wall 6 metres by 4 metres. The scale factor is 600 centimetres divided by 30 centimetres, which is 20. The border tape along the edge of the design is 100 centimetres long, so on the wall it is 20 times as long, 2000 centimetres, which is 20 metres. The design used 10 millilitres of paint covering 600 square centimetres; the wall is 20 squared, which is 400, times the area, so it needs 400 times as much paint, 4 litres, assuming the same coverage. Checking directly: 6 metres by 4 metres is 24 square metres, and 600 square centimetres times 400 is 240 000 square centimetres, which is 24 square metres.

A photograph enlarged from 10 by 15 to 20 by 30 centimetres uses 4 times as much paper, because k is 2. If the small print costs R5 in paper, the large print costs about R20 in paper, not R10. A pizza with a 30 centimetre diameter has k equal to 1.5 compared with a 20 centimetre pizza, so it has 1.5 squared, which is 2.25, times as much pizza on top, though it costs much less than 2.25 times as much in most shops. The scale factor turns a guess into a decision.

A builder's plan at scale factor one hundredth shows a room 5 centimetres by 4 centimetres. The room is 5 metres by 4 metres, with perimeter 18 metres for skirting and area 20 square metres for tiles. The plan's perimeter is 18 centimetres, one hundredth of 18 metres; the plan's area is 20 square centimetres, one ten-thousandth of 20 square metres.

The error museum, five exhibits. Exhibit one: multiplying area by k, so doubling the sides of a 12 square centimetre rectangle is said to give 24, when the answer is 48. Exhibit two: multiplying perimeter by k squared, the reverse mistake. Exhibit three: enlarging by adding a constant to every side, which changes the shape. Exhibit four: finding k from an area ratio without the square root, saying lengths multiply by 9 when areas multiply by 9. Exhibit five: forgetting that a reduction has k less than 1, so that a reduction by one third is wrongly calculated as dividing area by 3 instead of by 9.

Layout for marks: state the scale factor with its working, say whether each quantity is a length or an area, apply k or k squared, include units, and check one answer directly from the image dimensions. The questions for this section are with you now: the mural, the photograph, the plan, and the five exhibits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through same shape bigger or smaller, fence times k, and carpet times k squared — plain words, same rules.

## Subtopic: Same Shape, Bigger or Smaller

Making a shape bigger or smaller without changing its shape means multiplying every side by the same number. That number is the scale factor, called k. Bigger than 1, the shape grows. Between 0 and 1, the shape shrinks. The angles never change.

A rectangle 4 by 3 with k equal to 2 becomes 8 by 6. A triangle 6, 8, 10 with k equal to one half becomes 3, 4, 5. To find k, divide a new side by its old side: 12.5 over 5 is 2.5.

Adding is not enlarging. Add 2 to each side of the 4 by 3 rectangle and you get 6 by 5, which is a squarer shape, not the same shape bigger. Always multiply.

A photo 10 by 15 printed at 20 by 30 is the same picture bigger, because both sides doubled. Your questions on this section are ready: what k means, finding k by dividing, and why adding is wrong.

## Subtopic: Fence Times k

Perimeter is the length of the fence around a shape. Since every side gets multiplied by k, the whole fence gets multiplied by k.

The 4 by 3 rectangle needs 14 centimetres of fence. The 8 by 6 rectangle needs 28, exactly double. The 3, 4, 5 triangle has a fence of 12; times 3, the triangle 9, 12, 15 has a fence of 36, exactly triple.

Shrinking works the same way. A vegetable bed 12 by 9 metres needs 42 metres of fence. A plan at one third shows 4 by 3, with a fence of 14 on paper, one third of 42.

Working backwards, a fence that grew from 20 to 50 means k is 50 over 20, which is 2.5. Edging, skirting, piping, fencing: anything along an edge is times k. The questions for this section: fence times k, shrinking fences, and k from two fences.

## Subtopic: Carpet Times k Squared

Area is the carpet that covers the inside. Carpet has a length and a width, and both get multiplied by k, so the carpet gets multiplied by k twice: k squared.

The 4 by 3 rectangle needs 12 squares of carpet. The 8 by 6 rectangle needs 48, which is 4 times as much, not double. Draw it: four copies of the small rectangle fit inside the big one. With k equal to 3, nine copies fit.

Shrinking by one half makes the carpet a quarter. A 12 by 8 poster has area 96; at half size, 6 by 4, it has area 24.

The same idea tells you how much paint a bigger mural needs. Make the wall picture 20 times longer and 20 times taller, and you need 400 times as much paint, because the paint covers the inside.

Backwards needs a square root. Carpet grew from 20 to 180, nine times as much, so the sides grew three times. Watch for the usual traps: area times k instead of k squared, fence times k squared, adding instead of multiplying, and forgetting the square root. Fence times k; carpet times k squared. The final questions of the lesson are with you now: carpet times k squared, the four copies picture, shrinking, and the square root.
