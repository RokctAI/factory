### subtopic_1 — Reading the Rule from a Point and Its Image
**Assistant:** Why is a translation found as image minus original, and not the other way round?

**Tutor:** Because a translation describes the movement from where the point started to where it ended, and the change in any quantity is always the final value minus the starting value. If 3 semicolon 5 moves to 7 semicolon 2, the x co-ordinate went from 3 to 7, a change of 7 minus 3, which is positive 4, meaning 4 units to the right. The y co-ordinate went from 5 to 2, a change of 2 minus 5, which is negative 3, meaning 3 units down. So the rule is x semicolon y becomes x plus 4 semicolon y minus 3, and applying it to the original gives back the image, which is the check. Subtracting the other way, original minus image, gives negative 4 and positive 3, which describes the movement from the image back to the original: 4 left and 3 up. That is the inverse translation, the move that undoes the first one, and it is the commonest error in identification questions, because both subtractions produce the right numbers with the wrong signs. The same idea of final minus initial appears throughout mathematics and science: a temperature change, a change in a bank balance, the gradient of a line as change in y over change in x. So when you meet a translation, say it aloud as from the original to the image, compute image minus original for each co-ordinate, and then test the rule on the original point. If it lands on the image, the direction is right.

### subtopic_2 — When One Point Is Not Enough
**Assistant:** How do I know when a point is one of the ambiguous ones?

**Tutor:** Look at its co-ordinates before you start: a point is ambiguous when one co-ordinate is zero, or when the two co-ordinates have the same size. Those are the points that lie on the axes or on the diagonal lines y equals x and y equals negative x, and they are special because some transformations treat them identically. A zero has no sign to change, so for 0 semicolon 4 the rule that changes the sign of x, reflection in the y-axis, does nothing at all, and the rule that changes both signs, a half turn, has the same effect as changing only y, reflection in the x-axis. When the co-ordinates are equal in size, swapping them can look the same as changing signs: 2 semicolon negative 2 swapped is negative 2 semicolon 2, and with both signs changed it is also negative 2 semicolon 2. For any point whose co-ordinates are non-zero and of different sizes, such as 3 semicolon 5 or 4 semicolon 1, all six reflection and rotation fingerprints produce six different images, so one point decides. In practice, then, when you are given a whole figure, choose the vertex with the least special co-ordinates and identify the transformation from it, and then confirm on the other vertices. If you are given only an ambiguous point, the correct answer is to list every transformation that fits and say that more information is needed, which shows exactly the understanding the question is testing.

### subtopic_3 — Describing the Transformation Fully
**Assistant:** Why does a rotation need three details when a reflection only needs one?

**Tutor:** Because a reflection is completely fixed by its mirror line, while a rotation needs three independent choices before it is fixed. Once you know the mirror, every point's image is determined: it lies on the perpendicular from the point to the mirror, the same distance on the other side. There is nothing else to choose. A rotation, by contrast, could turn about any point in the plane, so the centre must be given; it could turn through any angle, so the angle must be given; and for any angle other than 180 degrees, turning clockwise and turning anticlockwise land in different places, so the direction must be given. Leave any one out and many different transformations fit your description. Rotation through 90 degrees about the origin could send 1 semicolon 2 to negative 2 semicolon 1 or to 2 semicolon negative 1. Rotation through 90 degrees clockwise could have its centre anywhere. Only the half turn lets you drop the direction, since turning 180 degrees either way ends in the same position, but even it needs its centre, which you can find as the midpoint of any point and its image. A translation needs two details, the horizontal shift and the vertical shift, which the arrow-notation rule carries in one line. Memoranda allocate a mark to each required detail, so a habit of writing reflection in the line y equals x, or rotation through 90 degrees anticlockwise about the origin, in full, protects marks that a correct but incomplete identification loses.

### subtopic_4 — Combined Transformations and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Why do two reflections in a row make a rotation and not another reflection?

**Tutor:** Because each reflection reverses orientation, and reversing it twice restores it, so the combined transformation keeps orientation, which a reflection never does. Think about a triangle labelled anticlockwise. After the first reflection it reads clockwise; after the second it reads anticlockwise again. A transformation that preserves lengths, angles and orientation, and keeps a point fixed, is a rotation. Both mirror lines in our examples pass through the origin, so the origin stays fixed throughout, and it becomes the centre of the combined rotation. The co-ordinates show it directly. Reflecting in the x-axis and then the y-axis changes the sign of y and then the sign of x, so both signs change, which is the half turn. Reflecting in y equals x and then in the x-axis swaps the co-ordinates and then changes the sign of the new second one, which is the rule for a quarter turn clockwise. There is a beautiful general fact behind this: the angle of the combined rotation is twice the angle between the two mirror lines. The x-axis and the y-axis meet at 90 degrees, giving a 180 degree rotation; the line y equals x and the x-axis meet at 45 degrees, giving a 90 degree rotation. It also explains why order matters for the second example: reflecting in the x-axis first and then in y equals x gives the quarter turn anticlockwise instead, because the direction of the rotation depends on which mirror is used first.
