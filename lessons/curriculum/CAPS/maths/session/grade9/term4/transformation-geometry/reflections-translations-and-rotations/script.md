# Part 1 — Expert

Transformation geometry moves figures around the Cartesian plane according to exact rules, and every rule can be written as what happens to the co-ordinates of a point. The CAPS instruction for today is to transform points, line segments and simple figures on a co-ordinate plane by reflection in the x-axis, the y-axis or the line y equals x, by translation, and by rotation about a given point. We work throughout with triangle ABC, with A at 1 semicolon 2, B at 4 semicolon 2 and C at 1 semicolon 4, and transform it each way, writing each rule in the arrow notation x semicolon y becomes a new pair. Along the way we establish what stays the same, side lengths, angles and the size and shape of the figure, and what changes, position and sometimes orientation. The next lesson runs the process backwards: given a point and its image, identify the transformation.

## Subtopic: Reflection in the Axes

A reflection flips a figure over a mirror line. Each point of the image is the same perpendicular distance from the mirror as the original point, on the opposite side, and the segment joining a point to its image crosses the mirror at right angles. The image is labelled with a dash: the image of A is A dash.

Reflection in the x-axis. The x-axis is the mirror, so a point's horizontal position is unchanged and its vertical position changes sign: a point 2 units above the axis lands 2 units below it. The rule is x semicolon y becomes x semicolon negative y. Triangle ABC reflects to A dash at 1 semicolon negative 2, B dash at 4 semicolon negative 2 and C dash at 1 semicolon negative 4. The triangle has moved from the first quadrant into the fourth.

Reflection in the y-axis. Now the vertical position is unchanged and the horizontal position changes sign. The rule is x semicolon y becomes negative x semicolon y. ABC reflects to negative 1 semicolon 2, negative 4 semicolon 2 and negative 1 semicolon 4, in the second quadrant.

Reflections are rigid transformations: the image is congruent to the original. AB is 3 units long, from x equals 1 to x equals 4, and A dash B dash is also 3 units; the right angle at A remains a right angle at A dash. What changes, besides position, is orientation. Reading the original triangle A to B to C goes anticlockwise; reading the image A dash to B dash to C dash goes clockwise. A reflection always reverses orientation, the way a mirror turns a right hand into a left hand.

Points on the mirror line do not move; they are called invariant points. The point 5 semicolon 0 lies on the x-axis and reflects in the x-axis onto itself, since negative 0 is 0. A line segment that crosses the mirror has its image crossing at the same point. The questions for this section are with you now: the two axis rules, the reflected triangle in each case, orientation reversed, and invariant points on the mirror.

## Subtopic: Reflection in the Line y = x

The line y equals x passes through the origin at 45 degrees, through every point whose two co-ordinates are equal: 1 semicolon 1, 2 semicolon 2, negative 3 semicolon negative 3. Reflecting in this line swaps the roles of the two axes, and the rule is x semicolon y becomes y semicolon x: the co-ordinates swap places.

Triangle ABC reflects to A dash at 2 semicolon 1, B dash at 2 semicolon 4 and C dash at 4 semicolon 1. Check one point geometrically. A is at 1 semicolon 2 and A dash at 2 semicolon 1. The midpoint of A and A dash is 1.5 semicolon 1.5, which lies on the line y equals x. The segment from A to A dash goes 1 right and 1 down, at 45 degrees downward, which is perpendicular to the mirror line rising at 45 degrees. So the mirror is the perpendicular bisector of A A dash, exactly as a reflection requires.

Notice what happens to the triangle's sides. AB was horizontal, from 1 semicolon 2 to 4 semicolon 2; its image A dash B dash, from 2 semicolon 1 to 2 semicolon 4, is vertical. AC was vertical; its image A dash C dash is horizontal. Reflection in y equals x turns horizontal lines into vertical ones and vertical lines into horizontal ones, which is the geometric meaning of swapping the co-ordinates. The lengths are unchanged, 3 and 2 units, and the orientation is reversed, as with every reflection.

The invariant points of this reflection are the points on the line itself: 3 semicolon 3 swaps to 3 semicolon 3. A point such as negative 2 semicolon 5 reflects to 5 semicolon negative 2, moving from the second quadrant to the fourth. A point on the x-axis, such as 4 semicolon 0, reflects onto the y-axis at 0 semicolon 4.

A common slip is to apply the swap and change a sign as well, which gives a rotation rather than a reflection; the reflection in y equals x only swaps. Another is to confuse the line y equals x with an axis. Plotting the mirror line first, through the origin and 1 semicolon 1, prevents both. Your questions on this section are ready: the swap rule, the reflected triangle, the midpoint check on the mirror, and horizontal sides becoming vertical.

## Subtopic: Translations

A translation slides every point of a figure the same distance in the same direction, with no turning and no flipping. It is described by a horizontal shift and a vertical shift. A translation of 3 units left and 2 units down has the rule x semicolon y becomes x minus 3 semicolon y minus 2. Right is plus in the first co-ordinate, left is minus; up is plus in the second, down is minus.

Triangle ABC translated 3 left and 2 down: A at 1 semicolon 2 goes to 1 minus 3 semicolon 2 minus 2, which is negative 2 semicolon 0; B at 4 semicolon 2 goes to 1 semicolon 0; C at 1 semicolon 4 goes to negative 2 semicolon 2. The image sits partly in the second quadrant with two vertices on the x-axis; a translation may carry a figure within a quadrant or across into others.

A translation preserves everything except position. Lengths and angles are unchanged, so the image is congruent; orientation is unchanged, so A dash to B dash to C dash still runs anticlockwise; and every side of the image is parallel to the matching side of the original. The segment joining each point to its image is the same length and direction for every point: A to A dash, B to B dash and C to C dash are all 3 left and 2 down. There are no invariant points unless the translation is zero.

Translations combine simply. Translating 3 left and 2 down, then 5 right and 1 up, gives a net translation of 2 right and 1 down, x semicolon y becomes x plus 2 semicolon y minus 1, because the shifts add. The reverse of a translation is the opposite shift: to undo 3 left and 2 down, translate 3 right and 2 up.

Computer games move characters on screen by translation: a sprite at 120 semicolon 80 in screen units that moves 15 right and 10 up each frame is at 135 semicolon 90 after one frame and 150 semicolon 100 after two. The questions for this section are ready: the rule from a description in words, the translated triangle, what a translation preserves, and two translations combined.

## Subtopic: Rotations About a Point and the Error Museum

A rotation turns a figure through a given angle about a fixed point called the centre of rotation, in a given direction, clockwise or anticlockwise. Each point stays the same distance from the centre, and the angle between the line from the centre to a point and the line from the centre to its image is the angle of rotation.

About the origin, the three rotations of Grade 9 have rules. Rotation through 90 degrees anticlockwise: x semicolon y becomes negative y semicolon x. Rotation through 90 degrees clockwise: x semicolon y becomes y semicolon negative x. Rotation through 180 degrees, in either direction: x semicolon y becomes negative x semicolon negative y. Check the first with A at 1 semicolon 2: the image is negative 2 semicolon 1. A was in the first quadrant, up and to the right; a quarter turn anticlockwise carries it into the second quadrant, which is where negative 2 semicolon 1 lies. Triangle ABC rotated 90 degrees anticlockwise about the origin becomes negative 2 semicolon 1, negative 2 semicolon 4 and negative 4 semicolon 1; rotated 90 degrees clockwise, 2 semicolon negative 1, 2 semicolon negative 4 and 4 semicolon negative 1; rotated 180 degrees, negative 1 semicolon negative 2, negative 4 semicolon negative 2 and negative 1 semicolon negative 4.

A rotation is rigid and preserves orientation: the image is congruent and still reads anticlockwise. The only invariant point is the centre.

About a centre other than the origin, work relative to the centre. Rotate P at 5 semicolon 3 through 180 degrees about the centre 2 semicolon 1. P is 3 right and 2 up from the centre, so its image is 3 left and 2 down from the centre: negative 1 semicolon negative 1. Rotating P through 90 degrees anticlockwise about the same centre instead: the displacement of 3 right and 2 up becomes 2 left and 3 up, by the 90 degree rule, so the image is 0 semicolon 4. On grid paper this is done by counting squares from the centre, or with tracing paper pinned at the centre and turned a quarter turn.

The error museum, five exhibits. Exhibit one: reflecting in the x-axis by changing the sign of x instead of y; the mirror axis keeps its own co-ordinate. Exhibit two: reflecting in y equals x by swapping and also changing a sign, which produces a rotation. Exhibit three: translating 3 left as x plus 3. Exhibit four: mixing up the clockwise and anticlockwise rules for 90 degrees; checking which quadrant the image should land in catches it. Exhibit five: rotating about the origin when the question names a different centre.

Layout for marks: state the transformation and its rule in arrow notation, apply the rule to each vertex in a short table, plot and label the image with dashes, and state what is preserved. The questions for this section are with you now: the three rotation rules, the quadrant check, rotation about 2 semicolon 1, and the five exhibits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through mirrors on the grid, sliding without turning, and turning around a pin — plain words, same rules.

## Subtopic: Mirrors on the Grid

Put a mirror along the x-axis. A point 2 squares above the axis has its reflection 2 squares below it, straight down, the same distance away. Left and right do not change; only up and down flip. So the second number changes sign: 1 semicolon 2 becomes 1 semicolon negative 2.

Put the mirror along the y-axis instead. Now up and down stay put and left and right flip: the first number changes sign. 1 semicolon 2 becomes negative 1 semicolon 2.

The easy way to remember: the axis you use as the mirror keeps its own number. Mirror on the x-axis, x stays. Mirror on the y-axis, y stays. The other number flips its sign.

The slanted mirror, the line y equals x, runs diagonally through 1 semicolon 1, 2 semicolon 2 and so on. In this mirror the two numbers simply swap places: 1 semicolon 2 becomes 2 semicolon 1, and 4 semicolon 2 becomes 2 semicolon 4. No signs change, just a swap.

A reflected shape is the same size and shape, but it is flipped, like your left hand in the mirror becoming a right hand. If you read the corners A, B, C going anticlockwise, the image reads clockwise. And any point sitting right on the mirror stays exactly where it is. Your questions on this section are ready: the x-axis mirror, the y-axis mirror, the swap for y equals x, and the flipped direction.

## Subtopic: Sliding Without Turning

A translation is a slide. Every point moves the same amount in the same direction, like pushing a book across a desk without turning it.

Say the slide is 3 left and 2 down. Left means take away from the first number; down means take away from the second. So 1 semicolon 2 slides to negative 2 semicolon 0, and 4 semicolon 2 slides to 1 semicolon 0. Right would be add to the first number, up would be add to the second.

Nothing about the shape changes: same size, same angles, not flipped, not turned, every side still pointing the same way. Only the position is different.

Two slides in a row just add up. Slide 3 left and 2 down, then 5 right and 1 up: overall that is 2 right and 1 down. To undo a slide, do the opposite: undo 3 left and 2 down by going 3 right and 2 up.

Video games do this all the time. A character at 120 semicolon 80 that moves 15 right and 10 up every frame is at 135 semicolon 90 after one frame and 150 semicolon 100 after two. The questions for this section: left and down mean subtract, right and up mean add, two slides together, and undoing a slide.

## Subtopic: Turning Around a Pin

A rotation turns a shape around a fixed point, as if a pin were stuck through the paper there. You need three things: the pin, how far to turn, and which way.

Pin at the origin, half a turn, 180 degrees: both numbers change sign. 1 semicolon 2 becomes negative 1 semicolon negative 2. Quarter turn anticlockwise, 90 degrees: swap the numbers and make the new first number negative. 1 semicolon 2 becomes negative 2 semicolon 1. Quarter turn clockwise: swap and make the new second number negative. 1 semicolon 2 becomes 2 semicolon negative 1.

Mixed up which is which? Check the quadrant. 1 semicolon 2 is top right. A quarter turn anticlockwise swings it to the top left, so the answer must have a negative first number: negative 2 semicolon 1, correct.

If the pin is not at the origin, count from the pin. Turn 5 semicolon 3 half a turn around a pin at 2 semicolon 1. The point is 3 right and 2 up from the pin, so after half a turn it is 3 left and 2 down from the pin: negative 1 semicolon negative 1. Tracing paper and a pencil point on the pin make this easy to check.

A turned shape is the same size and shape and is not flipped. Mirror: one sign flips, or swap for y equals x. Slide: add or subtract. Turn: swap and sign, or both signs for half a turn. The final questions of the lesson are with you now: the three turning rules, the quadrant check, and turning around a pin that is not at the origin.
