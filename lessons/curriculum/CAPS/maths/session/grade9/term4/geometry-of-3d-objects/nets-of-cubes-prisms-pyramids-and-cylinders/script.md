# Part 1 — Expert

A solid can be unfolded into a flat pattern, and a flat pattern can be folded into a solid; the pattern is called a net. The CAPS instruction for today is to use nets to make models of cubes, prisms, pyramids and cylinders, and the skill has two directions: drawing the correct net for a given solid, with every measurement right, and recognising whether a given pattern will fold into the intended solid. We find all eleven nets of a cube and the rules that rule out the impostors, draw nets of rectangular and triangular prisms in which the edges that meet are equal, draw a square pyramid on a 6 centimetre base and a cylinder whose rectangle is exactly as long as the circumference, and plan the glue tabs for building real models. Nets are also the bridge to the next lesson: the surface area of a solid is simply the area of its net.

## Subtopic: Nets and the Eleven Nets of a Cube

A net of a solid is a single flat shape, made of the solid's faces joined edge to edge, that folds along those edges into the solid with no faces overlapping and no gaps. Every face appears exactly once. A net of a polyhedron with F faces has F minus 1 fold lines, because the faces form one connected piece and each fold joins one more face to it.

A cube has 6 square faces, so every cube net is 6 congruent squares, but not every arrangement of 6 squares folds into a cube. There are exactly 11 different cube nets, counting two nets as the same if one is a rotation or reflection of the other. The most familiar is the cross: a row of 4 squares with one square attached above the second and one below it. Six of the eleven have a row of 4 with one square above it and one below it in different positions; four have a longest row of 3; and one has rows of only 2, arranged in a staircase.

Two rules dismiss most impostors at a glance. First, no row may contain 5 or more squares in a line, because a row of 4 already wraps all the way around the cube and a fifth square would land on top of the first. Second, no 2 by 2 block of squares may appear, because that would put 4 faces around a single vertex, while every vertex of a cube has exactly 3. A strip of 6 squares fails the first rule; a 2 by 3 rectangle of squares fails the second.

A third rule finds opposite faces. In any straight line of 3 squares in a net, the first and the third are opposite faces of the cube, because folding the middle one up brings them parallel. In the cross net, the row of 4 gives two opposite pairs, the first and third and the second and fourth, and the squares above and below are opposite each other. A standard die has opposite faces adding to 7, 1 opposite 6, 2 opposite 5 and 3 opposite 4, so a die net can be numbered correctly only after the opposite pairs are found.

To test a doubtful net, fold it mentally with one square as the base: lift the squares attached to the base as walls, then fold the remaining squares onto the walls or the top. If two squares land on the same face, the pattern is not a net. The questions for this section are with you now: the definition of a net, the two impostor rules, opposite faces in a line of 3, and the die.

## Subtopic: Nets of Prisms

A prism's net consists of its two congruent end faces and its rectangular side faces. The side rectangles are usually drawn in a strip, with the ends attached to opposite sides of the strip, and the strip's length is the perimeter of the end.

A rectangular prism measuring 4 centimetres by 3 centimetres by 2 centimetres has 6 rectangular faces in 3 congruent pairs: two of 4 by 3, two of 4 by 2 and two of 3 by 2. One net puts a 4 by 3 base in the middle, the four side faces around it, 4 by 2 on the long sides and 3 by 2 on the short sides, and the 4 by 3 lid hinged to one of the long side faces. The critical check is that every pair of edges that will meet when the net is folded has the same length: the 3 centimetre side of a 3 by 2 face meets the 3 centimetre edge of the base, the 2 centimetre side of a 4 by 2 face meets the 2 centimetre side of a 3 by 2 face, and so on round the box.

A triangular prism has 2 triangular ends and 3 rectangular sides. For a prism whose ends are right-angled triangles with sides 3, 4 and 5 centimetres and whose length is 10 centimetres, the net has a strip of three rectangles, 3 by 10, 4 by 10 and 5 by 10, side by side, making a strip 12 centimetres long, which is the perimeter of the triangle, and 10 centimetres wide. The two triangles are attached to opposite long edges of the strip, each one sitting on a rectangle whose short side matches the triangle's side. The triangle's 3 centimetre side must lie against a 3 centimetre edge; if a 4 centimetre side is placed against a 3 centimetre edge, the net will not close.

An equilateral triangular prism with ends of side 6 centimetres and length 15 centimetres has a strip of three congruent 6 by 15 rectangles, 18 centimetres long, and its triangles can be attached to any rectangle, since all three sides are equal. Drawing the triangles accurately needs a compass: from the two ends of the 6 centimetre edge, draw arcs of radius 6 centimetres to meet at the third vertex, or measure 60 degree angles with a protractor.

The general rule for any prism: the strip of side rectangles has length equal to the perimeter of the end and width equal to the length of the prism, and each end face sits against edges equal to its own sides. Your questions on this section are ready: the three pairs of faces of the 4 by 3 by 2 box, the 3, 4, 5 triangular prism and its 12 centimetre strip, and matching edges.

## Subtopic: Nets of Pyramids and Cylinders

A pyramid's net is its base with a triangle attached to every side of the base, all the triangles folding up to meet at the apex. A right square pyramid with a square base of side 6 centimetres has 4 congruent isosceles triangles, each with a base of 6 centimetres. The height of each triangle, measured from the middle of its base to its tip, is the slant height of the pyramid. If the slant height is 5 centimetres, the triangles fold up to meet at an apex whose height above the base is the square root of 5 squared minus 3 squared, which is 4 centimetres, by Pythagoras on the right triangle formed by the slant height, half the base and the height. The slant height must be greater than half the base side, or the triangles cannot rise to meet: a slant height of 3 centimetres on a 6 centimetre base would leave the triangles lying flat.

The regular tetrahedron's net is 4 equilateral triangles. The usual one is a large equilateral triangle divided into 4 by joining the midpoints of its sides: the middle triangle is the base and the three corner triangles fold up to meet. A strip of 4 triangles in a zigzag also works.

A cylinder's net is two circles and one rectangle. The rectangle is the curved surface unrolled, like a label peeled off a tin. Its width is the height of the cylinder, and its length must be the circumference of the circular end, so that it wraps exactly once around the circle. For a tin of radius 3.5 centimetres and height 10 centimetres, the rectangle is 10 centimetres wide and 2 pi times 3.5 long, which is about 21.99 centimetres, or exactly 22 centimetres using 22 over 7 for pi. The two circles of radius 3.5 centimetres are drawn touching opposite long sides of the rectangle, one on top and one on the bottom.

Using the diameter instead of the circumference is the classic error here: a rectangle 7 centimetres long would wrap less than a third of the way around the circle. Since the circumference is pi times the diameter, the rectangle is always a little more than three times as long as the circle is wide, and a sketch of the net should look like that. The questions for this section are ready: the square pyramid on a 6 centimetre base, the slant height and the apex height of 4, the tetrahedron's nets, and the 22 centimetre rectangle of the cylinder.

## Subtopic: Building Models and the Error Museum

A paper model needs more than a correct net; it needs a method. Draw the net accurately in pencil with a ruler, a set square or protractor for right angles, and a compass for circles and equilateral triangles. Score each fold line lightly with the back of a blade or a dry ballpoint pen against a ruler, so the card bends cleanly. Add glue tabs, small trapezium-shaped flaps, to edges that will be glued to another edge.

Count the tabs before cutting. Every edge of the solid is either a fold in the net or a join that needs one tab. A net of a polyhedron with F faces has F minus 1 folds, so the number of joins is E minus F plus 1. A cube has 12 edges and its net 5 folds, so 7 tabs. A triangular prism has 9 edges and 4 folds, so 5 tabs. A square pyramid has 8 edges and 4 folds, so 4 tabs. A tetrahedron has 6 edges and 3 folds, so 3 tabs. Put each tab on only one of the two edges that will meet, otherwise two tabs will collide inside the model. For a cylinder, tabs go along one short side of the rectangle and around the edges of the circles, cut as many small notched flaps.

Models are used to check reasoning and to make objects: a cube box for a gift, a triangular prism for a chocolate box, a cylinder for a pencil holder. A model that will not close tells you exactly which measurement was wrong.

The error museum, five exhibits. Exhibit one: a cube net with a row of 5 squares, a strip of 6, or a 2 by 2 block. Exhibit two: a cylinder net whose rectangle is as long as the diameter, 7 centimetres, instead of the circumference, about 22 centimetres. Exhibit three: a triangular prism net in which a triangle's side does not match the rectangle edge it sits against, such as the 5 centimetre side placed on the 3 by 10 rectangle. Exhibit four: a pyramid net with a slant height no greater than half the base, so the triangles cannot meet. Exhibit five: a net in which two faces fold onto the same position, leaving another face missing.

Layout for marks on a net question: draw the net with every length labelled, show the calculation of any derived length such as the circumference or the slant height, and state the check that matching edges are equal. The questions for this section are with you now: counting tabs, the cylinder rectangle, and the five exhibits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through unfolding a box, tents and pyramids, and unrolling a label — plain words, same rules.

## Subtopic: Unfold a Box

Take an empty box and cut along some of its edges until it lies flat in one piece. That flat shape is a net: every face once, all joined, and it folds back into the box with no gaps and no overlaps.

A cube's net is 6 equal squares. The famous one looks like a cross: a row of 4 squares, with one square sticking up from the second square and one hanging down from the second square. There are 11 different cube nets altogether, but a lot of 6-square shapes are fakes. Two quick tests catch them. A row of 5 or 6 squares is a fake, because 4 already wrap all the way round. A 2 by 2 block of squares is a fake, because 4 faces can never meet at one corner of a cube; only 3 do.

To find which faces end up opposite each other, look for 3 squares in a straight line: the first and third are opposites. On a real die the opposites add up to 7: 1 and 6, 2 and 5, 3 and 4. So before you number a die net, find the opposite pairs.

When you are not sure about a net, fold it in your head. Pick one square as the floor, stand the squares next to it up as walls, then fold the rest onto the walls or the roof. If two squares try to land in the same spot, it is a fake. Your questions on this section are ready: the cross net, the row-of-5 and 2-by-2 fakes, opposite faces on a die, and folding in your head.

## Subtopic: Tents and Pyramids

A triangular prism is a tent: two triangle ends and three rectangle sides. Lay its sides out in a strip. If the triangle has sides 3, 4 and 5 centimetres and the tent is 10 centimetres long, the strip is three rectangles, 3 by 10, 4 by 10 and 5 by 10, side by side. The strip is 12 centimetres long, which is all the way round the triangle. Stick one triangle on the top of the strip and one on the bottom, and make sure each triangle side sits against a rectangle edge of the same length: 3 against 3, never 4 against 3.

A box 4 by 3 by 2 has three pairs of matching faces: two 4 by 3, two 4 by 2, two 3 by 2. Every edge that meets another edge when you fold must be the same length.

A square pyramid is a square floor with a triangle on each side, all folding up to meet at the top. With a 6 centimetre square and triangles 5 centimetres tall, the point ends up 4 centimetres above the floor, thanks to Pythagoras with 3, 4 and 5. If the triangles are too short, only 3 centimetres tall on a 6 centimetre square, they lie flat and never meet.

A triangle pyramid with four equal triangles is easy: draw a big triangle, join the middles of its sides, and you have 4 small triangles. Fold the three corners up. The questions for this section: the tent's strip of three rectangles, matching edges, the square pyramid's 4 centimetre height, and the folded big triangle.

## Subtopic: Unroll the Label

A tin's net is a rectangle and two circles. The rectangle is the label peeled off and laid flat. How tall? As tall as the tin, 10 centimetres. How long? All the way round the tin, which is the circumference: 2 times pi times the radius. For a radius of 3.5 centimetres that is about 22 centimetres. The two circles, radius 3.5, touch the top and bottom of the rectangle.

The famous mistake is making the rectangle as long as the tin is wide, 7 centimetres. Wrap that round the tin and it covers less than a third of the way. The label is always a bit more than three times as long as the tin is wide.

To build any model: draw carefully with a ruler and compass, press a line along each fold so it bends neatly, and add glue tabs. How many tabs? Every edge is either a fold or a glued join. A cube has 12 edges and its net has 5 folds, so 7 tabs. A square pyramid has 8 edges and 4 folds, so 4 tabs. Put a tab on only one of the two edges that meet.

Unfold the box and test for fakes; match the edges of tents and pyramids; unroll the label to the circumference. The final questions of the lesson are with you now: the 22 centimetre label, the diameter mistake, and counting tabs.
