### subtopic_1 — Nets and the Eleven Nets of a Cube
**Assistant:** How can I tell at a glance whether six squares make a cube net without cutting it out?

**Tutor:** Use the two elimination rules first, then fold mentally only if the pattern survives them. Rule one: look for a straight run of 5 or more squares. Four squares in a row already wrap once around the cube, forming the four walls of a ring, so a fifth square in the same line would fold onto the first and two faces would overlap. Rule two: look for any 2 by 2 block. Four squares sharing a corner point would have to fold so that four faces meet at one vertex, but every vertex of a cube has exactly three faces, so one of the four must overlap another. Those two rules dismiss the strip of six, the 2 by 3 rectangle and most random arrangements immediately. If the pattern passes, use opposite faces as a check: in every straight line of 3 squares, the first and third must become opposite faces, and a cube has exactly three pairs of opposites, so you should be able to pair all six squares into three opposite pairs with no square paired twice. If some square ends up opposite two different squares, the pattern fails. Finally, for the stubborn cases, choose one square as the floor, fold its neighbours up as walls, and see where the remaining squares land; two squares landing in the same place means it is not a net. With practice you will also recognise the eleven genuine nets by family: six built on a row of four, four built on rows of three, and the staircase of twos. Recognising them is faster than any rule.

### subtopic_2 — Nets of Prisms
**Assistant:** Why must the strip of rectangles in a prism's net be as long as the perimeter of the end?

**Tutor:** Because the strip wraps once around the end face, with each rectangle covering exactly one side of it. Think of folding the net of the triangular prism with 3, 4, 5 ends. The strip of three rectangles stands up and bends at its two fold lines so that it forms a triangular tube; each rectangle becomes one side of the tube, and the triangular ends close the openings. For the tube to fit the triangle exactly, the rectangle that becomes the side against the 3 centimetre edge must be 3 centimetres wide, the next must be 4 and the last 5, so the strip's total length is 3 plus 4 plus 5, which is 12 centimetres, the perimeter. If the strip were longer, the tube would overlap itself; shorter, and it would not close. The same reasoning works for every prism: a rectangular prism's side strip is 4 plus 3 plus 4 plus 3, which is 14 centimetres, for a 4 by 3 end, and a hexagonal prism's strip is six times its side length. And it gives a useful check on any net you draw: measure the strip and compare it with the perimeter of the end you are attaching. It also explains a formula you will meet in the next lesson. The area of the strip is its length times its width, which is the perimeter of the end times the length of the prism, and that is exactly the lateral surface area of any prism. The net turns the formula into something you can see.

### subtopic_3 — Nets of Pyramids and Cylinders
**Assistant:** Why is the cylinder's rectangle not the same length as the tin's diameter? Both are measurements across.

**Tutor:** Because the rectangle has to go around the tin, not across it. Peel the label off a tin and lay it flat: its long side was wrapped once all the way around the circular end, so its length is the distance around the circle, the circumference, which is pi times the diameter. The diameter is the straight distance across the circle through its centre, and the circumference is always a little more than three times as long. For a tin 7 centimetres across, the diameter is 7 but the circumference is pi times 7, about 21.99, or 22 if you use 22 over 7 for pi. A 7 centimetre rectangle would cover less than a third of the curved surface. A good way to fix this in your mind is to roll a coin along a ruler through one full turn and measure how far it travels; it moves just over three of its own widths. The same idea of going around rather than across appears in the pyramid. Its triangles must have a slant height measured along the sloping face, which is longer than the vertical height of the apex. With a 6 centimetre base and a 5 centimetre slant height, the apex is only 4 centimetres high, because the slant height is the hypotenuse of a right triangle with legs of 3 and 4. In both cases, the length on the net is the length on the surface of the solid, and the surface length is never shorter than the straight-line shortcut.

### subtopic_4 — Building Models and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Why does the number of glue tabs equal the edges minus the folds?

**Tutor:** Because every edge of the finished solid is formed in exactly one of two ways. Either it was never cut, so it is a fold line in the net, where two neighbouring faces were already joined; or it was cut to flatten the solid, so it appears in the net as two separate edges on two different faces that must be glued back together, and each such join needs one tab. So edges equal folds plus joins, and joins equal edges minus folds. The number of folds is fixed by the number of faces. A net is one connected piece with no loops of faces, so starting from one face and attaching the others one at a time takes exactly one fold per additional face: F minus 1 folds for F faces. A cube therefore has 5 folds, and with 12 edges it needs 12 minus 5, which is 7 tabs, whichever of the eleven nets you draw. A square pyramid has 5 faces, 4 folds and 8 edges, so 4 tabs; a triangular prism has 5 faces, 4 folds and 9 edges, so 5 tabs. The count is a useful check on the drawing. If you have placed 8 tabs on a cube net, one join has a tab on both of its edges, and those two tabs will collide inside the box; if you have 6, one join will gape open. Count the tabs, then place each one on only one side of its join, and the model will close cleanly.
