# Part 1 — Expert

Last lesson's solids were built entirely from flat polygons; today's two are not. The CAPS instruction is to recognise and describe the properties of spheres and cylinders, the two most common curved solids in daily life, from a tin of beans and a rainwater tank to a netball and the Earth itself. We describe each one precisely, its flat faces, curved surfaces, edges and vertices, its radius, diameter and height, and the shapes produced when it is sliced, which are called cross-sections. We compare cylinders and spheres with prisms, pyramids and cones in one table, explain why Euler's formula does not apply to them, and practise describing real objects, including hollow ones such as pipes. The vocabulary is exact because next week's nets and the surface area and volume formulas depend on it: every term in the formula for the surface area of a cylinder corresponds to one of the parts we name today.

## Subtopic: The Cylinder: Surfaces, Edges and Cross-Sections

A right circular cylinder, usually just called a cylinder, has two flat faces that are congruent circles lying in parallel planes, joined by one curved surface. The line joining the centres of the two circles is the axis, and in a right cylinder the axis is perpendicular to both circles. A cylinder whose axis leans, like a stack of coins pushed sideways, is an oblique cylinder; in Grade 9 a cylinder means a right cylinder unless the question says otherwise.

The complete description of a cylinder: 2 flat circular faces, 1 curved surface, 2 edges, which are the circular rims where the flat faces meet the curved surface, and 0 vertices. The edges are curved, not straight, and there is no point at which edges meet, so there are no vertices.

Two measurements fix a cylinder: the radius r of the circular faces and the height h, which is the perpendicular distance between them. The diameter is twice the radius. A tin can with radius 3.5 centimetres and height 10 centimetres has a diameter of 7 centimetres, and the circumference of each circular face is 2 pi times 3.5, which is about 21.99 centimetres.

A cross-section is the flat shape exposed when a solid is cut by a plane. Cut the cylinder parallel to its base, at any height, and the cross-section is a circle congruent to the base, of radius 3.5 centimetres for the tin. A solid with the same cross-section all the way along is said to have a uniform cross-section, and this is the property the cylinder shares with every prism: it is why the volume of both is the area of the base times the height. Cut the cylinder vertically through its axis and the cross-section is a rectangle whose width is the diameter and whose height is the cylinder's height, 7 centimetres by 10 centimetres for the tin. Cut it vertically but not through the axis and the rectangle is narrower. Cut it at a slant through the curved surface and the cross-section is an oval, called an ellipse.

The cylinder's shape explains its uses. It stands steadily on a flat face, stacks in columns, and rolls in a straight line on its curved surface, which is why tins roll off a shelf but only in one direction. Your questions on this section are ready: the full description with faces, curved surface, edges and vertices, the radius, diameter and height of the tin, and the three kinds of cross-section.

## Subtopic: The Sphere: Centre, Radius and Great Circles

A sphere is the set of all points in space that are the same distance from a fixed point, its centre. That fixed distance is the radius r, and the diameter, any line segment through the centre with both ends on the sphere, is 2r. Compare the circle: a circle is the set of points in a plane at a fixed distance from a centre, a flat curve; a sphere is the same idea in three dimensions, a surface enclosing a solid ball.

The complete description of a sphere: 0 flat faces, 1 curved surface, 0 edges and 0 vertices. It has no rims and no corners anywhere, which is why it rolls equally well in every direction.

Every cross-section of a sphere is a circle. The largest is produced by a plane through the centre; it has the same radius as the sphere and is called a great circle. Any plane that misses the centre cuts a smaller circle, and the further the plane is from the centre, the smaller the circle. For a sphere of radius 10 centimetres cut by a plane 6 centimetres from the centre, the radius of the cross-section is found with Pythagoras: the square root of 10 squared minus 6 squared, which is the square root of 64, which is 8 centimetres. A plane exactly 10 centimetres from the centre just touches the sphere at a single point.

The Earth is very nearly a sphere with a radius of about 6 370 kilometres. The equator is a great circle, so its length is 2 pi times 6 370, which is about 40 024 kilometres, the famous figure of roughly 40 000 kilometres. Every other line of latitude, such as the Tropic of Capricorn that crosses Limpopo, is a smaller circle, while every line of longitude runs along a great circle through the poles.

A hemisphere is half a sphere, cut along a great circle. It has 1 flat circular face, 1 curved surface, 1 edge, which is the circular rim, and 0 vertices. A bowl, a dome and the roof of many planetariums are hemispheres. The questions for this section are ready: the definition of a sphere, its faces, edges and vertices, great and small circles, the cross-section 6 centimetres from the centre, and the hemisphere.

## Subtopic: Comparing Cylinders, Spheres, Prisms and Cones

Placing the curved solids beside the polyhedra in one table makes their properties clear. The columns are flat faces, curved surfaces, edges and vertices. Cube: 6 flat faces, no curved surface, 12 edges, 8 vertices. Cylinder: 2 flat faces, 1 curved surface, 2 edges, 0 vertices. Cone: 1 flat face, 1 curved surface, 1 edge, 1 vertex, the apex. Sphere: 0 flat faces, 1 curved surface, 0 edges, 0 vertices. Hemisphere: 1 flat face, 1 curved surface, 1 edge, 0 vertices.

Each curved solid has a polyhedron relative. A cylinder is like a prism with a circular end: both have two congruent parallel ends and a uniform cross-section. Imagine prisms on regular polygons with more and more sides, 6, then 12, then 100; their side faces become narrower and the prism looks more and more like a cylinder. A cone is like a pyramid with a circular base: both rise from a base to a single apex. The sphere has no polyhedron relative, though an icosahedron with its 20 faces is a rough approximation, and soccer balls are built that way and then inflated into a sphere.

Euler's formula, V minus E plus F equals 2, applies only to polyhedra and must not be forced onto curved solids. For a cylinder, counting the 2 flat faces and the curved surface as 3 faces, with 2 edges and 0 vertices, gives 0 minus 2 plus 3, which is 1, not 2. That is not a contradiction; it shows that the formula's counting of straight edges and polygon faces has no meaning for curved surfaces.

Symmetry separates the two solids sharply. A cylinder looks the same after any rotation about its axis, and the same after turning it upside down, but tipping it onto its side changes how it sits. A sphere looks the same after any rotation at all about any line through its centre; it is the most symmetrical solid possible. That perfect symmetry is why bubbles, raindrops and planets tend towards spheres, and why ball bearings are spheres. Your questions on this section are ready: the comparison table, the cylinder as a prism relative and the cone as a pyramid relative, why Euler's formula does not apply, and the symmetry of each.

## Subtopic: Describing Real Objects and the Error Museum

Describing a real object means naming its solid and giving the measurements that fix it. A plastic rainwater tank standing on its stand is a cylinder; a 2 000 litre tank is roughly 1.3 metres in diameter and 1.6 metres high, and those two measurements are what a description must give. A netball is a sphere with a diameter of about 22 centimetres. A tin of pilchards is a cylinder that is wider than it is tall, while a cool drink can is taller than it is wide; both are still cylinders, because the definition says nothing about proportion.

Hollow objects need two radii. A PVC drainpipe is a hollow cylinder, a cylinder with a narrower cylinder removed from its middle. Its description gives the outer diameter, the wall thickness and the length. A pipe with an outer diameter of 110 millimetres and walls 5 millimetres thick has an inner diameter of 110 minus 2 times 5, which is 100 millimetres, because the wall is on both sides. Its cross-section parallel to the end is a ring, called an annulus, between two concentric circles. A hollow ball, such as a tennis ball, is described the same way, by its outer radius and its thickness.

Real objects are approximations, and a good description says so. A tin can has rims and grooves, and the Earth is slightly flattened at the poles, but describing them as a cylinder and a sphere is accurate enough for calculation, and that modelling choice is stated.

The error museum, five exhibits. Exhibit one: calling a circle a sphere, or a sphere a circle; the circle is a flat two-dimensional curve, the sphere a three-dimensional surface. Exhibit two: an incomplete description of the cylinder, such as 3 faces and no edges, or 2 faces and nothing else; the full answer is 2 flat faces, 1 curved surface, 2 edges and 0 vertices. Exhibit three: using the diameter where the radius is needed, so that a tin of diameter 7 centimetres is given a radius of 7. Exhibit four: claiming that every cross-section of a cylinder is a circle; only those parallel to the base are, and vertical sections are rectangles and slanting ones ellipses. Exhibit five: giving a sphere 1 face and 1 edge, or a cone no vertex.

Layout for marks: name the solid, list flat faces, curved surfaces, edges and vertices separately, give the defining measurements with units, and state the cross-section with the direction of the cut. The questions for this section are with you now: the tank and the netball, the pipe's inner diameter, and the five exhibits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through a tin can, a ball, and slicing things to see what shape appears — plain words, same rules.

## Subtopic: The Tin Can

Pick up a tin of beans. It is a cylinder. It has a flat circle on the top and a flat circle on the bottom, the same size, and one curved surface wrapped around between them, like the label. Where the flat circles meet the curved surface there are two rims; those are its 2 edges, curved ones. Nowhere do edges meet in a point, so it has no corners, no vertices.

So the tin's full description is: 2 flat faces, 1 curved surface, 2 edges, 0 vertices. Say all four parts; leaving one out loses marks.

Two measurements describe any tin. The radius, from the middle of the top circle to its rim, and the height, from the bottom to the top. A tin with radius 3.5 centimetres is 7 centimetres across, because the diameter is twice the radius, and it is 10 centimetres tall.

The tin behaves like its shape. Stand it on a flat end and it is steady; stack tins and they make a neat tower; lay one on its side and it rolls, but only straight forwards or backwards. Thin tins, fat tins, a tall cool drink can, a flat tin of pilchards, a rainwater tank: all cylinders, because being a cylinder is about two equal flat circles and a curved side, not about being tall or short. Your questions on this section are ready: the four parts of the description, radius against diameter, and why a short fat tin is still a cylinder.

## Subtopic: The Ball

A netball is a sphere. Every point on its surface is exactly the same distance from the middle, and that distance is the radius. A sphere has no flat face at all, no edges and no corners: just 1 curved surface. That is why a ball rolls in every direction while a tin only rolls one way.

Do not mix up a sphere and a circle. A circle is flat, drawn on paper. A sphere is solid, you can hold it. A coin's outline is a circle; a marble is a sphere.

Cut a ball exactly in half and you get two hemispheres, like two bowls. A hemisphere has 1 flat circle face, 1 curved surface and 1 edge, the rim, and still no corners.

The Earth is very nearly a sphere with a radius of about 6 370 kilometres. The equator goes all the way round its fattest part, and its length is 2 times pi times 6 370, about 40 000 kilometres. Other lines of latitude, like the Tropic of Capricorn up near Polokwane, go round thinner parts of the Earth, so they are shorter circles. The questions for this section: the sphere's single curved surface, sphere against circle, the hemisphere's three parts, and the equator.

## Subtopic: Slice It and See

Imagine cutting things with a very sharp knife and looking at the flat cut surface. That shape is the cross-section.

Slice a tin across, parallel to the bottom, anywhere at all, and the cut is a circle exactly the same as the bottom: the same shape all the way along, just like a loaf of bread gives the same shaped slice every time. Slice the tin straight down through the middle and the cut is a rectangle, as wide as the tin and as tall as the tin: 7 by 10 centimetres for our tin. Slice it at a slant and you get an oval.

Slice a ball anywhere and the cut is always a circle. Through the middle, it is the biggest circle, as wide as the ball. Closer to the edge, the circle gets smaller, until right at the edge it is just a point. For a ball of radius 10 centimetres, a slice 6 centimetres from the middle gives a circle of radius 8, by Pythagoras.

Slicing also tells you about hollow things. Slice a drainpipe across and you get a ring. If the pipe is 110 millimetres across the outside and the wall is 5 millimetres thick, the hole is 110 take away 5 on each side, 100 millimetres across.

The tin: two flat circles, a curved side, two rims, no corners. The ball: one curved surface and nothing else. Slice to see: circles, rectangles, ovals and rings. The final questions of the lesson are with you now: slicing the tin three ways, slicing the ball, and the pipe.
