### subtopic_1 — The Cylinder: Surfaces, Edges and Cross-Sections
**Assistant:** Why do we say a cylinder has edges but no vertices? A cube has both.

**Tutor:** Because an edge and a vertex are different kinds of meeting, and a cylinder has only the first. An edge is where two surfaces meet along a line. On a cube, two square faces meet along a straight segment; on a cylinder, a flat circular face meets the curved surface along a circle. That circular rim is a genuine edge: run your finger over a tin and you feel the sharp change of direction from the flat lid to the curved side. A vertex is a point where edges meet, a corner. On a cube, three straight edges come together at each of 8 corners. On a cylinder, each circular edge runs all the way round and closes on itself without ever meeting another edge, and the top rim and the bottom rim never touch each other. So there is no point at which edges meet, and the cylinder has 0 vertices. The same reasoning gives the cone exactly one vertex, its apex, where the curved surface narrows to a point, and gives the sphere neither edges nor vertices, because it has only one surface and nothing for it to meet. When you describe any solid, ask the questions in order: which surfaces are flat and which curved, where do two surfaces meet, giving the edges, and where do edges meet, giving the vertices. That order prevents the common answer of "a cylinder has 3 faces and no edges", which counts the surfaces but forgets to look at where they meet.

### subtopic_2 — The Sphere: Centre, Radius and Great Circles
**Assistant:** What makes a great circle great, and why does it matter that the equator is one?

**Tutor:** A great circle is the cross-section made by a plane passing through the centre of the sphere, and it is great because it is the largest circle that can be drawn on the sphere's surface: its radius equals the sphere's own radius. Every other plane misses the centre and cuts a smaller circle, and the further the plane is from the centre, the smaller that circle, which you can calculate with Pythagoras: on a sphere of radius 10, a plane 6 from the centre cuts a circle of radius 8. On the Earth, the equator is the great circle halfway between the poles, so its length is 2 pi times the Earth's radius, about 40 000 kilometres, the longest possible line of latitude. Every other line of latitude is a small circle, shorter the nearer it is to a pole; the Tropic of Capricorn, which crosses Limpopo, is noticeably shorter than the equator. Great circles matter for a practical reason too: the shortest path between two points on a sphere runs along the great circle through them. That is why an aeroplane flying from Johannesburg to a city far to the north-west may curve over regions that look out of the way on a flat map. Flat maps distort the sphere, and the great circle route that looks curved on the map is actually the straight-as-possible route on the globe. So great circles are both the biggest cross-sections and the shortest roads.

### subtopic_3 — Comparing Cylinders, Spheres, Prisms and Cones
**Assistant:** If a cylinder is like a prism, why can we not just count it with Euler's formula?

**Tutor:** Because the resemblance is about cross-sections and volume, not about the counting that Euler's formula relies on. A cylinder and a prism share the property that every slice parallel to the base is the same shape, which is why both have volume equal to base area times height. But Euler's formula, V minus E plus F equals 2, is a statement about polyhedra: surfaces made of flat polygons, joined along straight edges that end at vertices. Its proof depends on that structure. A cylinder breaks it. If you count the 2 flat circles and the curved surface as 3 faces, the 2 circular rims as edges, and 0 vertices, you get 0 minus 2 plus 3, which is 1, not 2. That is not a mistake in arithmetic; it shows that the words face, edge and vertex do not mean the same thing on curved solids. You can see the connection another way. Build a prism on a regular polygon with many sides, say 100. It looks almost exactly like a cylinder, and it obeys Euler's formula: 200 vertices, 300 edges and 102 faces, and 200 minus 300 plus 102 is 2. The curved surface of the cylinder corresponds to 100 thin rectangular faces with 100 edges between them, all of which disappear when the surface becomes smooth. So the cylinder is the limit of prisms, but the counting does not survive the limit, and it is correct to say that Euler's formula applies to polyhedra only.

### subtopic_4 — Describing Real Objects and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Why do we subtract the wall thickness twice to get the inside of a pipe?

**Tutor:** Because a diameter crosses the whole pipe, and on its way across it passes through the wall twice, once on each side. Draw the cross-section: a ring between two circles with the same centre. The outer diameter, 110 millimetres, runs from the outside of the wall on the left, through the wall, across the hole, through the wall on the right, to the outside again. The wall is 5 millimetres thick, so the left wall takes 5 millimetres of that line and the right wall takes another 5, leaving 110 minus 10, which is 100 millimetres for the hole. In terms of radii the subtraction happens once: the outer radius is 55, the inner radius is 55 minus 5, which is 50, and doubling gives the same inner diameter of 100. Mixing the two, subtracting the thickness once from a diameter, gives 105 millimetres, the commonest error on pipe questions. The distinction matters in practice. A plumber joining pipes needs the outer diameter, since fittings slide over the outside, while the amount of water a pipe carries depends on the inner diameter, since water flows through the hole, not the wall. Next week the volume of the material in a pipe will be the outer cylinder's volume minus the inner cylinder's volume, and that needs both radii correct. So the habit is to sketch the ring, mark the wall on both sides, and decide whether you are working with radii or diameters before subtracting.
