### subtopic_1 — Polyhedra: Faces, Edges and Vertices
**Assistant:** Why is a cylinder not a polyhedron? It has faces and edges too.

**Tutor:** Because the definition of a polyhedron requires every part of the surface to be a flat polygon, and a cylinder fails on two counts. Its curved side is not flat at all: you cannot lay a ruler along it in every direction, and it cannot be cut into a finite number of flat polygons without changing the shape. Its two ends are flat but they are circles, and a circle is not a polygon, because a polygon is bounded by straight line segments. The rims where the ends meet the curved surface are sometimes called edges in everyday speech, and in the next lesson we will describe them that way for the cylinder, but they are curved, whereas every edge of a polyhedron is a straight segment between two vertices. The distinction matters because everything we prove about polyhedra uses the flatness. Euler's formula, V minus E plus F equals 2, depends on counting straight edges and vertices where they meet; a cylinder has no vertices, so the formula has nothing sensible to count. The angle argument for the Platonic solids depends on polygon interior angles meeting at a vertex. And when we calculate surface area later this term, the faces of a polyhedron are added as polygon areas, while the curved surface of a cylinder needs its own formula, 2 pi r h. So the line between polyhedra and solids with curved surfaces is not fussiness about words; it decides which tools apply.

### subtopic_2 — The Five Platonic Solids
**Assistant:** Two tetrahedra stuck together make a solid with six equal triangles. Why is that not a Platonic solid?

**Tutor:** Because it satisfies only the first of the two conditions. The solid you describe, a triangular bipyramid, does have six faces that are all congruent equilateral triangles, so the faces are identical regular polygons. But look at its vertices. The three vertices around the middle, where the two tetrahedra were joined, each have 4 triangles meeting at them, while the top and bottom vertices each have only 3. A Platonic solid must look the same from every vertex: the same number of faces meeting at each one, so that any vertex could be swapped for any other by turning the solid. The bipyramid fails that test, and you can feel it if you hold one: the points at the top and bottom are sharper than the ones around the waist. This is exactly why both conditions are written into the definition. Equal faces alone allow many solids, including the bipyramid and others with 10 or 16 triangular faces; equal faces plus equal vertices allow only five. It is also why the bipyramid would make an unfair die even though its faces have equal areas, because the faces are not all positioned the same way relative to the centre and the vertices. Every die in a board-game set is a Platonic solid precisely because both conditions make every face equivalent to every other.

### subtopic_3 — Euler's Formula and Counting from the Faces
**Assistant:** Why do we divide by 2 to get the edges but by a different number to get the vertices?

**Tutor:** Because each count overcounts by a different amount, and the divisor undoes the overcounting. When you multiply the number of faces by the number of sides on each face, you are going round every face and listing its sides. Each edge of a closed solid is the meeting line of exactly two faces, so each edge appears in that list twice, once from each face on either side of it. Dividing by 2 corrects that, always, for every polyhedron. For the dodecahedron, 12 faces times 5 sides is 60 sides listed, and 30 edges. Vertices behave differently. When you multiply the number of faces by the number of corners on each face, each vertex appears once for every face that meets at it, and that number depends on the solid: 3 for the tetrahedron, cube and dodecahedron, 4 for the octahedron, 5 for the icosahedron. So you divide by the number of faces at a vertex. The icosahedron's 20 triangles have 60 corners listed, each vertex counted 5 times, giving 12 vertices; the dodecahedron's 60 corners are each counted 3 times, giving 20. The method only works when every vertex has the same number of faces, which is guaranteed for Platonic solids by their definition. Then Euler's formula is an independent check: for the icosahedron, 12 minus 30 plus 20 is 2, so the two counts agree with each other and with the faces.

### subtopic_4 — Why Only Five, and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Why must the angles at a vertex add to less than 360 degrees, and not exactly 360?

**Tutor:** Because exactly 360 degrees means the faces fill a complete turn around the point, and a complete turn lies flat. Picture six equilateral triangles around a single point: each has a 60 degree angle there, together they make 360, and they fit perfectly into a flat hexagon on the table, the way floor tiles or the cells of a honeycomb do. Nothing pushes the point up out of the plane, so no corner of a solid forms. To make a corner that rises out of the plane, you need a gap: take away one triangle from the six, so that five meet with a total of 300 degrees, and the gap of 60 degrees can only close if the five triangles tilt upwards into a cone-like point. That point is a vertex of the icosahedron. The smaller the total, the sharper the point: three triangles with 180 degrees make the very sharp corner of the tetrahedron, while three pentagons with 324 degrees make the blunt corner of the dodecahedron. More than 360 is impossible in the other direction, because the faces overlap before they get round the point; you cannot fit four pentagons with 432 degrees around a single vertex without crumpling them. So less than 360 is required to form a corner at all, and at least three faces are required to enclose space, and those two conditions together leave only the five combinations. It is a short proof, and that is part of its beauty.
