# Part 1 — Expert

Last week we unfolded solids into nets; this week the nets pay off. The CAPS instruction is to use formulae and conversions between SI units to calculate the surface area, volume and capacity of cubes, rectangular prisms, triangular prisms and cylinders. Surface area is the total area of all the faces, which is exactly the area of the net, measured in square units. Volume is the amount of space the solid occupies, measured in cubic units, and capacity is how much a hollow container holds, measured in millilitres and litres. We build every formula from the net or from the idea of stacking layers, work with a 3, 4, 5 triangular prism, a tin of radius 3.5 centimetres and a rainwater tank, and convert between square and cubic centimetres and metres, where the commonest and most expensive errors are made. Every answer in this lesson carries a unit, and the unit is half the mark.

## Subtopic: Surface Area of Cubes and Prisms

The surface area of a polyhedron is the sum of the areas of all its faces. The most reliable method is to picture or sketch the net, list the faces, find each area and add, and the formulas are shortcuts for exactly that.

A cube has 6 congruent square faces, so its surface area is 6 times the side squared. A cube of side 5 centimetres has surface area 6 times 25, which is 150 square centimetres.

A rectangular prism of length l, breadth b and height h has three pairs of congruent rectangles: two of l times b, two of l times h and two of b times h. Its surface area is 2 times the quantity lb plus lh plus bh. For a box 4 by 3 by 2 centimetres: lb is 12, lh is 8, bh is 6; their sum is 26, and twice that is 52 square centimetres. Writing the three products separately before adding is the habit that prevents a face being forgotten.

A triangular prism has 2 congruent triangular ends and 3 rectangular sides. For ends that are right-angled triangles with sides 3, 4 and 5 centimetres and a length of 10 centimetres: each triangle has area one half times 3 times 4, which is 6, so the two ends give 12 square centimetres. The three rectangles are 3 by 10, 4 by 10 and 5 by 10, which is 30 plus 40 plus 50, or 120 square centimetres; this is the perimeter of the triangle, 12, times the length, 10. The total surface area is 12 plus 120, which is 132 square centimetres. The general formula for any prism follows: surface area equals 2 times the area of the end plus the perimeter of the end times the length.

Real objects are not always closed. A shoebox without its lid, 30 centimetres long, 18 wide and 12 high, has a base of 30 times 18, which is 540, two long sides of 30 times 12, which is 360 each, and two short sides of 18 times 12, which is 216 each. The open box needs 540 plus 720 plus 432, which is 1 692 square centimetres of cardboard. Reading the question for open or closed, with or without a lid, is part of the calculation.

When a triangular end is not right-angled, its area needs the perpendicular height of the triangle, not a slanted side. An isosceles end with base 6 and equal sides 5 has a perpendicular height of 4, by Pythagoras on half the base, so its area is one half times 6 times 4, which is 12. The questions for this section are with you now: the cube of side 5, the 4 by 3 by 2 box, the 3, 4, 5 triangular prism, and the open shoebox.

## Subtopic: Surface Area of Cylinders

The net of a cylinder is two circles and a rectangle, and its surface area is the sum of their areas. Each circle has area pi r squared, so the two ends together give 2 pi r squared. The rectangle has width h and length equal to the circumference, 2 pi r, so its area, the curved surface area, is 2 pi r h. The total surface area of a closed cylinder is 2 pi r squared plus 2 pi r h.

For a tin of radius 3.5 centimetres and height 10 centimetres, the two ends give 2 times pi times 3.5 squared, which is 2 pi times 12.25, about 76.97 square centimetres. The curved surface gives 2 times pi times 3.5 times 10, about 219.91 square centimetres. The total is about 296.88 square centimetres. Keep full calculator values until the last step and round only the final answer, normally to two decimal places, otherwise rounding errors accumulate.

Questions often ask for only part of the surface. The label of the tin covers only the curved surface: about 219.91 square centimetres, or exactly 220 if pi is taken as 22 over 7. A container with a bottom but no lid, such as a cylindrical pencil holder or an open water tank, has one circle and the curved surface: pi r squared plus 2 pi r h, which for the tin's dimensions is about 258.40 square centimetres. A pipe open at both ends has only the curved surface.

The formula can be factorised, 2 pi r times the quantity r plus h, which gives the same answer with fewer steps: 2 pi times 3.5 times 13.5, about 296.88 again. Either form is acceptable; what matters is that the radius, not the diameter, is substituted. A tin described as 7 centimetres across has radius 3.5, and substituting 7 for r gives four times the correct end area.

A practical example: a cylindrical water tank with radius 1 metre and height 2 metres is to be painted on its outside, including the top but not the base, which stands on a slab. The area is pi times 1 squared plus 2 times pi times 1 times 2, which is pi plus 4 pi, which is 5 pi, about 15.71 square metres. If one litre of paint covers 8 square metres, about 1.96 litres are needed, so two litres must be bought. Your questions on this section are ready: the tin's ends and curved surface, the label, the open container, and the painted tank.

## Subtopic: Volume and Capacity

The volume of a solid is the number of unit cubes that fit inside it. For any prism or cylinder, the solid is a stack of identical layers, each layer the shape of the base and 1 unit thick, so the volume is the area of the base times the height. This single rule covers every solid in today's lesson.

Cube of side 5 centimetres: base area 25, height 5, volume 125 cubic centimetres, which is side cubed. Rectangular prism 4 by 3 by 2 centimetres: base area 12, height 2, volume 24 cubic centimetres, which is length times breadth times height. Triangular prism with 3, 4, 5 ends and length 10: base area 6, length 10, volume 60 cubic centimetres. For a lying-down prism the height in the rule is the length between the two triangular ends. Cylinder of radius 3.5 and height 10: base area pi times 3.5 squared, about 38.48, times 10, which is about 384.85 cubic centimetres. The formula for the cylinder is pi r squared h.

Capacity is the volume of the inside of a container, measured in liquid units. The link between the two systems is exact: 1 cubic centimetre holds 1 millilitre, 1 000 cubic centimetres hold 1 litre, and 1 cubic metre holds 1 000 litres. So the tin holds about 384.85 millilitres, roughly 385 millilitres.

A rectangular fish tank 60 centimetres long, 30 centimetres wide and 40 centimetres high has a volume of 60 times 30 times 40, which is 72 000 cubic centimetres, a capacity of 72 litres. If it is filled to 5 centimetres below the top, the water is 35 centimetres deep and the volume of water is 60 times 30 times 35, which is 63 000 cubic centimetres, 63 litres.

A rainwater tank is a cylinder of radius 0.65 metres and height 1.6 metres. Its volume is pi times 0.65 squared times 1.6, about 2.12 cubic metres, so its capacity is about 2 120 litres, a little more than the 2 000 litres it is sold as, because a tank is never filled to the very top of its walls. A household using 150 litres a day from the tank would empty it in about 14 days. The questions for this section are ready: base area times height for each solid, the tin in millilitres, the fish tank filled to 35 centimetres, and the rainwater tank in litres.

## Subtopic: Converting SI Units and the Error Museum

Converting lengths is familiar: 1 metre is 100 centimetres, and 1 centimetre is 10 millimetres. Converting areas and volumes is not the same, because the conversion factor is applied once for each dimension. A square 1 metre on a side is 100 centimetres by 100 centimetres, so 1 square metre is 100 times 100, which is 10 000 square centimetres. A cube 1 metre on a side is 100 by 100 by 100 centimetres, so 1 cubic metre is 1 000 000 cubic centimetres. Likewise 1 square centimetre is 100 square millimetres and 1 cubic centimetre is 1 000 cubic millimetres.

So the tin's surface area of about 296.88 square centimetres is 296.88 divided by 10 000, about 0.0297 square metres, and a volume of 480 000 cubic centimetres is 480 000 divided by 1 000 000, which is 0.48 cubic metres.

The safest method with mixed units is to convert every length to one unit before calculating. A crate is 1.2 metres long, 80 centimetres wide and 50 centimetres high. In centimetres it is 120 by 80 by 50, a volume of 480 000 cubic centimetres, which is 480 litres or 0.48 cubic metres. Multiplying 1.2 by 80 by 50 directly gives 4 800, a meaningless number in mixed units.

A swimming pool 10 metres long, 5 metres wide and 1.5 metres deep holds 10 times 5 times 1.5, which is 75 cubic metres, which is 75 000 litres. At a filling rate of 25 litres per minute from a garden hose, filling it takes 3 000 minutes, which is 50 hours.

The error museum, five exhibits. Exhibit one: converting square centimetres to square metres by dividing by 100 instead of 10 000, or cubic centimetres to cubic metres by dividing by 100 or 1 000 instead of 1 000 000. Exhibit two: mixing units in one calculation, such as metres with centimetres. Exhibit three: substituting the diameter for the radius in a cylinder formula. Exhibit four: giving a surface area in cubic units or a volume in square units, or forgetting units altogether. Exhibit five: counting the wrong faces, such as including a lid that the container does not have, or using the slanted side of a triangle as its height.

Layout for marks: write the formula, substitute with units, calculate, and state the answer with the correct unit and a sensible rounding, converting at the start or the end with the factor shown. The questions for this section are with you now: square and cubic conversion factors, the crate, the pool, and the five exhibits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through wrapping paper, stacking layers, and filling with water — plain words, same rules.

## Subtopic: Wrap It: Add Every Face

Surface area is how much wrapping paper you need to cover a solid with no gaps: the area of every face, added up. The easiest way is to imagine the net laid flat and add up its pieces.

A cube of side 5 has 6 squares of 25 each: 150 square centimetres. A box 4 by 3 by 2 has three pairs of rectangles: two of 4 times 3, which is 12, two of 4 times 2, which is 8, and two of 3 times 2, which is 6. Add one of each, 26, and double it: 52 square centimetres. Writing the three pairs down first means you never forget a face.

A tent-shaped prism with 3, 4, 5 triangle ends and length 10: each triangle is half of 3 times 4, which is 6, so the two ends make 12. The strip of rectangles is 12 long, all the way round the triangle, and 10 wide, so 120. Together: 132 square centimetres.

A tin is two circles and a label. Each circle is pi times radius times radius; the label is the distance round, 2 pi times the radius, times the height. Radius 3.5, height 10: the ends make about 76.97, the label about 219.91, total about 296.88 square centimetres. Use the radius, never the width of the tin. And read whether there is a lid: a shoebox with no lid, or a pencil holder with no top, has one face fewer. Your questions on this section are ready: the cube, the box's three pairs, the tent, and the tin's two circles and label.

## Subtopic: Floor Times Height

Volume is how much space is inside. Picture building the solid out of thin layers, each one the shape of the floor. Every layer is the same, so the volume is the area of the floor times the height. One rule for every shape today.

Cube of side 5: floor 25, height 5, volume 125 cubic centimetres. Box 4 by 3 by 2: floor 12, height 2, volume 24. The tent prism lying on its side: the floor is the triangle, 6, and the height is the length, 10, so 60. The tin: floor is the circle, pi times 3.5 times 3.5, about 38.48, times 10, about 384.85 cubic centimetres.

Notice the units. Area is square centimetres, because it is two lengths multiplied. Volume is cubic centimetres, because it is three lengths multiplied. Wrapping paper gets a little 2; space inside gets a little 3.

Now the magic link to liquids: one cubic centimetre holds exactly one millilitre. So the tin holds about 385 millilitres. A thousand cubic centimetres is one litre. The questions for this section: floor times height for each solid, square against cubic units, and the tin in millilitres.

## Subtopic: How Many Litres Fit

A fish tank 60 by 30 by 40 centimetres: 60 times 30 times 40 is 72 000 cubic centimetres, which is 72 000 millilitres, which is 72 litres. Fill it only 35 centimetres deep and it holds 63 litres.

A rainwater tank is a big cylinder, radius 0.65 metres, height 1.6 metres: pi times 0.65 times 0.65 times 1.6 is about 2.12 cubic metres. One cubic metre holds 1 000 litres, so the tank holds about 2 120 litres. At 150 litres a day, that lasts about two weeks.

Now the trap that costs the most marks. A metre is 100 centimetres, but a square metre is 100 times 100, which is 10 000 square centimetres, and a cubic metre is 100 times 100 times 100, which is a million cubic centimetres. Changing units for area or volume uses the conversion once for every direction.

And never mix units in one sum. A crate 1.2 metres by 80 centimetres by 50 centimetres: change the 1.2 metres to 120 centimetres first, then 120 times 80 times 50 is 480 000 cubic centimetres, which is 480 litres.

Wrap it by adding every face; fill it with floor times height; convert with the factor used once for every direction. The final questions of the lesson are with you now: the fish tank, the rainwater tank, the square-metre trap, and the crate.
