# Part 1 — Expert

In Term 3 we found that doubling every dimension of a flat shape doubles its perimeter and multiplies its area by four. Today the same investigation moves into three dimensions. The CAPS instruction is to investigate how doubling any or all of the dimensions of right prisms and cylinders affects their volume, and the results are more dramatic than in two dimensions: doubling one dimension doubles the volume, doubling two multiplies it by four, and doubling all three multiplies it by eight. We build the results from tables of boxes, triangular prisms and cylinders, discover that a cylinder's radius behaves like two dimensions at once, generalise to any scale factor k, where volume is multiplied by k cubed and surface area by k squared, and apply the rules to rainwater tanks, packaging and the reason large animals and large ice blocks behave differently from small ones.

## Subtopic: Doubling One Dimension of a Prism

Begin with a rectangular prism 4 centimetres long, 3 centimetres wide and 2 centimetres high. Its volume is 4 times 3 times 2, which is 24 cubic centimetres. Double the length only, to 8 by 3 by 2: the volume is 8 times 3 times 2, which is 48 cubic centimetres, twice the original. Double the width only instead, 4 by 6 by 2: 48 again. Double the height only, 4 by 3 by 4: 48 again.

The reason is that volume is a product of three dimensions, and doubling any one factor of a product doubles the product, whichever factor it is. The picture confirms it: the 8 by 3 by 2 box is exactly two of the original boxes placed end to end.

A triangular prism behaves the same way. Take a prism whose ends are right-angled triangles with legs 3 and 4 centimetres, so each end has area one half times 3 times 4, which is 6 square centimetres, and whose length is 10 centimetres. Its volume is 6 times 10, which is 60 cubic centimetres. Double the length to 20 and the volume is 6 times 20, which is 120. Double one leg of the triangle instead, to 6 and 4, and the end area becomes one half times 6 times 4, which is 12, so the volume is 12 times 10, which is 120. Either way the volume doubles.

For a cylinder, the height is one dimension that can be doubled alone. A cylinder of radius 2 centimetres and height 5 centimetres has volume pi times 2 squared times 5, which is 20 pi, about 62.83 cubic centimetres. Double the height to 10: pi times 4 times 10, which is 40 pi, about 125.66 cubic centimetres, twice as much.

Record these results in a table with columns for the dimensions, the volume and the multiplier. The multiplier column reads 2 in every row where exactly one length has been doubled. This is the first result of the investigation: doubling one dimension of a prism or the height of a cylinder doubles the volume. The questions for this section are with you now: the 4 by 3 by 2 box stretched each way, the triangular prism, the taller cylinder, and why the multiplier is always 2.

## Subtopic: Doubling Two Dimensions, and the Cylinder's Radius

Double two dimensions of the box: 8 by 6 by 2. The volume is 8 times 6 times 2, which is 96 cubic centimetres, four times 24. Two of the three factors have doubled, so the product has been multiplied by 2 times 2. Any pair gives the same: 8 by 3 by 4 is 96, and 4 by 6 by 4 is 96. The picture is four original boxes, two along and two across.

For the triangular prism, doubling both legs of the triangle, to 6 and 8, makes the end area one half times 6 times 8, which is 24, four times the original 6, because area in two dimensions is multiplied by 4 when both dimensions double. The volume becomes 24 times 10, which is 240, four times 60. Doubling one leg and the length also gives four times: the end area is 12 and the length is 20, so 240 again.

Now the cylinder's radius, which is where most errors are made. The radius appears squared in pi r squared h, so it acts as two dimensions at once: it sets both the width and the depth of the circular base. Doubling the radius of the cylinder with radius 2 and height 5, to radius 4, gives pi times 4 squared times 5, which is 80 pi, about 251.33 cubic centimetres. That is four times 20 pi, not twice. Doubling the radius alone multiplies a cylinder's volume by 4, while doubling the height alone multiplies it by 2.

This explains a practical choice. A rainwater tank of radius 0.65 metres and height 1.6 metres holds pi times 0.65 squared times 1.6, about 2.12 cubic metres, about 2 124 litres. A tank twice as tall holds about 4 247 litres, twice as much; a tank twice as wide, with radius 1.3 metres and the same height, holds about 8 495 litres, four times as much. Widening is the more powerful way to increase capacity, and the wider tank also needs less plastic for each litre it holds.

The rule for the radius generalises: any measurement that appears squared in the volume formula multiplies the volume by 4 when doubled. Your questions on this section are ready: two dimensions of the box doubled, both legs of the triangle, the radius doubled giving 80 pi, and the two ways of enlarging the tank.

## Subtopic: Doubling Every Dimension: Eight Times the Volume

Double all three dimensions of the box: 8 by 6 by 4. The volume is 8 times 6 times 4, which is 192 cubic centimetres, eight times 24. Every factor of the product has doubled, so the product has been multiplied by 2 times 2 times 2, which is 8. The picture is eight original boxes: two along, two across and two up, a large box built of a two-by-two-by-two stack.

A cube of side 5 centimetres has volume 125 cubic centimetres. A cube of side 10 has volume 1 000, eight times as much. Eight small cubes fit inside the large one, in two layers of four.

For the cylinder, doubling every dimension means doubling the radius and the height. Radius 4 and height 10 give pi times 16 times 10, which is 160 pi, about 502.65 cubic centimetres, eight times 20 pi. The radius contributes a factor of 4 and the height a factor of 2, and 4 times 2 is 8. For the triangular prism, doubling both legs and the length gives an end area of 24 and a length of 20, a volume of 480, eight times 60.

Surface area behaves differently. Every face of the doubled box has both of its dimensions doubled, so every face has four times its area, and the surface area is multiplied by 4, not 8. The 4 by 3 by 2 box has surface area 52 square centimetres; the 8 by 6 by 4 box has 2 times the quantity 48 plus 32 plus 24, which is 208, four times 52. The cube of side 5 has surface area 150; the cube of side 10 has 600, four times as much, while its volume went up eight times.

So when every dimension doubles: lengths are multiplied by 2, surface areas by 4 and volumes by 8. Length is one-dimensional and doubling acts once; area is two-dimensional and doubling acts twice; volume is three-dimensional and doubling acts three times. That pattern, 2, 4, 8, is the whole investigation in one line. The questions for this section are ready: the 8 by 6 by 4 box, the cubes of side 5 and 10, the cylinder doubled every way, and surface area against volume.

## Subtopic: Scale Factor k, Applications and the Error Museum

Replace doubling by any scale factor k, applied to every dimension. Every length is multiplied by k, every area by k squared and every volume by k cubed. For k equal to 3, the volume is multiplied by 27: the 3, 4, 5 triangular prism of volume 60 becomes 1 620 cubic centimetres. For k equal to one half, the volume is multiplied by one eighth. For k equal to 1.5, the volume is multiplied by 1.5 cubed, which is 3.375.

The rule runs backwards with a cube root. If a container is enlarged in proportion so that it holds 8 times as much, its dimensions were multiplied by the cube root of 8, which is 2. If it holds 27 times as much, by 3. To double the volume of a tin while keeping its shape, multiply every dimension by the cube root of 2, about 1.26, so a tin 10 centimetres tall becomes about 12.6 centimetres tall, not 20.

Applications. A family pot twice as wide and twice as deep as a small one, in the same shape, holds 8 times as much stew but needs only 4 times as much metal. This is why large containers are cheaper per litre: material follows k squared, contents follow k cubed. The same mismatch explains nature. A cube of side 1 has surface area 6 and volume 1, a ratio of 6 to 1; a cube of side 2 has surface area 24 and volume 8, a ratio of 3 to 1. Bigger objects have less surface for each unit of volume, so a large block of ice melts more slowly in proportion than crushed ice, and a large animal loses heat more slowly than a small one, which is why an elephant needs its huge ears to shed heat while a mouse must eat almost constantly to stay warm.

The error museum, five exhibits. Exhibit one: claiming that doubling every dimension doubles the volume; it multiplies it by 8. Exhibit two: claiming that doubling a cylinder's radius doubles its volume; it multiplies it by 4. Exhibit three: using k squared for volume, so that tripling every dimension gives 9 times the volume instead of 27. Exhibit four: working backwards with the wrong root, saying that 8 times the volume means dimensions multiplied by 8, or by the square root of 8. Exhibit five: applying the volume factor to surface area, claiming that a box with doubled dimensions needs 8 times as much cardboard.

Layout for marks: state which dimensions change and by what factor, write the factor for the volume as a product of the individual factors, calculate the original and new volumes directly as a check, and state the conclusion as a multiplier. The questions for this section are with you now: k cubed for k equal to 3 and one half, the cube root backwards, the pot and the elephant, and the five exhibits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through stretching a box one way then two ways, a radius that counts twice, and a scale factor you cube — plain words, same rules.

## Subtopic: Stretch One Way, Then Two

A box 4 by 3 by 2 holds 24 little centimetre cubes. Stretch it one way, making the 4 into 8. Now it holds 48: two of the old boxes end to end. Stretch any one direction and you get exactly two old boxes, so the volume doubles.

Now stretch two ways: 8 by 6 by 2. It holds 96, which is four old boxes, two along and two across. Two directions doubled means times 2, times 2 again: four times.

A tent-shaped prism with a triangle end of area 6 and length 10 holds 60. Make it twice as long: 120. Make the triangle twice as wide instead: also 120. Make the triangle twice as wide and twice as tall: the triangle's area becomes 24, four times bigger, and the prism holds 240.

The pattern is simple. Count how many of the directions you doubled. One direction: times 2. Two directions: times 4. Every doubled direction multiplies the volume by another 2. Your questions on this section are ready: the box stretched one way and two ways, the tent prism, and counting the doubled directions.

## Subtopic: The Radius Counts Twice

A cylinder hides a trick. Its floor is a circle, and the radius sets how wide that circle is in every direction at once, both side to side and front to back. So doubling the radius is like doubling two directions.

Take a cylinder with radius 2 and height 5: pi times 2 times 2 times 5, which is 20 pi, about 63 cubic centimetres. Make it twice as tall: 40 pi, double. Make the radius twice as big instead: pi times 4 times 4 times 5, which is 80 pi, four times as much. Same doubling, very different result, because the radius appears twice in the formula.

That is why a farmer who wants more rainwater should buy a wider tank, not a taller one. A tank holding about 2 124 litres, made twice as tall, holds about 4 247 litres. Made twice as wide, it holds about 8 495 litres. Four times the water for the same doubling.

Double both the radius and the height: radius 4 and height 10 make 160 pi, eight times the original. The radius gave times 4 and the height times 2, and 4 times 2 is 8. The questions for this section: taller doubles, wider quadruples, the tank choice, and both together making eight.

## Subtopic: Times k Cubed

Double every direction and the volume goes up 8 times. A cube of side 5 holds 125; a cube of side 10 holds 1 000. Picture it: 8 of the small cubes fit inside, 4 on the bottom and 4 on top. But the wrapping paper, the surface area, only goes up 4 times: 150 becomes 600.

Any scale factor works the same way. Call it k. Lengths times k, areas times k squared, volumes times k cubed. Triple every direction: volume times 27. Halve every direction: volume times one eighth.

Backwards, use the cube root. If a pot holds 8 times as much and has the same shape, every measurement is 2 times bigger, because 2 times 2 times 2 is 8. To make a tin hold just twice as much in the same shape, multiply every measurement by about 1.26, not by 2.

This is why big things are different. A big pot holds 8 times as much stew but uses only 4 times the metal, so big containers are cheaper per litre. A big block of ice has less outside for each bit of inside, so it melts slowly in proportion; crushed ice melts fast. An elephant has the same problem with heat and flaps its giant ears to cool down.

Stretch one way for times 2, two ways for times 4; the radius counts twice; every way for times k cubed. The final questions of the lesson are with you now: the cubes of side 5 and 10, times 27, the cube root backwards, and the big pot.
