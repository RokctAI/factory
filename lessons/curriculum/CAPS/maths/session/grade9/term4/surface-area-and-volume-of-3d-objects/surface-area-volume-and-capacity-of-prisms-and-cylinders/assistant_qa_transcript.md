### subtopic_1 — Surface Area of Cubes and Prisms
**Assistant:** Why does the formula for a rectangular prism have a 2 in front of the brackets?

**Tutor:** Because the six faces of a rectangular prism come in three matching pairs, and the 2 counts each pair. Look at a box 4 by 3 by 2 centimetres. The top and bottom are both 4 by 3; the front and back are both 4 by 2; the two ends are both 3 by 2. Opposite faces of a rectangular prism are always congruent, since each is the same rectangle seen from the other side. So instead of adding six areas, 12 plus 12 plus 8 plus 8 plus 6 plus 6, you add one face from each pair, 12 plus 8 plus 6, which is 26, and double it to get 52 square centimetres. That is all the formula 2 times the quantity lb plus lh plus bh says. Understanding the pairs has two practical benefits. First, it makes the formula easy to adapt: a box without a lid is missing one of the l times b faces, so its area is lb plus 2lh plus 2bh, and you can write that down by thinking about the faces rather than memorising a second formula. Second, it is a check against the commonest slip, which is to calculate the three products and forget to double, giving 26 instead of 52. If your surface area for a closed box is less than the sum of any two different faces doubled, something is missing. The same pairing idea gives the prism formula, two ends plus the strip of sides, and the cylinder formula, two circles plus the curved surface: in every closed prism or cylinder the ends come in a pair.

### subtopic_2 — Surface Area of Cylinders
**Assistant:** Where does 2 pi r h come from? It does not look like length times breadth.

**Tutor:** It is length times breadth, once you unroll the curved surface. Peel the label off a tin and lay it flat: it becomes a rectangle. Its breadth is the height of the tin, h, because the label runs from the bottom rim to the top rim. Its length is the distance it wrapped around the tin, which is the circumference of the circular end, 2 pi r. So the area of the rectangle is 2 pi r times h, written 2 pi r h. For the tin of radius 3.5 and height 10, the label is about 21.99 centimetres long and 10 wide, about 219.91 square centimetres, exactly what the formula gives. Seeing it this way makes the full formula easy to rebuild if you forget it: two circles, each pi r squared, plus one rectangle, 2 pi r by h. It also explains why the radius must be used and not the diameter, because the circumference is 2 pi r; if you are given the diameter you can use pi d instead, but you must not put the diameter into a formula written for r. And it lets you handle variations without new formulas: an open-topped tank is one circle plus the rectangle, a pipe open at both ends is the rectangle alone, and a label that covers only part of the height is a rectangle with a smaller breadth. The curved surface area of a cylinder is the rectangle in its net, and the net is always the safest place to start.

### subtopic_3 — Volume and Capacity
**Assistant:** Why does area of base times height work for a cylinder when its base is round, not square?

**Tutor:** Because the rule does not depend on the shape of the base at all; it depends on the solid being a stack of identical layers. Imagine slicing the tin into layers 1 centimetre thick, like coins in a stack. Each layer is a circular disc with the same area as the base, pi times 3.5 squared, about 38.48 square centimetres, and since it is 1 centimetre thick its volume is about 38.48 cubic centimetres. A tin 10 centimetres high is 10 such layers, so its volume is about 384.85 cubic centimetres. Nothing in that argument used the fact that the base is a circle; it used only the fact that every cross-section parallel to the base is the same. That uniform cross-section is exactly the property that cylinders share with prisms, which is why one rule covers cubes, rectangular prisms, triangular prisms and cylinders. It also tells you when the rule does not apply. A cone or a pyramid narrows as it rises, so its layers shrink, and its volume is only a third of base times height, which you will meet in Grade 10. A bucket that widens towards the top is not a cylinder either. So before using base times height, ask whether every horizontal slice is the same as the base. If it is, the rule works for any base you can find the area of: a triangle, a trapezium, an L-shape, or a circle.

### subtopic_4 — Converting SI Units and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Why is a square metre ten thousand square centimetres and not just a hundred?

**Tutor:** Because area has two dimensions, and each one is converted separately. Draw a square 1 metre on each side. Its side is 100 centimetres, so if you divide it into 1 centimetre squares there are 100 squares along the bottom row and 100 rows, which is 100 times 100, or 10 000 small squares. The factor of 100 for metres to centimetres applies to the length and again to the breadth. For volume it applies three times: a cube 1 metre on each side holds 100 rows of 100 columns of 1 centimetre cubes in each of 100 layers, which is 1 000 000 cubic centimetres. Dividing by 100 when converting square centimetres to square metres gives an answer one hundred times too large, and the error is costly in practice: the tin's 296.88 square centimetres becomes 2.9688 square metres instead of the correct 0.0297, so anyone ordering material from that figure orders a hundred times too much. The most reliable defence is to convert lengths before calculating. A crate 1.2 metres by 80 centimetres by 50 centimetres becomes 120 by 80 by 50 centimetres, and only then is the volume calculated, 480 000 cubic centimetres; converting that to litres uses 1 000 cubic centimetres per litre, giving 480 litres, and to cubic metres uses 1 000 000, giving 0.48. When you must convert an area or a volume directly, write the factor as a power: 100 squared for areas, 100 cubed for volumes. The exponent matches the little 2 or 3 in the unit, and that is the rule to remember.
