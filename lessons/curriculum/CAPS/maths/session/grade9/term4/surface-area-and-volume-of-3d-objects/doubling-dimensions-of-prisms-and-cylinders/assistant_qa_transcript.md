### subtopic_1 — Doubling One Dimension of a Prism
**Assistant:** Does it matter which dimension I double? The box 8 by 3 by 2 looks very different from 4 by 3 by 4.

**Tutor:** It matters for the shape but not for the volume. Volume is the product of the three dimensions, and multiplication does not care about order: 8 times 3 times 2, 4 times 6 times 2 and 4 times 3 times 4 are all 48, because in each case one factor of the original product, 24, has been doubled. The boxes do look different, one long and low, one tall, but each holds exactly two of the original boxes: placed end to end, side by side, or one on top of the other. The surface areas, however, are not equal, and that is a useful contrast. The 8 by 3 by 2 box has surface area 2 times the quantity 24 plus 16 plus 6, which is 92 square centimetres, while the 4 by 3 by 4 box has 2 times the quantity 12 plus 16 plus 12, which is 80. Same volume, different amounts of material. Volume depends only on the product of the dimensions; surface area depends on how they are combined in pairs. That is why manufacturers choose box shapes carefully: two boxes that hold the same amount can need quite different amounts of cardboard. For volume, though, the rule is clean and worth stating exactly: doubling any single dimension of a prism doubles its volume, and doubling the height of a cylinder doubles its volume. The cylinder's radius is the exception, and it is the subject of the next section.

### subtopic_2 — Doubling Two Dimensions, and the Cylinder's Radius
**Assistant:** The radius is one measurement. Why does doubling it multiply the volume by four instead of two?

**Tutor:** Because one measurement can control two dimensions, and the radius of a cylinder does. The base of a cylinder is a circle, and its area is pi r squared, r times r. When you double the radius, the circle grows in every direction across the base at once: it becomes twice as wide from left to right and twice as wide from front to back. In a rectangular prism those would be two separate measurements, the length and the breadth, and doubling both multiplies the base area by 4. The circle does the same, with one number controlling both. So the base area of the cylinder with radius 2, which is 4 pi, becomes 16 pi when the radius becomes 4, four times as large, and with the height unchanged the volume is also four times as large, 80 pi against 20 pi. The formula shows it immediately: r appears squared, so a factor of 2 on r becomes a factor of 2 squared, which is 4, on the volume, while h appears to the first power, so a factor of 2 on h stays a factor of 2. A useful habit is to look at the exponent on each measurement in the formula before predicting anything. In pi r squared h the radius has exponent 2 and the height exponent 1, and doubling multiplies the volume by 2 raised to each exponent. That habit will serve you again in Grade 10 and beyond, for spheres and cones, where the radius appears cubed or squared in their formulas too.

### subtopic_3 — Doubling Every Dimension: Eight Times the Volume
**Assistant:** If the volume goes up eight times, why does the surface area only go up four times?

**Tutor:** Because surface area is built from two-dimensional pieces and volume from three-dimensional ones, so doubling acts twice on one and three times on the other. Every face of a box is a rectangle with two dimensions. When all the box's dimensions double, each face has both of its sides doubled, so each face has 2 times 2, which is 4 times, its original area, and the total surface, being the sum of those faces, is 4 times as large: the 4 by 3 by 2 box has 52 square centimetres and the 8 by 6 by 4 box has 208. The volume is the product of all three dimensions, so it picks up three factors of 2, which is 8: 24 becomes 192. You can see the mismatch by building. Eight small boxes fill the big one, but when they are packed together many of their faces are hidden inside, pressed against each other, and only the outside faces count as surface. The eight small boxes together have 8 times 52, which is 416 square centimetres of surface, but the big box shows only 208 of it; half the original surface has been buried in the middle. That burial is the reason the ratio of surface to volume falls as objects get bigger, and it has real consequences: large boxes use less cardboard per litre, and large animals have less skin per kilogram through which to lose heat. The 2, 4, 8 pattern for length, area and volume is one of the most useful facts in practical geometry.

### subtopic_4 — Scale Factor k, Applications and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** To make a tin hold twice as much in the same shape, why multiply by 1.26 and not by 2?

**Tutor:** Because keeping the same shape means scaling every dimension by the same factor k, and then the volume is multiplied by k cubed, not by k. If you multiply every dimension by 2, the volume is multiplied by 2 cubed, which is 8, far more than the double you wanted. You need the k for which k cubed equals 2, which is the cube root of 2, about 1.26: 1.26 times 1.26 times 1.26 is about 2.0. So a tin 10 centimetres tall with a radius of 3.5 centimetres becomes about 12.6 centimetres tall with a radius of about 4.41 centimetres, and it holds twice as much while looking like a slightly larger version of the original. This surprises people because the new tin does not look twice as big; it looks only about a quarter bigger. That is exactly the point: our eyes judge size by length, while capacity grows with the cube of length. It is also why a pizza or a pot that looks only a little bigger can hold much more, and why advertisers and packaging designers can mislead with a container that is slightly taller but narrower. Working backwards always uses the root that matches the dimension: a square root for areas, because area scales with k squared, and a cube root for volumes, because volume scales with k cubed. If a container in the same shape holds 27 times as much, the cube root of 27 is 3, so every length has tripled. Writing k cubed equals the volume factor, and then solving for k, keeps the reasoning honest.
