Today we investigate what happens to the volume of a prism or cylinder when we double some or all of its dimensions. Starting from a box 4 by 3 by 2 centimetres with a volume of 24, we find that doubling any one dimension doubles the volume to 48, doubling two multiplies it by four to 96, and doubling all three multiplies it by eight to 192. A cylinder holds a surprise: its radius counts twice, so doubling the radius alone multiplies the volume by four, which is why a wider rainwater tank gains far more than a taller one. We compare volume with surface area, which goes up only four times when every dimension doubles, and generalise to a scale factor k: lengths times k, areas times k squared, volumes times k cubed, with the cube root to work backwards. We finish with big pots, melting ice, elephants' ears and the five common errors.
