# Part 1 — Expert

This session applies percentage calculation to the financial contexts the Grade 9 curriculum names: profit and loss, discount, VAT, simple and compound interest, hire purchase, exchange rates, commission, budgets and accounts. One technique underlies all of them — a percentage of an amount is that amount multiplied by the percentage over one hundred — and the discipline is to state the formula, substitute, and write the answer with its unit on every line.

## Subtopic: Profit, Loss, Discount and VAT

Profit is selling price minus cost price when the selling price is higher; loss is cost price minus selling price when it is lower. The percentage is ALWAYS calculated on the cost price, never on the selling price — that is the single most common error in this topic.

A trader buys a pair of takkies for R250 and sells them for R320. Profit: 320 minus 250 is R70. Percentage profit: 70 over 250 times 100, which is 28 percent. A second trader buys a phone for R800 and is forced to sell it for R680. Loss: 800 minus 680 is R120. Percentage loss: 120 over 800 times 100, which is 15 percent.

Working forwards from a required percentage: cost price R250 and a mark-up of 40 percent. The selling price is 250 times 1,4, which is R350 — multiply by one plus the percentage as a decimal, because you want the original 100 percent plus the extra 40.

Discount reduces a marked price. A jacket marked R450 is discounted by 20 percent. The discount is 20 over 100 times 450, which is R90, and the sale price is R360. Faster: 450 times 0,8 is 360 — multiply by one minus the percentage, because you keep 80 percent of the price.

VAT, value-added tax, is charged at 15 percent in South Africa on most goods. A price excluding VAT of R200 becomes 200 times 1,15, which is R230 including VAT. The reverse is where marks are lost: a price INCLUDING VAT of R460 is already 115 percent of the pre-VAT price, so the price excluding VAT is 460 divided by 1,15, which is R400 — and the VAT portion is R60. Never take 15 percent off an inclusive price; divide by 1,15.

Take the questions for this section now: percentage on the cost price, multiplying by 1,4 or 0,8 for mark-up and discount, and dividing by 1,15 to strip VAT out of an inclusive price.

## Subtopic: Simple Interest and Hire Purchase

Interest is the price of borrowing money, or the reward for lending it. Simple interest is calculated on the original amount only, every period. The formula: interest equals P times i times n, where P is the principal, i is the interest rate per year as a decimal, and n is the number of years.

R5 000 is invested at 8 percent per annum simple interest for 3 years. Interest: 5 000 times 0,08 times 3, which is R1 200. The total amount is 5 000 plus 1 200, which is R6 200. Each year earns the same R400, because the interest is always computed on the unchanging R5 000.

Hire purchase is simple interest in a shop. You pay a deposit, the balance attracts simple interest for the agreement period, and the total is split into equal monthly instalments. A television costs R6 000 cash. A customer pays a 10 percent deposit and the balance over 24 months at 12 percent per annum simple interest. Deposit: 10 percent of 6 000 is R600. Balance: 6 000 minus 600 is R5 400. Interest on the balance: 5 400 times 0,12 times 2, which is R1 296. Total owed on the balance: 5 400 plus 1 296 is R6 696. Monthly instalment: 6 696 divided by 24, which is R279. Total paid for the television: deposit plus instalments, 600 plus 6 696, which is R7 296 — R1 296 more than the cash price. State that comparison in words; it is a mark.

Two habits. Convert the rate to a decimal before substituting — 12 percent is 0,12. And convert the time to YEARS, because the rate is per year: 24 months is 2 years, 18 months is 1,5 years.

This section's questions are with you now: the P i n formula, the deposit-balance-interest-instalment chain, and the comparison between cash price and total hire-purchase price.

## Subtopic: Compound Interest — Interest on the Interest

Compound interest is calculated on the growing balance: each period's interest is added, and the next period earns interest on the new total. The formula: A equals P times one plus i, all to the power n, where A is the final amount.

The same R5 000 at 8 percent per annum, compounded annually for 3 years. Year by year first, to see the engine. End of year one: 5 000 times 1,08 is R5 400. End of year two: 5 400 times 1,08 is R5 832. End of year three: 5 832 times 1,08 is R6 298,56. Now the formula in one line: 5 000 times 1,08 cubed. 1,08 cubed is 1,259712, and 5 000 times that is R6 298,56. The interest earned is 6 298,56 minus 5 000, which is R1 298,56.

Compare with simple interest on the same money: R6 200 against R6 298,56. Compound interest earned R98,56 more over three years, and the gap widens every year because the interest itself starts earning. Over long periods — a pension, a home loan — the gap becomes enormous; this is why savers want compound interest and borrowers fear it.

Three technical points. The rate and the period must match: an annual rate compounded annually uses n in years. Round only at the end — carry the full 1,259712 through, then round the rand answer to two decimal places. And when a question asks for the INTEREST, subtract the principal from A; when it asks for the AMOUNT, A is the answer. Reading the question for that one word is a mark.

Turn to the questions for this section: the year-by-year engine, the power formula, the comparison with simple interest, and the amount-versus-interest distinction.

## Subtopic: Exchange Rates, Commission, Budgets and Accounts

An exchange rate states how many rand one unit of foreign currency costs. Suppose one US dollar costs R18,50. To convert dollars to rands, multiply: 200 dollars is 200 times 18,50, which is R3 700. To convert rands to dollars, divide: R5 550 is 5 550 divided by 18,50, which is 300 dollars. The direction rule is simple — moving to the weaker currency, the rand, the number gets bigger, so multiply; moving to the stronger currency, the number gets smaller, so divide.

Commission is pay calculated as a percentage of sales. A car salesperson earns a basic salary of R4 000 plus 5 percent commission on sales. In a month with sales of R48 000, commission is 5 percent of 48 000, which is R2 400, and total pay is 4 000 plus 2 400, which is R6 400. Rentals follow the same idea when an agent earns a percentage of the rent collected.

A budget sets planned income against planned expenses. Income R6 400. Expenses: rent R2 500, food R1 800, transport R900, airtime and data R300. Total expenses: 2 500 plus 1 800 is 4 300; plus 900 is 5 200; plus 300 is 5 500. Surplus: 6 400 minus 5 500 is R900, available for saving or debt. If expenses exceed income, the budget shows a deficit, and the deficit must be closed by cutting spending or borrowing.

An account — a municipal bill, a store card statement — is read in the same arithmetic. Opening balance owed, plus new charges, minus payments made, gives the closing balance. A statement with an opening balance of R1 250, new charges of R860 and a payment of R1 000 closes at 1 250 plus 860 minus 1 000, which is R1 110. A loan statement adds interest as a charge each month.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: percentage profit on the selling price instead of the cost price. Two: subtracting 15 percent from a VAT-inclusive price instead of dividing by 1,15. Three: months left as months in the interest formula when the rate is annual. Four: multiplying when converting rand to dollars — a holiday budget of R5 550 does not buy 102 675 dollars; sense-check the size.

The closing questions of this part are in front of you now: the multiply-or-divide direction for currency, commission on top of a basic salary, the budget surplus, and the four exhibits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the whole lesson again from behind a spaza-shop counter, a savings jar and a holiday money-change desk — same numbers, everyday words.

## Subtopic: Buying Low, Selling High at the Spaza

Run a spaza shop for a minute. You buy a pair of takkies for R250 and sell them for R320. You made R70 — that is your profit. But R70 on its own does not say how good a deal it was. Compared with what you PAID, R70 is 70 out of 250, and 70 over 250 times 100 is 28 percent. Always compare with what you paid, the cost price, because that is the money you risked.

Sometimes you lose. You buy a phone for R800 and can only sell it for R680. You are R120 down, and 120 over 800 times 100 is a 15 percent loss. Same picture, opposite direction.

Discount is the shop being kind to the customer. A jacket is R450 and the sign says 20 percent off. Twenty percent of 450 is R90, so you pay R360. The quick way: you keep 80 percent of the price, so 450 times 0,8 is 360.

VAT is the government taking 15 cents on every rand you spend. A price of R200 before VAT becomes R200 plus 15 percent, which is 200 times 1,15, which is R230. Now the sneaky one. A till slip shows R460 INCLUDING VAT, and you want the price before VAT. Do not take 15 percent off R460 — that is 15 percent of the wrong number. The R460 is already 115 percent of the original, so divide by 1,15: 460 over 1,15 is R400, and the VAT was R60. Putting VAT on is multiply by 1,15; taking it off is divide by 1,15.

Your questions for this section are with you now: profit and loss compared with what you paid, discount as what you keep, and VAT on and VAT off.

## Subtopic: Interest — Rent You Pay on Money

Interest is rent on money. Borrow money and you pay rent for using it; save money and the bank pays you rent for using yours. The rent is a percentage per year.

Simple interest is rent on the ORIGINAL amount only. Put R5 000 in at 8 percent a year. Every year the bank pays 8 percent of R5 000, which is R400 — year one R400, year two R400, year three R400. Three years, R1 200, so you have R6 200. The rent never changes because it is always worked out on the same R5 000.

Compound interest is rent on the GROWING pile. Same R5 000, same 8 percent, but now the interest gets added to the pile before next year's rent is calculated. Year one: 5 000 times 1,08 is 5 400. Year two: 5 400 times 1,08 is 5 832 — notice the rent this year is R432, not R400, because the pile grew. Year three: 5 832 times 1,08 is 6 298,56. You end with R6 298,56 instead of R6 200. The extra R98,56 is interest earned by interest. Over three years that is pocket change; over thirty years, on a home loan, it is the difference between a house and two houses. Savers love compound interest. Borrowers must respect it.

Hire purchase is simple interest wearing a shop uniform. A TV is R6 000 cash. You put down 10 percent, R600, and owe R5 400. The shop charges 12 percent a year simple interest for two years: 5 400 times 0,12 times 2 is R1 296 of rent. So you owe 5 400 plus 1 296, which is R6 696, paid as 24 instalments of 6 696 over 24, which is R279 a month. Add the deposit and the TV cost you R7 296 — R1 296 more than the cash price. That is the price of paying later.

Your questions on this section are ready: rent on the same pile versus rent on the growing pile, and what the TV really costs on terms.

## Subtopic: Rands to Dollars and Making a Budget

Changing money. Suppose one US dollar costs R18,50. A dollar is the expensive one — it takes many rands to buy one — so going from dollars to rands the number gets BIGGER: multiply. 200 dollars is 200 times 18,50, which is R3 700. Going from rands to dollars the number gets SMALLER: divide. R5 550 is 5 550 over 18,50, which is 300 dollars. If you ever get more dollars than you had rands, you went the wrong way.

Commission is pay that depends on how much you sell. A car salesperson gets R4 000 basic plus 5 percent of sales. Sell R48 000 worth of cars and the commission is 5 percent of 48 000, which is R2 400, so the month pays R6 400. Sell nothing and you still get the R4 000 basic — the commission is the part you control.

Now a budget, which is just a list with two sides. Money in: R6 400. Money out: rent R2 500, food R1 800, transport R900, airtime R300. Add the out column: 2 500 plus 1 800 is 4 300, plus 900 is 5 200, plus 300 is 5 500. Money left: 6 400 minus 5 500 is R900. That R900 is the surplus — the part you can save or use to pay off a debt. If the out column had been bigger than the in column, that would be a deficit, and something would have to be cut or borrowed.

An account statement is the same list over time. You owed R1 250, the shop added R860 of new purchases, you paid R1 000: 1 250 plus 860 minus 1 000 is R1 110 still owing. Opening balance, plus charges, minus payments, equals closing balance — every statement in the country follows that line.

Hold the pictures: percentage of what you paid, rent on money, multiply towards rands and divide towards dollars, and two columns for a budget. The final questions of the lesson are with you now: the money-change direction, commission on top of basic pay, and the budget surplus.
