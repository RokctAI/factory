### subtopic_1 — The Family Tree of Numbers
**Assistant:** Why is zero a whole number but not a natural number — isn't that just a technicality?

**Tutor:** It is a definition, and definitions are what examiners test, so it is worth owning rather than memorising. The natural numbers are the counting numbers, and counting begins at one: you count the sheep in a kraal as one, two, three, and a kraal with no sheep is not counted at all — it is described, as empty. Zero was invented later, as a placeholder and as the name for "nothing", and when it is added to the counting numbers the resulting set gets its own name, the whole numbers, and its own symbol, N with a small zero. So zero is the single number that separates the two innermost boxes: it is whole but not natural. The practical consequence is a classic question — "give a number that is whole but not natural" — and there is exactly one correct answer, zero. Hold the nesting the other way too: every natural number is automatically a whole number, an integer, a rational and a real, because each box sits inside the next. Zero simply joins the family one box further out than the counting numbers do.

### subtopic_2 — Rational Numbers: Decimals That Stop or Repeat
**Assistant:** When we subtract to convert a recurring decimal, how can two infinite tails cancel exactly?

**Tutor:** Because the two tails are not merely similar — they are identical, digit for digit, for ever. Take x equal to 0,333... Multiplying by ten shifts every digit one place left, giving 3,333..., and the part after the comma is still a 3 in every position without exception. When you subtract x, each 3 after the comma meets a 3 in the same position, and 3 minus 3 is 0 in every one of those infinitely many places. Nothing is left over, because there is no last digit where a mismatch could appear. That is the entire reason the multiplier must match the block: for 0,2727... you multiply by one hundred so that the shifted decimal 27,2727... has the same block, 27, sitting in the same positions as the original. Subtract and the tails vanish, leaving 99x equals 27. If you multiplied by ten instead, the tails would be 7,2727... against 0,2727... — different digits in the same places — and the subtraction would not clean up. One power of ten per digit in the repeating block, and the infinite part disappears completely.

### subtopic_3 — Irrational Numbers: Roots and Pi
**Assistant:** If a calculator shows root 2 as 1,414213562, doesn't that make it a terminating decimal?

**Tutor:** The calculator is showing you a rounded approximation, not the number. A screen has ten or so digit positions, so it must cut the decimal off somewhere and round the last digit; 1,414213562 is a rational number that is extremely close to root 2, in exactly the way 3,14 is close to pi without being pi. The true square root of 2 has a decimal expansion that continues past any screen, past any page, with no block that ever repeats — and that has been proved, not merely observed. A quick sanity check shows the screen value cannot be exact: square 1,414213562 by hand and you get 1,999999998 and some change, not 2. The lesson for classification is this: never judge a number by its display. Judge it by what it is. Root 2 is the square root of a number that is not a perfect square, so it is irrational regardless of how a calculator chooses to show it. Root 9, by contrast, displays as 3 and is exactly 3, because 9 is a perfect square. The display is a hint; the perfect-square test is the proof.

### subtopic_4 — Classifying the Anchor List and the Exam Habits
**Assistant:** Which classification mistake do learners make most often, and how do you avoid it?

**Tutor:** The most common error is labelling by appearance instead of by value — a root sign is read as "irrational", a long decimal is read as "irrational", a fraction is read as "not an integer". Each of those reflexes fails on a standard example. Root 16 is 4, a natural number; 0,123456789 terminates and is rational; 8 over 2 is 4, an integer. The protective habit is one line of work before any label: simplify the number to its plainest form. Evaluate every root, divide out every fraction that divides exactly, and for any decimal decide whether it stops or repeats. Only then do you write the sets, and you write them from the smallest box outward so that nothing is omitted. The second-most common slip is forgetting that whole numbers are never negative, so negative 3 is wrongly called whole. Say the nesting to yourself — counting numbers, add zero, add negatives, add fractions — and the box for negative 3 opens at the third step, not the second. Simplify first, nest second, and the classification question becomes a guaranteed mark.
