### subtopic_1 — Prime Factorisation by the Ladder Method
**Assistant:** Does it matter which prime you divide by first? Could you start with 5 for 360?

**Tutor:** You could, and you would reach the same answer, because the prime factorisation of a number is unique — 360 is 2 cubed times 3 squared times 5 no matter which route you take to it. Starting with 5 gives 72, then 2, 2, 2 gives 9, then 3, 3, and the same six primes appear. So the order is not a matter of correctness. It is a matter of discipline, and discipline is what prevents errors under time pressure. Dividing by the smallest prime that fits, and continuing with it until it no longer divides, guarantees you never skip a prime and never accidentally divide by a composite number such as 6 or 15. It also hands you the primes already in ascending order, so the index form writes itself: count the 2s, count the 3s, count the 5s. Learners who jump around tend to leave a 9 or a 12 sitting in the list, believing they are finished. Smallest prime first, every time, and the ladder polices itself.

### subtopic_2 — The HCF — Reading Off What Two Numbers Share
**Assistant:** Why is the HCF built from the lower powers and not the higher ones?

**Tutor:** Because a common factor must divide into BOTH numbers, and a number can only lend out as many copies of a prime as it actually holds. Take the prime 2 in our anchor pair. 360 holds three 2s and 84 holds only two. If the HCF contained 2 cubed, it would need three 2s from 84, and 84 does not have them — 8 does not divide 84. So the shared amount is limited by the poorer of the two, which is the lower power. The same logic removes 5 and 7 entirely: 84 holds no 5, so 5 cannot be a factor of both, and 360 holds no 7. Think of it as a joint purchase — you can only buy what both of you can afford. The LCM reverses the question: it must be a multiple of both, so it must contain enough of every prime to cover the richer of the two, which is the higher power, and it must include the lonely primes as well. Lower powers for what both can give, higher powers for what both must receive.

### subtopic_3 — The LCM — Building the First Common Multiple
**Assistant:** Isn't multiplying the two numbers together always a common multiple? Why bother with the LCM?

**Tutor:** It is always a common multiple — 360 times 84, which is 30 240, is certainly divisible by both — but it is almost never the LOWEST one, and the lowest is what real problems demand. The taxis leaving every 12 and 18 minutes do not next coincide after 216 minutes; they coincide after 36. If you answered 216 you would have found a later meeting, not the first one. The product is too large precisely because it double-counts the shared primes: 360 and 84 both contribute 2 squared times 3, and the product includes that 12 twice. Dividing the product by the HCF removes the duplicate and leaves the LCM — 30 240 divided by 12 is 2 520 — which is the relationship we used as a check. When the two numbers share no prime at all, the HCF is 1 and the product genuinely is the LCM; that is the only case where the shortcut is exact. For every other pair, build the LCM from the primes or divide the product by the HCF, and reserve the bare product for the check.

### subtopic_4 — Which One Does the Question Want?
**Assistant:** What is the fastest way to tell, in an exam, whether a word problem wants the HCF or the LCM?

**Tutor:** Ask what the answer will be compared with. If the answer is going to be SMALLER than the given numbers — a tile that fits into a floor, a pack size that divides a quantity, the largest group that shares things equally — the problem is dividing things down, and that is the HCF. If the answer is going to be LARGER than the given numbers — a time when repeating events coincide, the smallest length both rods measure out exactly, the first page number two counters land on together — the problem is building up to a meeting point, and that is the LCM. Then let the boundary rules confirm the choice: an HCF never exceeds the smaller number and an LCM never falls below the larger. In the tile problem, 2 520 centimetres for an 84 centimetre floor is absurd on sight, so it cannot be the HCF question's answer. The vocabulary helps too — "largest", "greatest", "biggest possible" lean towards HCF; "smallest", "least", "next time", "together again" lean towards LCM — but the size test is the one that never fails, because it reasons from what the answer must mean.
