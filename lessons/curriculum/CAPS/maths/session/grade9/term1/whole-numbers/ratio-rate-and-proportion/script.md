# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers four linked skills: simplifying and sharing in a ratio, building a rate from two different units, and solving problems in direct and in indirect proportion. The anchor problems are a R2 400 prize shared in the ratio 5 to 3 to 2, a taxi travelling 180 km in two and a half hours, the price of bread, and a crew of workers finishing a job. Each has a standard written form that the examiner expects line by line.

## Subtopic: Ratio — Simplifying and Sharing in a Given Ratio

A ratio compares quantities of the SAME kind, part to part. Three red beads to five blue beads is written 3 to 5, or 3 colon 5. The order matters: 3 to 5 is not 5 to 3.

Simplify a ratio exactly as you simplify a fraction — divide every part by the highest common factor. 250 to 1 000: the HCF is 250, so the ratio is 1 to 4. Units must agree before you simplify: 500 grams to 2 kilograms is 500 to 2 000 once both are in grams, and that simplifies to 1 to 4. A ratio in simplest form carries no units.

Now sharing. R2 400 is shared among three learners in the ratio 5 to 3 to 2. Step one: add the parts — 5 plus 3 plus 2 is 10 parts. Step two: find the value of one part — 2 400 divided by 10 is R240. Step three: multiply out. Five parts: 5 times 240 is R1 200. Three parts: 3 times 240 is R720. Two parts: 2 times 240 is R480. Step four: check — 1 200 plus 720 plus 480 is 2 400. The check is not optional; it is the mark that confirms the method.

The reverse question also appears. Two learners share money in the ratio 3 to 7 and the smaller share is R450. One part is 450 divided by 3, which is R150; the larger share is 7 times 150, which is R1 050; the total is 10 times 150, which is R1 500.

A ratio can also be written as a fraction of the whole — the learner with 5 parts out of 10 receives five tenths, which is one half, of the total. Part-to-part and part-to-whole are different fractions; read which one the question wants.

Take the questions for this section now: simplifying with matching units, the four-step sharing method, and the difference between part-to-part and part-to-whole.

## Subtopic: Rate — Comparing Quantities of Different Kinds

A rate compares two quantities of DIFFERENT kinds, and it keeps its units. Kilometres per hour. Rand per kilogram. Litres per hundred kilometres. The word "per" means "for each one", and a unit rate is the quantity for exactly one of the second thing.

A taxi covers 180 km in two and a half hours. Its average speed is distance divided by time: 180 divided by 2,5 is 72 km per hour. Keep the units in the answer. Rearranged, distance is speed times time: at 72 km per hour for 3,5 hours the taxi covers 72 times 3,5, which is 252 km. And time is distance divided by speed: 180 km at 60 km per hour takes 3 hours.

Unit price is the rate that saves money. Two litres of milk cost R34; one and a half litres cost R27. Per litre: 34 divided by 2 is R17 per litre; 27 divided by 1,5 is R18 per litre. The two-litre bottle is the better buy, by one rand per litre. Mince at R45 for 2,5 kg is 45 divided by 2,5, which is R18 per kilogram. Always divide the cost by the quantity so that the answer reads "rand per unit", and then compare.

Rates convert too. 72 km per hour in metres per second: 72 kilometres is 72 000 metres, one hour is 3 600 seconds, so 72 000 divided by 3 600 is 20 metres per second. The conversion factor between km per hour and m per s is always divide by 3,6.

This section's questions are with you now: the speed-distance-time rearrangements, unit price as the comparison tool, and keeping units on every rate.

## Subtopic: Direct Proportion — Constant Ratio

Two quantities are in direct proportion when one is a constant multiple of the other: double one and the other doubles; halve one and the other halves. The ratio between them never changes, and that constant ratio is the whole method.

Three loaves of bread cost R51. What do seven loaves cost? Find the unit rate first: 51 divided by 3 is R17 per loaf. Then multiply: 7 times 17 is R119. Alternatively, write the proportion: 51 over 3 equals x over 7, so x is 51 times 7 over 3, which is 119. Both routes pass through the same constant, R17 per loaf.

Petrol at a fixed price. 25 litres cost R575, so one litre costs 575 divided by 25, which is R23. Forty litres cost 40 times 23, which is R920. The constant ratio is cost over litres, always 23.

In a table of directly proportional values, every output divided by its input gives the same number. Loaves 1, 2, 3, 7: cost 17, 34, 51, 119. Each cost over loaves is 17. On a graph, direct proportion is a straight line through the origin — zero loaves, zero rand — with gradient equal to the constant. If the line misses the origin, the relationship is not a direct proportion, however straight it looks.

A map scale is a direct proportion in disguise. On a 1 to 50 000 map, 3 cm represents 3 times 50 000, which is 150 000 cm, and that is 1 500 m, which is 1,5 km.

Turn to the questions for this section: the unit-rate route and the cross-multiplication route, the constant-ratio test on a table, and the straight line through the origin.

## Subtopic: Indirect Proportion — Constant Product

Two quantities are in indirect proportion — also called inverse proportion — when one goes UP as the other goes DOWN, so that their PRODUCT stays constant. Double one and the other halves.

Six workers take 10 days to build a wall. How long would four workers take, working at the same rate? The constant is the total work: 6 workers times 10 days is 60 worker-days. Four workers need 60 divided by 4, which is 15 days. Fewer workers, more days — and the product 4 times 15 is still 60. Twelve workers would need 60 divided by 12, which is 5 days.

Speed and time over a fixed distance are indirectly proportional. A trip takes 3 hours at 60 km per hour, so the distance is 60 times 3, which is 180 km. At 90 km per hour the same trip takes 180 divided by 90, which is 2 hours. Faster speed, shorter time, same product.

The written method mirrors direct proportion but with multiplication where there was division. Direct: output over input is constant. Indirect: output times input is constant. Before you calculate, ask the diagnostic question: when the first quantity increases, does the second increase or decrease? Increase with increase is direct; increase with decrease is indirect. Bread costs more when you buy more — direct. A job finishes sooner when more people work — indirect.

On a graph, indirect proportion is a curve that falls as it moves right and never touches either axis — the hyperbola you will meet formally in Grade 10 — because neither quantity can be zero while the product stays fixed.

The error museum, three exhibits. One: treating a workers-and-days problem as direct and concluding that four workers take fewer days than six. The diagnostic question prevents it. Two: in direct proportion, adding instead of multiplying — seven loaves do not cost 51 plus 4. Three: dropping units from a rate, so that 72 is written without km per hour and the marker cannot tell speed from distance.

The closing questions of this part are in front of you now: the constant-product method, the diagnostic question that separates the two proportions, the speed-time pair, and the three exhibits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same four ideas from a shared taxi fare, a shelf in the supermarket and a Saturday of painting a wall — in ordinary words.

## Subtopic: Sharing the Prize Fairly

Three friends enter a competition together and win R2 400. One did most of the work, one did a fair amount, one helped a little, so they agree to split it 5 to 3 to 2. A ratio is just that agreement written down: for every 5 rand the first gets, the second gets 3 and the third gets 2.

The simplest way to share is to think in parts. Count the parts: 5 plus 3 plus 2 is 10 parts. Imagine the R2 400 cut into 10 equal piles: 2 400 divided by 10 is R240 a pile. Now hand out piles. Five piles is 5 times 240, which is R1 200. Three piles is R720. Two piles is R480. Add them back: 1 200 plus 720 plus 480 is 2 400 — every rand accounted for. Count the parts, price one part, hand out the parts, add to check.

Ratios also tidy up. Suppose a spaza shop mixes 250 ml of concentrate with 1 000 ml of water. The ratio 250 to 1 000 is the same as 1 to 4 — one measure of concentrate for every four of water. Divide both sides by the same number and the recipe does not change; it is just written smaller. The one trap is units: 500 g to 2 kg is NOT 500 to 2. Put both in grams first — 500 to 2 000 — and then simplify to 1 to 4.

Sometimes you know a share and not the total. Two friends split money 3 to 7 and the smaller share is R450. Then one part is 450 divided by 3, which is R150, so the bigger share is 7 times 150, which is R1 050, and the whole amount was 10 times 150, which is R1 500. Same parts picture, walked backwards.

Your questions for this section are with you now: counting parts, pricing one part, and the units trap when you simplify.

## Subtopic: Per — The Little Word That Makes a Rate

Walk down the dairy aisle. Two litres of milk for R34. One and a half litres for R27. Which is the better deal? You cannot compare R34 with R27 directly, because the bottles are different sizes. You need the price PER litre — the price for exactly one litre.

Divide the price by the number of litres. 34 divided by 2 is R17 per litre. 27 divided by 1,5 is R18 per litre. The big bottle is cheaper per litre by one rand. That is a rate: a comparison of two different things, rand and litres, boiled down to "for one litre". The word "per" is the signal. Rand per litre, rand per kilogram, kilometres per hour — every time, divide the first thing by the second so that the second becomes one.

Speed works the same way. A taxi does 180 km in two and a half hours. Kilometres per hour means: how far in ONE hour? 180 divided by 2,5 is 72 km per hour. Once you have the rate, you can run it forwards or backwards. How far in 3,5 hours? 72 times 3,5 is 252 km. How long for 180 km at 60 km per hour? 180 divided by 60 is 3 hours.

Keep the units glued to the number. A bare 72 is nothing; 72 km per hour is a speed. In an exam the units are often the mark, and in a shop they are the difference between a bargain and a mistake.

Your questions on this section are ready: divide to get "per one", compare bottles by the unit price, and run a speed forwards and backwards.

## Subtopic: More Gives More, or More Gives Less?

Here is the only question you need before any proportion problem: when I increase the first thing, does the second thing go UP or go DOWN?

Bread. Three loaves cost R51. More loaves, more money — up with up. That is direct proportion, and the tool is the unit rate: 51 divided by 3 is R17 a loaf, so seven loaves cost 7 times 17, which is R119. Everything scales together; double the loaves, double the bill. If you ever drew it, it would be a straight line starting at zero loaves, zero rand.

Painting. Six people take 10 days to paint a school wall. If only four people turn up, do they finish sooner? No — fewer hands, more days. Up with down. That is indirect proportion, and the tool is the constant product: 6 people times 10 days is 60 person-days of work, and the wall needs 60 of them no matter who comes. Four people need 60 divided by 4, which is 15 days. Twelve people would need 60 divided by 12, which is 5 days. The product never changes; the two numbers trade places on a see-saw.

Driving is the same see-saw. At 60 km per hour a trip takes 3 hours, so it is 180 km. At 90 km per hour the same 180 km takes 180 divided by 90, which is 2 hours. Faster, shorter; slower, longer.

So the whole decision is one sentence. Up with up: divide to find the unit rate, then multiply — direct. Up with down: multiply to find the fixed total, then divide — indirect. Ask the question before you touch a number, and the method chooses itself.

Hold the three pictures: parts for sharing, "per" for rates, and the see-saw question for proportion. The final questions of the lesson are with you now: the painting crew, the speeding taxi, and the up-or-down question that sorts every proportion problem.
