# Part 1 — Expert

This session builds the real number system from the inside out. The anchor task is to classify one list of nine numbers — 7, 0, negative 3, two fifths, 0,75, 0,333 recurring, the square root of 9, the square root of 2, and pi — and to name, for each one, every set it belongs to. Classification is the opening question of Grade 9 number work and it returns in every examination, so the definitions must be exact and the tests must be mechanical.

## Subtopic: The Family Tree of Numbers

Begin with the smallest set. The natural numbers are the counting numbers: 1, 2, 3, 4 and onward without end. Their symbol is a capital N. Zero is not a natural number — you do not start counting at nothing.

Add zero and you have the whole numbers: 0, 1, 2, 3 and onward. The symbol is N with a small zero. Every natural number is a whole number; zero is the only whole number that is not natural.

Now add the negatives of the counting numbers: negative 1, negative 2, negative 3 and so on. Together with the whole numbers they form the integers, symbol Z. The integers run in both directions without end: ... negative 3, negative 2, negative 1, 0, 1, 2, 3 ... Every whole number is an integer.

Next come the rational numbers, symbol Q. A rational number is any number that can be written as a fraction a over b, where a and b are integers and b is not zero. The word comes from ratio. Two fifths is rational. So is 0,75, because it is 75 over 100, which is 3 over 4. So is every integer: 7 is 7 over 1, negative 3 is negative 3 over 1, and 0 is 0 over 1. Every integer is a rational number.

Then the irrational numbers, written Q with a dash: real numbers that CANNOT be written as a fraction of integers. The square root of 2 and pi are the famous members. Their decimals never end and never settle into a repeating block.

Put the rationals and the irrationals together and you have the real numbers, symbol R — every number that has a place on the number line. The structure is nested: N sits inside N-zero, which sits inside Z, which sits inside Q, and Q together with Q-dash fills R. Boxes inside boxes. A number belongs to its smallest box and to every box around it — 7 is natural, whole, integer, rational and real, all five at once.

Take the questions for this section now: the exact membership of each set, the status of zero, and the nesting that makes one number a member of several sets.

## Subtopic: Rational Numbers: Decimals That Stop or Repeat

The fraction definition is exact but slow to apply to a decimal. Here is the fast test: a decimal is rational if and only if it either terminates or recurs.

Terminating first. Three eighths: divide 3 by 8 and you get 0,375 — the division ends. Seven twentieths is 0,35. A terminating decimal is always rational because it is a fraction with a power of ten underneath: 0,375 is 375 over 1 000, which simplifies to 3 over 8.

Recurring next. Two thirds: divide 2 by 3 and you get 0,666... with the 6 repeating forever. One seventh gives 0,142857 142857... — a block of six digits that repeats. We mark a recurring decimal with a dot over the repeating digit, or a dot over the first and last digit of the repeating block. Recurring decimals are rational too, and the proof is a method you must be able to perform.

Convert 0,333 recurring to a fraction. Let x equal 0,333... Multiply both sides by 10: 10x equals 3,333... Subtract the first equation from the second: 10x minus x is 9x, and 3,333... minus 0,333... is exactly 3, because the infinite tails cancel. So 9x equals 3, and x equals 3 over 9, which is 1 over 3.

A two-digit block needs a hundred. Convert 0,272727... Let x equal 0,2727... Multiply by 100 — one power of ten for each digit in the block: 100x equals 27,2727... Subtract: 99x equals 27, so x equals 27 over 99, which simplifies to 3 over 11. Check by division: 3 divided by 11 is 0,2727... Confirmed.

The rule for predicting which fractions terminate is a mark-earner. Write the fraction in lowest terms and factorise the denominator. If the denominator contains only the prime factors 2 and 5, the decimal terminates; any other prime factor forces recurrence. Three eighths: 8 is 2 cubed — terminates. Seven twentieths: 20 is 2 squared times 5 — terminates. Two thirds: 3 — recurs. Five twelfths: 12 is 2 squared times 3, and that 3 forces recurrence: 5 over 12 is 0,41666... with the 6 repeating.

Your questions for this section are ready: the stop-or-repeat test, the subtraction method that turns a recurring decimal into a fraction, and the denominator rule that predicts which fractions terminate.

## Subtopic: Irrational Numbers: Roots and Pi

An irrational number fails both tests: its decimal neither stops nor repeats. The square root of 2 is 1,41421356... and no block ever recurs. Pi is 3,14159265... and the same is true. These decimals are not long — they are endless, and endlessly patternless.

A common confusion must be cleared at once. The fraction 22 over 7 is NOT pi. It is a rational approximation: 22 divided by 7 is 3,142857 142857... — recurring, hence rational — and it agrees with pi only to two decimal places. Likewise 3,14 is a rational number that is close to pi. Pi itself is irrational.

Square roots demand a test, not a reflex. The square root of 9 is exactly 3, because 3 squared is 9 — rational, in fact a natural number. The square root of 16 is 4; the square root of 2,25 is 1,5; the square root of 4 over 9 is 2 over 3. A square root is rational precisely when the number under the root is a perfect square, or a fraction of perfect squares. The square root of 10 is irrational, because 10 is not a perfect square. The cube root of 27 is 3 — rational; the cube root of 20 is irrational.

Irrational numbers still live on the number line, and you must be able to place them between two consecutive integers. Take the square root of 10. The perfect squares either side of 10 are 9 and 16, so the square root of 10 lies between 3 and 4. Tighten it: 3,1 squared is 9,61 and 3,2 squared is 10,24, so the square root of 10 lies between 3,1 and 3,2, closer to 3,2 since 10 is nearer 10,24 than 9,61. The calculator confirms 3,162... The method — bracket by perfect squares, then refine by squaring decimals — is exactly what the examiner wants to see written down.

One more member of the family deserves a name: negative irrationals. Negative root 2 is irrational and real, placed between negative 2 and negative 1 on the line.

Turn to the questions for this section: the two conditions an irrational number fails, the perfect-square test for roots, the difference between pi and 22 over 7, and the bracketing method that places a root between integers.

## Subtopic: Classifying the Anchor List and the Exam Habits

Return to the nine numbers and classify each, naming the smallest set first and then every set around it.

Seven: natural, whole, integer, rational, real. Zero: whole, integer, rational, real — not natural. Negative 3: integer, rational, real — not whole, because whole numbers are never negative. Two fifths: rational and real, and nothing smaller, because it is not an integer. 0,75: rational and real — it terminates, equal to 3 over 4. 0,333 recurring: rational and real — recurring, equal to 1 over 3. The square root of 9: it equals 3, so natural, whole, integer, rational, real — always simplify the root before you classify. The square root of 2: irrational and real. Pi: irrational and real.

Count the irrationals: exactly two, root 2 and pi. Everything else is rational.

Now the exam habits — five of them, each worth a mark somewhere. One: simplify before classifying. Negative root 16 is negative 4, an integer; 8 over 2 is 4, a natural number; 0 over 5 is 0, a whole number. The disguise comes off first, the label goes on second. Two: 5 over 0 is undefined — it is not a real number at all, so it is in none of the sets. Division by zero never produces a number. Three: a non-terminating decimal is not automatically irrational; check for a repeating block. 0,121212... recurs, so it is rational, 12 over 99 which is 4 over 33. Four: a terminating decimal, however long, is rational — 0,123456789 is a fraction with a power of ten underneath. Five: when asked for the smallest set, answer with the innermost box; when asked to classify, list every box.

The negative question also appears: "write down a number that is an integer but not a whole number" — any negative integer, say negative 7. "A rational number that is not an integer" — any proper fraction, say 1 over 2. "A real number that is not rational" — root 3 or pi. The definitions give you the answers directly, provided you hold the nesting in your head.

The closing questions of this part are in front of you now: the classification of each anchor number, the simplify-first habit, the undefined case, and the difference between a long decimal and an endless one.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the whole family tree again, from a taxi rank, a loaf of bread and a frosty morning in Sutherland — same boxes, same answers, ordinary words.

## Subtopic: Counting Sheep, Owing Money, Sharing Bread

Numbers were invented in the order people needed them, and the family tree remembers that order.

First, counting. A farmer in the Karoo counts sheep: one, two, three, and on and on. Those are the natural numbers — the counting numbers. Nobody counts a flock starting at zero, which is why zero is not in this first box.

Then somebody needed to say "no sheep at all". That gives zero, and when you add zero to the counting numbers you get the whole numbers. Tiny change: one extra number, one bigger box.

Then money was owed. If you have R50 and you owe R80, you are R30 below nothing — negative 30. And the thermometer in Sutherland reads negative 8 degrees on a winter morning — eight degrees below zero. Those negative numbers, together with the whole numbers, make the integers. Picture the number line: zero in the middle, counting numbers marching right, their mirror images marching left.

Then bread had to be shared. Three friends, one loaf: each gets one third. A taxi fare of R12,50 is twelve and a half rand. Any number you can write as one integer over another — a fraction — is a rational number. That includes every integer, because 7 is just 7 over 1. The rational box swallows the integer box whole.

So the picture is boxes inside boxes. Counting numbers in the smallest box. Around it the whole numbers, just zero added. Around that the integers, the negatives added. Around that the rationals, every fraction added. When you classify a number, you find the smallest box it fits in — and it automatically belongs to every box outside that one too. Seven is in all four. Negative 3 is in two: integers and rationals. One third is in one: rationals only.

Your questions for this section are with you now: which box each everyday number lives in, and why zero and the negatives arrive at the steps they do.

## Subtopic: Does the Decimal Ever Stop?

Here is the quickest test in the whole lesson. Turn the number into a decimal and ask: does it stop, does it repeat, or does it run on forever with no pattern?

If it stops, it is rational. A quarter of a rand is 25 cents — 0,25, done. Three eighths of a loaf is 0,375 of a loaf. Any decimal that ends can be written as a fraction with 10, 100 or 1 000 underneath, and that makes it rational by definition.

If it repeats, it is still rational. Try to split R1 among three people. Each should get 0,333... rand — the 3s never stop. You cannot pay that in coins, but the number is perfectly good: it is one third, a fraction, so rational. One seventh is 0,142857 142857... — a six-digit block that repeats forever. Repeating means a pattern, and a pattern means a fraction is hiding inside.

How do you get the fraction back out of a repeating decimal? With a trick that makes the endless tail disappear. Call the number x. Multiply by 10 to shift one digit past the comma — or by 100 if the repeating block is two digits long. Then subtract the original. The tails are identical, so they cancel completely. For 0,666...: 10x is 6,666..., subtract x which is 0,666..., and you get 9x equals 6, so x is 6 over 9, which is 2 over 3. Infinite tail in, tidy fraction out.

If it never stops AND never repeats, it is irrational. The square root of 2 begins 1,41421356... and goes on forever with no block that comes back. Pi begins 3,14159265... and does the same. No fraction will ever give you either of them — that is the definition, not a failure to look hard enough.

Watch for the two impostors. 22 over 7 looks like pi, but divide it out and you get 3,142857 142857... — repeating, so rational, and only close to pi. And 3,14 is just a decimal that stops. The real pi has no end and no pattern.

Your questions on this section are ready: stop, repeat or run on — and how to turn a repeating decimal back into its fraction.

## Subtopic: Putting Every Number in Its Box

Now classify the anchor list the way you would in a test, one number at a time, smallest box first.

Seven: counting number, so it is in every box — natural, whole, integer, rational, real. Zero: whole, integer, rational, real — but not natural, because nobody counts from nothing. Negative 3: an integer, so integer, rational, real — not whole, because whole numbers never go below zero. Two fifths: a fraction, so rational and real only. 0,75: it stops, so rational and real — it is three quarters in disguise. 0,333 recurring: it repeats, so rational and real — it is one third. The square root of 9: careful — work it out first. It is exactly 3, so it is a counting number and belongs to every box. The square root of 2: never stops, never repeats, so irrational and real. Pi: the same — irrational and real.

Three habits make this bulletproof. Habit one: simplify before you label. Negative root 16 is negative 4 — an integer, not an irrational; 8 over 2 is 4 — a counting number. Take the disguise off first. Habit two: a long decimal is not an endless decimal. 0,123456789 stops after nine digits, so it is rational. Habit three: 5 divided by 0 is not a number at all — you cannot share five loaves among nobody — so it goes in no box.

Finally, placing an irrational number on the line. Root 10 has no exact decimal, but you can still trap it. 3 squared is 9 and 4 squared is 16, so root 10 sits between 3 and 4. Closer to which? 3,1 squared is 9,61 and 3,2 squared is 10,24, so root 10 sits between 3,1 and 3,2 — and the calculator says 3,162. Trap it between two perfect squares, then tighten.

Hold the picture: boxes inside boxes, smallest box first, simplify before you label, and the stop-or-repeat test for anything with a comma in it. The final questions of the lesson are with you now: classify each anchor number, spot the disguised integers, and trap a root between two whole numbers.
