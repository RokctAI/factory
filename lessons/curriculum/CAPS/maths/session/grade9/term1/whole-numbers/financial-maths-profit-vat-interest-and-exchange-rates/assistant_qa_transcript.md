### subtopic_1 — Profit, Loss, Discount and VAT
**Assistant:** Why is percentage profit worked out on the cost price and not on the selling price?

**Tutor:** Because the percentage answers a specific question — how much did the money I put in grow? — and the money you put in is the cost price. A trader who pays R250 and receives R320 has turned R250 into R320; the R70 gain is 28 percent of the R250 that was risked. If you divided by the selling price you would get 70 over 320, about 21,9 percent, which measures the gain against money that only existed after the sale — it answers no question anyone asked. There is also a consistency reason. Mark-up and discount are applied to a base and then undone from the same base: a 40 percent mark-up on R250 gives R350, and the examiner expects the reverse question, "what was the cost price if R350 includes a 40 percent mark-up", to be solved by dividing by 1,4. That only works if the percentage lives on the cost price. So the rule is not an arbitrary convention; it is the only choice that makes forward and reverse calculations agree. Write "percentage profit equals profit over cost price times 100" as the first line of every such answer and the method mark is secured.

### subtopic_2 — Simple Interest and Hire Purchase
**Assistant:** In hire purchase, why is the interest charged on the balance rather than on the full cash price?

**Tutor:** Because interest is the rent on money you are actually borrowing, and the deposit is money you have already paid — you cannot be charged for borrowing what you have handed over. When the television costs R6 000 and the customer pays R600 up front, the shop is only out of pocket by R5 400, so R5 400 is the loan and R5 400 is what earns interest. That is why the chain of steps must run in order: deposit first, balance second, interest third, instalment fourth. A learner who applies the 12 percent to R6 000 computes R1 440 of interest instead of R1 296 and every later figure inherits the error. The same logic explains why a larger deposit makes the agreement cheaper: it shrinks the balance, and the interest shrinks with it. And notice what the deposit does not do — it is not subtracted again at the end. The total cost is deposit plus the whole repaid balance, R600 plus R6 696, which is R7 296. The comparison with the cash price, stated in a sentence, is the mark that shows you understood what the arithmetic was for.

### subtopic_3 — Compound Interest — Interest on the Interest
**Assistant:** Why does the compound formula use a power while simple interest only multiplies?

**Tutor:** Because compounding repeats the same multiplication on the result of the previous one, and repeated multiplication is exactly what a power expresses. Each year the balance is multiplied by 1,08 — the original 100 percent kept, plus 8 percent added. Three years means multiply by 1,08, then by 1,08 again, then by 1,08 a third time, and three multiplications by the same factor is 1,08 cubed. Simple interest never multiplies the result; it computes the same R400 from the unchanging principal and adds it three times, and repeated addition is multiplication — hence P times i times n, a flat product with no exponent. The two formulas therefore encode the two mechanisms directly: adding the same amount each period versus scaling the growing amount each period. Seeing it this way also protects you from a common slip — writing 1 plus 0,08 cubed, which cubes only the 0,08. The whole growth factor 1,08 is what gets raised to the power, because the whole balance is what grows. Carry the full 1,259712 through and round the rand answer only at the very end.

### subtopic_4 — Exchange Rates, Commission, Budgets and Accounts
**Assistant:** How do you decide whether to multiply or divide by an exchange rate without memorising a rule?

**Tutor:** Reason from the size of the answer, which is something you can always sense-check. An exchange rate of R18,50 to the dollar says that one dollar is worth many rands, so the dollar is the larger unit and the rand is the smaller one. Converting into the smaller unit, rands, must produce a bigger number — just as converting metres into centimetres does — so you multiply: 200 dollars becomes 3 700 rand. Converting into the larger unit, dollars, must produce a smaller number, so you divide: 5 550 rand becomes 300 dollars. If you ever finish a conversion and the rand figure is smaller than the dollar figure, or a R5 550 holiday fund has become over a hundred thousand dollars, the direction was wrong and no further checking is needed. The same sense-check serves the other contexts in this section. Commission plus basic pay must exceed the basic pay. A budget surplus must be smaller than the income. A closing balance after a payment must be lower than opening balance plus charges. Financial arithmetic rewards the student who looks at the answer and asks whether the size is believable.
