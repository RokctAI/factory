### subtopic_1 — Second Differences and Square Patterns
**Assistant:** Why does a constant second difference mean n squared is involved? Where does that come from?

**Tutor:** It comes from how the squares themselves grow. Look at the square numbers 1, 4, 9, 16, 25 as pictures: each square is the previous one with an L-shaped layer wrapped around it, and each layer is two dots larger than the one before — 3, 5, 7, 9. So the first differences of n squared grow by 2 every time, which is to say the second difference is 2, constant. Now, adding a constant to every term — n squared plus 1 — changes nothing about the differences, because the constant cancels when you subtract neighbours. Adding a multiple of n — n squared plus 3n — changes the first differences by a constant 3 but leaves the second difference at 2, because the 3n part has constant first difference and therefore zero second difference. And multiplying the squares by a number multiplies the second difference by that number: 2n squared has second difference 4. Put those facts together and you have the rule you used: a constant second difference signals an n squared term, and half the second difference is its coefficient. Grade 11 proves this with algebra for the general quadratic a n squared plus b n plus c; for now the picture of the growing layers is the justification, and comparing the terms with 1, 4, 9, 16 is the method.

### subtopic_2 — Triangular Numbers and Patterns From Previous Terms
**Assistant:** Where does the formula n times n plus 1 over 2 for the triangular numbers come from?

**Tutor:** From a picture that is worth remembering. The nth triangular number is a staircase of dots: one dot in the top row, two in the next, and so on down to n in the bottom row, so it is the sum 1 plus 2 plus up to n. Take a second identical staircase, turn it upside down, and fit it against the first. The two staircases together make a rectangle that is n dots tall and n plus 1 dots wide — the extra 1 appears because the rows pair as 1 with n, 2 with n minus 1, and so on, each pair totalling n plus 1. The rectangle has n times the quantity n plus 1 dots, and one staircase is half of it. Hence T n equals n times n plus 1, over 2. Test it: n equals 4 gives 4 times 5 over 2, which is 10, and 1 plus 2 plus 3 plus 4 is indeed 10. The same argument gives the 10th triangular number as 55 without adding ten numbers. I show the picture because it is a justification, not a coincidence — it explains why the division by 2 is there and why the formula must keep working. Next lesson is about producing arguments of exactly this kind for your own rules, and the triangular numbers are the model example.

### subtopic_3 — Identifying the Type
**Assistant:** When a pattern passes none of the tests, what do I do?

**Tutor:** First make sure it genuinely passes none of them, because the commonest failure is running one test on one pair of terms and stopping. Compute every first difference, every ratio, and the second differences; look at the terms themselves for the famous sequences — squares, cubes, triangular numbers, powers of 2, primes — and check whether each term is built from the ones before it, as a sum or as a product with something that changes. The cubes 1, 8, 27, 64 fail the first three tests, since their second differences are 12, 18, 24, but are instantly recognisable. A sequence like 2, 3, 5, 7, 11 has no algebraic rule at this level and is simply the primes, which you name. If after all that a pattern still resists, then the honest answer in an examination is a recursive description — "each term is obtained from the previous by adding 1, then 2, then 3" — with its starting terms stated, and you continue the pattern by that description. CAPS explicitly allows patterns that are not limited to constant difference or ratio and allows learners' own creations, so the examiner is testing whether you can describe what you see precisely, not whether you can always produce a position formula. Precision in words, with the differences shown, earns the marks.

### subtopic_4 — Creating and Describing Your Own Patterns
**Assistant:** Why does the description have to be so precise? Surely "add more each time" gets the idea across?

**Tutor:** It gets an idea across, but not a pattern, and the test of a description is whether a stranger could continue your list from the words alone. "Add more each time" fits 1, 2, 4, 7 — differences 1, 2, 3 — and equally fits 1, 2, 4, 8 — differences 1, 2, 4 — and 1, 3, 7, 15, and infinitely many others. A classmate given those words cannot produce your fifth term, so the puzzle is unfair and the description is unmarked. "The differences are 1, 2, 3, 4, growing by 1 each time" fits exactly one continuation, and so does "square the position and add 3", and so does T n equals n squared plus 3. Algebra is the most precise of all because it can be checked by substitution — anyone can put n equals 5 into n squared plus 3 and get 28 — which is why the mark scheme asks for a rule in algebraic language where one exists. My standard for a complete created pattern is four parts: the rule in algebra, or a recursive rule with its starting terms; at least five terms; the fingerprint shown — differences, ratios or the recursion; and one sentence in plain words. If any two different continuations fit what you have written, you have not finished writing.
