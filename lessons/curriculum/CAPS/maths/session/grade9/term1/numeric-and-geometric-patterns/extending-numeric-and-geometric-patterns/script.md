# Part 1 — Expert

This session investigates and extends numeric and geometric patterns presented as number sequences, as diagrams and in tables, and represents them algebraically with a general term. The anchor patterns are 5, 8, 11, 14, with general term T n equals 3n plus 2, and 3, 6, 12, 24, with general term T n equals 3 times 2 to the power n minus 1. Every rule is tested against at least two known terms before it is used, and that test is examinable.

## Subtopic: Numeric Patterns With a Constant Difference

A numeric pattern is a list of terms in order. We number the positions 1, 2, 3 and so on, and write T n for the term in position n. In 5, 8, 11, 14, the first term T 1 is 5, T 2 is 8, T 3 is 11, T 4 is 14.

The first thing to test is the difference between consecutive terms. 8 minus 5 is 3; 11 minus 8 is 3; 14 minus 11 is 3. The difference is constant, and we call it d. A constant difference means the general term has the form T n equals d n plus c, for some constant c. The reason: each step forward in position adds d, so after n steps from a fixed starting point, d has been added n times, and that is d times n.

To find c, substitute a known term. With d equal to 3, T 1 is 3 times 1 plus c, which must be 5, so c is 2. The rule is T n equals 3n plus 2. Equivalently c is T 1 minus d: 5 minus 3 is 2. Test with a second term before trusting the rule: T 4 is 3 times 4 plus 2, which is 14. Correct.

Now the rule answers questions that counting cannot. The 20th term is 3 times 20 plus 2, which is 62. The 100th term is 302. Which position holds the term 92? Solve 3n plus 2 equals 92: 3n is 90, so n is 30. Is 100 a term? 3n plus 2 equals 100 gives 3n equals 98, and 98 is not a multiple of 3, so no — 100 is not in the pattern. The rule, not the list, settles it.

A decreasing pattern has a negative difference. 20, 17, 14, 11: d is negative 3. T 1 is negative 3 plus c, which must be 20, so c is 23, and the rule is T n equals 23 minus 3n. Test: T 4 is 23 minus 12, which is 11. Correct. T 10 is 23 minus 30, which is negative 7; the pattern crosses zero and keeps going.

In a table the same work is laid out in two rows: position n across the top, term T n below. Extending the table one column at a time is investigation; writing the rule is representation; and the rule must reproduce every column.

The questions for this section are with you now: finding d and c, testing the rule on two terms, and using it to find far terms and positions.

## Subtopic: Geometric Patterns and Constant Ratio

In 3, 6, 12, 24 the differences are 3, 6, 12 — not constant. Test the ratio instead: 6 over 3 is 2; 12 over 6 is 2; 24 over 12 is 2. The ratio is constant, and we call it r. Each step multiplies by r, so this is the repeated multiplication of last week, and the general term is a power.

Starting from T 1, you multiply by r once to reach T 2, twice to reach T 3, and n minus 1 times to reach T n. So T n equals T 1 times r to the power n minus 1. For 3, 6, 12, 24: T n equals 3 times 2 to the power n minus 1. Test: T 1 is 3 times 2 to the power 0, which is 3 times 1, which is 3. T 4 is 3 times 2 cubed, which is 24. Correct. T 8 is 3 times 2 to the power 7, which is 3 times 128, which is 384.

The exponent is n minus 1, not n, because the first term has had no multiplications yet. Writing 3 times 2 to the power n gives 6, 12, 24, 48 — the right ratio but every term one position too early. The exponent counts jumps between terms, and n terms have n minus 1 jumps between them.

Another: 2, 6, 18, 54 has ratio 3, so T n equals 2 times 3 to the power n minus 1. T 6 is 2 times 3 to the power 5, which is 2 times 243, which is 486. A ratio less than 1 produces a decreasing pattern: 80, 40, 20, 10 has ratio one half, T n equals 80 times one half to the power n minus 1, and T 5 is 80 over 16, which is 5.

The two tests distinguish the two types. Constant difference: add the same amount each step, rule d n plus c, growth in a straight line. Constant ratio: multiply by the same amount each step, rule T 1 times r to the power n minus 1, growth that runs away. A pattern cannot have both unless it is constant, and some patterns have neither — next lesson.

Take this section's questions now: finding r, writing the power with exponent n minus 1, and testing it.

## Subtopic: Patterns in Diagrams and Tables

Patterns often arrive as pictures. A row of squares built from matchsticks: 1 square uses 4 sticks, 2 squares joined side by side use 7, 3 squares use 10. The numbers 4, 7, 10 have constant difference 3, so T n equals 3n plus c, and T 1 equals 4 gives c equal to 1. The rule is T n equals 3n plus 1. Test: T 3 is 9 plus 1, which is 10. Correct.

The picture explains the rule, which is better than the numbers alone. Every new square shares one side with the previous one, so it costs 3 new sticks, which is the 3n. The very first square needs one extra stick to close it, which is the plus 1. A rule justified by the structure of the picture is a rule you can defend; next week's lesson makes that justification the whole point.

Now the rule answers the hard questions. 15 squares need 3 times 15 plus 1, which is 46 sticks. If you have 31 sticks, how many squares? 3n plus 1 equals 31, so 3n is 30 and n is 10 squares. If you have 50 sticks, 3n plus 1 equals 50 gives 3n equals 49, not a multiple of 3, so 50 sticks make 16 squares with one stick spare.

Another picture: L-shapes made of dots, with 3, 5, 7 dots in the first three. Difference 2, so T n equals 2n plus c, and T 1 equals 3 gives c equal to 1: T n equals 2n plus 1. The L in position n has n dots up one arm, n dots along the other, and one corner dot joining them — that is n plus n plus 1, which is 2n plus 1.

Tables record the investigation. Write positions 1 to 5 across the top and terms below; extend by the difference or ratio; then write the rule and check it reproduces every column. A table with positions 1, 2, 3 and terms 4, 7, 10 is the matchstick pattern in disguise; a table with terms 3, 6, 12 is a geometric pattern. The table does not tell you which type it is — the test of differences and ratios does.

This section's questions are available now: reading the first terms from a diagram, explaining the rule from the picture, and working from sticks back to squares.

## Subtopic: Finding the Rule and Testing It

Here is the procedure for a pattern given numerically. Step one: compute the differences. If constant, d is the coefficient of n and c is T 1 minus d. Step two: if not, compute the ratios. If constant, the rule is T 1 times r to the power n minus 1. Step three: test the rule on two terms you did not use to build it — T 1 and the last given term are the natural choices. Step four: only then use the rule.

The test matters because a rule that fits one term fits nothing. T n equals 5n fits 5, 8, 11 at T 1 only; 5n at n equals 2 is 10, not 8. T n equals n plus 4 fits T 1 too, and fails at T 2. A rule tested on two terms with constant difference is reliable, because two points fix a straight line.

Using the rule in reverse requires solving an equation. For 5, 8, 11, 14, which term is 62? 3n plus 2 equals 62, so 3n is 60 and n is 20. For 3, 6, 12, 24, which term is 384? 3 times 2 to the power n minus 1 equals 384, so 2 to the power n minus 1 is 128, which is 2 to the power 7, so n minus 1 is 7 and n is 8.

The error museum for this topic has five exhibits. One: writing T n equals T 1 plus d n — adding d n to the first term — which gives 8 for T 1 of 5, 8, 11. The correct constant is T 1 minus d. Two: writing the geometric rule with exponent n instead of n minus 1, shifting every term one place. Three: testing a rule on one term only. Four: assuming a pattern is arithmetic because the first difference happens to equal the first term — 3, 6, 12 is not 3, 6, 9. Five: numbering positions from 0, so that T 1 is not the first term written. In this course the first term written is T 1.

Layout for marks: write the differences, or the ratios, explicitly; state d or r; write the rule; show the test on two terms; then answer the question with the rule. Each of those lines carries a mark in the Term 1 test, and the test on two terms is the line most often omitted.

The questions for this section are ready: the four-step procedure, solving for a position, and the five exhibits of the museum.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same patterns again through a taxi meter, a chain message and a row of matchstick squares — plain words, same rules.

## Subtopic: Taxi Fares and Steps

A metered taxi charges a flag fall of R2 when you get in, and R3 for every kilometre. After 1 kilometre you owe 5; after 2 kilometres, 8; after 3, 11; after 4, 14. That is the pattern 5, 8, 11, 14, and the meter IS the rule: 3 rand per kilometre, times the kilometres, plus the 2 you owed before you moved. T n equals 3n plus 2. The n is the kilometres, the 3 is the step, and the 2 is the flag fall.

That is every constant-difference pattern. The step — how much it goes up each time — is the number in front of n. The flag fall — what the meter would read at kilometre zero — is the constant on the end. To find the flag fall, take one step back from the first term: 5 minus 3 is 2. Not 5 — the first term already includes one step.

The rule lets you jump. After 20 kilometres: 3 times 20 plus 2, which is 62 rand. If the meter reads R92, how far did you go? Take off the flag fall, 90, and divide by the step, 30 kilometres. Could it ever read exactly R100? 100 minus 2 is 98, and 98 is not a multiple of 3, so no — the meter jumps from 98 to 101.

A pattern that goes down is a taxi running in reverse, or a phone battery: 20 percent, 17, 14, 11 — losing 3 each hour. The step is negative 3, the "flag fall" is 20 plus 3, which is 23, so T n equals 23 minus 3n. After 10 hours: 23 minus 30, negative 7, which the phone would call "dead for a while now". The algebra is the same; only the sign of the step changed.

Keep the taxi meter in mind and try the questions for this part: the step, the flag fall, and jumping to far terms.

## Subtopic: Doubling Patterns

Now a different kind of pattern. 3, 6, 12, 24 — a chain message that starts with 3 people, and each day everyone who has it sends it to one new person, so the count doubles. Taking differences gives 3, 6, 12 — not constant, and in fact the differences are the pattern itself, which is the fingerprint of doubling. Divide instead: 6 over 3, 12 over 6, 24 over 12 — all 2. This pattern multiplies.

How many doublings to reach day n? Day 1 has had none. Day 2, one. Day 3, two. Day n, n minus 1. So the count on day n is 3 times 2 to the power n minus 1. Check day 4: 3 times 2 cubed is 24. Day 8: 3 times 2 to the power 7, which is 3 times 128, which is 384 people.

Why n minus 1 and not n? Because the exponent counts jumps between terms, like fence posts and gaps. Four posts have three gaps between them. Four terms have three multiplications between them. If you wrote 3 times 2 to the power n, day 1 would be 6 — but day 1 is 3. Always check your rule on the first term; it is where the n minus 1 proves itself.

The multiplier does not have to be 2. 2, 6, 18, 54 triples: T n equals 2 times 3 to the power n minus 1, and day 6 is 2 times 243, which is 486. And it can shrink: 80, 40, 20, 10 halves each time, so T n is 80 times a half to the power n minus 1, and day 5 is 80 over 16, which is 5. Same recipe — first term, times the multiplier, to the power of the number of jumps.

So you have two tests and two recipes. Subtract neighbours: if the answer is always the same, it is a step pattern — step times n plus flag fall. Divide neighbours: if the answer is always the same, it is a multiplying pattern — first term times multiplier to the power n minus 1.

Hold the chain message and the fence posts and try this part's questions: spotting a multiplying pattern, and getting the exponent right.

## Subtopic: Reading a Picture Pattern

Picture patterns are the friendliest kind, because the picture tells you WHY the rule works. Take squares made from matchsticks, side by side in a row. One square: 4 sticks. Two squares: 7, because the second square shares a wall with the first and only needs 3 new sticks. Three squares: 10. Each new square costs 3 sticks. That is the step, so the rule is 3n plus something — and the something is the one extra stick the first square needed to close itself. T n equals 3n plus 1.

Look at the picture to see the two parts of the rule. The part that grows — 3 sticks per square — is the number in front of n. The part that stays the same no matter how many squares — the single closing stick — is the constant. Every picture pattern with a constant step has a growing part and a fixed part, and finding them in the picture is faster and safer than finding them from the numbers.

Then let the rule do the heavy lifting. 15 squares: 3 times 15 plus 1, which is 46 sticks. A box of 31 sticks: take off the fixed 1, leaving 30, and divide by 3 — exactly 10 squares. A box of 50: 49 over 3 is 16 and a bit, so 16 squares and one stick left over.

Another picture: L-shapes of dots, 3 dots, then 5, then 7. Each L has n dots up one arm, n dots along the other arm, and one corner dot joining them. Let the picture count for you: n plus n plus 1, which is 2n plus 1. Position 1 gives 3, position 2 gives 5. Correct.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Number the positions from 1. Subtract neighbours; if constant, it is a step pattern, rule step times n plus flag fall, with flag fall one step back from the first term. Divide neighbours; if constant, it is a multiplying pattern, rule first term times multiplier to the power n minus 1. Picture patterns: find the growing part and the fixed part. Test every rule on two terms. Then use the rule to jump as far as you like.

Hold the taxi, the chain message and the matchsticks, and take the final questions of the lesson: the growing part and the fixed part of a picture, and the rule that counts sticks you have not laid yet.
