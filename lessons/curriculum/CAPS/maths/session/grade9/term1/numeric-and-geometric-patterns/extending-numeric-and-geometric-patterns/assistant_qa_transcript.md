### subtopic_1 — Numeric Patterns With a Constant Difference
**Assistant:** Why is the constant T 1 minus d rather than T 1? The pattern starts at T 1, so why step back?

**Tutor:** Because the rule d n plus c is evaluated at n equals 1 for the first term, and at that moment d has already been added once. If the constant were T 1 itself, the rule at n equals 1 would give T 1 plus d, one step too far. The constant c is what the rule would produce at position zero — the taxi meter before the car moves, the flag fall — and the first term is one step beyond that. So c is T 1 minus d: for 5, 8, 11, 14, c is 5 minus 3, which is 2, and the rule 3n plus 2 gives 5 at n equals 1 exactly as required. I find it useful to think of c as the hidden term before the pattern begins. It is not written in the list, but the rule knows about it, and in Grade 10 you will recognise it as the y-intercept of the straight line that this pattern draws. The error of writing T 1 plus d n is the single most frequent mistake in the Term 1 test on this topic, and it is caught instantly by the habit of testing the rule at n equals 1 before doing anything else. If the rule does not give the first term, the constant is wrong, and the correction is always to subtract d.

### subtopic_2 — Geometric Patterns and Constant Ratio
**Assistant:** Could I avoid the n minus 1 by writing the rule differently?

**Tutor:** You can, and it is instructive to see how. 3 times 2 to the power n minus 1 is, by the division law, 3 times 2 to the power n divided by 2, which is three halves times 2 to the power n, or 1,5 times 2 to the power n. That rule has exponent n and gives 3 at n equals 1, 6 at n equals 2, and so on — perfectly correct. What has happened is that the first term has been replaced by the hidden term before the pattern begins, the value at position zero, which is 3 divided by 2. The two forms are equivalent in exactly the way 3n plus 2 and 3 times the quantity n minus 1 plus 5 are equivalent for the arithmetic pattern. In this course the standard form is T 1 times r to the power n minus 1, because it keeps the first term visible and the exponent interpretable as the number of multiplications, and markers expect it. But understanding that the exponent can be shifted provided the coefficient is adjusted protects you from a common confusion: a rule with exponent n is not automatically wrong, it is only wrong if the coefficient still says T 1. Whichever form you use, test it at n equals 1 and at the last given term; the test, not the form, is what earns trust.

### subtopic_3 — Patterns in Diagrams and Tables
**Assistant:** When a pattern comes as a picture, should I work from the numbers or from the picture?

**Tutor:** Both, in a particular order: let the picture tell you the shape of the rule and the numbers confirm it. In the row of matchstick squares, the picture shows that each new square shares a wall with the one before it and so costs exactly 3 new sticks, which tells you the growing part is 3n before you compute a single difference. It also shows the one stick that only the first square needs, which is the fixed part, plus 1. You have the rule 3n plus 1 from structure alone. Now the numbers 4, 7, 10 check it: differences of 3, and 3 times 1 plus 1 is 4. The reason for this order is that a rule justified by structure is a rule you can defend — you can say why it must continue — whereas a rule fitted to three numbers is only a conjecture until it is explained. Next lesson's work on justification makes that distinction central. Practically, in an examination, write one sentence linking each part of the rule to the picture: "each new square adds 3 sticks; the first square needs 1 extra." That sentence is the justification mark, and students who work only from the numbers leave it on the table.

### subtopic_4 — Finding the Rule and Testing It
**Assistant:** If a rule fits the first three terms, is it not certainly right? Why test more?

**Tutor:** For a pattern you have already established as arithmetic or geometric, a rule that fits two terms is right, because two terms fix the step and the start, and the third term is a check for slips. The deeper caution is about the establishing itself. Three terms are not enough to be sure what kind of pattern you have. 1, 2, 4 could continue to 8 as a doubling pattern, or to 7 if the differences are 1, 2, 3, or to 11 under other rules; nothing in those three numbers decides it. That is why examination questions give four or five terms, or a picture, or a description of how the pattern grows, and why you should compute all the available differences and ratios rather than the first one. Once the type is established — all differences equal, or all ratios equal — then the rule is determined and the test on two terms is a check against arithmetic slips: putting n equals 1 catches a wrong constant, and putting in the last given term catches a wrong step or ratio. The habit I teach is: establish the type from every term given, build the rule from d or r and the first term, test at n equals 1 and at the last term, then use it. Each stage is a line of working and a mark.
