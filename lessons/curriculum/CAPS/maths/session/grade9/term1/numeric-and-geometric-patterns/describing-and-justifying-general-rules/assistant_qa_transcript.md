### subtopic_1 — From Words to Algebra and Back
**Assistant:** Why do we need both the words and the algebra? Is one not enough?

**Tutor:** They do different jobs, and the examination asks for both because each exposes a different kind of misunderstanding. The words show that you understand what the rule does — that "double the position and add 1" is an action on the position, not on the previous term — and they are the form in which you first see a pattern and in which you explain it to another person. The algebra shows that you can make the rule precise and checkable: T n equals 2n plus 1 can be evaluated by anyone at n equals 50 and will give the same answer every time, whereas words can be ambiguous. "Multiply by the position" could mean n times the previous term or n times a constant. There is a third reason, which is forward-looking. In Grade 10 the position rule becomes a function, y equals 2x plus 1, and the words become its description: gradient 2, y-intercept 1. Students who have practised translating between words and symbols read those functions fluently; students who have only memorised symbols find Grade 10 opaque. So I ask for the rule in words, the rule in algebra, and the translation between them, and I mark the translation because it is the evidence of understanding.

### subtopic_2 — Justifying a Rule From the Structure
**Assistant:** How is "the two long sides and the two ends" different from just checking the numbers? Both get 2n plus 2.

**Tutor:** The check tells you the rule agrees with the pattern at the values you tried; the structural argument tells you the rule must agree at every value, including the ones you did not try, and says why. Consider what each would do if the pattern were extended to a thousand tables. The check is silent: you have not counted a thousand tables. The argument speaks: a row of a thousand tables has a top side with a thousand seats, a bottom side with a thousand, and two ends — 2 002 people — because every row, whatever its length, has exactly those four parts. The argument covers all n at once, which no finite number of checks can do. This is why the circle pattern fails: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 checked perfectly and there was no structural reason for doubling, so the sixth term was free to be 31. In marking, the justification mark goes to the sentence that links each piece of the rule to a piece of the structure — the coefficient to the thing that grows, the constant to the thing that is fixed. A student who writes "it works for n equals 1, 2, 3" has earned the check mark and left the justification mark on the table.

### subtopic_3 — Equivalent Rules
**Assistant:** If I write 4(n − 1) and the memorandum says 4n − 4, do I lose marks?

**Tutor:** No, provided your rule is correct and you can show it is the same rule, and in practice markers are instructed to accept any equivalent form. 4 times the quantity n minus 1 expands by the distributive property to 4n minus 4, so the two are identical for every n, and yours comes with its own justification — four sides each containing n minus 1 dots, counting from one corner up to but not including the next. That is a different and equally valid way of seeing the border. The practical advice is twofold. First, when you are unsure whether your rule matches the expected one, substitute two values of n into both: n equals 3 gives 8 from both, n equals 5 gives 16 from both, and that agreement is strong evidence. Second, settle it with algebra by expanding and simplifying both to the same standard form, which is proof. If the substitution had produced different values, one of the two rules would be wrong, and the mismatch would tell you to return to the picture. The broader point is that algebra gives you a way to compare descriptions objectively, which words alone do not, and that there is rarely one "correct" way to see a pattern.

### subtopic_4 — When a Rule Fails and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Why does one failing case prove a rule wrong when five fitting cases do not prove it right?

**Tutor:** Because a general rule is a claim about every position, and a claim about everything is broken by a single exception but confirmed only by covering everything. "All the terms are given by 2 to the power n minus 1" says something about n equals 6 as much as about n equals 1 to 5. The sixth term being 31 instead of 32 contradicts the claim directly, and no amount of agreement elsewhere can repair it — the claim was about all n, and it is false at one. The five fitting cases, on the other hand, say nothing about the sixth; they are consistent with the doubling rule and also with the rule that actually governs the circle regions, which gives 31, and with infinitely many others that agree up to n equals 5 and diverge after. This asymmetry is the logical foundation of mathematics: to disprove, one counter-example; to prove, an argument that covers every case, which is what the structural justification provides for patterns and what Grade 10 geometry provides with theorems. In practical terms it changes how you work. After finding a rule, spend your effort not on checking a fourth and fifth term but on finishing the sentence "this works because" from the structure. If you can, you have a justification; if you cannot, write the rule as a conjecture and say so — that honesty is also marked.
