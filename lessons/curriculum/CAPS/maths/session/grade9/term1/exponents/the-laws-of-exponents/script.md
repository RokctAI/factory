# Part 1 — Expert

This session revises the laws of exponents for natural-number exponents and the zero exponent: a to the m times a to the n equals a to the m plus n; a to the m divided by a to the n equals a to the m minus n; a to the m, all to the power n, equals a to the m n; a times t, all to the power n, equals a to the n times t to the n; and a to the power 0 equals 1. The anchor expression is 2 to the power 5 times 2 cubed, divided by 2 to the power 6, which simplifies to 2 squared, which is 4. Every law is derived by counting factors, and every derivation is examinable.

## Subtopic: Why the Laws Hold: Multiplying and Dividing Powers

Begin with multiplication. 2 cubed times 2 to the power 4 is three twos multiplied together, times four twos multiplied together. Altogether that is seven twos, so the product is 2 to the power 7. Check numerically: 2^3 × 2^4 = 128, and 2 to the power 7 is also 128. The first law: a to the m times a to the n equals a to the power m plus n. When the bases are the same, multiplying the powers ADDS the exponents.

The base stays as it is. Students write 4 to the power 7 for 2 cubed times 2 to the power 4, having multiplied the bases as well as adding the exponents. That is wrong: 4 to the power 7 is 16 384, not 128. The bases are not multiplied; the law simply counts how many copies of the one base you have.

The law requires the same base. 2 cubed times 3 squared cannot be combined into a single power; you evaluate it as 8 times 9, which is 72, and leave it. Likewise the law is for multiplication only: 2^3 + 2^4 = 24, since 8 plus 16 is 24, and there is no exponent law for adding powers.

Now division. 3 to the power 5 divided by 3 squared is five threes over two threes. Two of the threes cancel, leaving three threes: 3 cubed, which is 27. Check: 3^5 ÷ 3^2 = 27, since 243 divided by 9 is 27. The second law: a to the m divided by a to the n equals a to the power m minus n. Same base, dividing, SUBTRACT the exponents — top exponent minus bottom exponent.

With algebra the counting is identical. x to the power 4 times x cubed is x to the power 7. x to the power 6 divided by x squared is x to the power 4. The coefficients are handled separately as ordinary numbers: 3x squared times 4x to the power 5 is 12x to the power 7 — multiply 3 by 4, add 2 and 5.

A negative base behaves correctly if you keep the brackets: negative 2 in brackets cubed times negative 2 in brackets squared is negative 2 in brackets to the power 5, which is negative 32. Check: negative 8 times 4 is negative 32.

The questions for this section are with you now: adding and subtracting exponents, the base that stays fixed, and the cases where no law applies.

## Subtopic: Power of a Power and Power of a Product

Third law. 2 cubed, all squared, means two copies of 2 cubed multiplied: 2 cubed times 2 cubed, which by the first law is 2 to the power 6. Check: 2 cubed is 8, 8 squared is 64, and 2^6 = 64. The law: a to the m, all to the power n, equals a to the power m times n. A power of a power MULTIPLIES the exponents.

Distinguish this carefully from the first law. 2 cubed times 2 squared is 2 to the power 5 — add. 2 cubed, all squared, is 2 to the power 6 — multiply. The first has two separate powers side by side; the second has one power wrapped in brackets with an exponent outside. Read the bracket.

In algebra: x squared, all to the power 5, is x to the power 10. x to the power 3 all cubed is x to the power 9. The order of the two exponents does not matter, since m times n equals n times m — 2 squared all cubed and 2 cubed all squared are both 2 to the power 6.

Fourth law. 2 times 5, all cubed, means three copies of 2 times 5 multiplied together: 2 times 5 times 2 times 5 times 2 times 5. Rearranging by the commutative property gives 2 cubed times 5 cubed, which is 8 times 125, which is 1 000. Check directly: 2 times 5 is 10, and 10 cubed is 1 000. The law: a times t, all to the power n, equals a to the n times t to the n. An exponent outside a bracket is given to EVERY factor inside.

Every factor includes the coefficient. 3x, all squared, is 3 squared times x squared, which is 9x squared. Writing 3x squared — meaning 3 times x squared — is the most common error in the topic and costs the mark every time. Likewise 2a squared b, all cubed, is 2 cubed times a to the power 2 times 3 times b cubed, which is 8a to the power 6 b cubed. Each factor collects the outside exponent; the a already had an exponent, so its exponents multiply by the third law.

The fourth law is for products only. There is no law that sends an exponent inside a sum: a plus b, all squared, is NOT a squared plus b squared. Test with numbers: 3 plus 4, all squared, is 49; 3 squared plus 4 squared is 25. You will expand sums like that in Term 2 by multiplying out brackets, not by any exponent law.

Take the questions for this section now: multiplying exponents for a power of a power, distributing an exponent over a product, and the coefficient that must not be forgotten.

## Subtopic: The Zero Exponent

The fifth law states that a to the power 0 equals 1 for any non-zero base a. It is not an arbitrary definition; it is forced by the division law. Consider 5 to the power 4 divided by 5 to the power 4. Any non-zero number divided by itself is 1. But the division law says the result is 5 to the power 4 minus 4, which is 5 to the power 0. Two correct calculations of the same thing must agree, so 5 to the power 0 must be 1.

The pattern confirms it. 2 cubed is 8, 2 squared is 4, 2 to the power 1 is 2; each step down divides by 2. One more step down, 2 to the power 0, divides by 2 once more and gives 1. The same descent works for any base, which is why the law is universal.

Now the precision the examiner wants. 5 to the power 0 is 1. Negative 3 in brackets to the power 0 is 1. x to the power 0 is 1 for any non-zero x. 3 times 5 to the power 0 is 3 times 1, which is 3, because the exponent applies to the 5 only — the 3 is a coefficient standing outside. But 3 times 5, all to the power 0, is 1, because the whole product is the base. Bracket placement decides the answer, exactly as it did for negative bases.

Combining with other laws: 7 to the power 6 divided by 7 to the power 4 is 7 squared, which is 49; 7 to the power 4 divided by 7 to the power 4 is 7 to the power 0, which is 1. And x to the power 5 divided by x to the power 5, all times 4, is 4.

One exclusion: 0 to the power 0 is not defined in this course, because the argument above divides by the base, and division by zero is not permitted. Every statement of the fifth law should carry the condition a not equal to 0.

This section's questions are available now: the derivation of the zero exponent, the coefficient outside the power, and the bracket that changes the answer.

## Subtopic: Simplifying Mixed Expressions

Now the laws together. The anchor: 2 to the power 5 times 2 cubed, divided by 2 to the power 6. Multiply first: 2 to the power 5 plus 3, which is 2 to the power 8. Divide: 2 to the power 8 minus 6, which is 2 squared, which is 4. Check with values: 32 times 8 is 256, divided by 64 is 4. Agreed.

With algebra the layout is the same. x to the power 4 times x cubed, divided by x to the power 5: top is x to the power 7, divide to get x squared. 6x to the power 5 divided by 2x squared: coefficients 6 over 2 is 3; exponents 5 minus 2 is 3; so 3x cubed.

Brackets first, always. 2a squared b, all cubed, divided by 4a to the power 4: expand the bracket to 8a to the power 6 b cubed, then divide: 8 over 4 is 2, a to the power 6 minus 4 is a squared, b cubed stays. Result: 2a squared b cubed.

Different bases stay separate. 2 cubed times 3 squared times 2 squared times 3: collect the twos, 2 to the power 5; collect the threes, 3 cubed. 32 times 27 is 864, and that is as far as exponent laws take you — there is no single-power form.

The error museum for this topic has five exhibits, and every one of them is a wrong answer I have marked many times. One: multiplying bases — writing 4 to the power 7 for 2 cubed times 2 to the power 4. Two: multiplying exponents when the powers are multiplied — writing 2 to the power 12 for 2 cubed times 2 to the power 4. Three: forgetting the coefficient — writing 3x squared for 3x all squared, when the answer is 9x squared. Four: applying the product law to a sum — writing a squared plus b squared for a plus b all squared. Five: writing 0 for a to the power 0, when it is 1.

Layout for full marks: write the original, apply one law per line and name it in the margin if you like, keep the base visible at every step, and finish with the simplest form — single powers, positive exponents, no brackets. In the Grade 10 exponential equations next year, every line of the solution is one of these five laws.

The questions for this section are ready: combining laws in one expression, coefficients and variables handled separately, and the five exhibits of the error museum.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same five laws again, told with boxes of twos, stacked groups and a pattern that marches down to one — plain words, same rules.

## Subtopic: Counting the Twos

Forget the formulas for a moment and picture boxes. 2 cubed is a box holding three twos, all to be multiplied. 2 to the power 4 is a box holding four twos. Multiply the two boxes and you are multiplying three twos and then four more twos — seven twos in total. So 2 cubed times 2 to the power 4 is 2 to the power 7. You counted the twos. That is the whole law: same base, multiplying, add the counts.

Notice what did NOT happen. The twos did not become fours. There is one kind of thing in both boxes — twos — and you simply have more of them. If you ever catch yourself writing 4 to the power 7, ask: where did the fours come from? Nobody put fours in the box.

Now division. Five threes on top, two threes on the bottom. Each three on the bottom cancels one three on the top — like pairing socks — and two pairs cancel, leaving three threes on top. 3 to the power 5 over 3 squared is 3 cubed. Same base, dividing, subtract the counts: five take away two is three.

Why must the base be the same? Because you can only count things that are alike. Three twos and two threes is 8 times 9, which is 72, and there is no shorter way to say it — you cannot count "fives" of anything. The same goes for adding: 2 cubed plus 2 to the power 4 is 8 plus 16, which is 24, and that is that. The exponent laws are about multiplying and dividing. Adding is ordinary arithmetic.

A letter is just a base you do not know yet. x to the power 4 times x cubed is four x's and then three more, seven in total: x to the power 7. The numbers in front — the coefficients — are not in the boxes; multiply them like ordinary numbers. 3x squared times 4x to the power 5: 3 times 4 is 12, two x's and five x's is seven, so 12x to the power 7.

Keep the boxes in mind and try the questions for this part: counting copies when you multiply, cancelling pairs when you divide, and why unlike bases cannot be combined.

## Subtopic: Powers of Powers and Sharing the Exponent

Here are two laws that look alike and are often swapped. Take 2 cubed and square it: 2 cubed, all squared. That means two copies of the box of three twos — a box of three, and another box of three — six twos altogether. 2 cubed all squared is 2 to the power 6, which is 64. The outer exponent tells you how many boxes; the inner one tells you how many in each box; and boxes times contents is the total. A power of a power: multiply the counts.

The clue is the bracket. Two separate powers standing next to each other, 2 cubed times 2 squared, is more twos added together — five of them. One power wrapped in brackets with an exponent outside, 2 cubed all squared, is copies of a box — three times two is six. Add when the powers stand side by side; multiply when one is wrapped inside the other.

Now sharing. 2 times 5, all cubed, is three copies of the pair "2 times 5". Write them out: 2 times 5, times 2 times 5, times 2 times 5. Shuffle them so the twos sit together and the fives sit together — you may, because multiplication lets you swap — and you have three twos and three fives: 2 cubed times 5 cubed, which is 8 times 125, which is 1 000. Check: 2 times 5 is 10, and 10 cubed is 1 000. An exponent outside a bracket is shared with EVERYTHING inside the bracket.

Everything means the number too. 3x, all squared, is two copies of "3 times x": 3 times x times 3 times x, which is 9 times x squared. The 3 is inside the bracket, so it gets squared as well. Students leave the 3 alone and write 3x squared — but then where did the second 3 go? Share the exponent with every single factor.

One warning, in plain words: sharing works across multiplication only. 3 plus 4, all squared, is 7 squared, which is 49, and 3 squared plus 4 squared is 9 plus 16, which is 25. Not the same. A plus sign inside the bracket means you cannot share; you will learn to multiply those brackets out in Term 2.

Hold the picture of boxes inside boxes and try this part's questions: how many twos in a power of a power, and who gets the exponent when a bracket holds a product.

## Subtopic: Why Anything to the Zero Is One

The strangest law is the easiest to see. Write the powers of 2 going down: 2 to the power 4 is 16, 2 cubed is 8, 2 squared is 4, 2 to the power 1 is 2. Each time the little number drops by one, the big number halves. Keep going. Drop the little number to 0 and halve once more: 2 divided by 2 is 1. So 2 to the power 0 is 1. Not 0 — there is no reason for the pattern to suddenly crash to nothing. Try it with threes: 27, 9, 3, and one more divide by 3 gives 1. The pattern works for every base, which is why the law works for every base.

There is a second way to see it, using the cancelling socks. 5 to the power 4 over 5 to the power 4: four fives on top, four on the bottom, every pair cancels, and nothing is left but 1. The division law says to subtract the counts: four take away four is zero, so this is 5 to the power 0. Both answers describe the same thing, so 5 to the power 0 has to be 1.

The careful bit is where the zero exponent lands. 3 times 5 to the power 0: the zero sits on the 5 only, so the 5 to the power 0 becomes 1 and you are left with 3 times 1, which is 3. But 3 times 5, all to the power 0, has the zero on the whole bracket, so the whole thing is 1. Same digits, different answer, and the bracket is the only difference. Read the bracket before you touch the exponent.

Here is your pocket summary of the whole lesson. Multiplying powers of the same base: add the counts. Dividing: subtract. A power of a power: multiply. A bracket with a product inside: share the exponent with every factor, including the number. Anything non-zero to the power 0: one. Five lines, and all of them are just counting.

Hold the marching-down pattern and the cancelling socks, and take the final questions of the lesson: what the zero exponent means, where it lands, and the five laws in one breath.
