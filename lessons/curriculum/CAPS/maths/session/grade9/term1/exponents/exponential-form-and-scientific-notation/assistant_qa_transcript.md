### subtopic_1 — Exponential Form and Its Vocabulary
**Assistant:** Why does the bracket matter so much in (−2)^4 versus −2^4? They look almost the same.

**Tutor:** They look the same and they are different numbers, which is exactly why the examiner sets it. The exponent is an instruction that applies to whatever sits immediately beneath it. In the bracketed form, the thing beneath the exponent is the whole quantity negative 2, so four copies of negative 2 are multiplied: the signs pair off and the product is positive 16. In the unbracketed form, the thing beneath the exponent is just the 2. You evaluate 2 to the power 4, which is 16, and the minus sign — which was never part of the base — is applied to the result, giving negative 16. The convention is the same one that governs 3 times 2 squared: the power binds before the multiplication, and a leading minus is a multiplication by negative 1. A reliable habit is to ask "what is the base?" before anything else, and to write the base in brackets whenever it is negative, so that your own work cannot be misread. In an examination, writing 16 for negative 2 to the power 4 without brackets is marked wrong, not as a slip but as a misunderstanding of order.

### subtopic_2 — Scientific Notation for Large Numbers
**Assistant:** If 14,96 × 10^7 has the same value as 1,496 × 10^8, why does it lose marks?

**Tutor:** Because scientific notation is a convention, and the value of a convention lies in everybody writing the same number the same way. The rule is that a lies from 1 up to, but not including, 10 — one non-zero digit before the comma. With that rule, two people converting 149 600 000 independently must both arrive at 1,496 times 10 to the power 8, and anyone comparing two numbers can trust the exponents to tell the size directly. If 14,96 times 10 to the power 7 were allowed, so would 1 496 times 10 to the power 5 and 0,1496 times 10 to the power 9, and the exponent would stop meaning anything. So the mark scheme requires the standard form, and a correct value in a non-standard form earns the method but not the final mark. The correction is mechanical: each time you move the comma one place left in a, you have divided a by 10, so the exponent rises by 1 to pay for it. 14,96 becomes 1,496 and 7 becomes 8. Value unchanged, convention satisfied.

### subtopic_3 — Negative Exponents and Small Numbers
**Assistant:** Where does the negative exponent actually come from? It feels like a rule invented to annoy us.

**Tutor:** It comes from refusing to let a pattern break. Write the powers of 10 downwards: 10 to the power 3 is 1 000, 10 to the power 2 is 100, 10 to the power 1 is 10, 10 to the power 0 is 1. Each time the exponent drops by one, the value divides by 10. Nothing in that pattern says "stop at 1". Drop once more and the value must be 1 divided by 10, which is 0,1, and the natural label for it is 10 to the power negative 1. Drop again and 10 to the power negative 2 is 0,01. The negative exponent is not a new rule; it is the only label that keeps the old rule honest. That is also why a negative exponent never produces a negative number: every step in the pattern is a division by a positive 10, so the values shrink towards zero but never cross it. When you convert 0,00052 and find the comma has to move four places to the right to reach 5,2, you have discovered that 0,00052 is 5,2 divided by 10 four times, which the pattern names 5,2 times 10 to the power negative 4. Next lesson the laws of exponents will give the same result formally.

### subtopic_4 — Comparing, Ordering and Rounding in Scientific Notation
**Assistant:** When rounding, how can 9,97 × 10^4 end up with a different exponent? I thought the exponent never changes.

**Tutor:** The exponent changes only in one circumstance, and this is it. Rounding acts on a, and a must stay between 1 and 10. Round 9,97 to two significant figures: the third digit is 7, so the second digit rounds up, 9,9 becomes 10,0. Now a has broken the rule — it is 10, not below 10 — so you repair the standard form the usual way: move the comma one place left to get 1,00 and raise the exponent by one to pay for it. The answer is 1,0 times 10 to the power 5. The value is still about 99 700, which is indeed about 100 000, so the result makes sense. In every other case the exponent is untouched: 1,496 times 10 to the power 8 to two significant figures is simply 1,5 times 10 to the power 8, because 1,5 is still a legal a. The broader lesson is that every operation in this topic ends with the same check — is a between 1 and 10? — and if not, you slide the comma and adjust the exponent in the opposite direction. The check costs one glance and protects the final mark.
