### subtopic_1 — Why the Laws Hold: Multiplying and Dividing Powers
**Assistant:** Why can I add exponents for 2^3 × 2^4 but not for 2^3 × 3^2?

**Tutor:** Because the law is a counting law, and you can only count things that are alike. 2 cubed times 2 to the power 4 is three twos multiplied, then four more twos multiplied — seven factors, every one of them a 2, so the product is 2 to the power 7. The exponent is simply the tally of identical factors. In 2 cubed times 3 squared you have three twos and two threes; there is no single number of which you have five copies, so no single power describes it. The honest answer is 8 times 9, which is 72, and that is a perfectly acceptable final form. The same reasoning tells you why the base never changes under the first law: adding the counts does not alter what is being counted. Students who write 4 to the power 7 have changed the thing being counted, and the numbers expose it — 4 to the power 7 is 16 384, whereas the true product is 128. When you are unsure, write out two or three of the factors explicitly and the law reappears on its own. That habit is slow, but it is self-correcting, which no memorised rule is.

### subtopic_2 — Power of a Power and Power of a Product
**Assistant:** How do I tell whether to add or multiply the exponents? They both have two exponents in them.

**Tutor:** Read the bracket. Two powers standing side by side with a multiplication sign between them — 2 cubed times 2 squared — are two separate boxes of twos being combined, three and then two, so you add: 2 to the power 5. One power wrapped in a bracket with an exponent outside — 2 cubed, all squared — is a box being copied: two copies of a box of three, so you multiply: 2 to the power 6. The bracket is the signal that one exponent is acting on the result of another, and acting on a result means scaling the count, not extending it. If the symbols still feel ambiguous, fall back on values: 2 cubed times 2 squared is 8 times 4, which is 32, and 2 to the power 5 is 32; 2 cubed all squared is 8 squared, which is 64, and 2 to the power 6 is 64. The numbers choose the law for you. In an examination, writing the intermediate step — 2 cubed times 2 cubed, or 2 to the power 3 plus 2 — earns the method mark and makes your own choice visible so that a slip in arithmetic afterwards costs only the final mark.

### subtopic_3 — The Zero Exponent
**Assistant:** Is a^0 = 1 just something mathematicians decided, or does it have to be true?

**Tutor:** It has to be true, given the other laws. Take any non-zero number, say 5, and divide 5 to the power 4 by 5 to the power 4. One route: a non-zero number divided by itself is 1, no exponents needed. Second route: the division law says to subtract the exponents, giving 5 to the power 0. Both routes are valid calculations of the same quantity, so if the laws are to be consistent, 5 to the power 0 must be 1. Nothing was "decided" except that we want the division law to hold without exceptions, and once we want that, the value is forced. The pattern argument says the same thing from a different direction: 5 cubed, 5 squared, 5 to the power 1 are 125, 25, 5, each a fifth of the one before, and the next step down is 1. The only base excluded is zero, because the argument divides by the base, and division by zero is not permitted, which is why every statement of the law carries the condition a not equal to 0. In marking, I look for exactly that condition and for the bracket placement in expressions such as 3 times 5 to the power 0, which is 3, versus 3 times 5 all to the power 0, which is 1.

### subtopic_4 — Simplifying Mixed Expressions
**Assistant:** In an expression with brackets, coefficients and several laws, what order should I work in?

**Tutor:** Brackets first, then multiplication and division of powers, then the final tidy. Take 2a squared b, all cubed, divided by 4a to the power 4. The bracket is a product raised to a power, so share the exponent with every factor: 2 cubed is 8, a squared cubed is a to the power 6, b cubed stays b cubed — 8a to the power 6 b cubed. Only now divide. Coefficients are ordinary numbers: 8 over 4 is 2. Like bases subtract: a to the power 6 minus 4 is a squared. The b has nothing to divide by, so it stays. Result: 2a squared b cubed. The reason for this order is that the laws act on powers, and a bracket is not yet a set of powers until it has been expanded. If you divide first you will usually mis-share the exponent later. Within each stage, deal with one base at a time and keep the coefficient separate; write the coefficient calculation and each base's exponent arithmetic on the same line so the marker can follow it. Finish by checking that every exponent is positive and no bracket remains, because "simplest form" in the mark scheme means exactly that.
