### subtopic_1 — Adding and Subtracting Powers
**Assistant:** Why does 2^5 + 2^5 become 2^6 if there is no law for adding powers?

**Tutor:** Because that step is not an exponent law; it is counting, and then the ordinary multiplication law. Two copies of the same quantity added together is two times that quantity — that is the distributive property, which you revised with integers: a plus a is 2a for any a whatsoever. Here a happens to be 2 to the power 5, so the sum is 2 times 2 to the power 5. Now, and only now, you have a multiplication of powers with the same base, because 2 is 2 to the power 1, and the law adds the exponents: 2 to the power 6. The check is immediate: 32 plus 32 is 64. The same reasoning gives three copies of 3 to the power 4 as 3 times 3 to the power 4, which is 3 to the power 5. Notice that the trick depends entirely on the powers being identical. 2 to the power 5 plus 2 to the power 4 is 32 plus 16, which is 48, and 48 is not a power of 2, so no exponential shortcut exists — you could factor it as 2 to the power 4 times 3, but you cannot write it as a single power. The lesson is to be honest about what you are doing: counting identical things is allowed; inventing an addition law is not.

### subtopic_2 — Multiplying and Dividing Powers
**Assistant:** How do I decide quickly whether to combine the bases or the exponents when I multiply two powers?

**Tutor:** Ask what matches. If the bases match, the exponents combine: 2 cubed times 2 to the power 4 is 2 to the power 7, because you are counting copies of a single base. If the exponents match, the bases combine and the exponent stays: 2 cubed times 5 cubed is the quantity 2 times 5, all cubed, which is 10 cubed — three twos paired with three fives make three tens. That is the power-of-a-product law read from right to left. If neither matches, there is nothing to combine, and you evaluate: 2 cubed times 3 squared is 8 times 9, which is 72, final. The error to avoid is combining both at once, writing 10 to the power 6 for 2 cubed times 5 cubed. A quick size check exposes it: 8 times 125 is 1 000, which is 10 cubed, not a million. When bases are composite, factorise them first so that matches become visible: 12 squared times 3 squared over 6 squared looks awkward until you see that every exponent is 2, at which point it is the quantity 12 times 3 over 6, all squared, which is 6 squared, which is 36. Matching is the whole skill; the laws then do the rest.

### subtopic_3 — Order of Operations With Powers
**Assistant:** Where exactly do roots fit in the order of operations, and why can I halve the exponent under a square root?

**Tutor:** Roots sit on the same step as powers, because a root is a power running backwards, and both are evaluated after brackets and before multiplication. The halving rule comes from the power-of-a-power law. The square root of a number is the quantity that squares to give it. 2 squared, all squared, is 2 to the power 4 by multiplying exponents, so the thing that squares to 2 to the power 4 is 2 squared — the exponent halves. Likewise the cube root of 2 to the power 6 is 2 squared, because 2 squared cubed is 2 to the power 6 — the exponent divides by 3. So the square root of 2 to the power 4 plus the cube root of 2 to the power 6 is 4 plus 4, which is 8. The rule needs the exponent to be divisible: the square root of 2 cubed is not a whole power of 2, because 3 is odd, and in this course it stays written as a root. One more consequence: the square root of a product is the product of the roots, so the square root of 3 to the power 4 times 5 squared is 3 squared times 5, which is 45. Always evaluate the root before any multiplication outside it, and check with values when the exponents are small enough — 16 under a square root is 4, and 64 under a cube root is 4.

### subtopic_4 — Powers of Ten in Calculations and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Why do I have to make the powers of ten equal before adding, when I do not for multiplying?

**Tutor:** Because the power of ten is a place value, and addition works place by place while multiplication does not. 3 times 10 to the power 5 is 3 in the hundred-thousands place; 2 times 10 to the power 4 is 2 in the ten-thousands place. You cannot add a 3 and a 2 that sit in different columns any more than you can add 300 and 20 to get 5 of anything — you line up the columns first. Rewriting 3 times 10 to the power 5 as 30 times 10 to the power 4 puts both numbers in the ten-thousands column, and now the fronts add: 32 times 10 to the power 4, which is 3,2 times 10 to the power 5 in standard form. The full-number check confirms it: 300 000 plus 20 000 is 320 000. Multiplication has no such requirement because a product is not column-bound: 3 times 2 is 6, and 10 to the power 5 times 10 to the power 4 is 10 to the power 9 by the exponent law, regardless of the columns. The seductive wrong answer, 5 times 10 to the power 9, comes from applying the multiplication habit to a sum — adding fronts and adding exponents — and it is out by a factor of roughly ten thousand, which the full-number check shows instantly. Whenever a plus or minus appears between numbers in scientific notation, equalise the exponents first; whenever a times or divide appears, work fronts and powers separately.
