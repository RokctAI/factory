# Part 1 — Expert

This session solves problems in context involving numbers in exponential form, including scientific notation. The anchor problems are a bacterial culture of 500 cells doubling every hour for 6 hours, which gives 500 times 2 to the power 6, which is 32 000 cells; and the distance light travels in 500 seconds at 3 times 10 to the power 5 kilometres per second, which is 1,5 times 10 to the power 8 kilometres — the distance from the Sun to the Earth. Every problem is solved by translating the words into a power, applying the laws, and checking the size of the answer.

## Subtopic: Growth by Repeated Multiplication

The signature of an exponent in a word problem is repeated multiplication by the same factor: "doubles every hour", "triples each round", "grows by 8 percent a year". Each repetition multiplies the quantity by a fixed number, and after n repetitions the multiplier is that number to the power n.

The anchor. A culture starts with 500 bacteria and doubles every hour. After 1 hour there are 500 times 2; after 2 hours 500 times 2 times 2; after 6 hours 500 times 2 to the power 6. 2 to the power 6 is 64, so the count is 500 × 2^6 = 32 000. The formula is start times 2 to the power n, where n is the number of doublings. It is NOT 500 times 2 times 6, which is 6 000 — that would be adding 500 bacteria six times, not doubling six times.

The same structure with a different factor. A rumour reaches 3 new people from each person who hears it, each round. After 5 rounds the newest group numbers 3 to the power 5, which is 243. A population of 100 insects triples each week; after 4 weeks there are 100 times 3 to the power 4, which is 100 times 81, which is 8 100.

Compound interest is exponential growth with a factor slightly larger than 1. From the financial maths lesson: R5 000 invested at 8 percent per year compound grows by a factor 1,08 each year, so after 3 years it is 5 000 × 1,08^3 = 6 298,56 rand. The exponent counts years; the base is 1 plus the rate as a decimal.

Working backwards. If a culture doubles every hour and has 32 000 cells after 6 hours, how many did it start with? Divide by 2 to the power 6: 32 000 over 64 is 500. If it had 8 000 cells after 4 hours, the start was 8 000 over 16, which is 500 again. Dividing by the power undoes the growth — the law a to the m divided by a to the n at work.

Reading the exponent from the context is the skill. "Every hour for 6 hours" gives exponent 6. "Every 20 minutes for 2 hours" gives six doublings too, because 2 hours is six 20-minute periods — the exponent counts periods, not hours.

The questions for this section are with you now: identifying the factor and the number of repetitions, writing the power, and working backwards by dividing.

## Subtopic: Area, Volume and Powers

Squares and cubes are exponents with a geometric meaning, and they carry units with them. A square field of side 15 metres has area 15 squared, which is 225 square metres. A cube of side 2 times 10 squared centimetres has volume the quantity 2 times 10 squared, all cubed, which is 2 cubed times 10 to the power 6, which is 8 times 10 to the power 6 cubic centimetres. Check: 200 cubed is 8 000 000.

The unit is raised to the power as well. 1 kilometre is 10 cubed metres, so 1 square kilometre is the quantity 10 cubed, all squared, which is 10 to the power 6 square metres — a million, not a thousand. 1 cubic metre is the quantity 10 squared centimetres, all cubed, which is 10 to the power 6 cubic centimetres. 1 litre is 10 cubed cubic centimetres, so 5 litres is 5 times 10 cubed cubic centimetres, and since 1 cubic centimetre is 10 cubed cubic millimetres, 5 litres is 5 times 10 to the power 6 cubic millimetres.

Scaling is the power-of-a-product law. Double the side of a square and the area is the quantity 2 times s, all squared, which is 4 times s squared: four times the area. Double the side of a cube and the volume is 2 cubed times s cubed: eight times the volume. Triple a side and areas grow by 9, volumes by 27. In general, a scale factor k multiplies areas by k squared and volumes by k cubed — a fact that returns in the Term 4 measurement work and again in Grade 10.

Applied: a storage box has a volume of 2 times 10 to the power 4 cubic centimetres. A box with every edge doubled has volume 2 cubed times 2 times 10 to the power 4, which is 16 times 10 to the power 4, which is 1,6 times 10 to the power 5 cubic centimetres. A photograph enlarged by a factor of 3 needs 3 squared, which is 9, times as much paper.

Take this section's questions now: areas and volumes as powers with units, conversion factors as powers of ten, and the k squared and k cubed scaling rules.

## Subtopic: Scientific Notation Problems

Problems with very large or very small quantities are solved by writing every quantity in standard form, then multiplying or dividing fronts and powers of ten separately.

The anchor. Light travels at 3 times 10 to the power 5 kilometres per second and takes about 500 seconds to reach the Earth from the Sun. Distance is speed times time: 3 times 10 to the power 5, times 500. Write 500 as 5 times 10 squared. Fronts: 3 times 5 is 15. Powers: 10 to the power 5 plus 2 is 10 to the power 7. Result 15 times 10 to the power 7, which in standard form is 1,5 times 10 to the power 8 kilometres — about 150 million kilometres, which matches the Earth–Sun distance from two lessons ago.

Division. A phone has 2,5 times 10 to the power 9 bytes of free storage and each photo is 5 times 10 to the power 6 bytes. Number of photos: (2,5 × 10^9) ÷ (5 × 10^6) = 500. Working: fronts 2,5 over 5 is 0,5; powers 10 to the power 9 minus 6 is 10 cubed; 0,5 times 10 cubed is 500 photos. Here the front left the standard range and was simply multiplied out, since a plain number was wanted.

Tiny times huge. A single bacterium has a mass of about 1 times 10 to the power negative 12 grams. A colony of 2 times 10 to the power 12 bacteria has mass 2 times 10 to the power 12, times 10 to the power negative 12, which is 2 times 10 to the power 0, which is 2 grams. The negative exponent cancels the positive one exactly.

A body holds about 5 litres of blood, which is 5 times 10 to the power 6 cubic millimetres, and each cubic millimetre contains about 5 times 10 to the power 6 red blood cells. Total cells: fronts 5 times 5 is 25; powers 10 to the power 12. 25 times 10 to the power 12 is 2,5 times 10 to the power 13 cells — twenty-five trillion.

Time from distance. The Moon is 3,844 times 10 to the power 5 kilometres away; light at 3 times 10 to the power 5 kilometres per second takes 3,844 over 3 times 10 to the power 0 seconds, which is about 1,28 seconds. A quick estimate — a bit under 4 over 3, so a bit over 1 — confirms the size.

This section's questions are available now: speed, distance and time in standard form, counting by division, and exponents that cancel.

## Subtopic: Reading, Estimating and the Error Museum

Every answer to a context problem must pass three checks: size, units and a small-case test.

Size: before calculating, estimate the exponent. Light for 500 seconds: 3 times 10 to the power 5 is a few hundred thousand, times a few hundred, lands in the hundreds of millions — exponent 8. If the detailed work gives exponent 13, a power of ten has been mishandled. For growth, 2 to the power 10 is about a thousand, so ten doublings multiply by about a thousand: 500 cells become about half a million, not 5 000 and not 500 million.

Units: write the unit at every line and raise it with the number. 3,5 kilometres is 3,5 times 10 cubed metres, which is 3,5 times 10 to the power 5 centimetres. 0,0025 metres is 2,5 times 10 to the power negative 3 metres, which is 2,5 millimetres. A square kilometre is 10 to the power 6 square metres; writing 10 cubed because "kilo means a thousand" forgets that the unit is squared.

Small case: replace the exponent with 1 or 2 and check the formula. If your bacteria formula says 500 times 2n, then at n equals 1 it gives 1 000, correct, but at n equals 2 it gives 2 000 — and two doublings of 500 is 2 000 as well, so this test passes by coincidence. At n equals 3 it gives 3 000 against the true 4 000; the formula is exposed. Always test two values.

The error museum for this topic has five exhibits. One: 500 times 2 times 6 for six doublings — multiplying by the number of periods instead of raising to it. Two: counting hours when the period is 20 minutes — the exponent counts periods. Three: 3 times 10 to the power 5 times 500 written as 15 times 10 to the power 7 and left there — not standard form; the answer is 1,5 times 10 to the power 8. Four: 1 square kilometre written as 10 cubed square metres — the unit was not squared. Five: adding fronts and adding exponents when two quantities in standard form are added — the powers must be equalised first, as last lesson showed.

Layout for marks: write the quantities in standard form with units; write the operation as an expression; handle fronts and powers on separate lines; restore standard form; state the answer in words with units; and write the size check in the margin.

The questions for this section are ready: estimating the exponent in advance, raising the unit with the number, and the small-case test that exposes a wrong formula.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same problems again through a rumour that will not stop, a pizza that is twice as wide, and a recipe for taming any huge or tiny number — plain words, same rules.

## Subtopic: Doubling Is Faster Than You Think

Here is a bet you will lose. Someone offers you R1 000 a day for 30 days, or 1 cent on day one, doubled every day for 30 days. The first choice is R30 000. The second: 1 cent times 2 to the power 29 on the last day alone, and 2 to the power 10 is about a thousand, so 2 to the power 30 is about a thousand times a thousand times a thousand — a billion cents, ten million rand. Doubling looks slow at first and then runs away. That is what an exponent does.

The bacteria are the same story. 500 cells, doubling every hour. After one hour, 1 000. Two hours, 2 000. Three, 4 000. Four, 8 000. Five, 16 000. Six, 32 000. Each hour multiplies by 2, so six hours multiplies by 2 six times — 2 to the power 6, which is 64 — and 500 times 64 is 32 000. If you had multiplied by 6 instead, you would have 3 000, which is less than the real count after three hours. "Every hour for six hours" means six multiplications, not one multiplication by six.

The factor does not have to be 2. A rumour where each person tells three others multiplies by 3 each round: 3, 9, 27, 81, 243 after five rounds. Money at 8 percent interest multiplies by 1,08 each year: R5 000 becomes about R6 298,56 after three years, because 1,08 cubed is about 1,26 and a quarter more of 5 000 is 6 250 and a bit. Spot the phrase "every", "each", "per round" — it means a power.

Going backwards is dividing. 32 000 cells now, doubling every hour, and you want the count six hours ago: divide by 2 to the power 6, which is 64, and get 500. Undoing growth means dividing by the power, which is the subtracting-exponents law in disguise.

Keep the runaway doubling in mind and try the questions for this part: reading the factor and the number of periods, and why multiplying by 6 is not the same as doubling six times.

## Subtopic: Squares, Cubes and Scaling Up

A square and a cube are exponents you can hold. A square 15 metres on each side holds 15 squared, which is 225 square metres. A cube 200 centimetres on each edge holds 200 cubed — and 200 is 2 times 10 squared, so the volume is 2 cubed times 10 to the power 6, which is 8 million cubic centimetres. Notice the unit got cubed too: centimetres became cubic centimetres.

Now the pizza. Order a pizza twice as wide. Do you get twice as much? No — four times. Width doubled, and area is width times width, so the area is 2 times 2, which is 4, times bigger. The exponent on the length — 2 for area — becomes the exponent on the scale factor. For a box twice as long, twice as wide and twice as tall, the volume is 2 times 2 times 2, which is 8 times bigger. Scale a length by k and areas grow by k squared, volumes by k cubed. Triple the edge and the box holds 27 times as much.

This is why unit conversions for area and volume trip people up. A kilometre is a thousand metres, but a square kilometre is a thousand times a thousand square metres — a million — because the thousand gets squared along with the metre. A cubic metre is 100 cubed, which is a million, cubic centimetres. When the unit has a little 2 or 3 on it, so does the conversion factor.

A quick picture to keep: a sheet of paper 0,1 millimetres thick, folded in half ten times, is 0,1 times 2 to the power 10, which is about 102 millimetres — ten centimetres thick, from a sheet. Doubling, squares and cubes all grow faster than your instinct says, which is why we write them as powers and let the laws do the counting.

Hold the pizza and the folded paper and try this part's questions: area and volume as powers, and what happens to each when you scale a length.

## Subtopic: Working With Huge and Tiny Numbers

Every big-number word problem is tamed by the same four-step recipe. One: write each quantity in standard form with its unit. Two: write the calculation as an expression — speed times time, total divided by size of one. Three: handle the fronts and the powers of ten on separate lines. Four: tidy to standard form and check the size.

Try it on light. Speed 3 times 10 to the power 5 kilometres per second; time 500 seconds, which is 5 times 10 squared. Distance is speed times time. Fronts: 3 times 5 is 15. Powers: 5 plus 2 is 7. So 15 times 10 to the power 7, and tidying gives 1,5 times 10 to the power 8 kilometres. Size check: a few hundred thousand times a few hundred is hundreds of millions. Yes — 150 million kilometres, the distance to the Sun.

Try it on photos. Free space 2,5 times 10 to the power 9 bytes; one photo 5 times 10 to the power 6 bytes. Number of photos is space divided by photo size. Fronts: 2,5 over 5 is 0,5. Powers: 9 minus 6 is 3. 0,5 times a thousand is 500 photos. Size check: 2,5 billion over 5 million — billions over millions is thousands, and half of a thousand is 500. Yes.

Try it on something tiny meeting something huge. Two trillion bacteria, each weighing a trillionth of a gram: 2 times 10 to the power 12, times 10 to the power negative 12. Fronts: 2. Powers: 12 plus negative 12 is 0, and 10 to the power 0 is 1. Two grams. The huge and the tiny cancelled perfectly, which is exactly what you would hope — a trillion trillionths is one.

The size check is your safety net. Before you calculate, guess the exponent: how many digits should the answer have? If the final exponent is wildly off from your guess, a power of ten has gone astray, and you know where to look. And keep the unit on every line: metres squared, cubic centimetres, kilometres per second. If the unit at the end is not the unit the question asked for, the maths is not finished.

Hold the four-step recipe, the pizza and the runaway doubling, and take the final questions of the lesson: standard form in a story, fronts and powers separately, and the size check at the end.
