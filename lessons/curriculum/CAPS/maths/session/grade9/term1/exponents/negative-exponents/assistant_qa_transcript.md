### subtopic_1 — Extending the Division Law
**Assistant:** If a negative exponent is not a negative number, why do we even use a minus sign for it?

**Tutor:** Because the minus sign is doing exactly what minus always does — it records a direction along a scale — only here the scale is the exponent, not the answer. The exponents run 3, 2, 1, 0 as you divide by the base each time, and continuing the same descent takes you to negative 1, negative 2, negative 3. The exponent is counting steps below zero on its own number line, and the law is simply the statement of what the answer does at each of those steps: it keeps dividing by the base. So 2 to the power negative 3 means "three divisions by 2 past the number 1", which is one eighth. Nothing about that process can produce a negative result, because dividing a positive number by a positive number is positive at every step. The confusion arises only when a student carries the minus from the exponent down to the answer, which is like reading "three floors below ground" as "minus three metres tall" — the sign describes where you are on one scale, not the value on another. In marking, a negative answer for a positive base raised to a negative power is treated as a conceptual error, not an arithmetic slip, which is why the distinction is worth rehearsing until it is automatic.

### subtopic_2 — Evaluating Negative Exponents
**Assistant:** Why does (2/3)^−2 become 9/4? Where does the flip come from?

**Tutor:** Apply the law literally and the flip appears on its own. Two thirds to the power negative 2 is 1 over two thirds squared. Two thirds squared is four ninths, so you have 1 divided by four ninths, and dividing by a fraction is multiplying by its reciprocal: nine quarters. The shortcut — invert the fraction and make the exponent positive — is just that calculation compressed into one step, and it is worth seeing the long version once so that the shortcut is trusted rather than memorised. The same reasoning explains why a small fraction with a negative exponent becomes a large number: one half to the power negative 3 is 1 over one eighth, which is 8, because dividing by something small gives something big. The sign of the base still behaves normally. Negative two thirds in brackets to the power negative 2 inverts to negative three halves in brackets squared, which is positive nine quarters, and negative two thirds to the power negative 3 inverts to a cube, which is negative. Separate the two questions every time: what does the exponent's sign do to position, and what does the base's sign do to the answer.

### subtopic_3 — Negative Exponents in Simplifying
**Assistant:** Why should I leave the negative exponents alone until the last line instead of fixing them immediately?

**Tutor:** Because the exponent laws work perfectly well with negative exponents, and converting early turns a clean problem into fractions within fractions. Take 4a to the power negative 3 b squared over 2a b to the power negative 1. If you convert first, the numerator becomes 4b squared over a cubed and the denominator becomes 2a over b, and you must then divide one compound fraction by another, which is where sign and placement errors breed. If instead you treat the exponents as integers and apply the laws as they stand, the coefficients give 2, the a's give negative 3 minus 1, which is negative 4, and the b's give 2 minus negative 1, which is 3. One line: 2a to the power negative 4 b cubed. Now, and only now, apply the convention that answers carry positive exponents: the a hops below the bar, giving 2b cubed over a to the power 4. The mark scheme awards method for the laws and a final mark for the positive-exponent form, so the order I recommend matches how the marks are earned. It also has one practical safeguard built in: the only line where anything crosses the fraction bar is the last one, so there is exactly one place to check for a wrongly moved coefficient.

### subtopic_4 — Scientific Notation Revisited and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** How does the plug-in check work when the expression has letters in it?

**Tutor:** The check rests on one fact: if two expressions are truly equal, they give the same value for every number you substitute, so a single substitution that gives different values proves them unequal. Choose a small, easy number — 1 or 2, never 0 when a factor is in a denominator — and evaluate the original and your answer separately. Suppose you have written 1 over 2x squared for 2x all to the power negative 2. Put x equal to 1. The original is 2 to the power negative 2, which is a quarter. Your answer is 1 over 2, a half. They differ, so your answer is wrong, and the mismatch even hints at the cause: a factor of 2 has been lost, because the 2 inside the bracket should have been squared. Correct it to 1 over 4x squared and test again: a quarter and a quarter. The check cannot prove an answer right in general — a lucky coincidence at one value is possible — but it is extremely good at exposing the common errors in this topic, because every one of them changes the value at x equals 1 or x equals 2. In an examination it takes a few seconds in the margin and it has saved more marks than any rule I could teach.
