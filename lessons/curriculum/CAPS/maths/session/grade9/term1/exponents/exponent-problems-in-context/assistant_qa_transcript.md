### subtopic_1 — Growth by Repeated Multiplication
**Assistant:** How do I tell whether a problem wants a power or just ordinary multiplication?

**Tutor:** Ask what happens to the quantity at each step. If a fixed amount is added each time — R500 saved every month, 20 chairs delivered each week — the total after n steps is the amount times n, which is ordinary multiplication, and it grows in a straight line. If the quantity is multiplied by a fixed factor each time — doubles every hour, triples each round, grows by 8 percent a year — the multiplier after n steps is that factor to the power n, and it grows exponentially. The clue words "every", "each" and "per" appear in both kinds, so the words alone do not settle it; what settles it is whether the step adds or multiplies. Doubling multiplies by 2, so six doublings are 2 to the power 6, which is 64, and 500 cells become 32 000. If you mistake it for addition you get 500 plus 500 times 6, which is 3 500, or 500 times 2 times 6, which is 6 000, and both are far too small. A reliable test is to write out the first three steps explicitly: 500, 1 000, 2 000, 4 000. If each term is the previous one times the same number, it is a power. In the Grade 10 work on exponential growth this distinction becomes the difference between a linear and an exponential graph, so it is worth making automatic now.

### subtopic_2 — Area, Volume and Powers
**Assistant:** Why is a square kilometre a million square metres when a kilometre is only a thousand metres?

**Tutor:** Because the conversion is squared along with the unit. A square kilometre is a square with each side one kilometre, and each side is 10 cubed metres. Its area is side times side: 10 cubed times 10 cubed, which is 10 to the power 6 square metres by the multiplication law. Put differently, a square kilometre is 1 000 rows of 1 000 square metres, and a thousand thousands is a million. The same reasoning gives 10 to the power 6 cubic centimetres in a cubic metre: 10 squared cubed is 10 to the power 6. The general principle is that any length conversion factor k becomes k squared for area and k cubed for volume, which is precisely the scaling rule you use when a shape is enlarged — double the edge, eight times the volume. The error of writing 10 cubed square metres for a square kilometre comes from reading "kilo" as "a thousand" and stopping, without noticing the little 2 on the unit. My advice is to write the unit with its exponent at every line and treat the exponent as a multiplier on the conversion: kilometres squared means the thousand gets squared too. When Term 4 reaches surface area and volume this habit will already be in place.

### subtopic_3 — Scientific Notation Problems
**Assistant:** In the photo problem the front came out as 0,5, which is not between 1 and 10. Was that wrong?

**Tutor:** It was not wrong; it was unfinished until you asked what the question wanted. The calculation gives fronts of 2,5 over 5, which is 0,5, and powers of 10 to the power 9 minus 6, which is 10 cubed, so the raw result is 0,5 times 10 cubed. If the question asks for a number of photos, that is simply 500, a plain count, and standard form is irrelevant. If the question asks for the answer in scientific notation, you must repair the form: move the comma one place right to make 5, and lower the exponent by one to pay for it, giving 5 times 10 squared. Both are the same number; the difference is presentation. The rule to carry is that the laws may be applied with any fronts you like, and standard form is restored only at the end, in the form the question specifies. The same thing happened with the light problem: fronts 3 times 5 gave 15, powers gave 10 to the power 7, and 15 times 10 to the power 7 was correct but not standard, so it became 1,5 times 10 to the power 8. Treat the front as a working number during the calculation, and as a presentation requirement at the final line.

### subtopic_4 — Reading, Estimating and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** The size check feels like extra work. How much does it actually catch?

**Tutor:** In this topic it catches almost everything that goes wrong, because almost everything that goes wrong is a misplaced power of ten or a misread exponent, and both change the size of the answer by a factor of ten or more. A slip in the front — writing 14 instead of 15 — costs a mark; a slip in the exponent — writing 10 to the power 13 instead of 10 to the power 8 — produces an answer a hundred thousand times too big and costs the whole question. The size check is designed to catch the second kind. It takes a few seconds: round every quantity to its power of ten, combine those powers mentally, and write the expected exponent in the margin before you start. Light for 500 seconds: 10 to the power 5 times 10 to the power 2 or 3 gives 10 to the power 7 or 8. If your careful work lands on exponent 8, you proceed with confidence; if it lands on 13, you know a power of ten was mishandled and you know which lines to inspect. The second part of the check is the units — a volume answered in square centimetres has not been finished — and the third is the small-case test for growth formulas, which exposes 500 times 2n at n equals 3. None of these replaces the calculation; they make the calculation trustworthy, and in an examination they turn a question you hope you got right into one you know you did.
