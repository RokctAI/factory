# Part 1 — Expert

This session determines, interprets and justifies the equivalence of descriptions of a relationship presented verbally, in flow diagrams, in tables, as formulae, as equations and as graphs on the Cartesian plane. The anchor relationship is "multiply by 3, then add 2", y equals 3x plus 2, whose graph passes through the points 0 comma 2, 1 comma 5 and 2 comma 8. Two descriptions are equivalent when every input produces the same output in both; equivalence is tested by substitution and justified by algebra, and both steps are examinable.

## Subtopic: One Relationship, Five Descriptions

Begin with the verbal description: "take the input, multiply it by 3, then add 2". The flow diagram draws the same thing: x enters, passes through "times 3", then "plus 2", and y leaves. The formula records it in symbols: y equals 3x plus 2, with the multiplication before the addition by the order of operations.

The table lists specific inputs and outputs. Substitute x equals 0, 1, 2, 3: y equals 2, 5, 8, 11. Include a negative: x equals negative 1 gives y equals negative 1. A table is a sample of the relationship; the formula is the whole of it.

The graph plots each row of the table as a point on the Cartesian plane, with the input x along the horizontal axis and the output y up the vertical axis. The point for x equals 0, y equals 2 is written 0 comma 2 and sits on the vertical axis 2 units up. The point 1 comma 5 is one unit right and five up; 2 comma 8 is two right and eight up; negative 1 comma negative 1 is one left and one down. The ordered pair is always input first, output second.

The points lie on a straight line, and that is no accident. The constant difference in the table — y rises by 3 for every rise of 1 in x — means every step right is the same step up, which is precisely what a straight line is. Join the points, and the line is the graph of y equals 3x plus 2. Any point on the line is an input-output pair of the rule, and any input-output pair is a point on the line.

Each description answers a question best. Words explain; the diagram shows order; the table shows samples; the formula computes any output; the graph shows the whole relationship at a glance, including where it crosses the axes. All five describe one relationship, and converting between them changes the view, never the rule.

An equation is a sixth form: the formula with the output fixed. 3x plus 2 equals 20 asks which input produces 20; on the graph it asks where the line reaches a height of 20, and the answer, x equals 6, is the same from the algebra and from the picture.

The questions for this section are with you now: producing each description from the others, plotting ordered pairs correctly, and why the points lie on a line.

## Subtopic: From Table to Graph and Back

Plotting a table is mechanical if the conventions are kept. Draw and label both axes, mark a scale with equal spacing, and plot each ordered pair with x first. For y equals 3x plus 2 the table x equals negative 1, 0, 1, 2 gives y equals negative 1, 2, 5, 8; plot negative 1 comma negative 1, 0 comma 2, 1 comma 5, 2 comma 8, and draw the line through them.

Reading a graph reverses the process. To find the output for an input, go to the input on the horizontal axis, move vertically to the line, then horizontally to the vertical axis and read the output. To find the input for an output, start on the vertical axis, move horizontally to the line, then down to the horizontal axis. For y equals 3x plus 2, the output 11 is reached at x equals 3; the input negative 2 gives output negative 4.

Two features of the line carry meaning. Where it crosses the vertical axis is the output at input 0 — the constant in the formula, 2 for our rule. How steeply it rises is the rate — 3 up for every 1 across, the coefficient of x. A line with a larger coefficient is steeper; a negative coefficient makes the line fall from left to right, as y equals 23 minus 3x does.

Not every relationship graphs as a line. y equals x squared plus 1 has table x equals negative 2, negative 1, 0, 1, 2 giving y equals 5, 2, 1, 2, 5. Plotted, the points form a symmetric curve with its lowest point at 0 comma 1, not a line, because the differences 3, 1, negative 1, negative 3 are not constant. A table with a constant difference graphs as a line; a table without one does not, and a straight line drawn through such points is a wrong graph.

Two relationships on the same axes meet where they share an input-output pair. y equals 3x plus 2 and y equals 2x plus 5 meet where 3x plus 2 equals 2x plus 5, so x equals 3 and y equals 11. The point 3 comma 11 lies on both lines, and reading it off the graph is the same as solving the equation.

Take this section's questions now: plotting and reading points, the meaning of the crossing point and the steepness, and the curve that is not a line.

## Subtopic: Testing Equivalence

Two descriptions are equivalent when they produce the same output for every input. That is a strong condition, and it is tested in two stages: a numerical check that can disprove, and an algebraic argument that can prove.

Consider "multiply by 3, then add 2" against "add 2, then multiply by 3". Numerically: input 1 gives 5 in the first and 9 in the second. They differ at one input, so they are NOT equivalent — one counter-example is enough. Algebraically: the first is 3x plus 2; the second is 3 times the quantity x plus 2, which is 3x plus 6. Different constants, different rules.

Now "double, then add 4" against "add 2, then double". Numerically: input 1 gives 6 and 6; input 5 gives 14 and 14; input negative 3 gives negative 2 and negative 2. No counter-example appears, which is evidence. Algebraically: the first is 2x plus 4; the second is 2 times the quantity x plus 2, which by the distributive property is 2x plus 4. Identical, so they ARE equivalent, and the expansion is the justification.

Equivalence between a table and a formula is tested by substituting every input in the table. If the formula reproduces every output, the two agree on that sample; if the table was generated by the formula, they are equivalent descriptions. If a single row fails, the formula does not describe that table.

Equivalence between a graph and a formula is tested at points: read two or three points off the graph and substitute them. A straight line is fixed by two points, so if two points of the line satisfy a linear formula, the line is the graph of that formula. The vertical-axis crossing checks the constant; one more point checks the coefficient.

Verbal descriptions require care, because words can describe the same rule in several orders. "Three times a number, plus two" and "two more than three times a number" are the same rule, 3x plus 2. "Three times the sum of a number and two" is 3 times the quantity x plus 2, a different rule. Translate every verbal description into a formula before comparing; the formula is where ambiguity dies.

This section's questions are available now: disproving by one input, proving by expansion, and translating words into a formula before comparing.

## Subtopic: Interpreting Graphs and the Error Museum

In context, the parts of a linear relationship have meanings that an examination asks you to state. A taxi charging R2 flag fall and R3 per kilometre has fare C equals 3k plus 2. On the graph of C against k, the vertical-axis crossing at 2 is the fare before any distance — the flag fall. The steepness, 3 up for 1 across, is the rate — rand per kilometre. A different taxi with C equals 5k has no flag fall and a steeper line; the two lines cross where 3k plus 2 equals 5k, at k equals 1, where both charge R5. For journeys shorter than 1 kilometre the second taxi is cheaper; beyond it, the first.

A falling line describes a decreasing quantity. A phone battery at 20 percent losing 3 percent per hour has B equals 20 minus 3t; the crossing at 20 is the starting charge, the steepness is negative 3 — down 3 for every hour across — and the line reaches zero at t equals 6 and two thirds hours, after which the model stops meaning anything.

Interpreting also means reading the sensible domain. Kilometres cannot be negative, so the taxi graph begins at k equals 0. A table of tables-and-chairs uses whole numbers of tables, so its graph is a set of separate points, not a continuous line, even though the points are collinear.

The error museum for this topic has five exhibits. One: plotting output first — the point 2 comma 5 drawn as 5 comma 2. Two: joining the points of a non-linear table with a straight line, or of a whole-number context with a continuous line. Three: declaring two descriptions equivalent because they agree at one input — "times 3 plus 2" and "times 5" both give 5 at x equals 1. Four: reading "three times the sum of a number and two" as 3x plus 2. Five: calling the vertical-axis crossing the rate, or the steepness the starting value.

Layout for marks on an equivalence question: translate each description into a formula; substitute one input to look for a counter-example; if none, expand and simplify both formulae to the same form and state that they are equivalent; if one appears, state the input and the two outputs and conclude that they are not. For a graph question: label axes, plot with x first, state the crossing and the rate with their units and meanings.

The questions for this section are ready: the meaning of the crossing and the steepness in a context, where two graphs meet, and the five exhibits of the museum.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same ideas again through one taxi described five ways, a dot-to-dot picture and a pair of recipes that might or might not make the same cake — plain words, same rules.

## Subtopic: Same Taxi, Five Pictures

A taxi charges R2 to get in and R3 for every kilometre. That sentence is description number one: words. Here are the other four, all of the same taxi.

Number two, the flow diagram: kilometres go in, through "times 3", through "plus 2", fare comes out. Number three, the formula: C equals 3k plus 2. Number four, the table: 0 kilometres R2, 1 kilometre R5, 2 kilometres R8, 3 kilometres R11. Number five, the graph: put kilometres along the bottom and rand up the side, and plot each table row as a dot — 0 across and 2 up, 1 across and 5 up, 2 across and 8 up. The dots line up in a straight row, rising to the right.

Why straight? Because every kilometre costs the same R3. One step right is always three steps up. Same step, same slope, straight line. If the taxi charged more for later kilometres, the dots would curve upward instead.

Each picture is good at something. Words tell the story. The flow diagram tells you the order. The table gives you ready-made examples. The formula lets you work out any fare in one line. And the graph lets you SEE the whole taxi at once — where it starts on the money axis, which is the R2 flag fall, and how fast the fare climbs, which is R3 a kilometre.

Changing pictures never changes the taxi. If you move from the formula to the table to the graph and the fare for 3 kilometres is R11 in all three, you have translated correctly. If one of them says R12, you made a slip in the translation, not a discovery about the taxi.

Keep the five pictures of the one taxi in mind and try the questions for this part: moving between words, diagram, table, formula and graph without changing the fare.

## Subtopic: Plotting the Dots

Plotting is dot-to-dot with two rules. Rule one: the first number in the pair is how far ACROSS, the second is how far UP. The pair 2 comma 8 means 2 across, 8 up. Swap them and you have plotted a different point that belongs to a different taxi. Rule two: label the axes and keep the spacing even, so that 1, 2, 3 along the bottom are the same distance apart.

For C equals 3k plus 2, plot 0 comma 2, 1 comma 5, 2 comma 8, 3 comma 11. Lay a ruler along them: they line up. Draw the line. Now the line answers questions. How much for 5 kilometres? Find 5 along the bottom, go up to the line, go across to the money axis: 17. How far for R20? Find 20 on the money axis, go across to the line, go down: 6 kilometres. Reading the graph and using the formula must agree, and when they do not, one of your readings slipped.

Two things on the line have names worth knowing. Where it hits the money axis — at 2 — is the fare at zero kilometres: the flag fall, the "plus" part of the rule. How steep it is — up 3 for every 1 across — is the rate: the "times" part. A steeper line is a more expensive taxi per kilometre; a line that starts higher has a bigger flag fall.

A line can go downhill. A battery at 20 percent losing 3 percent an hour has the rule 20 minus 3t: it starts at 20 on the vertical axis and drops 3 for every hour across, hitting zero at about 6 and two-thirds hours. Downhill means the "times" part is negative.

And not everything is a line. The rule "square, then add 1" gives the table negative 2 to 5, negative 1 to 2, 0 to 1, 1 to 2, 2 to 5. Plot those and you get a U-shape, lowest at 0 comma 1. The jumps between outputs are not the same, so the dots do not line up. Never draw a straight line through dots that do not line up — the picture should tell the truth about the table.

Hold the dot-to-dot rules and try this part's questions: across then up, reading off the line both ways, and spotting a table whose dots will not line up.

## Subtopic: Are They Really the Same?

Two friends each describe a taxi. One says "times 3, then add 2". The other says "add 2, then times 3". Same taxi? Test it with a number. 1 kilometre: the first gives 5; the second gives 3 times 3, which is 9. Different fares for the same distance, so different taxis. One disagreement is enough to prove two descriptions are not the same.

Now two more. "Double, then add 4" and "add 2, then double". Try 1: 6 and 6. Try 5: 14 and 14. Try negative 3: negative 2 and negative 2. No disagreement yet — but trying numbers can never prove they always agree, because there are always more numbers. To be sure, turn both into formulas. The first is 2x plus 4. The second is 2 times the quantity x plus 2, and sharing out the 2 gives 2x plus 4. Identical. Now you know, for every input, with no exceptions.

So the method is two steps. Step one, try a number or two; if they ever disagree, stop — not the same. Step two, if they keep agreeing, write both as formulas, multiply out any brackets, and compare. If the formulas match, they are the same rule in different clothes.

The same test works for any pair of pictures. Table versus formula: put every input from the table into the formula and see if every output matches. Graph versus formula: read two points off the line and check them in the formula — two points pin down a straight line. Words versus words: translate both into formulas first, because "three times a number plus two" and "three times the sum of a number and two" sound similar and mean different things.

Pocket summary of the lesson. One rule, many pictures: words, diagram, table, formula, graph. Plot across then up. A constant step makes a straight line; the crossing on the vertical axis is the start, the steepness is the rate. Two descriptions are the same only if EVERY input agrees: one disagreement proves no; matching formulas prove yes. And always translate words into a formula before you compare.

Hold the one taxi, the dot-to-dot and the two-step test, and take the final questions of the lesson: proving two descriptions differ, proving they agree, and saying what the start and the rate mean.
