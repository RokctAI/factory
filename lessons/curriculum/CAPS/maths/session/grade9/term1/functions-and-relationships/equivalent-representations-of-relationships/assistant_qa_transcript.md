### subtopic_1 — One Relationship, Five Descriptions
**Assistant:** Why do we bother with a graph when the formula already tells us everything?

**Tutor:** Because the formula tells you everything one input at a time, and the graph tells you everything at once. From y equals 3x plus 2 you can compute any single output, but to know how the relationship behaves — whether it rises or falls, how fast, where it crosses zero, where it meets another relationship — you must either compute many values or look at the picture. The graph is the picture of all the values together. It also makes certain facts visible that the formula hides in symbols: the constant 2 appears as the height at which the line crosses the vertical axis, and the coefficient 3 appears as the steepness, 3 up for every 1 across. When two relationships are drawn on the same axes, their meeting point is the input at which they give the same output — the solution of an equation — read off without algebra. There is a further reason that looks ahead. In Grade 10 the study of functions is largely the study of graphs: straight lines, parabolas, hyperbolas, exponentials, each recognised by shape. Students who already see 3x plus 2 as a line through 0 comma 2 with slope 3 start that year fluent; students who see only symbols start it behind. So the graph is not decoration; it is the representation in which the behaviour of the rule becomes visible.

### subtopic_2 — From Table to Graph and Back
**Assistant:** How can I tell from a table, before plotting, whether the graph will be a straight line?

**Tutor:** Check whether equal steps in the input produce equal steps in the output. In a table with inputs 0, 1, 2, 3 and outputs 2, 5, 8, 11, every step of 1 in x gives a step of 3 in y, so each point is the same distance up and across from the one before, and the points must lie on one line — a constant rate is what a straight line means. If the inputs are not evenly spaced, compare the rate per unit input between consecutive rows instead: outputs 7, 16, 31 at inputs 2, 5, 10 give 9 over 3 and 15 over 5, both 3 per unit, so still a line. If the steps in y are not constant — 2, 5, 10, 17 gives steps 3, 5, 7 — the points curve, and in that case the differences growing by a constant amount signals a square, which graphs as a U-shape. The rule for drawing follows: join the points with a straight line only when the table has a constant rate, draw a smooth curve when it does not, and in a context where only whole-number inputs make sense — tables and chairs, number of people — leave the points separate even if they are collinear, because a fare for two and a half tables has no meaning. The picture must tell the truth about the table.

### subtopic_3 — Testing Equivalence
**Assistant:** If two rules agree for five different inputs, is that not good enough to say they are the same?

**Tutor:** It is good enough to make equivalence likely and not good enough to make it certain, and the examination asks for certainty. The reason is the same asymmetry you met with patterns: a single disagreement proves two rules differ, but no finite list of agreements proves they agree everywhere, because there is always another input you have not tried. In practice, for the linear rules of this course, agreement at two inputs does pin down a straight line and so does settle the matter — but you are not expected to know which cases are safe, and the algebraic argument is short, so the mark scheme requires it. Write both rules as formulae, expand any brackets with the distributive property, collect terms, and compare. "Add 2, then double" becomes 2 times the quantity x plus 2, which is 2x plus 4, the same as "double, then add 4"; the identity of the expanded forms is the proof. The numerical checks still earn their place: they are fast, they disprove instantly when a disagreement appears, and they catch slips in your expansion. My recommended layout is one line of substitution at an easy input, then the algebraic comparison, then a one-sentence conclusion that names which it is.

### subtopic_4 — Interpreting Graphs and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** In the taxi graph, why is the crossing point on the vertical axis the flag fall and not the rate?

**Tutor:** Because the vertical axis is the line where the input is zero, and the fare at zero kilometres is by definition the amount you pay before travelling — the flag fall. Substitute k equals 0 into C equals 3k plus 2 and the 3k term vanishes, leaving 2: the only part of the rule that survives at zero input is the constant, so the constant is what the crossing shows. The rate lives somewhere else entirely. It is not a single point but a comparison between points: move 1 kilometre to the right and the fare rises by 3, so the line climbs 3 units for every 1 across, and that climb per unit is the coefficient of k. A useful way to keep them apart is to ask what each would mean if it changed. Raise the flag fall to R4 and the whole line lifts by 2 without tilting — every fare is R2 more. Raise the rate to R5 and the line pivots steeper from the same crossing — short trips barely change, long trips cost much more. Confusing the two is exhibit five in the museum, and it is marked as a misunderstanding rather than a slip, because interpreting the parts of a linear rule in context is the entire point of the question. State each with its unit: "R2, the fare before any distance" and "R3 per kilometre, the increase for each extra kilometre".
