### subtopic_1 — Flow Diagrams: Input, Rule, Output
**Assistant:** Why does swapping the two boxes change the answer? Both machines multiply by 3 and add 2.

**Tutor:** Because multiplication acts on whatever is present at that moment, and what is present depends on what has already happened. In "times 3, then plus 2", the 3 multiplies the bare input, and the 2 is added afterwards and untouched: 4 becomes 12 becomes 14. In "plus 2, then times 3", the 2 is added first, so the 3 multiplies both the input and the added 2: 4 becomes 6 becomes 18. The second machine has effectively added 6, not 2, because the 2 was tripled along with everything else. The formulae record this precisely. The first is 3x plus 2. The second is 3 times the quantity x plus 2, and by the distributive property that is 3x plus 6 — the tripled 2 is visible. This is why the bracket exists: it says "this happened first" and tells you that the multiplication reaches inside. When you read a flow diagram, read the boxes left to right and write the formula box by box; when you read a formula, the order of operations tells you which box came first — multiplication before addition unless a bracket intervenes. In marking, writing 3 times the quantity x plus 2 for the first machine, or 3x plus 2 for the second, is a conceptual error about order and costs the method mark, not merely the answer.

### subtopic_2 — Tables and Finding the Rule
**Assistant:** For inputs that jump, like 2, 5, 10, why do I divide the output difference by the input difference?

**Tutor:** Because the multiplier in a rule like y equals 3x plus 1 describes how much the output changes for each single unit of input, and a table with jumps does not give you single units directly. Between inputs 2 and 5 the output rose from 7 to 16, a rise of 9, but that rise was spread over 3 units of input, so the rise per unit is 9 over 3, which is 3. Between 5 and 10 the output rose 15 over 5 units, again 3 per unit. The agreement of the two rates is also your evidence that the rule is linear — a straight-line rule has the same rate everywhere, and if the two rates had differed the rule would involve a square or something else. Having the rate, the constant follows from any single row: 3 times 2 plus c equals 7 gives c equal to 1. A student who uses the raw difference 9 as the multiplier gets y equals 9x minus 11, which happens to fit the first two rows and fails the third, which is exactly why the test on a row you did not use is compulsory. In Grade 10 this rate per unit input is called the gradient, and the calculation you have just done — change in y over change in x — is the gradient formula.

### subtopic_3 — Formulae and Equations: Finding Inputs
**Assistant:** Why must I do the same thing to both sides when solving 3x plus 2 equals 32?

**Tutor:** Because the equals sign is a claim that two quantities are the same number, and a claim like that survives only if you treat both quantities identically. Think of the equation as a balanced scale: 3x plus 2 on the left weighs exactly 32 on the right. Remove 2 from the left alone and the scale tips; the two sides no longer describe the same number, and anything you conclude afterwards is about a different problem. Remove 2 from both sides and the balance is preserved: 3x equals 30, still true. Divide both sides by 3: x equals 10, still true, and now the input is visible. The flow-diagram view says the same thing in different words. The machine took x, multiplied by 3, added 2 and produced 32; to recover x you undo those operations in reverse order, and each undo is applied to the output 32 as well as to the expression, because you are tracking the same number backwards through the machine on both sides. The two pictures — the balance and the reversed machine — are the two justifications for the same procedure, and the check by substitution, 3 times 10 plus 2 is 32, confirms that the balance was never broken.

### subtopic_4 — Choosing a Representation and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** If the four representations show the same rule, why does the examination ask for all of them?

**Tutor:** Because each one makes a different mistake visible, and each one is the natural tool for a different question. A flow diagram makes the order of operations explicit, so it is the representation in which "plus 2 then times 3" and "times 3 then plus 2" cannot be confused; a student who can draw the diagram for 3x plus 2 correctly has understood the order. A table makes the rule's behaviour over several inputs visible, which is how you discover the rule and how you catch a rule that fits one row only; it is also where the input 0 reveals the constant. A formula is the general statement, compact enough to carry into any calculation, and it is the form Grade 10 will call a function. An equation is the formula with the output fixed, and solving it is the backward run that recovers an input. The examination asks you to translate between them because translation is the test of understanding: anyone can memorise 3x plus 2, but producing the table from it, the diagram from the table and the equation for a given output shows that you know what the symbols mean. In marking, each translation is a line and a mark, and the most frequently lost mark is the forward check after finding an input — one substitution that would have exposed a reversed order.
