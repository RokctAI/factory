### subtopic_1 — Counting Terms: Monomial, Binomial, Trinomial
**Assistant:** Why is 3xy one term when it clearly has three things in it?

**Tutor:** Because the three things are factors, not terms, and the distinction is the operation between them. A term is a piece of an expression that is added to or subtracted from the other pieces; within a term, the parts are multiplied or divided together. In 3xy the 3, the x and the y are joined by multiplication — it means 3 times x times y — and no plus or minus appears anywhere inside it, so it is a single quantity, one term. Substitute numbers and the point is clear: at x equals 2 and y equals 5, 3xy is 30, one number, whereas 3 plus x plus y would be 10, the sum of three separate contributions. The same reasoning says 6x over 3 is one term, and that 2x times the quantity x plus 1 is one term as written, because the bracket is being multiplied; only when you expand it to 2x squared plus 2x does an addition appear and the count become two. The reason the count matters is that each term can be handled separately when adding expressions, while factors cannot be separated without changing the value. In marking, counting factors as terms — calling 3xy a trinomial — is a vocabulary error that signals a deeper confusion about what addition and multiplication do, which is why the question is set.

### subtopic_2 — Classifying by Degree Too
**Assistant:** Why do we need the degree as well? Is the number of terms not enough to describe an expression?

**Tutor:** The two names answer different questions, and both are needed because expressions with the same number of terms can behave completely differently. 3x plus 2 and 3x squared plus 2 are both binomials, but one graphs as a straight line and the other as a curve, one is solved as a linear equation and the other as a quadratic, and in Term 2 one has a common factor at most while the other may be a difference of squares. The degree — the highest power present — is what separates them, so the full name is linear binomial against quadratic binomial. Conversely 7x squared, x squared minus 9 and 3x squared minus 5x plus 2 are all quadratics and all factorise differently, and the number of terms is what separates those: monomial, binomial, trinomial. Putting the two words together gives an index to the toolkit. Quadratic trinomial means "look for two brackets"; quadratic binomial means "check for a difference of two squares"; linear binomial means "take out the common factor and stop". A student who reads only one of the two names picks up the wrong tool half the time. One caution: keep the words in their lanes. 4x cubed has a 3 in it, but that 3 is the degree, and the expression is a cubic monomial, not a trinomial.

### subtopic_3 — Expressions That Are Not Polynomials
**Assistant:** Why is x over 2 allowed but 2 over x is not? They look like the same kind of fraction.

**Tutor:** They look alike and they are structurally different, and the difference is where the variable sits. A polynomial is a sum of terms in which each term is a number times variables raised to whole-number powers — 0, 1, 2, 3 and so on. x over 2 is one half times x, with the variable raised to the power 1; the 2 is merely part of the coefficient, which may be any number, fraction or not. So x over 2 is a linear monomial. 2 over x is 2 times x to the power negative 1, and negative 1 is not a whole number, so the term fails the definition; it is not a polynomial and does not receive the names monomial, binomial or trinomial. The same test disposes of roots: the square root of x is x to the power one half, fractional, so square root of x plus 1 is not a binomial, whereas the square root of 2 times x is a monomial because the root sits on the number. The reason for the restriction is practical. Polynomials can be added, multiplied and factorised with the methods of Term 2 and graphed as smooth curves without gaps; expressions with variables in denominators can be undefined — 2 over x has no value at x equals 0 — and need the separate treatment of algebraic fractions. When asked to classify such an expression, write "not a polynomial" and give the reason, because the reason is what is marked.

### subtopic_4 — Standard Form and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** If an expression has the same value in any order, why does the memorandum insist on descending powers?

**Tutor:** Because order carries information for the reader even though it does not change the value, and the memorandum is written for readers. With the terms in descending powers the degree is the first thing you see, the leading coefficient is at the left, and the constant is at the right; two polynomials written this way can be added by lining up like terms column by column, and a factorising question can be recognised by shape at a glance — 3x squared minus 5x plus 2 announces itself as a quadratic trinomial in a way that 2 minus 5x plus 3x squared does not. The convention also protects you from a particular slip. Reordering requires each term to carry its sign to its new place, and students who write 2 minus 5x plus 3x squared as 3x squared plus 5x plus 2 have created a different expression; the habit of writing standard form deliberately, sign by sign, catches that. There is a forward reason too: Grade 10 writes every quadratic as a x squared plus b x plus c and reads a, b and c off the standard form, so the order you practise now is the order the formula will assume later. The procedure for a classification question is therefore fixed: simplify, write in descending powers with signs intact, then read the number of terms and the degree from that final line.
