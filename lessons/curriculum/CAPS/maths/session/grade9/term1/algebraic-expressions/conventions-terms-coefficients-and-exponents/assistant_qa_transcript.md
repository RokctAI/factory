### subtopic_1 — The Conventions for Writing Expressions
**Assistant:** Why does algebra bother with so many writing rules? Would 3 × x not be clearer than 3x?

**Tutor:** The rules exist because algebra is written by millions of people and read by markers, textbooks and machines, and a shared convention means every reader sees the same expression without ambiguity. The multiplication sign is dropped partly for speed and partly for safety: the symbol for times looks like the letter x, and an expression such as 3 times x times y with explicit signs is a thicket of crosses that is easy to misread. Writing 3xy removes the problem. The number goes first so that the coefficient is found in one place, and the letters go in alphabetical order so that xy and yx are recognised at a glance as the same product — which matters when you are deciding whether two terms are like. Writing 1x as x and negative 1x as negative x keeps expressions short, at the price of remembering the invisible 1 when you count coefficients. None of these rules changes the mathematics; 3 times x and 3x are the same number for every x. What the rules change is whether your work can be read and marked correctly, and in an examination the expression you write is the expression that is marked. Learn the spelling now and the grammar of Term 2 — multiplying and factorising — rests on it.

### subtopic_2 — Terms, Coefficients and Constants
**Assistant:** Why does the coefficient of x in 4x^2 − 3x + 7 include the minus sign? The minus is between the terms.

**Tutor:** Because the minus is not between the terms; it belongs to the second term. An expression is a sum of terms, and subtracting 3x is the same as adding negative 3x, so the expression is really 4x squared plus negative 3x plus 7. Read that way, the three terms are 4x squared, negative 3x and 7, and the coefficient of x is the whole numerical factor of its term, which is negative 3. The reason this reading is the correct one shows up as soon as you rearrange or collect. If you move the terms into a different order and leave the minus behind — 4x squared plus 7 plus 3x — you have changed the expression; at x equals 1 the original is 8 and the rearranged version is 14. If each term carries its sign, any order gives the same value. The same discipline governs collecting like terms: in 7a minus 3b plus 2a plus b, the b coefficients are negative 3 and positive 1, which sum to negative 2, and a student who reads them as 3 and 1 gets 4b and loses the mark. So when a question asks for the coefficient of x, answer with the sign — negative 3 — and when it asks for the constant, answer 7, positive because its sign is plus.

### subtopic_3 — Exponents and Degree
**Assistant:** Why does 2x at x = 3 give 6 but x^2 gives 9? Both have a 2 in them.

**Tutor:** Because the 2 is doing a different job in each. In 2x the 2 is a coefficient: it counts how many x's are added together, so 2x is x plus x, and at x equals 3 that is 3 plus 3, which is 6. In x squared the 2 is an exponent: it counts how many x's are multiplied together, so x squared is x times x, and at x equals 3 that is 3 times 3, which is 9. The two operations diverge quickly — at x equals 10, 2x is 20 and x squared is 100 — which is why confusing them produces answers that are not slightly wrong but wildly wrong. In a term such as 4x squared both jobs appear at once: the exponent acts first, x squared is 9 at x equals 3, and then the coefficient counts four of those, 36. Order of operations fixes that sequence — powers before multiplication — and brackets would be needed to change it, as in 4x all squared, which is 16x squared. A habit that prevents the confusion is to read every term aloud with the right verb: "two x" is two x's added; "x squared" is x times x; "four x squared" is four lots of x times x. The vocabulary of degree follows from the exponent alone: 4x squared has degree 2 regardless of the 4, because degree measures how many variable factors are multiplied, not how many terms are counted.

### subtopic_4 — Like and Unlike Terms
**Assistant:** Why is 2xy like 5yx but 4a^2 b not like 4ab^2? They have the same letters.

**Tutor:** Like terms must have the same variable part as a product, and a product does not care about the order of its factors but does care about how many of each there are. In 2xy and 5yx the variable parts are xy and yx; by the commutative property of multiplication, x times y and y times x are the same product, so the two terms are like and sum to 7xy. In 4a squared b and 4ab squared the variable parts are a squared b and a b squared. The first is a times a times b — two a's and one b; the second is a times b times b — one a and two b's. Those are different products, as a check with numbers shows: at a equals 2 and b equals 3, a squared b is 12 and ab squared is 18. Same letters, different exponents on them, unlike terms, and they stay apart. The test to apply is therefore: list the letters, and next to each letter write its exponent; if the two lists match exactly, ignoring order, the terms are like. This is also why 3x and 3x squared are unlike — same letter, exponents 1 and 2 — and why constants are like each other, having no letters at all. Only when the lists match may you add the coefficients, and when you do, the list itself is untouched: 2x squared plus 3x squared is 5x squared, with the exponent exactly where it was.
