# Part 1 — Expert

This session recognises and differentiates between monomials, binomials and trinomials, and extends the naming to include degree. The anchor expressions are 3x squared minus 5x plus 2, a quadratic trinomial; x squared minus 9, a quadratic binomial; and 4x cubed, a cubic monomial. The governing rule is that terms are counted after the expression has been simplified, and that factors within a term are not terms. Both parts of the rule are examinable.

## Subtopic: Counting Terms: Monomial, Binomial, Trinomial

An algebraic expression is classified by its number of terms, where terms are the parts separated by plus and minus signs. One term is a monomial: 4x cubed, negative 7, 3xy, x over 2. Two terms is a binomial: x squared minus 9, 3x plus 2, a plus b. Three terms is a trinomial: 3x squared minus 5x plus 2, a squared plus 2ab plus b squared. Expressions with more terms are simply called polynomials, a word that also covers all three: "polynomial" means many terms, and a monomial is a polynomial with one term.

Factors are not terms. 3xy is a product of three factors — 3, x and y — but it is one term, because no plus or minus separates its parts. 2x times the quantity x plus 1 is written with a bracket and is a single product, one term, until it is expanded; after expansion it becomes 2x squared plus 2x, a binomial. Division does not create terms either: 6x over 3 is a single term, equal to 2x.

A bracket preceded by a sign is treated as its contents for counting. The quantity x plus 3 in brackets, with nothing multiplying it, is simply x plus 3, a binomial. The quantity x plus 3 in brackets, multiplied by 2, is one term until expanded and a binomial, 2x plus 6, afterwards. Classify the expression in the form the question presents it, and if the question says "simplify and classify", classify the simplified form.

The variables do not affect the count. x plus y plus z is a trinomial with three different variables and degree 1. x squared y plus xy squared is a binomial. 5 is a monomial — a constant monomial. Zero is also regarded as a monomial, though it rarely appears alone.

The anchor examples: 3x squared minus 5x plus 2 — three terms, trinomial. x squared minus 9 — two terms, binomial. 4x cubed — one term, monomial. 2x squared plus 3x plus 1 plus x — four terms as written, but 3x and x are like terms, and after simplifying to 2x squared plus 4x plus 1 it is a trinomial. The count is taken after simplifying.

The questions for this section are with you now: counting terms, factors against terms, and brackets that hide a count.

## Subtopic: Classifying by Degree Too

The degree of an expression is the highest degree among its terms, and the degree gives a second name. Degree 1 is linear: 3x plus 2, 5x, a minus b. Degree 2 is quadratic: 3x squared minus 5x plus 2, x squared minus 9, 7x squared. Degree 3 is cubic: 4x cubed, x cubed minus 8, 2x cubed plus x. Degree 0 is a constant: 7, negative one half. Combining both names gives the full classification. 3x squared minus 5x plus 2 is a quadratic trinomial. x squared minus 9 is a quadratic binomial. 4x cubed is a cubic monomial. 3x plus 2 is a linear binomial. 5x is a linear monomial. 7 is a constant monomial.

With more than one variable the degree of a term is the sum of its exponents. 3x squared y has degree 3, so x squared y plus xy squared is a cubic binomial, and 5ab is a quadratic monomial — degree 2 from one a and one b.

The leading term is the term of highest degree, and its coefficient is the leading coefficient. In 3x squared minus 5x plus 2 the leading term is 3x squared and the leading coefficient is 3. In 2 minus x cubed the leading term is negative x cubed, with leading coefficient negative 1, regardless of the fact that it is written last.

The two names answer two different questions and must not be confused. The number of terms says how many pieces are added; the degree says how high the powers go. A monomial can have any degree — 7, 5x, 7x squared, 4x cubed are monomials of degrees 0, 1, 2, 3. A quadratic can have any number of terms up to three with one variable — 7x squared, x squared minus 9, 3x squared minus 5x plus 2.

Why the names matter: Term 2 factorising is organised by them. A quadratic binomial of the form x squared minus 9 is a difference of two squares. A quadratic trinomial like 3x squared minus 5x plus 2 factorises into two linear binomials. A linear binomial like 3x plus 6 has a common factor and nothing more. Recognising the shape is the first step of every factorising question.

Take this section's questions now: linear, quadratic and cubic, the full two-word name, and the leading coefficient when the terms are out of order.

## Subtopic: Expressions That Are Not Polynomials

The names monomial, binomial and trinomial apply to polynomials, and a polynomial has a specific form: a sum of terms, each a number times variables raised to whole-number exponents — 0, 1, 2, 3 and so on. Any expression of that form is a polynomial and may be classified. Expressions outside it are not.

A variable in a denominator breaks the form. 2 over x is 2 times x to the power negative 1, and negative 1 is not a whole number, so 2 over x is not a polynomial and is not a monomial. By contrast x over 2 is one half times x, a perfectly good linear monomial — the number is in the denominator, not the variable. 3x over 2 is fine; 3 over 2x is not.

A variable under a root breaks the form. The square root of x is x to the power one half, a fractional exponent, so square root of x plus 1 is not a binomial. The square root of 2 times x, however, is a monomial — the root is on the number, and the coefficient is simply the irrational number root 2.

A negative exponent on a variable breaks the form, as 5x to the power negative 2 does; written as 5 over x squared the same objection applies. Negative exponents on numbers are fine: 2 to the power negative 1 times x is one half x.

Expressions such as 3 over x plus 2 are still legitimate algebra and will be simplified in Term 2 as algebraic fractions; they simply do not receive the polynomial names. If an examination question asks "classify the expression", the expected answer for 2 over x plus 1 is "not a polynomial, because a variable appears in a denominator", and the reason is marked.

A useful test: write every term as a coefficient times variables with exponents; if every exponent on every variable is a whole number, classify by terms and degree; if any is negative or fractional, it is not a polynomial.

This section's questions are available now: variables in denominators, roots on variables against roots on numbers, and the whole-number-exponent test.

## Subtopic: Standard Form and the Error Museum

Standard form writes a polynomial in one variable with the terms in descending powers: highest degree first, then the next, down to the constant. 2 minus 5x plus 3x squared becomes 3x squared minus 5x plus 2. x plus x cubed minus 4 becomes x cubed plus x minus 4. The standard form makes the degree and leading coefficient visible at the left, makes like terms easy to find when adding two polynomials, and is the form the memorandum expects.

Each term carries its sign into the new position. 2 minus 5x plus 3x squared has terms 2, negative 5x and 3x squared, and reordering them gives 3x squared, negative 5x, 2 — written 3x squared minus 5x plus 2. A sign left behind in the move is a new and wrong expression.

Simplifying and standardising together: 5x minus 2x squared plus 3 plus 4x squared minus x. Like terms: negative 2x squared plus 4x squared is 2x squared; 5x minus x is 4x; 3 stays. Standard form: 2x squared plus 4x plus 3 — a quadratic trinomial with leading coefficient 2. The classification is read only from this final line.

The error museum for this topic has five exhibits. One: counting terms before simplifying — calling 2x plus 3x a binomial, when it is the monomial 5x. Two: counting factors as terms — calling 3xy a trinomial. Three: calling 2 over x a monomial, when it is not a polynomial at all. Four: confusing the two names — calling 4x cubed a trinomial because of the 3, when the 3 is its degree and it has one term. Five: reordering without the signs — writing 2 minus 5x plus 3x squared as 3x squared plus 5x plus 2.

Layout for marks on a classification question: simplify if necessary, write the expression in standard form, state the number of terms with the name, state the degree with the name, and if the expression is not a polynomial, say so with the reason. Three short lines earn the full classification.

A final word on what the names are for. They are not labels for their own sake; they are the index of the Term 2 toolkit. Monomial times anything: distribute. Binomial times binomial: four products. Quadratic binomial x squared minus a squared: difference of squares. Quadratic trinomial: two brackets. Naming the shape correctly is choosing the right tool, and that is why the vocabulary earns marks now and saves marks later.

The questions for this section are ready: descending powers with signs intact, simplify then classify, and the five exhibits of the museum.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same names again through counting the pieces of a sandwich, tidying a room before you count what is in it, and a full name tag — plain words, same rules.

## Subtopic: Count the Pieces

An algebraic expression is a plate of food, and the plus and minus signs are the gaps between the pieces. Count the pieces. One piece is a monomial — "mono" means one, like a monorail. Two pieces is a binomial — "bi" like a bicycle. Three pieces is a trinomial — "tri" like a tricycle. 4x cubed is one piece. x squared minus 9 is two pieces. 3x squared minus 5x plus 2 is three pieces.

The trick is knowing what counts as a gap. Only plus and minus make gaps. Times does not — 3xy is one piece with three ingredients mashed together, not three pieces. Divide does not — 6x over 3 is one piece. A bracket with something multiplying it is one piece until you open it: 2 lots of "x plus 1" is one piece as written, and becomes 2x plus 2, two pieces, when you multiply it out.

Different letters are fine. x plus y plus z is three pieces. A plain number is one piece: 7 is a monomial, a boring one, but a monomial. Even a messy-looking piece like 3x squared y over 4 is still one piece, because there is no plus or minus inside it.

Say the number out loud as you count: "4x cubed... one. x squared, minus 9... two. 3x squared, minus 5x, plus 2... three." Monomial, binomial, trinomial. It is genuinely that simple — as long as you have tidied first, which is the next idea.

Keep the plate of food in mind and try the questions for this part: where the gaps are, what counts as one piece, and the names for one, two and three.

## Subtopic: Tidy First, Then Name

Here is the trap that catches almost everyone. 2x plus 3x looks like two pieces. But 2x and 3x are the same fruit — like terms — so they collapse into 5x, which is one piece. 2x plus 3x is a monomial in disguise. Always collect like terms BEFORE you count. It is like tidying a room before counting what is in it: two halves of the same book on the floor are one book.

Try 2x squared plus 3x plus 1 plus x. Four pieces as written. But 3x and x collect to 4x, so it is really 2x squared plus 4x plus 1 — three pieces, a trinomial. And 5x minus 2x squared plus 3 plus 4x squared minus x: collect the x squareds, negative 2 and plus 4 make 2x squared; collect the x's, 5 and negative 1 make 4x; the 3 stands alone. 2x squared plus 4x plus 3: three pieces, trinomial.

Once tidied, line the pieces up in order, biggest power first: x cubed before x squared before x before the plain number. 2 minus 5x plus 3x squared becomes 3x squared minus 5x plus 2. Every piece carries its own sign when it moves — the minus 5x stays minus 5x wherever it lands. This order is called standard form, and it is how the memorandum writes answers, so write yours the same way.

Tidying also catches the pretenders. 2 over x — a letter underneath a fraction bar — is not one of our pieces at all. Neither is the square root of x. Our names only apply to expressions built from letters with whole-number powers on top, times ordinary numbers. x over 2 is fine, because the letter is on top and the 2 is just a number. 2 over x is not fine, because the letter is underneath. If you see a letter in a denominator or under a root, the answer is "not a polynomial", and you say why.

Hold the tidy-first rule and try this part's questions: collecting before counting, biggest power first with its sign, and spotting the pretenders.

## Subtopic: The Full Name Tag

Every expression gets a two-word name tag, like "quadratic trinomial". The second word is the number of pieces, which you now know. The first word is the biggest power in it. Biggest power 1 is linear — a straight line if you graph it. Biggest power 2 is quadratic — "quad" from the square. Biggest power 3 is cubic — the cube. No letter at all, just a number, is constant.

So 3x squared minus 5x plus 2: biggest power 2, three pieces — quadratic trinomial. x squared minus 9: biggest power 2, two pieces — quadratic binomial. 4x cubed: biggest power 3, one piece — cubic monomial. 3x plus 2: linear binomial. 5x: linear monomial. 7: constant monomial.

Do not let the two words leak into each other. 4x cubed has a 3 in it, but that 3 is the power, not the number of pieces. One piece: monomial. Cubic monomial, not trinomial. And the biggest power is not always written first — in 2 minus x cubed the x cubed is hiding at the end, so the expression is cubic, and when you write it in standard form, negative x cubed plus 2, the minus comes with it.

Why bother with name tags? Because next term, the name tells you which tool to pick up. A quadratic binomial like x squared minus 9 is a "difference of two squares" and factorises one specific way. A quadratic trinomial factorises into two brackets. A linear binomial like 3x plus 6 just has a common factor to pull out. Read the name tag, pick the tool. Students who skip the naming spend Term 2 trying every tool on every expression.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Count the pieces between plus and minus signs; times and divide do not make gaps. Tidy first — collect like terms, then count. One, two, three pieces: monomial, binomial, trinomial. Biggest power one, two, three: linear, quadratic, cubic. Write in standard form, biggest power first, each piece carrying its sign. A letter underneath a fraction or under a root means it is not a polynomial at all.

Hold the plate of food, the tidy room and the name tag, and take the final questions of the lesson: the two-word name, the leading piece, and why the names will matter next term.
