Today we revise the grammar of algebra — the conventions for writing expressions, the names of their parts, and the rule for which terms may be combined. 3 times x is written 3x; x times x is x squared; x divided by 2 is x over 2. In 4x squared minus 3x plus 7 there are three terms, the coefficients are 4 and negative 3, the constant is 7, and the sign travels with its term. Like terms share the same letters with the same exponents, and only like terms may be added: 3x plus 5x is 8x, but 3x plus 5x squared stays as it is. These conventions are not optional style; they are the shared language in which every algebra mark for the next four years is awarded, and getting them precise now makes Term 2 — multiplying, factorising and simplifying — a matter of applying rules you already trust.
