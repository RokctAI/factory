# Part 1 — Expert

This session revises the structural properties of the integers — commutative, associative and distributive — together with the identities and inverses for addition and multiplication. The anchor calculations are 7 times 98, evaluated without a calculator, and negative 7 plus 15 plus 7, regrouped to be instant. Every property has a precise statement, a test case where it fails, and a strategic use, and all three are examinable.

## Subtopic: The Commutative and Associative Properties

The commutative property says that order does not matter. For addition: a plus b equals b plus a. For multiplication: a times b equals b times a. Negative 7 plus 15 is 8, and 15 plus negative 7 is 8. Negative 6 times 4 is negative 24, and 4 times negative 6 is negative 24.

Subtraction and division are NOT commutative. 5 minus 3 is 2, but 3 minus 5 is negative 2. 12 divided by 4 is 3, but 4 divided by 12 is one third. One counter-example is sufficient to kill a property, and the examiner will ask you to supply it.

The associative property says that grouping does not matter when the operation is the same throughout. For addition: the quantity a plus b, plus c, equals a plus the quantity b plus c. For multiplication: the quantity a times b, times c, equals a times the quantity b times c. Check: the quantity 2 plus 3, plus 4, is 5 plus 4, which is 9; and 2 plus the quantity 3 plus 4 is 2 plus 7, which is 9.

Again, subtraction and division fail. The quantity 8 minus 3, minus 2, is 5 minus 2, which is 3. But 8 minus the quantity 3 minus 2 is 8 minus 1, which is 7. Different answers, so subtraction is not associative. For division: the quantity 24 divided by 6, divided by 2, is 4 divided by 2, which is 2; but 24 divided by the quantity 6 divided by 2 is 24 divided by 3, which is 8.

The strategic use: regroup to create zeros and tens. Negative 7 plus 15 plus 7: commute to put negative 7 next to 7, associate them, and they sum to zero, leaving 15. Negative 2 times 17 times negative 5: pair negative 2 with negative 5 to get 10, then 10 times 17 is 170. You are allowed to do this only because both properties hold for addition and for multiplication.

Take the questions for this section now: the two properties stated precisely, the counter-examples for subtraction and division, and the regrouping that creates zeros and tens.

## Subtopic: The Distributive Property

The distributive property links multiplication to addition: a times the quantity b plus c equals a times b plus a times c. The multiplier outside the bracket is distributed to every term inside. It also runs over subtraction: a times the quantity b minus c equals a times b minus a times c.

Use it to compute 7 times 98 without a calculator. Write 98 as 100 minus 2. Then 7 times the quantity 100 minus 2 is 7 times 100 minus 7 times 2, which is 700 minus 14, which is 686. Check by long multiplication if you wish; the distributive route is faster and leaves a visible method.

With negatives, the sign rules travel with the terms. Negative 3 times the quantity 4 minus 9: distribute to get negative 3 times 4, which is negative 12, and negative 3 times negative 9, which is positive 27. The result is negative 12 plus 27, which is 15. Confirm by working the bracket first: 4 minus 9 is negative 5, and negative 3 times negative 5 is 15. Both routes agree, as they must.

The property also runs backwards, which is called factorising. 6 times 23 plus 6 times 7: the common factor 6 comes out, leaving 6 times the quantity 23 plus 7, which is 6 times 30, which is 180. Collecting a common factor is the distributive property read from right to left, and it is the first factorising method of Grade 9 algebra.

The bridge to algebra is immediate. 3 times the quantity x plus 4 equals 3x plus 12 for exactly the same reason that 3 times the quantity 10 plus 4 equals 30 plus 12. And the negative sign in front of a bracket is a multiplier of negative 1: negative of the quantity 5 minus 9 is negative 5 plus 9, which is 4 — every sign inside flips.

This section's questions are with you now: distributing over a sum and a difference, the sign rules inside the distribution, and factorising as the property run backwards.

## Subtopic: Identities and Inverses

An identity element leaves a number unchanged. Zero is the additive identity: a plus 0 equals a. One is the multiplicative identity: a times 1 equals a. Negative 8 plus 0 is negative 8; negative 8 times 1 is negative 8.

An inverse undoes a number back to the identity. The additive inverse of a is negative a, because a plus negative a equals 0. The additive inverse of 8 is negative 8; the additive inverse of negative 5 is 5; the additive inverse of 0 is 0 itself. Every integer has an additive inverse, and it is the same number with the opposite sign.

The multiplicative inverse of a is 1 over a — the reciprocal — because a times 1 over a equals 1. The multiplicative inverse of 4 is one quarter: 4 times one quarter is 1. The multiplicative inverse of negative 2 thirds is negative 3 halves: the signs multiply to positive, and two thirds times three halves is one. Flip the fraction, keep the sign. Zero has NO multiplicative inverse, because nothing multiplied by zero gives 1 — this is the same fact as "division by zero is undefined".

Inverses are the engine of equation solving. To solve x plus 7 equals 3, add the additive inverse of 7 to both sides: x plus 7 plus negative 7 equals 3 plus negative 7, so x equals negative 4. To solve negative 5x equals 35, multiply both sides by the multiplicative inverse of negative 5, which is negative one fifth: x equals 35 times negative one fifth, which is negative 7. Check: negative 5 times negative 7 is 35.

Notice that subtraction and division do not need to exist as separate operations: subtracting 7 is adding negative 7, and dividing by negative 5 is multiplying by negative one fifth. That is why the commutative and associative properties can always be used — rewrite in terms of addition and multiplication first.

Turn to the questions for this section: the two identities, how to write each inverse, the number with no multiplicative inverse, and inverses as the solving step.

## Subtopic: Using the Properties Strategically

Mental arithmetic is the properties applied deliberately. 25 times 17 times 4: commute and associate to pair 25 with 4, giving 100, then 100 times 17 is 1 700. 48 plus negative 19 plus 52 plus 19: pair negative 19 with 19 to get 0, pair 48 with 52 to get 100, total 100. 4 times negative 7 plus 4 times negative 3: factor out the 4 to get 4 times negative 10, which is negative 40. 12 times 11: distribute as 12 times 10 plus 12 times 1, which is 120 plus 12, which is 132.

In an examination, "name the property used" is a direct question. Rewriting 5 plus negative 8 as negative 8 plus 5 is the commutative property of addition. Rewriting the quantity 3 times 4, times 25, as 3 times the quantity 4 times 25, is the associative property of multiplication. Rewriting 6 times the quantity 20 plus 3 as 120 plus 18 is the distributive property. Rewriting negative 9 plus 9 as 0 uses the additive inverse. Match the words to the shape of the rewrite.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: treating subtraction as commutative — writing 3 minus 8 as 8 minus 3 and losing a sign. Two: distributing over multiplication — 2 times the quantity 3 times 4 is 24, not 2 times 3 times 2 times 4, which is 48; the distributive property runs over addition and subtraction only. Three: dropping the second product — writing 7 times the quantity 100 minus 2 as 700 minus 2. Four: forgetting that a leading minus sign flips every term in the bracket — negative of the quantity 5 minus 9 is negative 5 PLUS 9, which is 4, not negative 5 minus 9.

Why this matters beyond Grade 9: every algebraic manipulation you will ever perform is one of these properties applied to letters instead of numbers. Collecting like terms is the distributive property backwards. Expanding brackets is the distributive property forwards. Moving a term across an equals sign is adding an additive inverse to both sides. Learn the names now and the algebra later has reasons, not just rules.

The closing questions of this part are in front of you now: the mental calculations, naming the property from the shape of a rewrite, the four exhibits, and the bridge to algebra.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same properties again from a shopping trolley, a taxi full of passengers and a row of undo buttons — ordinary words, same rules.

## Subtopic: Swap It, Group It — The Rules That Let You Rearrange

Picture a trolley at the till. Bread R17, milk R34, eggs R49. The cashier can scan them in any order and the total is the same: 17 plus 34 plus 49 is 100, and 49 plus 17 plus 34 is 100. That freedom to swap the order is called the commutative property, and it works for adding and for multiplying. Grouping is free too: scan bread and milk together, then eggs; or bread, then milk and eggs together — same total. That is the associative property.

Now the catch. Not everything swaps. Your bank balance is R5 and you spend R3: 5 minus 3 is R2 left. Swap it — 3 minus 5 — and you have negative R2, overdrawn. Subtraction does not swap. Sharing R12 among 4 people gives R3 each; sharing R4 among 12 people gives 33 cents each. Division does not swap either. Only adding and multiplying give you the freedom.

Here is the payoff. Negative 7 plus 15 plus 7 looks like three steps. But you may swap and regroup, so put the negative 7 next to the 7: they cancel to zero, and the answer is 15 in one glance. Negative 2 times 17 times negative 5: pair the negative 2 and the negative 5 — two minuses, so positive 10 — and 10 times 17 is 170. You are hunting for pairs that make zero or make ten, and the two properties are your permission slip.

One trick for subtraction: turn it into adding a negative first. 48 minus 19 plus 52 is 48 plus negative 19 plus 52, and now everything is addition, so you may swap freely: 48 plus 52 is 100, and 100 plus negative 19 is 81.

Your questions for this section are with you now: which operations swap and group, the counter-examples that prove subtraction and division do not, and the hunt for zeros and tens.

## Subtopic: Sharing Out the Multiplication

Seven friends each want a packet of 98 sweets. How many sweets? Nobody wants to multiply 7 by 98 directly. But 98 is 100 minus 2, so each friend gets 100 sweets minus 2 sweets. Seven friends: 7 times 100 is 700, minus 7 times 2 is 14, so 686 sweets. The 7 was shared out — distributed — to both parts of the bracket. That is the distributive property: a number outside a bracket multiplies EVERY term inside.

It works with negatives too, as long as the sign rules ride along. Negative 3 times the quantity 4 minus 9: negative 3 times 4 is negative 12, and negative 3 times negative 9 is positive 27, so negative 12 plus 27, which is 15. Check the lazy way — 4 minus 9 is negative 5, and negative 3 times negative 5 is 15. Same answer, as it must be.

Run it backwards and you have a different superpower. You buy 6 of one item at R23 and 6 of another at R7. Instead of 6 times 23 plus 6 times 7, notice the 6 is common: it is 6 times the quantity 23 plus 7, which is 6 times 30, which is R180. Pulling the shared number out of two products is called factorising, and it is the distributive property read from right to left.

Two warnings. A minus sign in front of a bracket is a secret negative one: negative of the quantity 5 minus 9 is negative 5 plus 9, which is 4 — every sign inside flips. And the property only shares over plus and minus, not over times: 2 times the quantity 3 times 4 is 24, and you do NOT share the 2 to both the 3 and the 4.

Your questions on this section are ready: sharing out to every term, the signs riding along, and pulling a common number back out.

## Subtopic: Undo Buttons — Zero, One and the Inverses

Some numbers do nothing. Add zero to anything and it stays the same — zero is the do-nothing number for adding. Multiply anything by one and it stays the same — one is the do-nothing number for multiplying. Those are called the identities.

Now the undo buttons. If you add 8, what undoes it? Adding negative 8: 8 plus negative 8 is 0, back to doing nothing. Negative 8 is the additive inverse of 8, and 5 is the additive inverse of negative 5. Every integer has one: same size, opposite sign.

If you multiply by 4, what undoes it? Multiplying by a quarter: 4 times a quarter is 1, back to doing nothing. A quarter is the multiplicative inverse of 4 — flip the number upside down. For negative two thirds, the undo button is negative three halves: flip it and keep the sign, because negative times negative is positive and two thirds times three halves is one. Zero has no undo button for multiplying — nothing times zero gets you back to 1 — which is the same reason you can never divide by zero.

Why do you care? Because solving an equation is pressing undo buttons. x plus 7 equals 3. Something was added; undo it by adding negative 7 to BOTH sides: x equals 3 plus negative 7, which is negative 4. Negative 5 times x equals 35. Something was multiplied; undo it by multiplying both sides by negative one fifth: x equals 35 times negative one fifth, which is negative 7. Check it: negative 5 times negative 7 is 35. Every equation you solve this year is this picture.

And here is the quiet bonus: subtraction and division are not really separate operations. Taking away 7 is adding negative 7; dividing by negative 5 is multiplying by negative one fifth. Rewrite them that way and suddenly the swap-and-group freedom applies to everything.

Hold the pictures: the trolley for swapping and grouping, the sweets for sharing out, and the undo buttons for inverses. The final questions of the lesson are with you now: the do-nothing numbers, the undo button for each operation, and the number that has none.
