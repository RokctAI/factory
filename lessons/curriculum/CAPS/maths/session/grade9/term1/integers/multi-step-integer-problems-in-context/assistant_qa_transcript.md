### subtopic_1 — Translating a Context Into Signs
**Assistant:** Why is "difference between" always positive but "change from A to B" can be negative?

**Tutor:** Because the two phrases ask different questions. "Difference between" asks how far apart two values are, and distance has no direction — the gap between Johannesburg and the Dead Sea shore is 2 183 metres whichever one you stand in. So you subtract the lower value from the higher, and the result is positive by construction. "Change from A to B" asks what happened as you moved from A to B, and movement does have a direction: a change from 9 degrees to 1 degree is negative 8 degrees, a fall, while a change from 1 to 9 is positive 8, a rise. So the order is fixed — final minus initial — and the sign is the information. The examiner uses both phrasings deliberately, and the test is whether you honour the sign when it is wanted and discard it when it is not. A reliable habit is to ask, before subtracting, whether the question wants a gap or a direction. Gap: higher minus lower, write a positive number. Direction: end minus start, keep whatever sign appears, and say what it means in words — "a drop of 8 degrees".

### subtopic_2 — Multi-Step Money Problems
**Assistant:** Why insist on writing one long expression when a running balance gets the same answer?

**Tutor:** The running balance is how you think; the single expression is how you communicate, and in an examination communication is what is marked. A marker reading negative 250 plus 1 200 minus 3 times 180 minus 45 can see at a glance that you identified the starting sign, the deposit, the repeated event and the fee, and can award the method mark even if a slip occurs in the arithmetic afterwards. A column of running totals shows only the numbers, and one wrong line hides the reasoning behind it. The expression also protects you from the most common error in this topic: a repeated event counted once. When the three debit orders appear as 3 times 180 in the line, the multiplication is visible and deliberate; when you simply write 950 minus 180, the other two orders have silently vanished. My recommendation is to use both: build the running balance in the margin to keep your bearings, then write the single expression as the first line of your answer and evaluate it in the standard order. The two methods must agree, which gives you a free check as well.

### subtopic_3 — Temperature, Height and Scores
**Assistant:** Can a mean really be negative, and does it mean the average day was below freezing?

**Tutor:** Yes, and yes — a mean is a signed number like any other, and its sign tells you which side of zero the data leaned towards. If three night readings are negative 4, negative 1 and negative 7, their sum is negative 12 and the mean is negative 4 degrees; the typical reading was four degrees below freezing. When the data straddles zero, the signs fight it out in the sum before the division: negative 4, negative 1, 3, 6 and 1 add to 5, so the mean of 1 degree says the warm readings slightly outweighed the cold ones. The mechanical point is that the sum must be formed with the integer rules — same signs add and keep, different signs subtract and take the larger's sign — and only then divided by the count. A frequent error is to average the sizes and then guess the sign, which fails as soon as the data is mixed. Treat the mean as an integer calculation first and a statistic second, and the sign will look after itself.

### subtopic_4 — Estimating, Checking and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** How does estimating the sign first actually help if I still have to do the full calculation?

**Tutor:** It converts an invisible error into a visible one. Multi-step integer problems fail quietly — a dropped minus, a repeated event counted once — and the wrong answer looks just as plausible as the right one unless you have something to compare it with. The sign estimate gives you that comparison for free. In the account problem, adding everything that leaves the account — 250 of overdraft, 540 of debit orders, 45 of fees — gives 835, which is clearly less than the 1 200 coming in, so the month must end positive. If your detailed work then produces negative 365, you know immediately that a sign was lost, probably at the start, and you know to look there rather than re-checking every line at random. The estimate costs one addition and takes seconds in the margin. The reverse walk is the second net: starting from your answer and undoing each event should land exactly on the opening value, and if it does not, the mismatch tells you the size of the slip. Together they turn a topic that feels like luck into one you can verify.
