### subtopic_1 — Adding and Subtracting Integers
**Assistant:** Why do we rewrite every subtraction as an addition — isn't that an extra step?

**Tutor:** It is one extra step that removes four separate rules. Subtraction with integers has four sign cases — positive minus positive, positive minus negative, negative minus positive, negative minus negative — and learners who try to memorise all four confuse them under pressure. Rewriting "minus a number" as "plus the opposite of that number" collapses all four into addition, which has only two rules: same signs add the sizes and keep the sign, different signs subtract the sizes and take the larger's sign. So negative 4 minus negative 6 becomes negative 4 plus 6, different signs, sizes differ by 2, the 6 is larger, answer positive 2. The rewrite is also what the examiner's memorandum shows, so the line earns a method mark rather than costing time. After enough practice the step happens in your head, but write it down until it does; the error rate on written rewrites is a fraction of the error rate on mental ones. The temperature picture confirms the logic: a rise from negative 8 to 7 is 7 minus negative 8, and nobody doubts the thermometer climbed 15 degrees.

### subtopic_2 — Multiplying and Dividing — The Sign Rules
**Assistant:** Does the count-the-negatives rule also work when there is division in the chain?

**Tutor:** Yes, because division obeys exactly the same sign rule as multiplication — same signs positive, different signs negative — so a division is, for sign purposes, just another factor. In a chain such as negative 12 divided by negative 4, times negative 3, count the negatives: three, an odd number, so the result is negative; the sizes give 12 over 4 is 3, times 3 is 9; the answer is negative 9. What the rule does NOT cover is addition and subtraction mixed into the chain — negative 2 plus negative 3 is not "two negatives, so positive". Counting negatives is a rule for products and quotients only, which is why the order of operations matters so much: you must separate the multiplying and dividing parts, settle their signs by counting, and only then combine the results by adding and subtracting with the number-line rules. One more caution: a negative base raised to a power is a product of that many copies, so negative 3 cubed has three negative factors, odd, hence negative 27 — the count rule explains the cube's sign as well.

### subtopic_3 — Squares, Cubes and Their Roots
**Assistant:** If both 5 and negative 5 square to 25, why is the square root of 25 only 5?

**Tutor:** Because mathematics needs the root symbol to name ONE number, otherwise it could not be used inside a calculation. If the square root of 25 meant "5 or negative 5", then an expression like 3 plus the square root of 25 would have two values and no calculation involving a root could ever be finished. So the symbol is defined to mean the non-negative root — the principal square root — and the square root of 25 is 5, full stop. When a problem genuinely needs both numbers that square to 25, you write them explicitly as plus or minus the square root of 25, and that is exactly what happens in Grade 10 when you solve x squared equals 25 and write x equals plus or minus 5. The equation has two solutions; the root symbol still names only one number. Keep the two ideas apart: the equation question "what squares to 25" has two answers, 5 and negative 5; the symbol question "what is the square root of 25" has one answer, 5. Cube roots avoid the issue entirely, since only one real number cubes to any given value — negative 3 is the only real cube root of negative 27.

### subtopic_4 — Order of Operations with Integers
**Assistant:** Why does the order of operations put multiplication before addition? Who decided?

**Tutor:** It is a convention, but not an arbitrary one; it is the convention that makes written mathematics shortest and least ambiguous. Multiplication is repeated addition, so an expression like 5 plus 2 times 9 is naturally read as five plus two nines — the "two nines" is one quantity, 18, and then you add. If addition were done first, every product in a sum would need its own bracket, and algebra like 3x plus 2y would have to be written with brackets around each term. Putting powers above multiplication follows the same logic one level up: a power is repeated multiplication, so negative 3 squared is a single quantity, 9, before anything is multiplied by it. Brackets then exist precisely to override the default when you need the other reading — the quantity negative 3 plus 4, times negative 2, is negative 2 because the bracket says to add first. So the order is a grammar, agreed worldwide, and calculators are built to obey it. The practical consequence is that you must read an expression the way the grammar reads it, not left to right like a sentence; the anchor expression evaluates to negative 15 only because 2 times 9 is settled before the 5 is touched.
