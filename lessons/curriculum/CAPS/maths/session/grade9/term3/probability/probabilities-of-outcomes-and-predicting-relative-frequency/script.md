# Part 1 — Expert

Probability has two faces, and this lesson puts them side by side. The first face is calculated before anything happens: a fair die has six equally likely faces, so the probability of a 6 is 1 over 6. The second face is counted after something has happened: roll the die 60 times, count the sixes, and divide by 60 to get the relative frequency. The CAPS instruction for today joins the two: determine the probabilities of outcomes, and use them to predict the relative frequency, and the actual frequency, in simple experiments. We work with dice, coins, spinners and bags of marbles, write probabilities as fractions, decimals and percentages on the scale from 0 to 1, build a relative frequency table from a real experiment, and predict how many times an outcome should occur in a given number of trials, including the prize budget for a spinner game at a school fun day. The next lesson compares the two faces and explains why they rarely match exactly.

## Subtopic: Probability of Equally Likely Outcomes

An experiment is any action with an uncertain result: rolling a die, tossing a coin, spinning a spinner, drawing a marble from a bag. Each possible result is an outcome, and the full list of outcomes is the sample space. An event is the set of outcomes we are interested in, such as rolling an even number. When all the outcomes are equally likely, the probability of an event is the number of favourable outcomes divided by the total number of outcomes.

A fair die has six equally likely outcomes, 1 to 6. The probability of a 6 is 1 over 6. The probability of an even number counts three favourable outcomes, 2, 4 and 6, so it is 3 over 6, which simplifies to 1 over 2. The probability of a number less than 3 counts 1 and 2, so it is 2 over 6, which is 1 over 3. The probability of a 7 is 0 over 6, which is 0, because a 7 is impossible. The probability of a number less than 7 is 6 over 6, which is 1, because it is certain.

Every probability lies on the scale from 0 to 1. Zero means impossible, one half means as likely as not, one means certain, and the words unlikely and likely cover the stretches between. A probability may be written as a fraction, a decimal or a percentage: 1 over 4, 0.25 and 25 percent are the same probability. A probability greater than 1 or less than 0 is always an error.

A bag holds 5 red, 3 blue and 2 yellow marbles, 10 in total, and one is drawn without looking. Each marble is equally likely, so the probability of red is 5 over 10, which is 1 over 2; of blue, 3 over 10; of yellow, 2 over 10, which is 1 over 5. Add the three: 5 plus 3 plus 2 is 10, over 10, which is 1. The probabilities of all the outcomes of an experiment always add to 1, and that is a built-in check on every calculation.

The complement of an event is everything that is not the event, and its probability is 1 minus the probability of the event. The probability of not blue is 1 minus 3 over 10, which is 7 over 10, the same as counting the 5 red and 2 yellow directly. The probability of not rolling a 6 is 1 minus 1 over 6, which is 5 over 6.

Equally likely is a condition, not a habit. The letters of the word MATHEMATICS are written on 11 cards; the probability of drawing an M is 2 over 11, because there are two Ms, and the probability of drawing a vowel is 4 over 11, counting A, A, E and I. A spinner that is one quarter red and three quarters blue has two colours but they are not equally likely, so the probability of red is 1 over 4, not 1 over 2. The questions for this section are with you now: the die events, the bag of 10 marbles, the complement, and the spinner whose two colours are not equally likely.

## Subtopic: Relative Frequency from an Experiment

The second face of probability comes from doing the experiment. Each repetition is a trial. The frequency of an outcome is the number of trials in which it happened, and the relative frequency is that frequency divided by the total number of trials. Relative frequency is also called experimental probability, and it is written, like probability, as a fraction, a decimal or a percentage.

A die was rolled 60 times and the results were recorded with tally marks in a table with four columns: outcome, tally, frequency and relative frequency. The frequencies were: 1 came up 8 times, 2 came up 11 times, 3 came up 9 times, 4 came up 12 times, 5 came up 10 times and 6 came up 10 times. The first check is that the frequencies add to the number of trials: 8 plus 11 plus 9 plus 12 plus 10 plus 10 is 60, as it should be.

The relative frequency of a 4 is 12 over 60, which is 1 over 5, which is 0.2, or 20 percent. The relative frequency of a 6 is 10 over 60, which is 1 over 6, about 0.167. The relative frequency of an even number adds the frequencies of 2, 4 and 6, which is 11 plus 12 plus 10, which is 33, over 60, which is 0.55, or 55 percent. The relative frequencies of all six outcomes add to 1, because the frequencies add to the number of trials, and that is the same built-in check as before.

A coin was tossed 50 times and showed heads 27 times. The relative frequency of heads is 27 over 50, which is 0.54, or 54 percent, and of tails 23 over 50, which is 0.46. Notice the denominator: it is always the number of trials, 50, never the number of possible outcomes, 2.

Relative frequency is how probability is found when no calculation is possible. A drawing pin dropped on a desk can land point up or point down, but the two are not equally likely and no symmetry argument tells us the probability. A learner who drops it 100 times and sees point up 62 times has a relative frequency of 0.62, which is the best estimate available. A netball player who scores 18 of her 25 shots has a relative frequency of 18 over 25, which is 0.72, and a coach uses that number to judge who takes the next penalty shot. Your questions on this section are ready: frequency against relative frequency, the 60 die rolls, the 50 coin tosses, and why the denominator is the number of trials.

## Subtopic: Predicting Frequency: Probability Times Trials

Probability makes a prediction about experiments that have not happened yet. If an outcome has probability P and the experiment is repeated n times, the expected frequency is P times n, and the predicted relative frequency is P itself.

A fair die is rolled 120 times. The probability of a 6 is 1 over 6, so the expected number of sixes is 1 over 6 times 120, which is 20. The expected number of even numbers is 1 over 2 times 120, which is 60. The expected number of rolls that are not a 6 is 5 over 6 times 120, which is 100. The predicted relative frequency of a 6 is 1 over 6, about 0.167, or 16.7 percent, whatever the number of rolls.

The bag of 5 red, 3 blue and 2 yellow marbles is used for 50 draws, and each marble is replaced and the bag shaken before the next draw, so the bag is the same for every trial. Expected reds: 1 over 2 times 50, which is 25. Expected blues: 3 over 10 times 50, which is 15. Expected yellows: 1 over 5 times 50, which is 10. The three expected frequencies add to 50, the number of trials.

A spinner has 8 equal sectors numbered 1 to 8. The probability of a number greater than 5 is 3 over 8, counting 6, 7 and 8. In 200 spins the expected number of results greater than 5 is 3 over 8 times 200, which is 75.

Predictions like these plan real events. A Grade 9 class runs a spinner game at the school fun day. The spinner has 8 equal sectors and one of them wins a prize. If 240 learners each play once, the expected number of prizes is 1 over 8 times 240, which is 30. At R15 a prize, the class budgets 30 times 15, which is R450 for prizes. A careful class buys a few extra, because 30 is what to expect on average, not a promise; the actual number of winners could easily be 26 or 35.

That last point is the heart of the prediction. The expected frequency is the most likely neighbourhood for the result, not the exact result. Rolling a die 120 times does not guarantee 20 sixes; 17 or 24 sixes would be perfectly ordinary. A weather forecast of a 30 percent chance of rain on each of 10 days predicts about 3 rainy days, and nobody is surprised by 2 or 4. The prediction is always stated with the word about. The questions for this section are ready: the 120 die rolls, the 50 draws with replacement, the spinner in 200 spins, the fun-day prize budget, and why about belongs in every prediction.

## Subtopic: Running a Fair Experiment and the Error Museum

An experiment produces useful relative frequencies only if it is run fairly, and the conditions are part of the method that a report must state. Every trial must be carried out in the same way: the die thrown from the same height onto the same surface, the spinner flicked firmly enough to turn several times, the marble drawn without looking. Trials must not influence each other: a drawn marble is replaced and the bag shaken before the next draw, otherwise the bag changes and the probabilities change with it. The number of trials must be stated and should be reasonably large, at least 50 for a die experiment, because a handful of trials gives relative frequencies that jump about. Results are recorded as they happen, with tally marks, never reconstructed from memory afterwards.

The relative frequency table is the standard record. Its columns are outcome, tally, frequency and relative frequency, with a final row of totals: the frequencies total the number of trials and the relative frequencies total 1. A second table, or two extra columns, can set the theoretical probability and the expected frequency beside each result, ready for the comparison of the next lesson. For the 60 die rolls the expected frequency of each face is 1 over 6 times 60, which is 10, and the observed frequencies of 8, 11, 9, 12, 10 and 10 sit around that value.

The error museum, five exhibits. Exhibit one: a probability greater than 1, such as writing 6 over 1 for the chance of a 6, or a relative frequency of 1.2; any probability outside 0 to 1 is wrong at sight. Exhibit two: treating unequal outcomes as equally likely, such as saying a pass or a fail are two outcomes so each has probability one half, or giving one half for red on a spinner that is one quarter red. Exhibit three: dividing by the number of possible outcomes instead of the number of trials, writing the relative frequency of heads as 27 over 2. Exhibit four: treating the expected frequency as a guarantee, such as claiming that 120 rolls will give exactly 20 sixes, or that a die which has not shown a 6 for ten rolls is now due one. Exhibit five: drawing marbles without replacing them in an experiment meant to have the same probabilities every time.

Layout for marks. For a calculated probability: list or count the sample space, state that the outcomes are equally likely, count the favourable outcomes, write the fraction and simplify it. For a relative frequency: state the frequency and the number of trials, write the fraction, and convert to a decimal or percentage if asked. For a prediction: write the probability, multiply by the number of trials, and state the result with the word about. In every case, check that the probabilities or relative frequencies of all outcomes add to 1. The questions for this section are with you now: the conditions for a fair experiment, the totals row, and the five exhibits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through counting the ways that win, tallying what really happened, and multiplying the chance by the number of tries — plain words, same rules.

## Subtopic: Count the Ways That Win

Before you do anything, you can work out the chance. Count all the ways the thing can turn out, count the ways you want, and make a fraction: ways you want over all the ways. That only works when every way is equally likely, like the faces of a fair die.

A die has six faces. The chance of a 6 is 1 out of 6. The chance of an even number is 2, 4 or 6, three out of six, which is a half. The chance of less than 3 is 1 or 2, two out of six, which is a third. A 7 cannot happen at all, so its chance is 0. Less than 7 always happens, so its chance is 1. Every chance lives between 0 and 1. If your answer is bigger than 1, something has gone wrong, every time.

A bag has 5 red, 3 blue and 2 yellow marbles, ten altogether. Red is 5 out of 10, a half. Blue is 3 out of 10. Yellow is 2 out of 10, a fifth. Add them up and you get 10 out of 10, which is 1. That is your check: all the chances together always make 1. And the chance of not blue is just 1 take away 3 tenths, which is 7 tenths.

Careful with spinners. A spinner that is one quarter red and three quarters blue has two colours, but red is not one of two equal ways; it covers a quarter of the circle, so its chance is a quarter. Equal ways only when the pieces are equal. Your questions on this section are ready: ways you want over all the ways, the bag of ten, the not-blue trick, and the lopsided spinner.

## Subtopic: Tally What Happened

Now actually do it. Roll the die 60 times and make a tally mark every time. Count the marks for each face: that count is the frequency. Divide the frequency by the number of rolls: that is the relative frequency, the fraction of the time it really happened.

One class got 8 ones, 11 twos, 9 threes, 12 fours, 10 fives and 10 sixes. First check: they add up to 60, the number of rolls. Fours came up 12 out of 60 times, which is a fifth, or 20 percent. Sixes came up 10 out of 60, which is a sixth. Even numbers came up 11 plus 12 plus 10 times, 33 out of 60, which is 55 percent.

A coin tossed 50 times showed heads 27 times: 27 out of 50, which is 54 percent. Always divide by the number of tries, 50, never by the number of sides, 2.

Tallying is how you find chances you cannot work out on paper. Drop a drawing pin 100 times; if it lands point up 62 times, your best guess for the chance of point up is 62 out of 100. A netball player who scores 18 of 25 shots has a scoring fraction of 72 percent, and the coach can use that. The questions for this section: frequency is the count, relative frequency is the count over the tries, the 60 rolls, and the drawing pin.

## Subtopic: Chance Times Tries

The chance also tells you what to expect. Multiply the chance by the number of tries. A die rolled 120 times: the chance of a 6 is a sixth, and a sixth of 120 is 20, so expect about 20 sixes. Evens: half of 120, about 60.

The bag of ten marbles, 50 draws, putting each marble back and shaking before the next: red is a half, so about 25 reds; blue is 3 tenths, so about 15; yellow is a fifth, so about 10. And 25 plus 15 plus 10 is 50, all the draws accounted for.

The fun-day spinner has 8 equal pieces and one piece wins a prize. If 240 learners play, expect an eighth of 240, which is about 30 winners. At R15 a prize, that is about R450 to budget, and a sensible class buys a few spare, because 30 is a target the results circle around, not a promise.

That word about is the whole secret. 120 rolls will not give exactly 20 sixes every time; 17 or 24 is completely normal. And the die has no memory: if no 6 has come up for ten rolls, the next roll is still a sixth, not more. Run your experiments fairly, the same way every time, putting marbles back, at least 50 tries, tallying as you go.

Ways you want over all the ways; count over tries; chance times tries, said with about. The final questions of the lesson are with you now: the 120 rolls, the 50 draws, the fun-day budget, and why the die is never due a 6.
