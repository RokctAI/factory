### subtopic_1 — Relative Frequency Against Probability
**Assistant:** If the probability of heads is exactly one half, what is the point of tossing a coin to find a relative frequency?

**Tutor:** The point is that the experiment tests the assumptions behind the calculation, and it teaches what chance actually looks like. The value of one half is not a fact about every coin in the world; it is a consequence of assuming that this coin is symmetrical and that it is tossed in a way that gives each face an equal chance. A relative frequency is the evidence for or against that assumption. If a large number of tosses gives a relative frequency close to 0.5, the assumption is supported; if it gives something far away, such as 0.65 after a thousand tosses, the coin or the tossing method is not what we assumed, and the calculated probability is the wrong model for it. The experiment also shows what a probability of one half does and does not promise. It does not promise 25 heads in 50 tosses; it promises that the relative frequency will settle near 0.5 as the tosses accumulate, with differences in either direction along the way. Seeing 27 heads, then 46 in 100, then 512 in 1000, makes that concrete in a way the fraction alone cannot. And there is a practical reason too. For a coin we have a calculation to compare with, but for a drawing pin, a bottle cap or a morning taxi we do not, and the same habit of recording trials and computing a relative frequency is the only method available. Practising it where the answer is known builds trust in it where the answer is unknown.

### subtopic_2 — Why Results Differ: Chance Variation and Bias
**Assistant:** How do I decide whether a difference is just chance or a sign that the die is biased?

**Tutor:** Compare the size of the difference with the number of trials, and then repeat. Chance variation is always present, but its size is predictable: in 600 rolls of a fair die the number of sixes usually lands within about 18 of the expected 100, and in 60 rolls within about 6 of the expected 10. A difference inside that range is ordinary, and the explanation is chance; a difference far outside it is rare for a fair die, and the explanation is probably bias. So 12 fours in 60 rolls, two above 10, is chance, while 160 sixes in 600 rolls, sixty above 100, is almost certainly bias. Between those cases, repetition decides. Bias is systematic: a weighted face pulls the results the same way in every set of trials, so it shows up again when you repeat. Chance is not: a run of extra sixes in one set is followed by an ordinary set next time. In a written answer, do not simply say "chance" or "bias". Give the size of the gap, say whether it is small or large for the number of trials, and name the cause specifically: "chance variation, since 2 more fours than expected in 60 rolls is well within normal" or "possible bias, such as a weighted die or the die being placed rather than thrown, since 60 more sixes than expected in 600 rolls is far beyond chance". The specific cause and the reasoning about size are what earn the marks.

### subtopic_3 — More Trials, Closer Relative Frequencies
**Assistant:** After 1000 tosses we had 12 more heads than expected, more than after 10 tosses. How is that getting closer?

**Tutor:** Because what gets closer is the relative frequency, not the count, and the two behave differently. After 10 tosses there were 7 heads against an expected 5, a count gap of 2 and a relative frequency of 0.7, which is 0.2 away from the probability. After 1000 tosses there were 512 heads against an expected 500, a count gap of 12 but a relative frequency of 0.512, only 0.012 away. The gap in the count grew, yet it is divided by a hundred times as many tosses, so its effect on the fraction became small. That is the law of large numbers precisely: the relative frequency approaches the probability. It makes no promise about the count, and in fact the typical count gap grows slowly as the number of trials grows, roughly in proportion to the square root of the number of trials, while the fraction gap shrinks. This is the cleanest answer to anyone who believes in the law of averages as a force that makes outcomes catch up. If the coin had to repay its early excess of heads, the count gap would shrink back to zero, and it does not; the 12 extra heads stay extra. They are simply outweighed by the thousand tosses around them. So when you read a running table or a broken-line graph of relative frequency, expect the line to settle towards the probability, and do not expect the tally of heads and tails to become equal.

### subtopic_4 — Estimating Probability from Data and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Is a probability estimated from 40 mornings of taxi records really a probability, or just a guess?

**Tutor:** It is an estimate of a probability, which is more than a guess and less than a certainty, and how much to trust it depends on two things you should always state. The first is the number of observations. Six late mornings in 40 gives 0.15, but with only 40 days chance variation is large, and the true probability could reasonably be anywhere from about 0.05 to 0.25. Six hundred days of records would pin it down much more tightly. That is the same law of large numbers we saw with the coin, applied to a situation where no symmetry gives us the answer in advance. The second is whether the conditions stay the same. The coin is the same coin on every toss, but the taxi route is not the same in every season: road works, school holidays, a new driver or the rainy months can all change the chance of being late. An estimate from last term describes last term, and using it for next term assumes nothing important has changed. Weather services, insurers and medical researchers use exactly this method, with very large data sets and careful attention to changing conditions, and their probabilities are estimates of the same kind. So write it like this: "Based on 40 mornings, the estimated probability that the taxi is late is 0.15, so about 30 late mornings are expected in a 200-day year, assuming conditions stay similar; more observations would make the estimate more reliable." That sentence is a probability statement a reader can use and check.
