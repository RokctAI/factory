### subtopic_1 — Listing Outcomes of Compound Events
**Assistant:** Why are head-tail and tail-head counted as two different outcomes when they look the same?

**Tutor:** Because the two coins are two separate objects, and the outcome of the pair records what each one did. Call them the first coin and the second coin, or paint one red: red heads with silver tails is a different event from red tails with silver heads, even though both show one head and one tail. The four outcomes HH, HT, TH and TT are therefore all distinct and, with fair coins that do not influence each other, all equally likely, each with probability 1 over 4. Collapsing HT and TH into a single outcome produces three outcomes that are not equally likely, since one head and one tail is really two of the four ways, and leads to the wrong answer of 1 over 3 for two heads; the correct probability of two heads is 1 over 4, of exactly one head 2 over 4, which is a half, and of at least one head 3 over 4. The same reasoning gives the counting rule: when the first experiment has m equally likely outcomes and the second has n, every outcome of the first pairs with every outcome of the second, so there are m times n equally likely compound outcomes. A coin and a die give 2 times 6, which is 12, so heads and a 6 has probability 1 over 12, heads and an even number 3 over 12, and tails or a 6, counting the six tails outcomes and heads-6, 7 over 12. Listing in a fixed order, H1 to H6 then T1 to T6, is how you make sure nothing is missed or repeated.

### subtopic_2 — Two-Way Tables for Two Dice
**Assistant:** How do I use the two-dice table to find probabilities, and why does it beat listing?

**Tutor:** The table makes all 36 outcomes visible at once, with the first die's score 1 to 6 down the side and the second's across the top, so counting an event is a matter of pointing at cells rather than hoping a list is complete. Each cell is one equally likely outcome with probability 1 over 36. Write the sum of the two scores in each cell and the structure appears: the sums run along diagonals, with a single cell for 2 at the top left, a single cell for 12 at the bottom right, and six cells for 7 from 1 and 6 down to 6 and 1. The probability of a sum of 7 is therefore 6 over 36, which is 1 over 6, and of a sum of 2, 1 over 36, which is why the eleven sums are not equally likely and why 7 is six times as likely as 2. Other events are counted the same way: a double is the six cells of the main diagonal, 1 over 6; a sum of at least 10 is three cells of 10, two of 11 and one of 12, six cells, 1 over 6; a sum of 7 or 11 is 8 cells, 8 over 36, which is 2 over 9; a sum that is a multiple of 4 is 3 plus 5 plus 1 cells, 9 over 36, which is a quarter. The table also holds a coin and a die as 2 by 6 or two four-sector spinners as 4 by 4, and it holds real counts: from the two-way table of 60 learners, a learner chosen at random is a girl who uses a taxi with probability 12 over 60, which is 1 over 5, and walks with probability 33 over 60, which is 11 over 20. In an answer, draw or describe the table, circle or list the favourable cells, and write the count over 36 simplified.

### subtopic_3 — Tree Diagrams and Multiplying Along Branches
**Assistant:** When do I multiply and when do I add on a tree diagram, and what changes when there is no replacement?

**Tutor:** Multiply along a path and add across paths. A path from the start to a tip is one complete compound outcome, and its probability is the product of the probabilities on its branches, because the second event must happen after the first; an event made of several outcomes, such as one of each colour, is the sum of the probabilities of the paths that belong to it. For a spinner one third red and two thirds blue spun twice, the paths are red-red with 1 over 3 times 1 over 3, which is 1 over 9, red-blue and blue-red with 2 over 9 each, and blue-blue with 4 over 9; the tips add to 9 over 9, which is the check, and one of each colour is 2 over 9 plus 2 over 9, which is 4 over 9. Without replacement, the first draw changes what remains, so the second set of branches carries different probabilities depending on which first branch you are on. From a bag of 3 red and 2 green, the first draw is red 3 over 5 or green 2 over 5; after a red, 2 red and 2 green remain, giving second branches of 2 over 4 each; after a green, 3 red and 1 green remain, giving 3 over 4 and 1 over 4. Red-red is 3 over 5 times 2 over 4, which is 6 over 20, which is 3 over 10; green-green is 2 over 5 times 1 over 4, which is 1 over 10; and different colours is the remaining 3 over 5. With replacement the bag is restored, the second branches repeat the first, and red-red is 3 over 5 times 3 over 5, which is 9 over 25, larger because the drawn red is available again. The words with or without replacement therefore decide the whole second stage of the tree, and the branches leaving every point must add to 1 as a running check.

### subtopic_4 — Choosing the Tool and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** How do I decide between a table and a tree, and what do I write to earn full marks?

**Tutor:** Choose the table for two experiments with few outcomes each when the event combines the two results, as with a sum or a difference on two dice, because every cell is visible and the counting is direct; choose the tree for experiments in sequence, especially when the second depends on the first, as in drawing without replacement, or when there are more than two stages, because the tree carries unequal and changing probabilities on its branches where the table assumes equally likely cells. Two fair dice want a table; a spinner with unequal sectors or a bag without replacement wants a tree; a coin and a die work either way. Both tools then follow the same method: identify the outcomes that make up the event, find each one's probability, one over the number of cells or the product along a path, and add. Full marks come from showing that method. State the size of the sample space with the m times n rule or draw the table or tree with every branch labelled; mark the favourable cells or paths; write each probability as a simplified fraction with its working, a product along a path or a count over the total; show the addition for an event with several outcomes; and check that the tips add to 1. The error museum collects the five habitual losses: HT and TH merged into one outcome; the eleven dice sums treated as equally likely; adding along a path instead of multiplying; leaving the second-draw probabilities unchanged after drawing without replacement; and branch or tip probabilities that do not add to 1 left unchecked. A fraction may stay a fraction unless the question asks for a decimal or a percentage.
