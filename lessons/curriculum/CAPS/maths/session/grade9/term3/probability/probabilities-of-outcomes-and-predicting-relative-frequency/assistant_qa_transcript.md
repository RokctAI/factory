### subtopic_1 — Probability of Equally Likely Outcomes
**Assistant:** Passing or failing a test are two outcomes, so why is the probability of passing not simply one half?

**Tutor:** Because the rule of favourable over total works only when the outcomes are equally likely, and pass and fail are not. Counting outcomes is a shortcut that rests on symmetry. A fair die is a cube with identical faces, so nothing in the physics favours one face over another, and each face genuinely carries one sixth of the probability; that is why 3 favourable faces out of 6 gives one half for an even number. A test has no such symmetry. Whether you pass depends on how much you studied, how hard the paper is and how you feel on the day, and a well-prepared learner might pass with a probability of 0.9 while an unprepared one might pass with 0.3. Listing two outcomes says nothing about their weights. The same trap appears in a spinner that is one quarter red and three quarters blue: two colours, but red has only a quarter of the circle and so a probability of 1 over 4. The habit to build is to ask, before dividing, whether every outcome in the list is equally likely, and to say so in the working. When they are, count. When they are not, either split the outcomes into equal pieces, as with the spinner divided into four equal quarters of which one is red, or estimate the probability from data, which is exactly what relative frequency is for. A memorandum will reward the sentence "the outcomes are equally likely" because it is the condition that makes the method valid.

### subtopic_2 — Relative Frequency from an Experiment
**Assistant:** If the die is fair, why did our class get 12 fours and only 8 ones instead of 10 of each?

**Tutor:** Because a fair die is fair on average, not in every short run, and 60 rolls is a short run. Each roll is independent and random, so the six faces do not take turns; over 60 rolls some faces will come up a few more times than 10 and some a few fewer, purely by chance. Your results of 8, 11, 9, 12, 10 and 10 are a very ordinary set. They add to 60, they cluster around the expected 10 each, and the biggest gap from 10 is 2, which is about what chance variation produces in a sample this size. If you flip the question round and ask how likely it is that a fair die gives exactly 10 of every face in 60 rolls, the answer is tiny, well under one percent, so a perfectly even table would actually be more suspicious than yours. What would make you doubt the die is a pattern that persists and grows: if after 600 rolls the fours were still running at 20 percent and the ones at 13 percent, the die, or the way it was thrown, would deserve a closer look. That is the bridge to the next lesson, where we compare relative frequency with probability and explain the differences. For now, read your relative frequencies as estimates: 12 over 60 is 0.2 for a four, close to the theoretical 0.167, and the closeness, not an exact match, is what a fair experiment delivers.

### subtopic_3 — Predicting Frequency: Probability Times Trials
**Assistant:** No six has come up in ten rolls. Is a six now more likely on the next roll?

**Tutor:** No, and believing it is so common that it has a name, the gambler's fallacy. The die has no memory. Each roll is a fresh, independent trial, the six faces are as symmetrical on the eleventh roll as on the first, and the probability of a six is still exactly 1 over 6. A run of ten rolls without a six is not even rare: the probability is 5 over 6 multiplied by itself ten times, which is about 0.16, so roughly one learner in six who tries it will see exactly that. The confusion comes from the prediction we made today. We said that in 120 rolls we expect about 20 sixes, and it feels as if a shortage early on must be made up later to reach 20. But the expectation for the remaining rolls is unchanged: if 10 rolls have produced no sixes, the expected number in the next 110 is 110 over 6, about 18, so the predicted total falls to about 18 rather than the die working harder to reach 20. Over thousands of rolls the early shortage becomes a negligible fraction of the total, which is why the relative frequency still drifts towards 1 over 6; the shortage is diluted, never repaid. The same reasoning protects you in real decisions. A family that has had three sons is not due a daughter; a fun-day spinner that has paid out no prizes for twenty turns is not due a winner. Predictions describe the average behaviour of many future trials, not a debt that the next trial must settle.

### subtopic_4 — Running a Fair Experiment and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Why does putting the marble back matter so much? I am still drawing from the same bag.

**Tutor:** Because without replacement it stops being the same bag, and the probabilities you calculated no longer describe it. Start with 5 red, 3 blue and 2 yellow, so red has probability 5 over 10. Draw a red and keep it out, and the bag now holds 4 red among 9 marbles, so the probability of red on the next draw has fallen to 4 over 9. Draw another red and it is 3 over 8. Every trial changes the conditions for the next one, so the trials are no longer repetitions of the same experiment, and comparing a relative frequency with the single probability of 1 over 2 makes no sense. After 10 draws without replacement the bag is empty and you have drawn exactly 5 reds every time, which tells you about the bag's contents but nothing about chance. Replacing the marble and shaking the bag restores the original 5 red, 3 blue and 2 yellow before every draw, so each trial has the same probabilities and the trials are independent, which are the two conditions that make the prediction of about 25 reds in 50 draws valid. Drawing without replacement is a perfectly good experiment in its own right, the one the tree diagrams of the compound events lesson handle, but it answers a different question. In a written method, the sentence "each marble was replaced and the bag shaken before the next draw" earns its mark, because it shows you know what keeps the experiment fair.
