### subtopic_1 — The Polygon Formulas and Their Units
**Assistant:** Why do the area formulas keep saying perpendicular height, and how do I find it on a diagram?

**Tutor:** Because area measures how much surface a shape covers, and only the straight-across distance between the base and the opposite side tells you that. A parallelogram with base 8 and slanted side 6 can be squashed flatter and flatter without changing either of those lengths, and its area shrinks towards zero, so the slanted side cannot be in the formula; the perpendicular height, the distance measured at right angles from the base to the opposite side, is what shrinks with the area. On a diagram the perpendicular height is the dashed line with a small square where it meets the base, sometimes drawn outside the shape for an obtuse triangle or a leaning parallelogram, and it is often given alongside the slanted sides precisely to test whether you choose correctly. The triangle formula, half of base times height, is half the rectangle built on the same base and height; the parallelogram formula, base times height, is the rectangle you get by cutting off the triangle at one end and moving it to the other; the trapezium formula, half of the sum of the parallel sides times the height, is the average width times the height. The rhombus and kite formula, half the product of the diagonals, works because the perpendicular diagonals cut the shape into four right-angled triangles that fill exactly half of the rectangle drawn around them. Perimeter uses the actual sides, including the slanted ones, and never the height; a triangle with base 10, height 6 and slanted sides 7 and 8 has area 30 square centimetres and perimeter 25 centimetres, two different calculations with two different units.

### subtopic_2 — The Circle: Circumference and Area
**Assistant:** How do I avoid the mistakes with circles, especially when the question gives a diameter or a semicircle?

**Tutor:** Convert to the radius before anything else, and for a semicircle treat the arc and the straight edge separately. The two formulas, circumference 2 pi r and area pi r squared, both use the radius, so a diameter of 20 centimetres becomes a radius of 10, and the area is pi times 100, about 314.2 square centimetres; squaring the 20 instead gives about 1256.6, four times too much, and the factor of four is the signature of this mistake. For radius 7 the circumference is 2 times pi times 7, about 43.98, and the area is pi times 49, about 153.94; use the calculator's pi key and round only the final answer, since rounding pi to 3.1 early moves the circumference to 43.4 and loses the accuracy mark. A semicircle of radius 10 has half the area, half of pi times 100, about 157.1 square centimetres, and its perimeter has two parts: the arc, half of 2 pi times 10, about 31.42, and the straight diameter of 20, giving about 51.4 centimetres; most wrong answers either forget the diameter or forget to halve the full circumference. For a ring between circles of radii 10 and 6, subtract the areas, pi times 100 minus pi times 36, about 201.1 square centimetres, never the radii, since pi times 4 squared is about 50.3 and is wrong. Working backwards, a pond of area 50.27 square metres has r squared equal to 50.27 divided by pi, which is 16, so the radius is 4 metres and the fence is 2 pi times 4, about 25.1 metres. The pizza comparison shows why the square matters: a 30 centimetre pizza is four 15 centimetre pizzas, not two.

### subtopic_3 — Converting Between SI Units of Length and Area
**Assistant:** Why is a square metre 10 000 square centimetres and not 100, and when should I convert?

**Tutor:** Because a square metre is a square 100 centimetres by 100 centimetres, and 100 times 100 is 10 000; the length factor is 100, so the area factor is 100 squared. The same reasoning gives 1 square centimetre as 100 square millimetres, 1 square kilometre as 1 000 000 square metres, and the hectare, a square 100 metres on each side, as 10 000 square metres. Convert before you use a formula, and convert to the unit the question wants the answer in. A floor 2.5 metres by 1.6 metres is 4 square metres; converted to centimetres first it is 250 by 160, which is 40 000 square centimetres, and 4 times 10 000 gives the same 40 000, so either order works provided the area factor is 10 000 and not 100. A field 250 metres by 400 metres is 100 000 square metres, which is 10 hectares. For the tiling problem, a 300 millimetre tile becomes 0.3 metres and covers 0.09 square metres, so a floor of 4.5 by 3, which is 13.5 square metres, needs 13.5 divided by 0.09, which is 150 tiles; the count of 15 along one side and 10 along the other confirms it and shows there is no cutting. Costs follow the same path: carpet at R145 per square metre for a 5 by 3.6 room costs 18 times 145, which is R2 610, and paint covering 8 square metres per litre for a 6 by 2.4 wall needs 1.8 litres, so one 2 litre tin at R189 is enough. The unit on the final line must be the one asked for, squared for area, and a correct number in the wrong unit does not earn the mark.

### subtopic_4 — Composite Shapes and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** What is the method for a composite shape, and why does cutting a corner off a rectangle not change its perimeter?

**Tutor:** Split the shape into basic pieces, name each piece, find the measure for each on its own line, and then add or subtract; for the perimeter, walk round the outside once and include only edges that are actually on the outside. For the rectangle 10 by 6 with a semicircle of radius 3 on one 6 centimetre side, the area is 60 plus half of pi times 9, about 74.1 square centimetres, and the perimeter is 10 plus 6 plus 10 for the three exposed sides plus the arc of about 9.42, about 35.4 centimetres; the 6 centimetre side where the semicircle joins is inside the shape and is not counted. For an L-shape made by removing a 4 by 5 rectangle from a corner of a 12 by 8 rectangle, the area is 96 minus 20, which is 76 square centimetres, but the perimeter is still 40 centimetres: the cut removes a 4 and a 5 from the outline and adds a new 4 and a new 5 running the other way, so the outline has the same total length. Removing a corner always leaves a rectangle's perimeter unchanged while reducing its area, which is a good example of the two measures being independent. The error museum collects the standard losses: a slanted side used as a height, the diameter squared in the circle area, a semicircle without its diameter or without the halving, an unsquared area unit or a factor of 100 instead of 10 000, and an internal join counted as perimeter or radii subtracted instead of areas in a ring. The full-marks layout draws and splits the shape, writes each formula in letters, substitutes with units, keeps the full value of pi until the end, rounds to the accuracy asked, and states the unit on every answer.
