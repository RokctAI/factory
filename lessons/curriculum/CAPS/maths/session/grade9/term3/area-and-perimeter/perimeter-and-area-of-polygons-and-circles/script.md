# Part 1 — Expert

Perimeter is the distance around a shape and area is the surface it covers, and Grade 9 collects every formula from earlier grades, adds the circle, and insists on correct units with conversions between them. Today we revise the formulas for the square, rectangle, triangle, parallelogram, trapezium, rhombus and kite, use the circle's circumference and area, convert between square millimetres, square centimetres, square metres, hectares and square kilometres, and apply the formulas to composite shapes and to working backwards from an area to a length. Every answer carries a unit, and every area unit is squared, which is where most marks are lost. Next lesson we investigate what doubling a dimension does to each measure.

## Subtopic: The Polygon Formulas and Their Units

Perimeter adds the sides. A rectangle with length 12 centimetres and breadth 7 centimetres has perimeter 2 times 12 plus 7, which is 38 centimetres, and area 12 times 7, which is 84 square centimetres. The unit of perimeter is a length; the unit of area is a length squared, because area counts unit squares: 84 square centimetres means 84 squares each one centimetre by one centimetre.

A square of side s has perimeter 4s and area s squared. A triangle with base b and perpendicular height h has area half of b times h; the height is measured at right angles to the base, not along a slanted side. A triangle with base 10 centimetres and height 6 centimetres has area 30 square centimetres, and if its slanted sides are 7 and 8 its perimeter is 25 centimetres. The height and the perimeter use different numbers, and confusing a slanted side with the height is the first error of the lesson. In a right-angled triangle one of the two shorter sides is the height when the other is the base, so a 6, 8, 10 triangle has area half of 6 times 8, which is 24 square centimetres, and perimeter 24 centimetres; the two numbers agree by coincidence and carry different units.

A parallelogram with base b and perpendicular height h has area b times h, because it can be cut along the height and the triangle moved to the other end to make a rectangle. Base 8 centimetres, height 5 centimetres: area 40 square centimetres. A trapezium with parallel sides a and b and perpendicular height h has area half of a plus b, times h, the average of the parallel sides times the height. Parallel sides 6 and 10 centimetres with height 4: half of 16 is 8, times 4 is 32 square centimetres.

A rhombus or kite with diagonals d1 and d2 has area half of d1 times d2, because the diagonals are perpendicular and cut the shape into four right-angled triangles that together fill half the rectangle d1 by d2. Diagonals 6 and 8 centimetres: area 24 square centimetres. Since the diagonals of a rhombus bisect each other at right angles, each side is the hypotenuse of a 3-4-5 triangle, so the side is 5 and the perimeter is 20 centimetres.

Perpendicular height is the recurring word: every area formula with an h means the distance measured at right angles between the base and the opposite side or vertex. The questions for this section are with you now: the formulas, the rectangle 12 by 7, the trapezium 6, 10 and 4, and the rhombus with diagonals 6 and 8.

## Subtopic: The Circle: Circumference and Area

A circle has a radius r from the centre to the edge and a diameter d equal to 2r across the middle. Its perimeter is called the circumference, C equals 2 pi r or pi d, and its area is A equals pi r squared. Pi is the ratio of circumference to diameter for every circle, about 3.14159, and the calculator's pi key gives the full value; round only at the end. Rounding pi to 3 or to 3.1 in the middle of a calculation shifts a 44.0 to 42 or 43.4, and the marker's answer will not match.

Radius 7 centimetres. Circumference 2 times pi times 7, which is 43.98, so 44.0 centimetres to one decimal place. Area pi times 49, which is 153.94, so 153.9 square centimetres. Diameter 20 centimetres: the radius is 10, the area is pi times 100, which is 314.2 square centimetres, and a learner who squares the diameter gets 1256.6, four times too big, which is the circle's commonest error.

A semicircle of radius 10 centimetres. Its area is half of pi times 100, which is 157.1 square centimetres. Its perimeter is the curved arc plus the straight diameter: half of 2 pi times 10 is 31.42, plus 20 for the diameter, which is 51.4 centimetres. Forgetting the diameter is the semicircle's commonest error, and forgetting to halve is its second.

Working backwards. A circular pond has an area of 50.27 square metres. Then r squared is 50.27 divided by pi, which is 16.0, so r is 4 metres and the fence around it is 2 pi times 4, which is 25.1 metres. A pizza of diameter 30 centimetres has area pi times 225, which is 706.9 square centimetres; a 15 centimetre pizza has area pi times 56.25, which is 176.7, one quarter as much, so the large pizza is four small ones, not two.

A ring, the region between two circles with the same centre, has area pi R squared minus pi r squared. Outer radius 10 and inner radius 6: pi times 100 minus 36, which is pi times 64, which is 201.1 square centimetres. Subtract the areas, never the radii: pi times 4 squared would be 50.3, which is wrong. Your questions on this section are ready: C and A for radius 7, the semicircle of radius 10, the pond, and the ring.

## Subtopic: Converting Between SI Units of Length and Area

Lengths convert by one factor: 1 metre is 100 centimetres, 1 centimetre is 10 millimetres, 1 kilometre is 1000 metres. Areas convert by that factor squared, because a square metre is a square 100 centimetres by 100 centimetres, which is 10 000 square centimetres. Likewise 1 square centimetre is 100 square millimetres, 1 square kilometre is 1 000 000 square metres, and the hectare, used for land, is 10 000 square metres, a square 100 metres by 100 metres.

A floor 2.5 metres by 1.6 metres has area 4 square metres, which is 40 000 square centimetres. Converting the lengths first gives 250 centimetres by 160 centimetres, which is 40 000 square centimetres again; either order works, but the area factor is 10 000, not 100. A field 250 metres by 400 metres has area 100 000 square metres, which is 10 hectares. A stamp 20 millimetres by 25 millimetres has area 500 square millimetres, which is 5 square centimetres.

Mixed units in one problem must be converted before any formula is used, and the answer's unit must be the one the question asks for; a question in metres that is answered in square centimetres loses the mark even when the number is right. A rectangular floor 4.5 metres by 3 metres is to be tiled with square tiles of side 300 millimetres. Convert: 300 millimetres is 0.3 metres, so one tile is 0.09 square metres. The floor is 13.5 square metres, so the number of tiles is 13.5 divided by 0.09, which is 150. Alternatively, 15 tiles fit along 4.5 metres and 10 along 3 metres, giving 15 times 10, which is 150, a check that also shows no cutting is needed.

Rates in rands follow the same path. Paint covers 8 square metres per litre and a wall is 6 metres by 2.4 metres, so 14.4 square metres needs 1.8 litres; with 2 litre tins at R189 each, one tin suffices and the cost is R189, with 0.2 litres over. Carpet at R145 per square metre for a room 5 metres by 3.6 metres costs 18 times 145, which is R2 610. The questions for this section are ready: the 10 000 factor, the hectare, the 150 tiles, and the carpet.

## Subtopic: Composite Shapes and the Error Museum

A composite shape is built from the basic ones, and the method is to split it, name each piece, find each measure, and combine. A rectangle 10 centimetres by 6 centimetres with a semicircle attached to one 6 centimetre side. Area: 60 plus half of pi times 3 squared, which is 60 plus 14.14, which is 74.1 square centimetres. Perimeter: the three exposed rectangle sides, 10 plus 6 plus 10, which is 26, plus the arc, half of 2 pi times 3, which is 9.42, giving 35.4 centimetres. The 6 centimetre side where the semicircle is attached is inside the shape and is not part of the perimeter.

An L-shape made of a 12 by 8 rectangle with a 4 by 5 rectangle removed from one corner. Area: 96 minus 20, which is 76 square centimetres, and the perimeter is the same as the full 12 by 8 rectangle, 40 centimetres, because the two cut edges simply replace the two removed edges; removing a corner never changes the perimeter of a rectangle, which surprises learners every year.

A running track with two straights of 100 metres and two semicircular ends of radius 31.83 metres has perimeter 200 plus 2 pi times 31.83, which is 200 plus 200.0, which is 400 metres; the odd-looking radius is chosen so that the lap is exactly 400.

The error museum, five exhibits. Exhibit one: a slanted side used as the height of a triangle, parallelogram or trapezium. Exhibit two: diameter squared instead of radius squared in the circle area, giving four times the answer. Exhibit three: a semicircle perimeter without its diameter, or a semicircle area without the halving. Exhibit four: an area unit left unsquared, or 1 square metre converted as 100 square centimetres. Exhibit five: counting an internal join as part of a composite perimeter, or subtracting radii instead of areas in a ring.

Layout for marks: draw and split the shape, write each formula in letters, substitute with units, calculate with the calculator's full pi, round only the final answer to the accuracy asked, state the unit, squared for area, and for a composite shape show each piece on its own line before adding or subtracting. The questions for this section are with you now: the rectangle with the semicircle, the L-shape, the track, and the five exhibits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through a fence and a carpet, a pizza and a pie, and a floor covered in tiles — plain words, same rules.

## Subtopic: Fence and Carpet

Perimeter is the fence around a garden; area is the carpet that covers the floor. Fence is measured in metres, carpet in square metres, because a square metre is a one-metre tile and the area counts how many tiles cover the floor. Write the little 2 on every area unit; it is the difference between a length and a surface.

A rectangle 12 by 7: the fence is 12 plus 7 plus 12 plus 7, which is 38 centimetres; the carpet is 12 rows of 7 tiles, 84 square centimetres. A square of side s: fence 4s, carpet s squared.

A triangle is half a rectangle, so its carpet is half of base times height, and the height is the straight-up distance, not the sloping side. Base 10, height 6: 30 square centimetres. If its sloping sides are 7 and 8, the fence is 10 plus 7 plus 8, which is 25 centimetres. The height does not appear in the fence and the sloping sides do not appear in the carpet.

A parallelogram is a pushed-over rectangle; push it back and the carpet is base times height, 8 by 5 is 40. A trapezium is a rectangle whose top is a different length from its bottom; use the average of the top and bottom as the width, then times the height: top 6, bottom 10, average 8, height 4, carpet 32. A kite or a rhombus is half of the rectangle drawn around its two sticks, so carpet is half of one stick times the other: sticks 6 and 8 give 24. Your questions on this section are ready: fence against carpet, the straight-up height, and the average width of a trapezium.

## Subtopic: Pizza and Pie

A circle's fence is its circumference, and it is always a bit more than three times the diameter: pi times d, or 2 pi r. A circle's carpet is pi times the radius squared. The radius is from the middle to the edge; the diameter goes all the way across and is twice the radius.

Radius 7: fence 2 times pi times 7, about 44.0; carpet pi times 49, about 153.9. A 20 centimetre diameter plate: the radius is 10, the carpet is pi times 100, about 314.2. If you square the 20 instead you get about 1256.6, four times too much, so always find the radius first.

Pizza. A 30 centimetre pizza has a radius of 15 and a carpet of pi times 225, about 706.9 square centimetres. A 15 centimetre pizza has a radius of 7.5 and a carpet of about 176.7. The big one is four small ones, not two, because doubling the radius squares to four.

Half a pie, a semicircle of radius 10: carpet is half of pi times 100, about 157.1. The fence is the curved crust, half of 2 pi times 10, about 31.4, plus the straight cut edge, 20, so about 51.4. Leaving out the straight edge is the mistake that costs the mark.

A ring, like a washer, is the big circle's carpet minus the small circle's carpet: radii 10 and 6 give pi times 100 minus pi times 36, about 201.1. Do not subtract the radii first; pi times 4 squared is about 50.3 and is wrong. Backwards: a round pond with carpet 50.27 square metres has r squared equal to 50.27 divided by pi, about 16, so the radius is 4 metres. The questions for this section: radius first, the pizza that is four pizzas, the semicircle's straight edge, and the ring.

## Subtopic: A Floor Covered in Tiles

Changing the unit of a length is one jump: 1 metre is 100 centimetres. Changing the unit of an area is the jump squared, because a one-metre tile is 100 by 100 little one-centimetre tiles, which is 10 000 of them. So 1 square metre is 10 000 square centimetres, 1 square centimetre is 100 square millimetres, and a hectare, the farmer's unit, is a 100 by 100 metre square, 10 000 square metres.

A floor 2.5 metres by 1.6 metres is 4 square metres, which is 40 000 square centimetres, not 400. A field 250 by 400 metres is 100 000 square metres, which is 10 hectares.

Tiles. A floor 4.5 metres by 3 metres and tiles 300 millimetres square. Change the tile to metres first: 0.3 by 0.3, which is 0.09 square metres, so that the tile and the floor speak the same unit before you divide. The floor is 13.5 square metres, so 13.5 divided by 0.09 is 150 tiles. Or count: 15 tiles along the 4.5 and 10 along the 3, which is 150 tiles, no cutting. Carpet at R145 a square metre for a 5 by 3.6 room: 18 square metres times 145 is R2 610.

Composite shapes: split, label, add or take away. A 10 by 6 rectangle with a semicircle on a 6 side: carpet 60 plus half of pi times 9, about 74.1; fence 10 plus 6 plus 10 plus the arc, about 9.4, which is about 35.4, and the joined 6 is not fence. An L-shape cut from a 12 by 8 rectangle has less carpet but the same fence, 40, because the cut edges replace the edges they removed.

Fence and carpet, radius first, jump squared for area units, split composite shapes. The final questions of the lesson are with you now: 10 000 not 100, the 150 tiles, the R2 610 carpet, and the L-shape with the same fence.
