### subtopic_1 — Doubling One Dimension of a Rectangle
**Assistant:** If I double just the length, why does the area double neatly but the perimeter goes up by an awkward amount?

**Tutor:** Because area and perimeter are built from the dimensions in different ways. Area is the product length times breadth, so doubling one factor doubles the product, whatever the other factor is: 6 by 4 is 24, and 12 by 4 is 48, exactly double, and 6 by 8 is also 48. Perimeter is a sum, 2 times length plus 2 times breadth, so doubling the length adds 2 times the original length to the total while the two breadths contribute what they did before. From 20, doubling the 6 adds 12 to give 32, and doubling the 4 adds 8 to give 28. The increase is twice the stretched dimension, so there is no single multiplier for the perimeter; it depends on which side was stretched and on how long the other side is. The investigation records this in a table with rows for the original, the length doubled and the breadth doubled, and the area column shows a clean doubling in both rows while the perimeter column shows two different numbers. A square of side 5 makes the point another way: doubling one side produces a 10 by 5 rectangle, so the shape itself changes, with perimeter 30 and area 50. For a triangle with base 8 and height 5, doubling either the base or the height doubles the area from 20 to 40, since the formula is half of base times height and one factor doubled; the perimeter of a triangle involves slanted sides that do not scale simply when only one dimension changes, so the one-dimension investigation of triangles stays with area.

### subtopic_2 — Doubling All Dimensions: Perimeter Doubles, Area Quadruples
**Assistant:** How can I convince myself, without just trusting the numbers, that doubling both dimensions makes the area four times bigger?

**Tutor:** Draw it. Take the 6 by 4 rectangle and draw the 12 by 8 rectangle next to it, then cut the large one with a vertical line through the middle and a horizontal line through the middle. Each of the four pieces is 6 by 4, an exact copy of the original, two across and two down, so the large rectangle contains four of the small one and its area is four times 24, which is 96. The perimeter is a different story: walk round the large rectangle and every side is twice as long as the corresponding side of the small one, so the walk is twice as long, 40 instead of 20. The numbers confirm the picture: 2 times 12 plus 8 is 40 and 12 times 8 is 96. The same reasoning holds for every shape, because perimeter adds lengths and doubling each length doubles the sum, while area multiplies two lengths and doubling each multiplies the product by 2 times 2. The square of side 5 goes to side 10 with perimeter 40 and area 100, four times 25; tripled to side 15 it has perimeter 60 and area 225, nine times 25; halved to side 2.5 it has perimeter 10 and area 6.25, a quarter of 25. The right-angled triangle with sides 6, 8 and 10 has perimeter 24 and area 24, equal by coincidence; doubled to 12, 16 and 20 its perimeter is 48 and its area is 96, so the coincidence disappears and the two measures show their different behaviour. The statement to memorise is that doubling every dimension doubles the perimeter and quadruples the area.

### subtopic_3 — Triangles, Circles and the General Scale Factor
**Assistant:** Does the rule change for circles, since there is a pi in the formulas, and what happens for scale factors other than 2?

**Tutor:** The rule is unchanged, because pi is a constant that appears in both the original and the scaled measure and cancels when they are compared. A circle of radius 3 has circumference 2 pi times 3, about 18.85, and area pi times 9, about 28.27; a circle of radius 6 has circumference 2 pi times 6, about 37.70, which is double, and area pi times 36, about 113.10, which is four times. In the circumference formula the radius appears once, so doubling it doubles the result; in the area formula the radius is squared, so doubling it multiplies the result by 4. For a general scale factor k, every length is multiplied by k, so the perimeter is multiplied by k, and every area formula multiplies two lengths, so the area is multiplied by k squared. A scale factor of 3 gives 9 times the area, 10 gives 100 times, a half gives a quarter, and 1.5 gives 2.25 times, which the trapezium confirms: parallel sides 6 and 10 with height 4 give area 32, and scaled by 1.5 to 9, 15 and 6 the area is half of 24 times 6, which is 72, equal to 32 times 2.25. The rule also runs backwards. If an area is multiplied by 9, the scale factor is the square root of 9, which is 3, and lengths are tripled; if a perimeter is multiplied by 5, the area is multiplied by 25; if you want exactly double the area, the scale factor is the square root of 2, about 1.41, so lengths grow by about 41 percent rather than doubling.

### subtopic_4 — Applications and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Where does this rule matter in real problems, and what are the mistakes to avoid?

**Tutor:** It matters whenever a cost or a quantity depends on area while another depends on length. A farm plot 50 by 30 metres needs 160 metres of fence and encloses 1500 square metres; doubled to 100 by 60 it needs 320 metres, twice as much, and encloses 6000 square metres, four times as much, so at R85 per metre the fence costs R13 600 and then R27 200, twice the money for four times the land. A photograph enlarged from 10 by 15 to 20 by 30 needs four times the paper but only twice the frame moulding. A 30 centimetre pizza has four times the area of a 15 centimetre one, so at R120 against R45 the large is the better buy, since four smalls would cost R180. On a map at 1 to 50 000 a 2 centimetre square represents a 1 kilometre square, which is 100 hectares, because lengths scale by 50 000 and areas by 50 000 squared. The error museum holds five exhibits: saying that doubling the dimensions doubles the area, when it quadruples it; saying that doubling one dimension doubles the perimeter, when it adds twice that dimension; giving a scale factor of 3 an area factor of 3 or 6 instead of 9; working backwards from an area factor of 9 to a scale factor of 9 or 4.5 instead of the square root, 3; and applying a map's length scale to areas. The layout that avoids them states k, states that perimeter is multiplied by k and area by k squared, computes the original, applies the factor, and confirms by direct calculation on the new dimensions with units on every line.
