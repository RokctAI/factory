# Part 1 — Expert

Last lesson we calculated perimeter and area; today we investigate what happens to them when the shape changes size. The CAPS instruction is to investigate how doubling any or all dimensions of a two-dimensional figure affects its perimeter and area, and the investigation produces a result that is surprising the first time and indispensable afterwards: when every dimension is doubled, the perimeter doubles but the area becomes four times as large. When only one dimension is doubled, the two measures behave differently again. We build the result from tables of rectangles, squares, triangles and circles, generalise to any scale factor k, where perimeter is multiplied by k and area by k squared, and apply it to photographs, fields and pizzas. Grade 10 will meet the same idea in similar figures and in Grade 12 volumes; today it is established by calculation.

## Subtopic: Doubling One Dimension of a Rectangle

Begin with a rectangle 6 centimetres by 4 centimetres. Its perimeter is 2 times 6 plus 4, which is 20 centimetres, and its area is 6 times 4, which is 24 square centimetres. Now double the length only, to 12 by 4. The perimeter is 2 times 12 plus 4, which is 32 centimetres, and the area is 12 times 4, which is 48 square centimetres. The area has doubled. The perimeter has not doubled: it went from 20 to 32, an increase of 12, which is twice the original length, because the two long sides each gained 6 and the two short sides are unchanged.

Double the breadth only instead, to 6 by 8. The perimeter is 2 times 6 plus 8, which is 28, and the area is 48 again. The area doubles whichever dimension is doubled, because area is the product of the two and doubling one factor doubles the product. The perimeter increases by twice the doubled dimension, 12 when the length is doubled and 8 when the breadth is doubled, so the new perimeter depends on which side was stretched.

Record this in a table with columns for length, breadth, perimeter and area, and rows for the original, length doubled, and breadth doubled. The pattern in the area column is immediate. The pattern in the perimeter column is not a single multiplier, and that is the first lesson: doubling one dimension doubles the area but does not double the perimeter.

The same holds for a square, with a twist. A square of side 5 has perimeter 20 and area 25. Double one side only and it is no longer a square; it is a 10 by 5 rectangle with perimeter 30 and area 50. The shape has changed, the area has doubled, and the perimeter has risen by 10, which is twice the stretched side. A triangle with base 8 and height 5 has area 20; double the base to 16 and the area is 40; double the height to 10 instead and the area is 40 again. For the triangle's perimeter the slanted sides change in a way that needs the next topic in Grade 10, so the investigation of one-dimension doubling in triangles is confined to area. The questions for this section are with you now: the 6 by 4 rectangle stretched each way, why the area doubles, and why the perimeter does not.

## Subtopic: Doubling All Dimensions: Perimeter Doubles, Area Quadruples

Now double both dimensions of the 6 by 4 rectangle, to 12 by 8. The perimeter is 2 times 12 plus 8, which is 40 centimetres, exactly twice the original 20. The area is 12 times 8, which is 96 square centimetres, four times the original 24. Every side doubled, so the sum of the sides doubled; both factors in the product doubled, so the product was multiplied by 2 times 2.

The square of side 5 becomes a square of side 10: perimeter 40, twice 20; area 100, four times 25. Triple the side instead, to 15: perimeter 60, three times 20; area 225, nine times 25. Halve the side to 2.5: perimeter 10, half of 20; area 6.25, a quarter of 25. The perimeter follows the multiplier, and the area follows the multiplier squared.

The picture explains the four. Draw the original 6 by 4 rectangle, then the 12 by 8 rectangle. The large one can be divided by one horizontal and one vertical line into four copies of the original, two across and two down. Four copies means four times the area. Around the outside, each of the four sides is twice as long, so the boundary is twice as long. Area is two-dimensional and doubling acts twice; perimeter is one-dimensional and doubling acts once.

A triangle with base 8 and height 5 has area 20. Doubling both to 16 and 10 gives area half of 16 times 10, which is 80, four times 20. If the triangle is right-angled with sides 6, 8 and 10, its perimeter is 24 and its area is 24; doubled to 12, 16 and 20, the perimeter is 48 and the area is half of 12 times 16, which is 96, so the perimeter doubled and the area quadrupled, and the numbers that were equal by coincidence are no longer equal.

Write the general statement and commit it to memory: when every dimension of a figure is multiplied by 2, the perimeter is multiplied by 2 and the area by 4. Doubling the dimensions is not doubling the area; it is quadrupling it. Your questions on this section are ready: 12 by 8 against 6 by 4, four copies in the picture, and the square tripled.

## Subtopic: Triangles, Circles and the General Scale Factor

The result does not depend on the shape, because every perimeter is a sum of lengths and every area formula is a product of two lengths, sometimes with a constant like a half or pi in front. A circle of radius 3 has circumference 2 pi times 3, which is 18.85 centimetres, and area pi times 9, which is 28.27 square centimetres. Doubling the radius to 6 gives circumference 2 pi times 6, which is 37.70, exactly double, and area pi times 36, which is 113.10, exactly four times. The constant pi is the same in both and cancels in the comparison; only the radius changed, and it appears once in the circumference and twice, as r squared, in the area.

Replace 2 by any scale factor k. Multiply every dimension by k and every side is k times longer, so the perimeter is k times longer. Every area formula contains two lengths multiplied, so the area is k times k, which is k squared, times larger. For k equal to 3 the area is 9 times larger; for k equal to 10 the area is 100 times larger; for k equal to a half the area is a quarter, and for k equal to 1.5 the area is 2.25 times larger.

A trapezium with parallel sides 6 and 10 and height 4 has area 32; scaled by 1.5 its parallel sides are 9 and 15 and its height is 6, so its area is half of 24 times 6, which is 72, and 32 times 2.25 is 72, as predicted. A rhombus with diagonals 6 and 8 has area 24; scaled by 3 its diagonals are 18 and 24 and its area is 216, which is 24 times 9.

The rule runs backwards too. If a photograph is enlarged so that its area is 9 times as large, the scale factor is the square root of 9, which is 3, and every length, including the perimeter, is 3 times as long. If a shape's perimeter is multiplied by 5, its area is multiplied by 25. If the area is multiplied by 2, the scale factor is the square root of 2, about 1.41, so lengths grow by about 41 percent; to double the area of a pizza you do not double the diameter, you multiply it by about 1.41. The questions for this section are ready: the circle of radius 3 and 6, k and k squared, the trapezium scaled by 1.5, and the backwards rule.

## Subtopic: Applications and the Error Museum

A farmer fences a plot 50 metres by 30 metres: fence 160 metres, area 1500 square metres. Doubling both dimensions to 100 by 60 needs 320 metres of fence, twice as much, and encloses 6000 square metres, four times as much. Per metre of fence the larger plot encloses more land, which is why larger fields are cheaper to fence per hectare. Fencing at R85 per metre costs R13 600 for the small plot and R27 200 for the large one, twice the cost for four times the land.

A photograph 10 centimetres by 15 centimetres is enlarged to 20 by 30. The print needs four times the paper and four times the ink, and if paper is charged by area the enlargement costs four times as much, not twice. A frame around it needs twice the length of moulding.

The pizza returns. A 30 centimetre pizza has four times the area of a 15 centimetre pizza, because the scale factor 2 squares to 4. If the large costs R120 and the small costs R45, the small is R45 for a quarter of the large, which would be R180 for four smalls, so the large is the better buy by a wide margin.

A map at a scale of 1 to 50 000 shows a square farm as a square 2 centimetres on a side. The real side is 2 times 50 000 centimetres, which is 100 000 centimetres, which is 1 kilometre, and the real area is 1 square kilometre, which is 100 hectares; the area scale is 1 to 50 000 squared, which is 2 500 000 000, so a square centimetre on the map is 2 500 000 000 square centimetres, which is 250 000 square metres, which is 25 hectares, and 4 square centimetres is 100 hectares, consistent.

The error museum, five exhibits. Exhibit one: doubling the dimensions doubles the area; it quadruples it. Exhibit two: doubling one dimension doubles the perimeter; it adds twice that dimension, and the multiplier depends on the shape. Exhibit three: a scale factor of 3 giving an area 3 times or 6 times larger; it is 9 times, k squared. Exhibit four: working backwards from an area multiplied by 9 to a scale factor of 9 or 4.5; it is the square root, 3. Exhibit five: applying the area factor to a map's length scale, or the length factor to its area.

Layout for marks: state the scale factor k, state that lengths and perimeter are multiplied by k and area by k squared, compute the original measure, apply the factor, and confirm by direct calculation on the new dimensions where possible, with units on every line. The questions for this section are with you now: the farm, the photograph, the pizza, the map, and the five exhibits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through stretching a shape one way then both ways, a fence that doubles while the carpet quadruples, and a scale factor you square — plain words, same rules.

## Subtopic: Stretch One Way, Then Both Ways

Take a 6 by 4 rectangle: fence 20, carpet 24. Stretch it one way, so the 6 becomes 12. Fence: 12 plus 4 plus 12 plus 4 is 32. Carpet: 12 times 4 is 48. The carpet doubled, because you have two of the old rectangles side by side. The fence did not double: it went up by 12, the two long sides each gaining 6, while the short sides stayed as they were.

Stretch the other way instead, so the 4 becomes 8: fence 28, carpet 48. Carpet doubled again, fence up by 8 this time. So stretching one way always doubles the carpet, and adds twice the stretched side to the fence, which is a different amount each time.

Now stretch both ways: 12 by 8. Fence: 12 plus 8 plus 12 plus 8 is 40, exactly twice 20. Carpet: 12 times 8 is 96, exactly four times 24. Draw it and see why: the 12 by 8 holds four of the 6 by 4, two across and two down. Four copies of the carpet, but the fence just has every side twice as long.

A 5 by 5 square: fence 20, carpet 25. Stretch one way: a 10 by 5 rectangle, fence 30, carpet 50. Stretch both ways: a 10 by 10 square, fence 40, carpet 100. A triangle with base 8 and height 5 has carpet 20; double the base, 40; double the height instead, 40; double both, 80. Your questions on this section are ready: one way doubles the carpet only, both ways doubles the fence and quadruples the carpet, and the four copies.

## Subtopic: Fence Doubles, Carpet Quadruples

Say it as a rule: double every length, and the fence doubles but the carpet becomes four times bigger. The fence is made of lengths, and each length doubled, so the total doubled. The carpet is length times width, and both doubled, so the carpet got two times two, which is four.

Circles obey the same rule. Radius 3: fence about 18.85, carpet about 28.27. Radius 6: fence about 37.70, which is double; carpet about 113.10, which is four times. Pi did not change; only the radius did, and the radius appears once in the fence formula and twice, squared, in the carpet formula.

Triple instead of double, and the fence is three times, the carpet nine times: the 5 by 5 square becomes 15 by 15, fence 60, carpet 225. Halve instead, and the fence halves while the carpet quarters: side 2.5, fence 10, carpet 6.25. Whatever you do to the lengths, the fence does the same, and the carpet does it twice.

Real life. A farm plot 50 by 30 metres needs 160 metres of fence for 1500 square metres. Double both sides to 100 by 60 and you need 320 metres of fence, twice as much, for 6000 square metres, four times as much land. At R85 a metre the fence costs R13 600 and then R27 200, double the money for four times the land. A 10 by 15 photo blown up to 20 by 30 needs four times the paper but only twice the frame. The questions for this section: the circle of radius 3 and 6, triple and halve, the farm and the photo.

## Subtopic: Scale Factor k: Times k, Times k Squared

Call the multiplier the scale factor, k. Every length is multiplied by k, so the fence is multiplied by k. The carpet is multiplied by k times k, which is k squared. k equal to 2: fence times 2, carpet times 4. k equal to 3: fence times 3, carpet times 9. k equal to 10: fence times 10, carpet times 100. k equal to 1.5: fence times 1.5, carpet times 2.25, so a trapezium of carpet 32 scaled by 1.5 has carpet 72.

Backwards is the useful direction. If a photo's carpet became 9 times bigger, the scale factor is the square root of 9, which is 3, and the frame is 3 times longer. If the fence is 5 times longer, the carpet is 25 times bigger. If you want twice the carpet, you do not double the sides; the scale factor is the square root of 2, about 1.41, so the sides grow by about 41 percent.

The pizza decides it. A 30 centimetre pizza is four 15 centimetre pizzas, because k is 2 and k squared is 4. Large at R120 against small at R45: four smalls would be R180, so the large wins.

Maps carry two scales. At 1 to 50 000, lengths are multiplied by 50 000 and areas by 50 000 squared. A 2 centimetre square on the map is a 1 kilometre square on the ground, 100 hectares, which is why map areas go wrong so often: the length scale was used where the area scale belonged.

One way doubles the carpet only; both ways doubles the fence and quadruples the carpet; scale factor k gives times k and times k squared; backwards means square root. The final questions of the lesson are with you now: k and k squared, the square root for backwards, the pizza, and the map.
