### subtopic_1 — Congruent Triangles and the Four Conditions
**Assistant:** Why does the order of the letters in a congruence statement matter so much?

**Tutor:** Because the statement is a set of six equalities packed into one line, and the order is what unpacks it. Triangle ABC congruent to triangle DEF says that A corresponds to D, B to E and C to F, and therefore that AB equals DE, BC equals EF, AC equals DF, and the angles at A, B and C equal the angles at D, E and F. If the second triangle is written as triangle EFD instead, the statement claims AB equals EF, which may be false even though the triangles are congruent. The marker reads the order, and every later line that claims a side or angle equal with the reason congruent triangles is checked against it. The safe habit is to list the three facts you are using in the same order for both triangles, so that the first fact names the first pair of matching parts, and to write the congruence statement by reading down your list. For the four conditions themselves, the investigation by construction is the justification: three sides give arcs that meet in one point, two sides and the included angle give a single third side, two angles and a side fix the third angle and so the whole triangle, and a right angle with the hypotenuse and one side leaves the remaining side no choice. Each condition is abbreviated in brackets after the statement: SSS, SAS, AAS or RHS.

### subtopic_2 — Why Three Angles or Two Sides and a Non-Included Angle Are Not Enough
**Assistant:** If SSA sometimes gives only one triangle, why is it never allowed as a reason?

**Tutor:** Because a congruence condition must guarantee one triangle every time, and SSA guarantees it only in special cases that the reason itself does not identify. Take sides 7 and 5 with an angle of 40 degrees at the end of the 7 and opposite the 5. Draw the 7, construct the 40 degree arm at one end, and swing an arc of radius 5 from the other end: it crosses the arm twice, so two different triangles share the data, one with an acute angle at the third vertex and one with an obtuse angle. When the arc happens to be too short to reach the arm there is no triangle, and when the side opposite the given angle is the longer of the two the arc crosses the extended arm only once on the correct side, which is the special case that includes RHS. A reason that works only sometimes is not a reason, so SSA and its rearrangement ASS are never written; if the given angle is a right angle the condition is RHS, and otherwise the problem must supply something else. Three angles fail for a different reason: they fix the shape completely but say nothing about size, so the 40, 60, 80 triangle can be drawn with a base of 4 or 8 or anything at all. AAA is therefore the condition for similarity rather than congruence. Reading a diagram for usable facts avoids both traps: common sides, equal radii, vertically opposite angles and bisected angles or sides supply the third fact that turns SSA into SAS or AAS.

### subtopic_3 — Similar Triangles: Equal Angles, Proportional Sides
**Assistant:** How do I test two triangles for similarity when I am given only their side lengths?

**Tutor:** Order the sides of each triangle from smallest to largest, pair them in that order, and divide each pair; the triangles are similar exactly when the three quotients are equal, and that common quotient is the scale factor. Sides 3, 4, 5 against 6, 8, 10 give 2, 2 and 2, so the triangles are similar with scale factor 2, and since 3 squared plus 4 squared is 25 both are right-angled. Sides 6, 9, 12 against 4, 6, 8 give 1.5 each way round, so they are similar with scale factor 1.5 from the smaller to the larger. Sides 4, 6, 8 against 6, 9, 11 give 1.5, 1.5 and 1.375; one disagreement is enough to rule similarity out, and no amount of agreement in the other two pairs rescues it. The ordering step matters because dividing the largest side of one triangle by the smallest of the other produces a meaningless number. When angles are given instead, two equal pairs are sufficient because the third pair follows from the angle sum: 50 and 60 in one triangle with 60 and 70 in the other leaves 70 and 50 for the third angles, so both triangles are 50, 60, 70. Once similarity is established, a missing side comes from a ratio statement. For triangle PQR similar to triangle XYZ with PQ equal to 5 and XY equal to 15, the scale factor is 3, and QR equal to 7 gives YZ equal to 21; the layout writes XY over PQ equals YZ over QR with the reason similar triangles, substitutes the three known values, and solves.

### subtopic_4 — Problems and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** What does a full-marks congruence or similarity answer look like on paper?

**Tutor:** It has three parts in a fixed order: the facts, the statement, and the deduction. For the problem where AB equals DC, angle ABC equals angle DCB and BC is common, write the heading in triangle ABC and triangle DCB, then three numbered lines: AB equals DC, given; angle ABC equals angle DCB, given; BC is common. The statement follows: triangle ABC is congruent to triangle DCB, SAS, with a glance to confirm that the angle really is between the two sides in each triangle. The deduction is the line asked for: AC equals DB, congruent triangles, reading the matching letters. For similarity the facts are angle pairs or side ratios. In the problem with DE parallel to BC, D on AB and E on AC, the facts are angle A common and angle ADE equals angle ABC, corresponding angles with DE parallel to BC; the statement is triangle ADE similar to triangle ABC, AAA; the deduction is the ratio AB over AD equals BC over DE, similar triangles, then 10 over 4 equals BC over 5, so BC is 12.5. The error museum shows what loses marks: SSA or ASS as a reason, AAA for congruence, letters out of order, mismatched sides in a ratio, and unmarked facts such as the common side or vertically opposite angles left unused. Every line carries a reason, the statement carries the vertex order and the condition, and the ratio is written in letters before any numbers go in.
