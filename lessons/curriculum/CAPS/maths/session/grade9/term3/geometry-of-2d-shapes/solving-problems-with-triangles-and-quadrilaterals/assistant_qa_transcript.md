### subtopic_1 — Angle Chasing in Quadrilaterals
**Assistant:** When a quadrilateral problem has an unknown angle, how do I choose which property to start from?

**Tutor:** Start from the property that connects a given angle to the unknown in one step, and if there is none, find an angle that both can reach. In the kite with angles of 50 and 110 at the ends of the axis, the unknown side angles are connected to the knowns only through the angle sum, so the kite's own property, that the two side angles are equal, is the first line, and the angle sum of 360 is the second: 50 plus 110 plus 2x equals 360, giving x equal to 100. In the rhombus with angle ABC equal to 70, the unknown angles at the diagonals are reached through angle BAD, so the first line is the consecutive-angle property giving 110, and the second is the diagonal property that bisects it into 55. In the trapezium with AB parallel to DC, angle A equal to 115 and angle D equal to x, the only property a trapezium offers is the co-interior pair on each non-parallel side, so 115 plus x equals 180 is forced as the first line, then angle B as 2x plus 10 evaluates to 140, and angle C follows from the other co-interior pair as 40. Two things keep the choice honest: the shape must be named in the reason, so that a parallelogram property is never used on a trapezium, and the final check against 360 catches a wrong first step. With algebra the property becomes an equation, as with the parallelogram whose consecutive angles 3x minus 20 and x plus 40 give 4x plus 20 equals 180 and x equal to 40.

### subtopic_2 — Triangles Inside Quadrilaterals
**Assistant:** How do I know when to switch from quadrilateral rules to triangle rules inside the same problem?

**Tutor:** Switch whenever the unknown lies inside a triangle that a diagonal or an extra line has created, and keep the quadrilateral rules for angles at the quadrilateral's own vertices. In the rectangle with E the midpoint of AB, the lines ED and EC create triangles AED and BEC, and the question about ED and EC is a triangle question: the three facts AE equals EB, angle A equals angle B equals 90, and AD equals BC come from the midpoint and from the rectangle, and the congruence by SAS is a triangle conclusion that yields ED equals EC. Once angle ADE is given as 32, the angle sum in triangle AED gives angle AED as 58, the congruence transfers 58 to angle BEC, and angles on a straight line give angle DEC as 64. In the isosceles triangle with BC extended and the exterior angle at C equal to 124, everything is a triangle: angles on a straight line give 56, angles opposite equal sides give 56 again, and the angle sum gives 68 at A, with the exterior angle fact as a check since 68 plus 56 is 124. In the parallelogram with diagonal AC, angle DAC equal to 35 and angle B equal to 80, the alternate angles between the parallel sides move the 35 across to angle ACB, the triangle ABC then gives angle BAC as 65, and the quadrilateral rule returns to give angle BCD as 100 by opposite angles. The one property to handle with care is the diagonal of a square bisecting its right angle into 45s: a rectangle's diagonal does not do this unless the rectangle is a square.

### subtopic_3 — Unknown Sides from Congruent and Similar Triangles
**Assistant:** Which tool finds a missing side, and how do I set out the similar-triangles version?

**Tutor:** Three tools find sides: a shape property that says two sides are equal, a congruence that makes a side in one triangle equal to a side in another, and a similarity that makes sides proportional. Shape properties are the quickest: opposite sides of a parallelogram are equal, so 2x plus 1 equals 3x minus 4 and x is 5; the diagonals of a rectangle are equal and bisect each other, so AO equal to 6.5 gives BD equal to 13; all sides of a rhombus are equal, so a side of 7 gives a perimeter of 28. Congruence follows the facts-statement-deduction layout, and the deduction line names the equal sides with the reason congruent triangles, as ED equals EC did in the rectangle. Similarity needs the ratio written in letters before any numbers. For the triangle with DE parallel to BC, AD equal to 3, DB equal to 5 and DE equal to 4.5, first establish the similarity: angle A is common, angle ADE equals angle ABC by corresponding angles with DE parallel to BC, so triangle ADE is similar to triangle ABC by AAA. Then write BC over DE equals AB over AD with the reason similar triangles, note that AB is 8, substitute to get BC over 4.5 equals 8 over 3, and solve for BC equal to 12. The same layout measures the building from shadows: the triangles are similar because the sun's rays are parallel, the scale factor is 15 over 1.2, which is 12.5, and 1.8 times 12.5 gives 22.5 metres. Matching the right pair of sides in the ratio is the step that goes wrong most often, so write the small triangle's sides under the large triangle's sides in the same order.

### subtopic_4 — Multi-Step Problems and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** What does a full-marks multi-step answer look like, and where are the marks usually lost?

**Tutor:** The full-marks answer follows collect, name, chain, check. For the rhombus ABCD with diagonals meeting at O and angle ADC equal to 100, the collected facts are four equal sides and diagonals that bisect each other at right angles and bisect the angles; the name is rhombus; the chain is angle DAB equals 80 by consecutive angles of a rhombus, angle OAB equals 40 by diagonals of a rhombus bisect the angles, angle AOB equals 90 by diagonals of a rhombus are perpendicular, and angle ABO equals 50 by the angle sum in triangle AOB; the check is that angle ABC equals 100 by opposite angles and half of it is 50. In the isosceles trapezium PQRS with PQ parallel to SR and angle P equal to 70, the base angles are equal so angle Q is 70, the co-interior pairs give 110 at S and R, and with diagonal PR and angle QPR equal to 40 the alternate angles give angle PRS equal to 40, angle SPR is 30, and the triangle PSR check reads 30 plus 110 plus 40 equals 180. Marks are lost in five ways, which the error museum displays: a square property used in a rectangle, such as a 45 degree diagonal angle; a parallelogram property used on a kite or on the non-parallel pair of a trapezium; a congruence written without its three facts or with the letters out of order; a similarity ratio with mismatched sides; and correct numbers with no reasons, which earn the answer mark alone. The layout that avoids all five lists the facts, names every shape and triangle used, writes statement, equals, value, reason on each line, solves equations with their reasons, substitutes back, gives units, and ends with a check.
