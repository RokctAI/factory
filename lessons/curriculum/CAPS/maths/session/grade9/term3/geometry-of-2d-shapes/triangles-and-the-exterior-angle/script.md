# Part 1 — Expert

Today we begin the geometry of two-dimensional shapes with the triangle, the shape every other polygon is built from. We revise the definitions that classify triangles by their sides and by their angles, we use the interior angle sum of 180 degrees that last term's parallel lines proved, and we investigate by construction the exterior angle of a triangle, discovering and then proving that it equals the sum of the two interior angles opposite it. Every result is stated as a fact with a name, because in Grade 9 geometry the name is the reason, and the reason is the mark.

## Subtopic: Classifying Triangles by Sides and Angles

A triangle is a closed figure with three straight sides and three angles. Triangles are classified in two independent ways. By sides: a scalene triangle has three sides of different lengths; an isosceles triangle has two equal sides, marked with matching dashes; an equilateral triangle has three equal sides. By angles: an acute-angled triangle has all three angles less than 90 degrees; a right-angled triangle has one angle of exactly 90 degrees, marked with a small square; an obtuse-angled triangle has one angle greater than 90 degrees. A triangle can never have two right angles or two obtuse angles, because two such angles would already use up 180 degrees or more.

The two classifications combine. A triangle can be isosceles and right-angled, with angles 90, 45 and 45. It can be scalene and obtuse. It cannot be equilateral and right-angled, because equilateral forces every angle to 60 degrees.

The isosceles triangle carries a fact that is used constantly: the angles opposite the equal sides are equal. They are called the base angles, whichever way the triangle is drawn, and the reason is written as angles opposite equal sides, or base angles of an isosceles triangle. The converse is also true: if two angles of a triangle are equal, the sides opposite them are equal, and the triangle is isosceles. An equilateral triangle therefore has three equal angles, each 60 degrees.

Naming follows the same convention as last term: triangle ABC has vertices A, B and C, side AB is the side joining A and B, and the angle at A is angle BAC or simply angle A when no confusion is possible. The side opposite a vertex is the one that does not touch it: side BC is opposite A. The isosceles fact is always stated in terms of opposite: the angle opposite one equal side equals the angle opposite the other.

Reading the marks on a diagram is the first step in every problem. Matching dashes mean equal sides; a small square means 90 degrees; matching arcs mean equal angles. Nothing that the marks do not show, or the question does not state, may be assumed, however the sketch looks. The questions for this section are with you now: the three names by sides, the three names by angles, and the base angles of an isosceles triangle.

## Subtopic: The Interior Angle Sum of a Triangle

The three interior angles of any triangle add to 180 degrees. Last term's two-transversal figure proved it: with the triangle's base on one of two parallel lines and its apex on the other, alternate angles carry the two base angles up to the apex, where they and the apex angle fill a straight line. The reason is written as sum of angles in a triangle, or angle sum of a triangle, and it is the most used reason in the whole of Grade 9 geometry.

Finding a third angle: interior angles of 50 and 60 degrees give a third of 180 minus 110, which is 70 degrees, sum of angles in a triangle. In an isosceles triangle with apex angle 40 degrees, the two base angles are equal and together make 180 minus 40, which is 140, so each is 70 degrees. The chain has two reasons: sum of angles in a triangle, and base angles of an isosceles triangle.

With algebra: a triangle has angles x, 2x and 3x. Then x plus 2x plus 3x equals 180, sum of angles in a triangle, so 6x equals 180 and x equals 30. The angles are 30, 60 and 90 degrees, and the triangle is right-angled. The algebra from Term 2 is unchanged; only the reason attached to the equation is new.

Problems often chain two triangles. If two triangles share a side, or one triangle sits inside another, find all the angles of the first triangle before moving to the second, and write the chain in the order the angles can actually be found. The angle sum applies to each triangle separately, and the same angle may serve as a known value in both.

A right-angled triangle has its other two angles adding to 90 degrees, because the right angle has already used 90 of the 180. Those two angles are complementary. Knowing one of them, say 35 degrees, gives the other as 55 degrees in one step, sum of angles in a triangle. Your questions on this section: the 180 and its proof, two reasons in an isosceles chain, and an equation from three expressions.

## Subtopic: The Exterior Angle by Construction

Extend one side of a triangle beyond a vertex. The angle between the extension and the other side at that vertex is an exterior angle of the triangle. Construct a triangle with interior angles of 50 degrees at A and 60 degrees at B, so that the angle at C is 70 degrees. Extend side BC beyond C and measure the exterior angle at C: it reads 110 degrees. Repeat with a different triangle, angles 35 and 85 at A and B, so 60 at C, and the exterior angle at C measures 120 degrees. In each case the exterior angle equals the sum of the two interior angles at the other two vertices: 50 plus 60 is 110, and 35 plus 85 is 120.

The investigation suggests the fact; the proof settles it. The exterior angle at C and the interior angle at C lie on the straight line BC extended, so exterior angle equals 180 minus angle C, angles on a str line. The angle sum of the triangle gives angle A plus angle B equals 180 minus angle C, sum of angles in a triangle. Both are 180 minus angle C, so the exterior angle equals angle A plus angle B. The reason is written as exterior angle of a triangle, and it is stated as: the exterior angle of a triangle is equal to the sum of the two interior opposite angles.

The word opposite matters. The exterior angle at C is related to the interior angles at A and B, the two vertices away from C, not to the interior angle at C, which is simply its supplement on the straight line. Learners who add the adjacent interior angle have used the wrong pair.

Measurement in a construction is never exact, and a result that reads 109 or 111 for a predicted 110 is within the tolerance of a protractor. The investigation's job is to suggest the relationship across several triangles; the proof's job is to show it holds exactly for every triangle, which no number of measurements can do.

Each vertex has two exterior angles, one on each extended side, and they are equal to each other by vertically opposite angles, so there is no ambiguity in speaking of the exterior angle at a vertex. Going round a triangle, the three exterior angles, one at each vertex, add to 360 degrees, a fact that Term 3 will extend to every polygon. The questions for this section are ready: the construction and the measurement, the two-line proof, and which two interior angles are the opposite ones.

## Subtopic: Problems and the Error Museum

An exterior angle at C measures 120 degrees and the interior angle at A is 45 degrees. Find angle B. Exterior angle equals A plus B, so 120 equals 45 plus B and B is 75 degrees, exterior angle of a triangle. Check through the interior angle at C: 180 minus 120 is 60, and 45 plus 75 plus 60 is 180.

With algebra: the exterior angle at C is 2x plus 10, and the interior angles at A and B are x plus 15 and 35. Then 2x plus 10 equals x plus 15 plus 35, exterior angle of a triangle, so 2x plus 10 equals x plus 50 and x is 40. The exterior angle is 90 degrees and the interior angles are 55 and 35 degrees. Check: 55 plus 35 is 90.

An isosceles triangle with an exterior angle of 100 degrees at the apex: the apex interior angle is 80 degrees, angles on a str line, and the base angles are each 50 degrees, sum of angles in a triangle and base angles of an isosceles triangle. Alternatively, the exterior angle 100 equals the sum of the two base angles, so each is 50 directly.

The error museum, five exhibits. Exhibit one: the exterior angle set equal to the sum of the adjacent interior angle and one other, instead of the two opposite interior angles. Exhibit two: an answer in which an exterior angle exceeds 180 degrees, or an interior angle is negative, accepted without a check; the sizes must make sense. Exhibit three: the equal angles of an isosceles triangle placed at the wrong vertices; the base angles are opposite the equal sides, not next to them. Exhibit four: a right angle assumed from a sketch that has no square mark. Exhibit five: the correct value with the reason sum of angles in a triangle when the exterior angle fact was used, or vice versa; the reason must match the step.

A question can also run the exterior angle fact in reverse to find an exterior angle from two interior ones, or combine it with the isosceles fact across two triangles that share a vertex. Whatever the combination, the method is one fact per line, named, and a check at the end.

Layout for marks: read the marks on the diagram and list the facts available; write each statement as angle, equals, value or expression, reason; form and solve any equation with its reason; substitute back; state every angle asked for with its unit; check the angle sum. The questions for this section are with you now: exterior angle problems with numbers and with algebra, the isosceles chain, and the five exhibits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same triangle facts again through sorting boxes, a half-turn folded into three corners, and an outside angle that equals the two far corners — plain words, same rules.

## Subtopic: Sorting Triangles Into Boxes

Every triangle goes into two boxes at once, one for its sides and one for its angles. Sides box: three different sides is scalene; two the same is isosceles, marked with little dashes on the two equal sides; all three the same is equilateral. Angles box: all three corners sharp, under 90, is acute; one corner exactly a square corner is right-angled, marked with a tiny square; one corner wider than a square corner is obtuse.

A triangle can be in any sides box and any angles box, with one exception: an equilateral triangle is always acute, because its corners are all 60. You can have a right-angled isosceles triangle, 90, 45, 45, like a square cut along the diagonal. You can have an obtuse scalene triangle. But you can never have two square corners or two wide corners, because they would use up the whole half-turn before the third corner got anything.

The isosceles box has a bonus rule. The two equal sides sit opposite two equal angles, called the base angles. Picture a roof: two equal rafters, and the two angles where they meet the floor are equal. The angle at the top can be anything. It works backwards too: two equal angles mean the two sides facing them are equal.

Names: the corners are A, B and C, the side between two corners uses their two letters, and the side opposite a corner is the one that does not touch it. The roof rule is about opposites: equal sides face equal angles.

Before you do anything with a triangle, read its marks. Dashes mean equal sides. A tiny square means 90. Matching arcs mean equal angles. If the picture looks right-angled but has no square, it is not right-angled as far as you are concerned. Your questions on this section are ready: the two boxes, the roof rule for isosceles, and reading the marks.

## Subtopic: Half a Turn Folded Into Three Corners

Tear the three corners off a paper triangle and put them together, point to point. They make a straight line, a half-turn, 180. Every triangle does this. Last term proved why with two parallel lines and a Z twice, but the paper shows it in ten seconds. The reason you write is sum of angles in a triangle.

Two corners known, third corner found: 50 and 60 use up 110 of the 180, leaving 70. A roof with a 40 at the top: the two floor angles share the remaining 140, so 70 each. Two reasons in that chain: the half-turn, and the roof rule.

Letters: corners x, 2x and 3x. Together they are the half-turn: 6x equals 180, so x is 30. The corners are 30, 60 and 90, and the 90 means it is secretly right-angled. Put x back; x is the key, not the corner.

A square corner takes 90, so the other two corners share the other 90. One is 35, the other is 55. Half-turn, read the marks, write the reason. The questions for this section: the torn-corner half-turn, the roof chain, and the equation from three letters.

## Subtopic: The Outside Angle Equals the Two Far Corners

Take a triangle and stretch one side straight out past a corner, like a ramp running off the end. The angle between the ramp and the other side at that corner is the outside angle, the exterior angle. Measure it. Now measure the two corners at the FAR end of the triangle, the two corners you did not stretch past. Add them. Same number. A triangle with far corners 50 and 60 has an outside angle of 110. One with far corners 35 and 85 has an outside angle of 120. Every time.

Why? The outside angle and the corner next to it sit on the ramp's straight line, so they make 180 together. The two far corners and that same corner make 180 too, the half-turn in every triangle. Both are 180 take away the same corner, so the outside angle equals the two far corners. Two lines, done. The reason: exterior angle of a triangle.

Use it forwards: outside angle 120, one far corner 45, so the other far corner is 75. Use it with letters: outside angle 2x plus 10, far corners x plus 15 and 35. 2x plus 10 equals x plus 50, so x is 40. Outside angle 90, far corners 55 and 35, and 55 plus 35 is 90.

Each corner actually has two outside angles, one on each ramp, and they are the same size because they are opposite twins through the X. So it does not matter which side you stretch.

The trap is grabbing the near corner instead of the far ones. The near corner is just the half-turn partner of the outside angle. The far corners are the two it equals. Say it as a rhythm: outside equals the two far corners.

Letters work the same way: write outside equals far plus far, sort the fruit, undo the buttons, put x back, and check that the two far corners really do add to the outside angle.

The roof again: a roof with an outside angle of 100 at the top has two floor angles adding to 100, so 50 each. Sort into boxes, tear the corners into a half-turn, outside equals the two far corners. The final questions of the lesson are with you now: the ramp, the two-line reason, and far corners not the near one.
