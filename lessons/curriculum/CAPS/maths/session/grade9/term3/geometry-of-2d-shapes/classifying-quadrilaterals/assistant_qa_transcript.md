### subtopic_1 — Defining the Six Quadrilaterals
**Assistant:** Why does the definition of a rectangle mention only one right angle when every rectangle has four?

**Tutor:** Because a definition states the least that forces the shape, and one right angle is enough once the shape is already a parallelogram. In a parallelogram the consecutive angles are co-interior between the parallel sides, so they add to 180 degrees. If one angle is 90, its two neighbours are each 180 minus 90, which is 90, and the opposite angle equals the first, which is 90 again. All four right angles follow from one, so writing four right angles in the definition claims more than is needed. The same economy governs the rhombus: a parallelogram already has opposite sides equal, so two adjacent sides being equal makes all four equal, and the definition says adjacent sides equal rather than four equal sides. The square can be defined as a rectangle with two adjacent sides equal or as a rhombus with a right angle, and the two definitions describe the same shape. The trapezium and kite are defined directly from the general quadrilateral: one pair of opposite sides parallel for the trapezium, two pairs of adjacent sides equal for the kite. On a diagram the definitions are read from marks: matching arrowheads for parallel sides, matching dashes for equal sides, a small square for a right angle. A shape is named only from its marks or from what the question states, never from how it looks.

### subtopic_2 — Properties of Sides and Angles
**Assistant:** How do I lay out a parallelogram angle problem so that each line earns its reason?

**Tutor:** Name the shape, then use one property per line with the shape in the reason. Suppose ABCD is a parallelogram with angle A equal to 50 degrees. Line one: angle C equals 50 degrees, opposite angles of a parallelogram. Line two: angle B equals 180 minus 50, which is 130 degrees, co-interior angles with AD parallel to BC, or consecutive angles of a parallelogram. Line three: angle D equals 130 degrees, opposite angles of a parallelogram. Finish with the check that 50 plus 130 plus 50 plus 130 is 360, the sum of angles in a quadrilateral. With algebra the same chain produces an equation: consecutive angles x and 2x plus 30 add to 180, so 3x plus 30 equals 180, 3x equals 150 and x equals 50; substitute back for 50 and 130, then fill the opposite angles. The angle sum of 360 is itself a reason, used when three angles of any quadrilateral are known and the fourth is wanted: 90, 85 and 110 add to 285, so the fourth is 75 degrees. The trapezium offers one angle property only, the co-interior pair at each end of a non-parallel side, and the kite offers one pair of equal opposite angles, at the vertices where the unequal sides meet. Using a parallelogram property on a trapezium or kite is the commonest way to lose the reason mark.

### subtopic_3 — Properties of Diagonals
**Assistant:** Which diagonal properties belong to which shape, and how do I keep them straight?

**Tutor:** Memorise the properties in a fixed order and attach the shapes to each one. First, diagonals bisect each other: true for every parallelogram, so for the rectangle, rhombus and square as well; false for the trapezium, and only one diagonal of a kite is bisected. Second, diagonals are equal: rectangle and square only. Third, diagonals cross at right angles: rhombus and square, and the kite's axis of symmetry crosses the other diagonal at right angles. Fourth, diagonals bisect the angles at their ends: rhombus and square, and the kite's axis bisects the angles at its own two ends. Each property becomes a reason in a problem and creates triangles in which the triangle facts apply. In a rhombus with angle A equal to 60 degrees and diagonals meeting at O, the diagonal from A bisects the angle to give 30 degrees, the diagonals cross at 90 degrees, and the angle sum in the small triangle gives 60 degrees at the third vertex. In a rectangle the two equal diagonals bisect each other, so the four segments from the centre are equal and the figure is divided into four isosceles triangles whose base angles follow from the angle sum. The investigation next lesson draws each shape, measures the pieces of the diagonals and the angles at the crossing, and confirms this table by construction before it is used as a set of reasons.

### subtopic_4 — The Family Tree and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** How do I decide whether a statement like every rhombus is a rectangle is true or false?

**Tutor:** Test it against the definitions, and look for one counterexample if you suspect it is false. Every rhombus is a rectangle asks whether having four equal sides forces a right angle. It does not: a rhombus with angles of 60 and 120 degrees has four equal sides and no right angle, so the statement is false and that one shape settles it. Every rectangle is a parallelogram asks whether the definition of a rectangle includes the definition of a parallelogram; it does, since a rectangle is a parallelogram with a right angle, so the statement is true and the reason is the definition. In the family tree the arrows run downward from quadrilateral to parallelogram to rectangle and rhombus to square, and a statement every X is a Y is true exactly when Y sits above X on the same branch or is X itself. The error museum collects the ways the tree is misread: saying a square is not a rectangle because its sides are equal, when it meets the definition of a rectangle; using diagonals at right angles in a parallelogram not marked as a rhombus; drawing a kite with equal opposite sides, which makes it a parallelogram; claiming a trapezium's diagonals bisect each other; and writing a long list of properties where a short definition was asked for. The layout for marks reads the marks, names the shape, states each property with the shape in the reason, and checks with the 360 sum.
