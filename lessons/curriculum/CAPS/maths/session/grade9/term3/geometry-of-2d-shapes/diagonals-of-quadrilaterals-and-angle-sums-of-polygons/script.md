# Part 1 — Expert

Last lesson we stated what the diagonals of each quadrilateral do. Today we check those statements with a ruler and a protractor, because a property you have measured for yourself is a property you believe and remember, and then we turn the same triangle-splitting idea on polygons with five, six, eight and more sides to find their interior angle sums. The two halves of the lesson share a method: draw accurately, split into triangles, measure or count, and write the result as a rule with its reason. By the end you will be able to use diagonal properties as reasons in problems and to find the angle sum of any polygon, and the size of each angle when the polygon is regular.

## Subtopic: Constructing and Measuring Diagonals

An investigation by construction has four steps: draw the shape accurately from its definition, draw both diagonals, measure the pieces and the angles at the crossing, and record what you find. Begin with a rectangle 8 centimetres by 6 centimetres. Draw the two diagonals and label the crossing point O. Measure each diagonal: both are 10 centimetres, because a rectangle 8 by 6 has a diagonal of 10 by the 3-4-5 pattern scaled by 2. Measure the four pieces from O to the corners: all four are 5 centimetres. Measure the angles at O: two equal acute angles and two equal obtuse angles, not 90. Record: the diagonals of a rectangle are equal and bisect each other, but are not perpendicular.

Now a square of side 6 centimetres. The diagonals are again equal and the four pieces from O are equal, and this time the protractor at O reads 90 degrees, and each diagonal makes 45 degrees with the sides at its ends. Record: equal, bisect each other, perpendicular, bisect the angles.

A parallelogram with sides 7 and 4 and an angle of 60 degrees. The two diagonals are visibly unequal; measure them to confirm. But the two pieces of each diagonal are equal to each other: O is the midpoint of both. The angles at O are not 90. Record: bisect each other, nothing more.

A rhombus of side 5 centimetres with an angle of 60 degrees. The pieces of each diagonal are equal, the angle at O is 90, and the diagonal from the 60 degree vertex makes 30 degrees with each side, so it bisects the angle. The diagonals are unequal. Record: bisect each other, perpendicular, bisect the angles, not equal.

Finally a kite with sides 4, 4, 6, 6. The axis of symmetry is the diagonal joining the two vertices where unequal sides meet. Measure: the axis cuts the other diagonal into two equal pieces and crosses it at 90 degrees, and it bisects the angles at its own two ends. The other diagonal does not cut the axis into equal pieces. Record: one diagonal is the perpendicular bisector of the other.

A measurement within a degree or a millimetre of the claim counts as confirming it; protractors and pencils are not exact, and an investigation looks for a pattern across several shapes rather than a perfect single reading. The questions for this section are with you now: the four steps, the rectangle 8 by 6, and what each shape's diagonals were measured to do.

## Subtopic: Recording the Investigation as a Table

The results go into a table with the five shapes down the side and four properties across the top: diagonals bisect each other, diagonals equal, diagonals perpendicular, diagonals bisect the angles. The parallelogram ticks the first. The rectangle ticks the first two. The rhombus ticks the first, third and fourth. The square ticks all four. The kite ticks a modified third, since only the axis is perpendicular to the other diagonal and bisects it, and a modified fourth, since only the axis bisects its own end angles. The trapezium, had we drawn it, would tick none.

The table reads in both directions, and the backward direction is what the investigation adds. Forwards: a rectangle has equal diagonals. Backwards: if a quadrilateral's diagonals are equal and bisect each other, it is a rectangle. Forwards: a parallelogram's diagonals bisect each other. Backwards: if a quadrilateral's diagonals bisect each other, it is a parallelogram. Forwards: a rhombus has diagonals that are perpendicular bisectors of each other. Backwards: a quadrilateral whose diagonals are perpendicular bisectors of each other is a rhombus. Grade 10 will prove these converses; Grade 9 observes them by construction and uses them to name a shape from information about its diagonals.

Diagonals as reasons. ABCD is a rectangle with diagonals meeting at O and AO equal to 5 centimetres. Then BD is 10 centimetres, because the diagonals of a rectangle are equal and bisect each other, so each of the four pieces is 5. Next, in the same rectangle, the diagonal AC makes an angle of 35 degrees with side AB. Triangle AOB has AO equal to BO, so it is isosceles, and angle OBA is also 35 degrees, angles opposite equal sides. The angle AOB is 180 minus 70, which is 110 degrees, sum of angles in a triangle. The neighbouring triangle BOC has angle BOC equal to 70 degrees, angles on a straight line, and its two base angles are 55 each.

The rhombus version. ABCD is a rhombus with angle A equal to 60 degrees and diagonals meeting at O. Angle OAB is 30, the diagonals of a rhombus bisect the angles; angle AOB is 90, the diagonals of a rhombus are perpendicular; angle ABO is 60, sum of angles in a triangle. Every statement names the shape. Your questions on this section are ready: the table, reading it backwards, and the rectangle with the 35 degree angle.

## Subtopic: The Interior Angle Sum of Polygons

A polygon is a closed figure with straight sides, and an n-gon has n sides and n interior angles. Choose one vertex and draw every diagonal from it. A quadrilateral splits into 2 triangles, a pentagon into 3, a hexagon into 4, an octagon into 6; in general an n-gon splits into n minus 2 triangles, because the chosen vertex and its two neighbours are not joined by diagonals. Each triangle contributes 180 degrees and the triangle angles together make up exactly the polygon's interior angles, so the interior angle sum of an n-gon is n minus 2, times 180.

Build the table. Triangle: 180. Quadrilateral: 360. Pentagon: 3 times 180, which is 540. Hexagon: 720. Heptagon: 900. Octagon: 1080. Decagon: 1440. Dodecagon: 1800. Each extra side adds one triangle and 180 degrees.

A regular polygon has all sides equal and all angles equal, so each interior angle is the sum divided by n. Regular pentagon: 540 divided by 5, which is 108 degrees. Regular hexagon: 720 divided by 6, which is 120. Regular octagon: 1080 divided by 8, which is 135. Regular decagon: 144. The 120 of the hexagon is why hexagons tile a floor and bees build in hexagons: three of them meet at a point to make a full 360.

Exterior angles have their own sum. Extend each side in turn and the exterior angle at each vertex is 180 minus the interior angle. Walking round the polygon you turn through every exterior angle once and end up facing the way you started, a full turn, so the exterior angles of any polygon add to 360 degrees. For a regular n-gon each exterior angle is 360 divided by n: hexagon 60, octagon 45, decagon 36. Interior and exterior at a vertex add to 180, which is a fast check: hexagon 120 plus 60, octagon 135 plus 45.

Finding n from an angle. A regular polygon has interior angles of 150 degrees. The exterior angle is 30, so n is 360 divided by 30, which is 12. Alternatively n minus 2 times 180 divided by n equals 150 gives 180n minus 360 equals 150n, 30n equals 360, n equals 12. Both routes land on the dodecagon, and the exterior route is shorter. The questions for this section are ready: the triangle count, the table, the regular formulas, and n from 150.

## Subtopic: Problems and the Error Museum

Problem one. Four angles of a pentagon are 100, 110, 120 and 130 degrees. The sum is 540, sum of angles in a pentagon; the four known angles add to 460; the fifth is 80 degrees.

Problem two, with algebra. The angles of a pentagon are x, x plus 10, x plus 20, x plus 30 and x plus 40. Their sum is 5x plus 100, which equals 540, so 5x equals 440 and x equals 88. The angles are 88, 98, 108, 118 and 128 degrees. Check: 88 plus 98 is 186, plus 108 is 294, plus 118 is 412, plus 128 is 540.

Problem three. A regular polygon has an exterior angle of 24 degrees. Then n is 360 divided by 24, which is 15, and each interior angle is 156 degrees. Check: 13 times 180 is 2340, divided by 15 is 156.

Problem four, diagonals. PQRS is a parallelogram with diagonals meeting at N, PN equal to 3x minus 1 and NR equal to x plus 7. The diagonals of a parallelogram bisect each other, so 3x minus 1 equals x plus 7, 2x equals 8 and x equals 4. PN is 11, NR is 11 and PR is 22. If PQRS had been given as a rectangle with QS equal to 22 as well, the equal-diagonals property would be the extra reason; if it had been a rhombus, angle PNQ would be 90.

The error museum, five exhibits. Exhibit one: the n minus 2 formula applied with n instead of n minus 2, giving a hexagon 1080 instead of 720; count the triangles from one vertex to recover the subtraction. Exhibit two: dividing the angle sum by n for a polygon that is not regular; equal angles need the word regular or marks on every angle. Exhibit three: exterior angles adding to something other than 360; the walk round the block always turns through one full turn. Exhibit four: using equal diagonals as a reason in a parallelogram or rhombus; only the rectangle and square earn that line. Exhibit five: drawing diagonals from several vertices when counting triangles, so that triangles overlap and the count exceeds n minus 2; use one vertex only.

Layout for marks: name the polygon and state its sum with the reason, add the known angles, subtract, substitute back when there is algebra, and check that the total is right. In diagonal problems, name the quadrilateral, name the property with the shape in the reason, write the equation, solve, and check that the lengths or angles agree with the picture. The questions for this section are with you now: the pentagon problems, n from an exterior angle, the parallelogram with 3x minus 1, and the five exhibits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through two sticks you measure yourself, a polygon cut into pizza slices from one corner, and a walk around the block — plain words, same rules.

## Subtopic: Draw It, Measure It, Believe It

Last time you were told what the two sticks inside each shape do. Today you draw the shapes and check with a ruler and protractor, like testing a rumour before you repeat it.

Draw a rectangle 8 by 6. Put in both diagonals, the two sticks, and mark the crossing O. Measure the sticks: both 10. Measure the four pieces from O to the corners: all 5. So the sticks are equal and they cut each other in half. Put the protractor at O: not 90. So they do not cross at a right angle. That is the rectangle's report card.

Draw a square of side 6. Equal sticks, pieces all equal, and now the protractor at O says 90, and each stick cuts its corner into two 45s. Full marks on every property.

Draw a pushed-over rectangle, a parallelogram. Sticks of different lengths, but each stick is cut exactly in half by the other. Draw a diamond, a rhombus, with a 60 corner. Sticks cut in half, cross at 90, and the stick from the 60 corner splits it into two 30s. Draw a kite with sides 4, 4, 6, 6. Only the stick down the middle does anything: it cuts the crossways stick in half at a right angle, and halves the two angles at its own ends.

Readings a millimetre or a degree off are fine; a pencil line has width. You are looking for a pattern that repeats every time you draw the shape, not a single perfect reading. The questions for this section are with you now: the rectangle 8 by 6, what the protractor at O said for each shape, and which kite stick does the work.

## Subtopic: Cutting a Polygon Into Triangles

Any many-sided shape can be cut into triangles from one corner, like pizza slices that all start at the same point on the crust. Stand at one corner and draw a line to every corner you are not already joined to. A five-sided shape gives 3 triangles, a six-sided shape gives 4, an eight-sided shape gives 6. Always two fewer triangles than sides, because the corner you stand on and its two neighbours are already connected by the sides.

Each triangle's corners add to 180, and the triangle corners fit together exactly to make the polygon's corners. So the polygon's angle sum is the number of triangles times 180: sides minus 2, times 180. Pentagon 540, hexagon 720, octagon 1080. Every extra side is one extra slice and 180 more degrees.

Use it like the quadrilateral's 360. A pentagon with four corners of 100, 110, 120 and 130 has a fifth corner of 540 minus 460, which is 80. With letters, corners x, x plus 10, x plus 20, x plus 30 and x plus 40 add to 5x plus 100, set that equal to 540, x is 88, and the corners are 88, 98, 108, 118 and 128.

Regular means every side the same and every corner the same, so one corner is the total shared equally. Regular pentagon: 540 shared by 5 is 108. Regular hexagon: 720 shared by 6 is 120, which is why three hexagons fit together at a point with no gap, 3 times 120 is a full 360, the beehive pattern. Regular octagon: 135, the stop-sign corner. Do not share the total equally unless the word regular is there. Your questions on this section are ready: slices from one corner, sides minus 2, and the beehive 120.

## Subtopic: Regular Shapes and the Walk Around the Block

Walk around any polygon along its edges. At each corner you turn through the outside angle, the exterior angle, to face along the next side. When you get back to the start you are facing the way you began, so all your turns together make one full turn: 360. Any polygon, any number of sides, the exterior angles add to 360.

For a regular polygon every turn is the same size, so each exterior angle is 360 shared by the number of sides. Hexagon: 60. Octagon: 45. Twelve sides: 30. The inside and outside angle at a corner sit on a straight line, so they add to 180, which gives a fast check: hexagon 120 inside plus 60 outside, octagon 135 plus 45.

The walk is the quickest way to find the number of sides. A regular polygon with inside angles of 150: the outside angle is 30, and 360 shared into 30s gives 12 sides. Exterior angle 24: 360 divided by 24 is 15 sides, and each inside angle is 156.

Back to the sticks for one problem. PQRS is a parallelogram, sticks crossing at N, PN is 3x minus 1 and NR is x plus 7. A parallelogram's sticks cut each other in half, so the two pieces are equal: 3x minus 1 equals x plus 7, x is 4, each piece is 11, the whole stick is 22.

Say it: measure the sticks, slice from one corner, sides minus 2 times 180, walk the block for 360. The final questions of the lesson are with you now: the full turn, 360 shared by the sides, sides from 150 and from 24, and the parallelogram with 3x minus 1.
