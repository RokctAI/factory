### subtopic_1 — Constructing and Measuring Diagonals
**Assistant:** My measurements never come out exactly right. How do I know whether the investigation has confirmed a property?

**Tutor:** Judge the pattern, not a single reading, and allow the tolerance that pencils and protractors impose. A sharp pencil line is about half a millimetre wide and a school protractor is marked in single degrees, so a piece that should be 5 centimetres may read 4.9 or 5.1, and an angle that should be 90 degrees may read 89 or 91. The investigation confirms a property when every shape of that kind shows it within that tolerance: draw two or three rectangles of different sizes, and in each one both diagonals come out equal to each other and the four pieces from the crossing come out equal. The rectangle 8 by 6 is a good first case because its diagonal is exactly 10, by the 3-4-5 pattern doubled, so the halves of 5 are easy to check. What the investigation cannot do is prove the property for every rectangle, since you can only draw finitely many; that is the job of the Grade 10 proofs. What it can do, which a stated rule cannot, is make the property your own, and expose a false belief at once: a learner who expects a parallelogram's diagonals to be equal measures 9.2 and 6.1 centimetres and abandons the belief on the spot. Record the measurements in a table as you go, one row per shape, so the pattern is visible when you finish.

### subtopic_2 — Recording the Investigation as a Table
**Assistant:** What does it mean to read the table backwards, and when would I need to?

**Tutor:** Reading forwards starts from the shape and gives the property: a rectangle has equal diagonals that bisect each other. Reading backwards starts from the property and names the shape: a quadrilateral whose diagonals are equal and bisect each other is a rectangle. The backward reading matters when a question gives information about the diagonals and asks what the quadrilateral is, or when a question asks for the extra condition that turns one shape into another. If the diagonals of a quadrilateral bisect each other, it is a parallelogram; if in addition they are equal, it is a rectangle; if in addition they are perpendicular, it is a rhombus; if they are equal and perpendicular and bisect each other, it is a square. The construction supports each backward statement: draw two segments that bisect each other and join their ends, and the figure is always a parallelogram. In problems the forward reading supplies the reasons. In rectangle ABCD with diagonals meeting at O and AO equal to 5, BD is 10 because the diagonals of a rectangle are equal and bisect each other. If AC makes 35 degrees with AB, triangle AOB is isosceles with AO equal to BO, so angle OBA is 35 by angles opposite equal sides and angle AOB is 110 by the angle sum; the neighbouring angle BOC is 70 by angles on a straight line and the base angles of triangle BOC are 55 each. The shape is named in every reason, since the property belongs to the rectangle and not to quadrilaterals in general.

### subtopic_3 — The Interior Angle Sum of Polygons
**Assistant:** Why is it n minus 2 triangles and not n, and why must the diagonals come from one vertex?

**Tutor:** Stand at one vertex of an n-gon. It is already joined by sides to its two neighbours, so diagonals can be drawn only to the remaining n minus 3 vertices, and n minus 3 diagonals fanning from one point cut the polygon into n minus 2 triangles. A pentagon gives 2 diagonals and 3 triangles, a hexagon 3 diagonals and 4 triangles, an octagon 5 diagonals and 6 triangles. The triangles do not overlap and together they fill the polygon exactly, and every triangle angle is a piece of some interior angle of the polygon, so adding the triangle sums gives the polygon sum: n minus 2, times 180. Drawing diagonals from several vertices breaks the argument, because the diagonals then cross inside the polygon and create extra triangles whose angles at the crossings are not parts of the polygon's angles; a learner who draws all five diagonals of a pentagon counts eleven regions and gets a wrong total. The one-vertex fan is the whole method. From the sum the regular case follows by dividing by n: pentagon 108, hexagon 120, octagon 135, decagon 144. The exterior angles give a second route, since walking round any polygon turns through a full 360, so a regular n-gon has exterior angles of 360 over n, and interior plus exterior is 180 at each vertex. To find n from an interior angle of 150, take the exterior angle 30 and divide 360 by it to get 12; the algebraic route, n minus 2 times 180 over n equals 150, gives the same 12 after rearranging to 30n equals 360.

### subtopic_4 — Problems and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** How do I set out a polygon angle problem and a diagonal problem so that nothing is lost?

**Tutor:** Begin each with the name of the figure and the rule that belongs to it, then let the arithmetic or algebra follow in order. For the pentagon with angles 100, 110, 120 and 130, write that the sum of the angles of a pentagon is 3 times 180, which is 540, add the four known angles to 460, and subtract to get 80 degrees. For the pentagon with angles x, x plus 10, x plus 20, x plus 30 and x plus 40, write the sum as 5x plus 100, set it equal to 540 with the reason, solve for x equal to 88, substitute to list 88, 98, 108, 118 and 128, and add them to check 540. For a regular polygon with an exterior angle of 24, write that exterior angles add to 360, divide to get 15 sides, and give the interior angle 156 with the check that 13 times 180 divided by 15 is 156. For the parallelogram PQRS with diagonals meeting at N, PN equal to 3x minus 1 and NR equal to x plus 7, write that the diagonals of a parallelogram bisect each other, so PN equals NR, solve 3x minus 1 equals x plus 7 to get x equal to 4, and state PN and NR as 11 and PR as 22. The error museum holds the ways these go wrong: using n instead of n minus 2, dividing by n without the word regular, exterior angles that do not add to 360, equal diagonals claimed in a parallelogram or rhombus, and diagonals from several vertices when counting triangles. Finish every problem with the check: the angles add to the stated sum, or the two halves of the diagonal are equal.
