### subtopic_1 — Classifying Triangles by Sides and Angles
**Assistant:** Why can a triangle be isosceles and right-angled but never equilateral and right-angled?

**Tutor:** Because the side classification and the angle classification are independent except where one forces the other. Isosceles means two equal sides, and the angles opposite them are equal, but the third angle is free to be anything, including 90 degrees; the right-angled isosceles triangle has angles 90, 45 and 45, and it is exactly half a square cut along a diagonal. Equilateral means three equal sides, and the isosceles fact applied twice forces all three angles to be equal, so each is a third of 180, which is 60 degrees. No angle can then be 90, so an equilateral triangle is always acute-angled. The same reasoning rules out two right angles or two obtuse angles in any triangle: two angles of at least 90 degrees would use the whole half-turn before the third angle received anything. The isosceles fact itself must be stated in terms of opposite sides and angles: the angles opposite the equal sides are equal, with the reason written as angles opposite equal sides or base angles of an isosceles triangle, and its converse holds, so a triangle with two equal angles has the two sides opposite them equal. In a diagram, equal sides are marked with matching dashes, a right angle with a small square and equal angles with matching arcs, and only what the marks or the question state may be used.

### subtopic_2 — The Interior Angle Sum of a Triangle
**Assistant:** How do I lay out a problem where the angle sum and the isosceles fact are both needed?

**Tutor:** As a chain in the order the facts become usable, with each statement carrying its own reason. Take an isosceles triangle with apex angle 40 degrees and equal sides from the apex. The two base angles are equal by angles opposite equal sides, so call each one x. The angle sum gives 40 plus x plus x equals 180, sum of angles in a triangle, so 2x equals 140 and x equals 70. Each base angle is 70 degrees. The layout is: the statement that the base angles are equal with its reason, the equation with its reason, the solution, and the angle sizes with units. If the known angle is a base angle instead, say 70 degrees, the other base angle is 70 degrees by angles opposite equal sides, and the apex is 180 minus 140, which is 40 degrees, by the angle sum. Algebraic versions follow the same pattern: angles of x, 2x and 3x give 6x equals 180 with the angle-sum reason, x equals 30, and angles of 30, 60 and 90 degrees, after which the triangle can be classified as right-angled. In a right-angled triangle the two remaining angles add to 90 degrees, which shortens the chain by one step. When two triangles share a side or one sits inside another, finish one triangle's angles before starting the other, and note that a shared angle serves as a known value in both.

### subtopic_3 — The Exterior Angle by Construction
**Assistant:** Why do we both construct the exterior angle and prove the relationship?

**Tutor:** Because construction and proof answer different questions. The construction answers what seems to be true: draw a triangle with interior angles of 50 and 60 at A and B, extend side BC beyond C, measure the exterior angle at C and find 110 degrees, which is 50 plus 60; repeat with angles of 35 and 85 and measure 120, which is 35 plus 85. After several triangles the pattern is convincing, and the CAPS curriculum asks for exactly this kind of investigation. But measurements are approximate and finite; a protractor reading of 109 or 111 is within tolerance, and no number of triangles can cover every triangle. The proof answers why it must be true for all of them. The exterior angle at C and the interior angle at C lie on the straight line BC extended, so the exterior angle is 180 minus angle C, angles on a str line. The angle sum gives angle A plus angle B equals 180 minus angle C, sum of angles in a triangle. Two quantities each equal to 180 minus angle C are equal to each other, so the exterior angle at C equals angle A plus angle B. The result is stated as the exterior angle of a triangle equals the sum of the two interior opposite angles, and the word opposite is essential: the relevant angles are at the two vertices away from the extended corner. Each vertex has two exterior angles, equal by vertically opposite angles, and the three exterior angles of a triangle, one per vertex, add to 360 degrees.

### subtopic_4 — Problems and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** How do I check that an exterior-angle answer makes sense before moving on?

**Tutor:** Run the angle sum as an independent check and confirm every size is possible. In the problem with an exterior angle of 120 degrees at C and an interior angle of 45 degrees at A, the exterior angle fact gives angle B equals 120 minus 45, which is 75 degrees. For the check, the interior angle at C is 180 minus 120, which is 60 degrees by angles on a straight line, and 45 plus 75 plus 60 is 180, as the angle sum requires. In the algebraic problem with exterior angle 2x plus 10 and opposite interior angles x plus 15 and 35, the equation 2x plus 10 equals x plus 50 gives x equals 40, so the exterior angle is 90 degrees and the interior angles are 55 and 35 degrees; 55 plus 35 is 90, and the third interior angle of 90 completes a sum of 180. Sizes that fail the sanity test signal an error: an exterior angle above 180 degrees, a negative angle, or an interior angle sum that is not 180. The error museum collects the ways problems go wrong: adding the adjacent interior angle instead of the two opposite ones; accepting an impossible size; placing the equal angles of an isosceles triangle next to the equal sides rather than opposite them; assuming a right angle from a sketch with no square mark; and attaching the wrong reason to a correct step. The full-marks layout reads the diagram's marks, writes each statement with its reason, solves any equation with its reason, substitutes back, gives units, and ends with the angle-sum check.
