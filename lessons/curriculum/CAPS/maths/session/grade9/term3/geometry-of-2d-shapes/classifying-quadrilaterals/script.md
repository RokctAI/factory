# Part 1 — Expert

Today the four-sided shapes. A quadrilateral is a closed figure with four straight sides, and Grade 9 names six special ones: the parallelogram, rectangle, square, rhombus, trapezium and kite. Each is defined by a property of its sides, and each then carries further properties of its angles and its diagonals that follow from the definition. The examinable skill is to state a definition precisely, to know which properties belong to which shape, and to understand the family tree, in which a square is a rectangle and a rhombus, and all three are parallelograms. Every property is a reason you can write in brackets, and next lesson we investigate the diagonals by construction.

## Subtopic: Defining the Six Quadrilaterals

A definition gives the minimum that makes a shape what it is. A parallelogram is a quadrilateral with two pairs of opposite sides parallel. A rectangle is a parallelogram with one angle equal to 90 degrees; once one angle is 90, the parallel sides force all four to be 90. A rhombus is a parallelogram with two adjacent sides equal; once two adjacent sides are equal, the opposite sides being equal makes all four equal. A square is a rectangle with two adjacent sides equal, or equivalently a rhombus with a right angle. A trapezium is a quadrilateral with one pair of opposite sides parallel. A kite is a quadrilateral with two pairs of adjacent sides equal.

Notice how much the definitions leave out. The definition of a parallelogram says nothing about equal sides or equal angles; those are properties that can be proved from the definition, not part of it. The definition of a rectangle does not say four right angles, because one is enough. A good definition is short, and a test question that asks you to define a shape wants the short version.

Marks on a diagram declare the shape. Matching arrowheads on opposite sides mean parallel. Matching dashes mean equal sides. A small square means a right angle. A quadrilateral drawn with arrowheads on both pairs of opposite sides is a parallelogram whether or not it looks like one; a quadrilateral drawn to look like a square but marked with only one right angle and no equal sides is a rectangle at most, and only if the sides are also marked parallel.

The trapezium and the kite stand slightly apart. A trapezium has only one pair of parallel sides, so it is not a parallelogram. A kite has its equal sides adjacent rather than opposite, so it too is generally not a parallelogram; a rhombus is the special kite whose two pairs of adjacent equal sides happen to be all equal. Naming the six shapes by their definitions, and nothing more, is the first skill. Label the vertices in order around the shape, ABCD, so that A and C are opposite vertices and B and D are opposite vertices; side AB is opposite side CD, and angle A is opposite angle C. A problem that says ABCD is a parallelogram with AB parallel to DC is telling you which pair of sides to read as the parallel ones, and the letters settle every later statement. The questions for this section are with you now: the six definitions, minimum conditions, and reading the marks.

## Subtopic: Properties of Sides and Angles

Every quadrilateral has an interior angle sum of 360 degrees, because a diagonal splits it into two triangles of 180 each. The reason is written as sum of angles in a quadrilateral. Three angles of 90, 85 and 110 give a fourth of 360 minus 285, which is 75 degrees.

A parallelogram has opposite sides equal, as well as parallel, and opposite angles equal. Its consecutive angles, two angles sharing a side, are co-interior between parallel lines and so add to 180 degrees. If one angle of a parallelogram is 50 degrees, the opposite angle is 50, opp angles of a parallelogram, and the other two are 130 each, co-int angles or consecutive angles of a parallelogram. With algebra: consecutive angles x and 2x plus 30 give 3x plus 30 equals 180, so x is 50, and the angles are 50, 130, 50 and 130.

A rectangle has all the parallelogram properties plus four right angles. A rhombus has all the parallelogram properties plus four equal sides. A square has everything: four equal sides and four right angles. The inheritance runs downwards through the family: whatever is true of a parallelogram is true of a rectangle, a rhombus and a square.

A trapezium has one pair of parallel sides, and the two angles between a parallel side and one of the non-parallel sides are co-interior, so they add to 180 degrees; that is its only general angle property. In a trapezium with parallel sides AB and DC, angle A plus angle D is 180 and angle B plus angle C is 180, but angle A and angle B are unrelated unless the trapezium is marked with equal non-parallel sides, in which case it is an isosceles trapezium and the angles on each parallel side are equal. A kite has one pair of opposite angles equal, the angles between the unequal sides, where the two pairs of equal sides meet each other; the angles at the ends of the axis of symmetry are generally not equal.

Each property is a reason. A problem may state that ABCD is a parallelogram and ask for an angle; the chain uses opposite angles of a parallelogram or co-interior angles between the parallel sides, each named, exactly as in Term 2. Your questions on this section: the 360 and why, the parallelogram's angle pairs, and the single angle property of a trapezium and of a kite.

## Subtopic: Properties of Diagonals

A diagonal joins two opposite vertices, and every quadrilateral has two. What the diagonals do distinguishes the family members, and next lesson's construction will verify each claim with a ruler and protractor.

In a parallelogram the diagonals bisect each other: they cross at their shared midpoint, so each diagonal is cut into two equal halves. They are not in general equal to each other, and they do not in general cross at right angles. In a rectangle the diagonals bisect each other and are equal in length. In a rhombus the diagonals bisect each other and cross at right angles, and each diagonal bisects the pair of angles at its ends. In a square the diagonals do everything: bisect each other, are equal, cross at right angles, and bisect the angles, cutting each 90 into two 45s.

In a kite one diagonal, the axis of symmetry joining the two vertices where unequal sides meet, bisects the other diagonal at right angles and bisects the two angles at its ends; the diagonals are not equal and the other diagonal does not bisect the axis. In a trapezium the diagonals have no special property in general; they neither bisect each other nor cross at right angles. Drawing the two diagonals of a trapezium and measuring the four pieces next lesson will show four different lengths, which is the clearest way to see that a property absent from the list is genuinely absent and not merely unmentioned.

Diagonals create triangles, and the triangle facts return. In a rhombus with one interior angle of 60 degrees, the diagonal from that vertex bisects it into two 30s, the diagonals cross at 90, and the small triangle formed has angles 30, 90 and 60, sum of angles in a triangle. In a rectangle the two diagonals, being equal and bisecting each other, cut the figure into four isosceles triangles, two congruent pairs; the base angles of each isosceles triangle are found with the angle sum.

The diagonal properties are the ones most often confused, so the table deserves memorising in a fixed order: bisect each other, parallelogram and everything below it; equal, rectangle and square; at right angles, rhombus and square, and the kite's axis; bisect the angles, rhombus and square, and the kite's axis at its own ends. The questions for this section are ready: which diagonals bisect each other, which are equal, which are perpendicular, and the triangles they create.

## Subtopic: The Family Tree and the Error Museum

Arrange the six shapes in a tree. At the top, quadrilateral. Below it, trapezium, kite and parallelogram. Below parallelogram, rectangle and rhombus. Below both of those, square, which inherits from each. Every shape has all the properties of the shapes above it on its branch, and additional properties of its own. The arrows of inheritance point downward only: a square is a rectangle, but a rectangle is not necessarily a square. A useful test for any statement of the form every X is a Y is to ask whether a shape could satisfy the definition of X while failing the definition of Y; a long thin rectangle satisfies the definition of a rectangle and fails the definition of a square, so every rectangle is a square is false, and one counterexample is enough.

The language of the tree is the language of true-or-false questions. Every rectangle is a parallelogram: true, by definition. Every parallelogram is a rectangle: false, since a parallelogram need not have a right angle. Every square is a rhombus: true, four equal sides. Every rhombus is a square: false. A rhombus is a kite: true, because two pairs of adjacent sides are equal, though the CAPS convention sometimes treats the kite as having exactly two pairs, so follow the wording of the question. A square is a trapezium under the at-least-one-pair reading but not under the exactly-one-pair reading; Grade 9 uses the one-pair definition and keeps the trapezium outside the parallelogram family.

The error museum, five exhibits. Exhibit one: saying a square is not a rectangle because it has equal sides; a square meets the definition of a rectangle and is one. Exhibit two: using diagonals at right angles as a reason in a parallelogram that is not marked as a rhombus. Exhibit three: a kite drawn with equal opposite sides, which makes it a parallelogram, not a kite. Exhibit four: claiming the diagonals of a trapezium bisect each other. Exhibit five: defining a rectangle as a shape with four right angles and two pairs of equal sides, which is true but not minimal; a definition gives the least that suffices.

Layout for marks: name the shape from its marks, state the property used with the shape named, as in opp sides of a parallelogram or diagonals of a rhombus, write statement, equals, value, reason, and check with the quadrilateral angle sum where angles are involved. The questions for this section are with you now: the tree and its downward arrows, true-or-false statements, and the five exhibits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same six shapes again through a family tree, four corners that make a full turn, and two sticks crossed inside each shape — plain words, same rules.

## Subtopic: The Family Tree

Think of the six shapes as a family. Grandparent: any four-sided shape. Three children: the trapezium, with one pair of parallel sides, like a bridge; the kite, with two pairs of neighbouring sides equal, like the kite you fly; and the parallelogram, with both pairs of opposite sides parallel, like a pushed-over rectangle. The parallelogram has two children of its own: the rectangle, a parallelogram that straightened up to make a right angle, and the rhombus, a parallelogram whose sides all became equal, like a diamond. And those two share one child: the square, which straightened up AND made its sides equal.

Children inherit everything from their parents. A rectangle has everything a parallelogram has, plus right angles. A square has everything a rectangle has and everything a rhombus has. So a square IS a rectangle, IS a rhombus, IS a parallelogram. The arrows only go down: a rectangle is not necessarily a square, because it may not have equal sides.

A definition is the shortest thing you have to say to pin down the shape. A rectangle is a parallelogram with one right angle; one is enough, because the parallel sides make the other three follow. A rhombus is a parallelogram with two neighbouring sides equal; the rest follow. Say the least, not the most. If a test asks you to define a square and you write four equal sides and four right angles and diagonals that are equal and halve each other, the marker will accept the definition part and ignore the rest, but you have spent time proving nothing; the extras are properties, and properties are what you use after the shape is named.

Read the marks before you name anything. Arrowheads on both pairs of opposite sides: parallelogram, at least. Add a tiny square: rectangle. Add dashes on all four sides: rhombus. Both: square. Arrowheads on one pair only: trapezium. Dashes on two pairs of neighbouring sides: kite. Your questions on this section are ready: the tree, arrows only go down, and say the least.

## Subtopic: Four Corners Make a Full Turn

Cut any four-sided shape along a diagonal and you get two triangles. Each triangle's corners make a half-turn, 180, so the four corners of the quadrilateral make a full turn, 360. Three corners known, 90, 85 and 110, add up to 285, so the fourth is 75. Reason: sum of angles in a quadrilateral.

The parallelogram family has corner rules of its own. Opposite corners are twins: equal. Neighbouring corners are a couple that adds to 180, because they are C angles between the parallel sides from last term. One corner of a parallelogram is 50: the opposite one is 50, and the two neighbours are 130 each. Check: 50 plus 130 plus 50 plus 130 is 360. Letters: neighbours x and 2x plus 30 make 180, so 3x plus 30 is 180, x is 50, and the corners are 50, 130, 50, 130 again.

Rectangles and squares have four corners of 90, which is the full turn shared equally. A rhombus has the parallelogram corner rules, twins and couples, but not right angles unless it is a square.

A trapezium only promises one thing: the two corners at each end of a non-parallel side are a C couple, adding to 180. A kite promises one pair of equal corners, the two corners where the unequal sides meet, like the two side points of a flying kite; the top and bottom points are usually different. The questions for this section: the full turn, twins and couples in a parallelogram, and the one promise each of the trapezium and the kite.

## Subtopic: What the Diagonals Tell You

Cross two sticks inside the shape from corner to corner. Those are the diagonals, and how they cross tells you which family member you have.

Parallelogram: the sticks cut each other exactly in half where they cross. They are not the same length, and they do not cross at a right angle. Rectangle: they cut each other in half AND they are the same length, like the two laces of a shoebox lid. Rhombus: they cut each other in half AND they cross at a right angle AND each stick cuts its two corner angles in half. Square: everything at once, equal, halved, right angle, corners halved into 45s.

Kite: only one stick, the one down the middle of the kite, does the work. It cuts the other stick in half at a right angle and halves the two angles at its own ends. The crossways stick does none of that. Trapezium: the sticks promise nothing special. They just cross.

The sticks make triangles, and the triangle facts come back. A rhombus with a 60 corner: the stick from that corner halves it into 30s, the sticks cross at 90, so the little triangle is 30, 90 and 60. A rectangle's two equal sticks that halve each other make four isosceles triangles, two matching pairs, with roof-rule base angles.

Memorise the stick table in one fixed order. Halve each other: parallelogram and all its descendants. Equal: rectangle and square. Right angle: rhombus and square, and the kite's middle stick. Halve the corners: rhombus and square, and the kite's middle stick at its own ends. Family tree, full turn, two sticks. The final questions of the lesson are with you now: which sticks halve each other, which are equal, which cross at right angles, and the triangles they make.
