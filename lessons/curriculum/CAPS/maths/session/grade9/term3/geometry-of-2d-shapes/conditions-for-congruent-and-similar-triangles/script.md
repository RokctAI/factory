# Part 1 — Expert

Two triangles can be identical, or the same shape in different sizes, or neither. Identical triangles are congruent; same-shape triangles are similar. Today we establish by investigation the minimum information that forces two triangles to be congruent, which turns out to be four conditions, and the minimum that forces them to be similar, which is equal angles or sides in the same ratio. We also find, by trying to construct a counterexample, which pieces of information are not enough. The skill is to look at two triangles, decide which condition applies, write the statement with the correct vertex order and the condition as the reason, and use the result to find a side or an angle. Grade 10 proves theorems with these conditions; Grade 9 establishes them.

## Subtopic: Congruent Triangles and the Four Conditions

Two triangles are congruent when one fits exactly on the other, so that all three pairs of sides are equal and all three pairs of angles are equal. We write triangle ABC is congruent to triangle DEF with the vertices in matching order, so that A matches D, B matches E and C matches F; the order is part of the statement, since it says which side equals which. The symbol is three lines, like an equals sign with an extra bar.

Six pieces of information describe a triangle, three sides and three angles, but fewer than six are enough to force congruence. The investigation asks: given certain measurements, can two people construct different triangles? If every construction gives the same triangle, the measurements are a congruence condition.

Side, side, side. Construct a triangle with sides 5, 6 and 7 centimetres using a ruler and compasses: draw the 7, swing an arc of 5 from one end and an arc of 6 from the other, and the arcs meet in exactly one point above the line. Everyone's triangle is the same. Three sides force congruence: SSS.

Side, angle, side. Construct a triangle with sides 5 and 7 and an angle of 40 degrees between them. Draw the 7, measure 40 at one end, mark 5 along the arm, join. Only one triangle is possible. Two sides and the included angle force congruence: SAS. The word included matters, as the next section shows.

Angle, angle, side. Construct a triangle with angles 40 and 60 and a side of 7 between them, or with the 7 opposite one of the angles. In either case the third angle is fixed by the angle sum, so the triangle is fixed: AAS, often written as ASA when the side lies between the two angles.

Right angle, hypotenuse, side. Construct a right-angled triangle with hypotenuse 10 and one other side 6. The third side is forced by the right angle, and the triangle is unique: RHS. This is the only case where a non-included angle is enough, and only because the angle is 90.

Four conditions: SSS, SAS, AAS, RHS. Each becomes a reason in brackets after a congruence statement. The questions for this section are with you now: the definition of congruent, the vertex order, and the four conditions with their constructions.

## Subtopic: Why Three Angles or Two Sides and a Non-Included Angle Are Not Enough

The investigation also shows what fails, and the failures are the examinable traps.

Three angles. Construct a triangle with angles 40, 60 and 80 degrees. One learner draws it with a base of 4 centimetres, another with a base of 8. Both triangles have the right angles, and they are plainly not identical: one is twice the size of the other. Three angles fix the shape but not the size, so AAA is not a congruence condition. It is, as we will see, exactly the condition for similarity.

Two sides and a non-included angle. Construct a triangle with sides 5 and 7 and an angle of 40 degrees that is not between them, say the 40 at the end of the 7 and opposite the 5. Draw the 7, measure 40 at one end, and swing an arc of radius 5 from the other end: the arc cuts the arm in two places, giving two different triangles, one with an acute angle and one with an obtuse angle at the third vertex. Two constructions from the same data means SSA is not a congruence condition. The angle must be included between the two given sides for SAS to apply.

Two sides only, or one side and one angle, obviously leave freedom, so nothing shorter than three pieces works, and of the three-piece combinations only SSS, SAS, AAS and the special RHS succeed.

Reading diagrams for a condition. Matching dashes mark equal sides, matching arcs mark equal angles, and a side shared by two triangles is common, equal to itself. Equal radii of a circle are equal sides. Vertically opposite angles at a crossing are equal angles. A line that bisects an angle gives a pair of equal angles; a midpoint gives a pair of equal sides. Each of these is written as a line with its reason before the congruence statement: AB equals AC, given; angle BAD equals angle CAD, AD bisects angle A; AD is common. Then triangle ABD is congruent to triangle ACD, SAS.

Once two triangles are congruent, every remaining pair of corresponding parts is equal, and the reason for that line is simply congruent triangles, with the matching letters telling you which side equals which. Your questions on this section are ready: why AAA fails, why SSA fails, and reading dashes, arcs, common sides and radii.

## Subtopic: Similar Triangles: Equal Angles, Proportional Sides

Two triangles are similar when they have the same shape: all three pairs of angles equal, and all three pairs of corresponding sides in the same ratio. The symbol is three vertical lines, and the vertex order matters exactly as for congruence. Congruent triangles are similar with ratio 1.

The investigation of AAA just showed that equal angles force the same shape. Measure the sides of the two triangles with angles 40, 60 and 80, base 4 and base 8, and every side of the larger is twice the matching side of the smaller. Equal angles, in fact two pairs since the third follows, is the first minimum condition for similarity, written as AAA or equiangular.

The second condition is sides in proportion. A triangle with sides 3, 4 and 5 and a triangle with sides 6, 8 and 10: each ratio, 6 over 3, 8 over 4, 10 over 5, is 2. The triangles are similar with scale factor 2, and measuring confirms that their angles are equal. A triangle with sides 6, 9 and 12 and a triangle with sides 4, 6 and 8: the ratios are 1.5, 1.5 and 1.5, similar. A triangle with sides 4, 6 and 8 and a triangle with sides 6, 9 and 11: the ratios are 1.5, 1.5 and 1.375, not similar; all three ratios must agree, and the smallest side must be matched with the smallest, the largest with the largest.

Using similarity to find a side. Triangle PQR is similar to triangle XYZ, with PQ equal to 5, XY equal to 15 and QR equal to 7. The scale factor from PQR to XYZ is 15 over 5, which is 3, so YZ is 3 times 7, which is 21. The layout writes the ratio statement, XY over PQ equals YZ over QR, with the reason similar triangles, substitutes, and solves.

The shadow problem. A vertical pole 2 metres tall casts a shadow 3 metres long; at the same moment a tree casts a shadow 12 metres long. The sun's rays are parallel, so the two triangles have equal angles and are similar. Tree height over 2 equals 12 over 3, so the tree is 2 times 4, which is 8 metres tall. Similar triangles measure what cannot be climbed. The questions for this section are ready: the two conditions, 3-4-5 against 6-8-10, the 4-6-8 against 6-9-11 failure, and the tree.

## Subtopic: Problems and the Error Museum

Problem one. In the diagram, AB equals DC and angle ABC equals angle DCB, with BC common to triangles ABC and DCB. Statement: AB equals DC, given; angle ABC equals angle DCB, given; BC is common. Therefore triangle ABC is congruent to triangle DCB, SAS. Then AC equals DB, congruent triangles. Notice that the common side is one of the two sides, and the given angle lies between the two sides in each triangle, which is what SAS requires.

Problem two. Two triangles have angles 50 and 60 in the first and 60 and 70 in the second. The third angles are 70 and 50 respectively, sum of angles in a triangle, so the triangles have angles 50, 60, 70 in both and are similar, AAA. Then the side opposite the 60 in each is in the same ratio as the side opposite the 50 in each.

Problem three. Triangle ABC is similar to triangle ADE, with D on AB and E on AC, DE parallel to BC, AD equal to 4, DB equal to 6 and DE equal to 5. AB is 10, so the scale factor from ADE to ABC is 10 over 4, which is 2.5, and BC is 2.5 times 5, which is 12.5. The parallel line gives the equal angles, corresponding angles with DE parallel to BC, and the angle at A is common; that is the AAA reason.

Problem four. Decide whether triangles with sides 5, 12, 13 and 10, 24, 26 are similar: ratios 2, 2, 2, so yes, with scale factor 2, and since 5 squared plus 12 squared is 169, both are right-angled.

The error museum, five exhibits. Exhibit one: writing SSA or ASS as a reason; it is not a condition, and the two-triangle construction is the proof. Exhibit two: AAA as a reason for congruence; it gives similarity only. Exhibit three: congruence statements with the letters in the wrong order, so that the wrong sides are later declared equal. Exhibit four: in a similarity ratio, matching the wrong sides, such as dividing the longest of one triangle by the shortest of the other. Exhibit five: forgetting the common side or the vertically opposite angles because they are not marked; they are there to be used, with their reasons.

Layout for marks: in triangle ABC and triangle DEF, list three numbered facts with reasons, state the congruence or similarity with vertex order and the condition, then deduce what was asked with the reason congruent triangles or similar triangles, and for similarity write the ratio equation before substituting. The questions for this section are with you now: the four problems and the five exhibits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through identical twins and photocopies, four keys that lock a triangle, and a scale factor you multiply by — plain words, same rules.

## Subtopic: Photocopies and Identical Twins

Two triangles are congruent when they are identical twins: cut one out and it drops exactly onto the other, every side and every angle matching. Two triangles are similar when one is a photocopy of the other at a different size, enlarged or reduced: same shape, same angles, every side multiplied by the same number. Identical twins are a special photocopy at 100 percent.

Writing it down: triangle ABC is congruent to triangle DEF means A sits on D, B on E and C on F. The letters are not decoration; they tell you AB matches DE, BC matches EF and AC matches DF. Get the order wrong and you will later say the wrong two sides are equal. Same for similar: triangle PQR similar to triangle XYZ pairs P with X, Q with Y, R with Z.

To decide which you have, do not trust your eyes. Twins are proved by the four keys of the next section. Photocopies are proved by equal angles or by checking that every pair of matching sides divides to the same number.

On a diagram, dashes mean equal sides, arcs mean equal angles, and a side that two triangles share is common, equal to itself; two radii of the same circle are equal; a point where two lines cross gives equal vertically opposite angles. Collect these before you decide anything. Your questions on this section are ready: twins against photocopies, what the letter order promises, and what to collect from a diagram.

## Subtopic: Four Keys That Lock a Triangle

A triangle has six measurements, three sides and three angles, but you do not need all six to lock it. Try to build two different triangles from the same information; if you cannot, the information is a key.

Key one, SSS: three sides. Draw the longest side, swing a compass arc from each end with the other two lengths, and they cross in one place. Locked.

Key two, SAS: two sides with the angle between them. Draw one side, measure the angle at its end, mark the other side along the arm, join up. One triangle only. Locked. The angle has to be between the two sides, the hinge of the two arms.

Key three, AAS: two angles and a side. Two angles give you the third for free, so the shape is fixed, and one side fixes the size. Locked.

Key four, RHS: a right angle, the hypotenuse and one other side. Locked, because the right angle leaves the third side no choice. This is the one time an angle not between the sides is enough, and only because it is 90.

Two things that look like keys but are not. Three angles: a triangle with angles 40, 60 and 80 can be drawn tiny or huge, so AAA fixes the shape but not the size; it is a photocopy key, not a twin key. Two sides and an angle not between them: draw the 7, make 40 at one end, swing an arc of 5 from the other end and it cuts the arm twice, two different triangles. SSA opens two doors, so it is not a key.

Use a key like this: list the three matching facts with their reasons, write the congruence with the letters in order and the key in brackets, then claim any other matching side or angle with the reason congruent triangles. The questions for this section: the four keys, the two fake keys, and why the angle must be the hinge.

## Subtopic: Scaling Up With a Scale Factor

A photocopy has a scale factor, the number every side is multiplied by. From a 3, 4, 5 triangle to a 6, 8, 10 triangle the scale factor is 2: 6 over 3, 8 over 4 and 10 over 5 are all 2. From 4, 6, 8 to 6, 9, 12 it is 1.5. To test for a photocopy, match smallest with smallest, biggest with biggest, divide each pair, and demand that all three answers agree. Sides 4, 6, 8 against 6, 9, 11 give 1.5, 1.5 and 1.375: not a photocopy.

Equal angles also prove a photocopy, and two matching pairs are enough because the third pair comes free from 180. Angles 50 and 60 in one triangle, 60 and 70 in the other: the third angles are 70 and 50, both triangles are 50, 60, 70, similar.

Finding a missing side: find the scale factor from a pair you know, then multiply. Triangle PQR similar to XYZ, PQ 5 and XY 15, so the scale factor is 3; QR 7, so YZ is 21. Write the ratio first, XY over PQ equals YZ over QR, then put in the numbers.

The tree. A 2 metre pole throws a 3 metre shadow; a tree throws a 12 metre shadow at the same time. Sunlight comes in parallel, so the two sun-pole-shadow triangles are photocopies of each other. Shadow 3 to shadow 12 is a scale factor of 4, so the tree is 2 times 4, which is 8 metres. No ladder needed.

Letters in order, four keys for twins, scale factor for photocopies, and never SSA, never AAA for twins. The final questions of the lesson are with you now: scale factor from 3-4-5 to 6-8-10, the 1.375 failure, YZ equal to 21, and the 8 metre tree.
