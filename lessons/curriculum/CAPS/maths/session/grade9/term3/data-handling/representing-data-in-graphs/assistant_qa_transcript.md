### subtopic_1 — Bar Graphs, Double Bar Graphs and Pie Charts
**Assistant:** When should I draw a pie chart instead of a bar graph, and how do I get the slices right?

**Tutor:** Draw a pie chart when the point is each category's share of a whole and there are few enough categories to read, about six at most; draw a bar graph when the actual counts matter, when there are many categories, or when two groups are being compared. The pie chart's slices are calculated before anything is drawn. Each angle is the category's frequency over the total, multiplied by 360 degrees: for sixty learners, walking at 33 gives 33 over 60 times 360, which is 198 degrees; taxi at 21 gives 126 degrees; car at 6 gives 36 degrees. The three angles add to 360, and that sum is the check that must be written down; rounding each angle upward is the usual way the total drifts to 361 or 362. The same fractions give percentages of 55, 35 and 10, which label the slices along with the category names, and the chart carries a title. The slices are drawn with a protractor from a radius, each new slice starting where the last ended. For the bar graph, the categories sit along the horizontal axis with a gap between each bar because they are separate things, the frequency axis has an even scale from zero, and the tallest bar is the mode. For a double bar graph comparing boys and girls across the three categories, each category has two bars side by side in two shades with a key, and the comparison is fair only because the groups are both 30; with unequal groups the bars would show percentages instead of counts.

### subtopic_2 — Histograms With Given and Own Intervals
**Assistant:** How do I decide on the class intervals for a histogram when none are given, and what makes a histogram different from a bar graph?

**Tutor:** Start from the range. For distances between 0.2 and 4.8 kilometres the range is 4.6, and you want between five and eight intervals of equal width with round boundaries that cover it, so five intervals of width 1 kilometre from 0 to 5 is a natural choice. Write the boundaries so that every value belongs to exactly one interval, 0 to under 1 and 1 to under 2 rather than 0 to 1 and 1 to 2, since otherwise a distance of exactly 1 kilometre has two homes. Tally each value into its interval, and check that the tallies add to the total of 30 before drawing. Fewer intervals than five hide the shape in a couple of blocks; more than eight scatter the data into spikes of one or none; unequal widths make a wide interval look busier than it is, so Grade 9 keeps the widths equal, with the single allowed exception of stretching the last interval by one mark so that 40 to 50 catches a mark of 50. The histogram differs from the bar graph in two linked ways: its horizontal axis is a continuous number line rather than a row of category names, and its bars touch because adjacent intervals meet with no gap in the data between them. For the distance data the frequencies 8, 11, 6, 3 and 2 give a tallest bar over 1 to under 2, the modal interval, and a shape that piles up near the school and tails off to the right, a right skew typical of distances. Individual values are not visible, so the mean and median can only be estimated from a histogram, not read exactly.

### subtopic_3 — Broken-Line Graphs and Scatter Plots
**Assistant:** What is the difference between a broken-line graph and a scatter plot, and why must I not join the dots in a scatter plot?

**Tutor:** A broken-line graph follows one quantity through time, so each point is a measurement at a moment and the segments between consecutive points show how the quantity moved from one moment to the next; a scatter plot shows two numerical variables measured on the same individuals, so each point is one individual and there is no order in which the points follow one another, which is why joining them would draw a meaningless zigzag. For the household's electricity use, the months January to June sit at equal spacing on the horizontal axis, the kilowatt hours of 320, 300, 310, 350, 420 and 480 are plotted and joined by straight segments, and the picture shows a level start followed by a climb into winter, with the steepest segment from May to June marking the fastest rise of 60 kilowatt hours. The vertical axis starts at zero, or carries a clearly marked break, because an axis starting at 300 would turn a 50 percent rise into what looks like a rise from nothing. For the eight learners' study hours and marks, from one hour and 35 up to seven hours and 80, each learner is a dot with hours across and marks up, and the dots form a cloud rising from left to right, a positive relationship: more hours tend to go with higher marks. A cloud falling to the right would be a negative relationship and a formless cloud would be none. The conclusion must say association rather than cause, since the plot cannot tell whether studying raised the marks or whether some third factor moved both, and a dot far from the cloud, such as seven hours and 40, is an outlier to investigate rather than ignore.

### subtopic_4 — Choosing and Labelling a Graph, and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** How do I justify my choice of graph in an answer, and what do markers check on the finished graph?

**Tutor:** Justify the graph by naming the type of data it represents, because the data type determines the graph. Categorical data, such as mode of transport, takes a bar graph, or a pie chart when shares of a whole are the point, or a double bar graph when two groups are compared across the same categories. Numerical data grouped in intervals, such as distances or marks, takes a histogram. A quantity measured over time takes a broken-line graph. Two numerical variables measured on the same individuals take a scatter plot. A full justification reads: a histogram, because distance is continuous numerical data grouped into equal intervals. Markers then check the equipment on the graph itself: a title saying what, who and when; both axes labelled with the variable and its unit; an even scale with equal steps starting at zero for frequencies and quantities; a key where two series share the axes; clearly marked categories or intervals; bars ruled and points plotted accurately; and for a pie chart the angle table with its sum of 360 shown. The error museum collects the standard losses: histogram bars with gaps or bar graph bars touching; pie angles that do not sum to 360 or slices drawn by eye; a vertical axis starting above zero with no break, exaggerating differences; a double bar graph without a key or comparing counts from groups of very different sizes; and scatter plot dots joined up or a conclusion that one variable causes the other. The layout names the graph with its data-type reason, shows the frequency or angle table with its check, draws with ruler and protractor, adds title, labels, units, scale and key, and ends with one sentence stating what the graph shows.
