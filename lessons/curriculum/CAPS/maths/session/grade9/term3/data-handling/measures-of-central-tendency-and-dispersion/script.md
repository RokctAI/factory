# Part 1 — Expert

Once data has been collected it must be organised and summarised, and today's lesson is the summarising stage of the data cycle. We organise numerical data in ordered lists, frequency tables, stem-and-leaf displays and two-way tables that sort by more than one criterion; we calculate the three measures of central tendency, the mean, the median and the mode, and the measures of dispersion that Grade 9 uses, the range and the extremes; and we examine how an outlier, a value far from the rest, pulls the mean while leaving the median alone. The skill that examiners prize is choosing the right measure for the situation and justifying the choice, because an average is only honest when it describes the typical member of the data. Grade 10 adds quartiles and box plots; today's measures are their foundation.

## Subtopic: Organising Data: Ordered Lists, Frequency Tables and Two-Way Tables

Raw data arrives in the order it was collected and tells you little. Twelve learners' tuck shop spending for a week, in rands, came in as 12, 60, 10, 15, 8, 12, 20, 5, 25, 10, 18 and 12. The first step is to order it from smallest to largest: 5, 8, 10, 10, 12, 12, 12, 15, 18, 20, 25, 60. Now the smallest and largest, the clusters and the gaps are visible, and every later calculation starts from this ordered list.

A frequency table counts how often each value occurs. Shoe sizes of 25 learners: size 4 occurs 3 times, size 5 occurs 7 times, size 6 occurs 8 times, size 7 occurs 5 times and size 8 occurs 2 times. The frequencies add to 25, which is the check that no learner has been lost. When the data has many different values, group it into class intervals of equal width: distances to school in intervals 0 to under 1 kilometre, 1 to under 2, 2 to under 3, and so on, each learner tallied into exactly one interval.

A stem-and-leaf display keeps every value while showing the shape. Test marks out of 50 for ten learners, 23, 31, 35, 35, 38, 40, 42, 44, 45 and 47, have stems 2, 3 and 4 for the tens and leaves for the units: stem 2 with leaf 3; stem 3 with leaves 1, 5, 5, 8; stem 4 with leaves 0, 2, 4, 5, 7. The display is both an ordered list and a picture of where the marks cluster.

A two-way table organises data by two criteria at once. Sixty Grade 9 learners sorted by gender and by transport: boys who walk 18, boys by taxi 9, boys by car 3; girls who walk 15, girls by taxi 12, girls by car 3. Row totals give 30 boys and 30 girls; column totals give 33 walkers, 21 taxi users and 6 by car; the grand total is 60. The table answers questions a single list cannot, such as whether girls are more likely than boys to use a taxi: 12 of 30 against 9 of 30. The questions for this section are with you now: ordering the tuck shop data, the shoe size table, the stem-and-leaf, and the two-way table.

## Subtopic: Mean, Median and Mode

The mean is the sum of the values divided by the number of values. For the tuck shop data the twelve values add to 207, and 207 divided by 12 is 17.25 rands. The mean uses every value and is the measure most people call the average.

The median is the middle value of the ordered list. With twelve values there is no single middle, so the median is the mean of the sixth and seventh values, 12 and 12, which is 12 rands. With an odd number of values the median is the single middle one: for the ten test marks the median is the mean of the fifth and sixth, 38 and 40, which is 39; for an eleven-value list it is the sixth value. The median must be found from the ordered list, never from the raw order.

The mode is the value that occurs most often: 12 rands appears three times, more than any other, so the mode is 12. Data can have two modes, bimodal, or no mode when every value appears once. The mode is the only measure that works for categorical data: the modal transport is walking, 33 of 60.

From a frequency table the mean is found by multiplying each value by its frequency, adding, and dividing by the total frequency. Shoe sizes: 4 times 3 is 12, 5 times 7 is 35, 6 times 8 is 48, 7 times 5 is 35, 8 times 2 is 16, and the sum 146 divided by 25 gives a mean of 5.84. The mode is 6, the size with the highest frequency. The median is the 13th of 25 ordered values; the cumulative frequencies run 3, 10, 18, so the 13th value lies in size 6, and the median is 6.

Working backwards. The mean of five numbers is 20, so their sum is 100; if four of them are 18, 22, 15 and 25, which add to 80, the fifth is 20. A class of 30 has a mean mark of 60 percent and a second class of 20 has a mean of 75 percent; the combined mean is not 67.5 but the total of all marks, 1800 plus 1500, which is 3300, divided by 50, which is 66. Your questions on this section are ready: the three measures for the tuck shop data, median of an even count, the mean from the shoe table, and the missing fifth number.

## Subtopic: Range, Extremes and Outliers

Two data sets can share a mean and still be very different. Marks of 48, 49, 50, 51 and 52 and marks of 30, 40, 50, 60 and 70 both have mean 50 and median 50, but the first set is tightly bunched and the second is spread out. Dispersion measures the spread, and Grade 9 uses the range, the largest value minus the smallest, together with the extremes themselves, the minimum and the maximum. The first set has range 4; the second has range 40.

For the tuck shop data the extremes are 5 and 60 and the range is 55 rands. For the test marks the extremes are 23 and 47 and the range is 24. The range is quick and easily understood, but it depends on only two values, so one unusual value changes it completely.

An outlier is a value that lies far from the rest of the data. The 60 in the tuck shop data is an outlier: the next largest value is 25, and 60 is more than double it. Outliers may be genuine, one learner really did spend 60 rands, or errors, such as 6 recorded as 60, and the first step is to check the source. Either way, the outlier's effect on each measure must be understood.

Remove the 60 and recalculate. The eleven remaining values add to 147, and the mean falls from 17.25 to 13.36 rands. The median, the sixth of eleven ordered values, is 12, unchanged. The mode is 12, unchanged. The range falls from 55 to 20. The mean and the range are sensitive to the outlier; the median and the mode are resistant. This is the single most examinable fact of the lesson: an outlier pulls the mean towards itself and stretches the range, while the median stays where the middle of the data is.

The extremes are also worth reporting in their own right. The lowest mark tells a teacher who needs help; the highest water reading tells a municipality where a leak may be. A summary that gives the mean alone hides both. The questions for this section are ready: range and extremes for the two data sets, the definition of an outlier, and what happens to each measure when the 60 is removed.

## Subtopic: Choosing the Right Measure and the Error Museum

Each measure answers a different question, and the choice must fit the data and the purpose. The mean is best when the data is roughly symmetrical with no outliers, because it uses every value: the mean mark of a class on a test is a fair summary. The median is best when there are outliers or the data is skewed, because it is not pulled: house prices, incomes and spending are typically summarised by the median for this reason. The mode is best for categorical data or when the most common value is what matters: a shoe shop stocks the modal size.

A small firm has eight workers earning R6 000 a month and a manager earning R40 000. The mean salary is 88 000 divided by 9, which is R9 777.78; the median is R6 000; the mode is R6 000. An advertisement saying the average salary is nearly R10 000 is true of the mean and misleading about the typical worker, who earns R6 000. The honest report gives the median, and states that one salary is far above the rest.

When a question asks which measure best represents the data, the answer names the measure and gives the reason in terms of the data: the median, because the value 60 is an outlier that pulls the mean up to 17.25 while most learners spent between 8 and 20 rands. A measure named without a reason earns half the marks.

The error museum, five exhibits. Exhibit one: a median found from the unordered list. Exhibit two: an even-count median given as one of the two middle values instead of their mean. Exhibit three: the mean from a frequency table calculated by adding the values and dividing by the number of rows, ignoring the frequencies, which gives 6 for the shoe sizes instead of 5.84. Exhibit four: a combined mean found by averaging two means with different group sizes, 67.5 instead of 66. Exhibit five: the mean reported for skewed data with an outlier, and no mention of the outlier.

Layout for marks: write the ordered list, state n, show the sum and the division for the mean, identify the middle position for the median and show the two middle values when n is even, state the mode with its frequency, give the extremes and the range with units, identify any outlier and say which measures it affects, and finish with the measure you recommend and the reason. The questions for this section are with you now: the three situations for the three measures, the salary firm, and the five exhibits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through lining up the class by height, three different kinds of middle, and one giant who spoils the average — plain words, same rules.

## Subtopic: Line Them Up First

Numbers that arrive in a jumble tell you nothing. Twelve learners' tuck shop spending came in as 12, 60, 10, 15, 8, 12, 20, 5, 25, 10, 18, 12. Line them up from smallest to biggest, like lining the class up by height: 5, 8, 10, 10, 12, 12, 12, 15, 18, 20, 25, 60. Now you can see the smallest, the biggest, the crowd in the middle and the one standing far off at the end.

If there are lots of repeats, count them in a frequency table instead of writing every one out. Shoe sizes of 25 learners: three size 4s, seven 5s, eight 6s, five 7s, two 8s. Add the counts, 3 plus 7 plus 8 plus 5 plus 2 is 25, to check nobody is missing. If the values are all different, group them in equal-width bands, like 0 to under 1 kilometre, 1 to under 2, and tally.

A stem-and-leaf is a frequency table that keeps the actual numbers. Marks 23, 31, 35, 35, 38, 40, 42, 44, 45, 47: tens on the left, units on the right. The 3 row reads 1, 5, 5, 8 and the 4 row reads 0, 2, 4, 5, 7, so you can see the marks pile up in the forties.

Sorting by two things at once needs a two-way table: boys and girls down the side, walk, taxi and car across the top. Boys: 18, 9, 3. Girls: 15, 12, 3. Add across for 30 boys and 30 girls, add down for 33 walkers, 21 taxi riders and 6 in cars, and 60 in the corner. Now you can ask whether girls use taxis more than boys: 12 against 9. Your questions on this section are ready: lining up, the shoe size table with its check, the stem-and-leaf, and the two-way table.

## Subtopic: Three Kinds of Middle

There are three ways to say what is typical, and they can give three different answers.

The mean shares everything out equally: add it all up and divide by how many. Tuck shop: 207 divided by 12 is 17.25 rands. Pretend every learner spent the same; that is the mean.

The median is the person standing in the middle of the line. Twelve learners means two in the middle, the sixth and the seventh, 12 and 12, so the median is 12. If they had been 12 and 14, the median would be 13, halfway between. With an odd number there is one person in the middle and the median is their value. Always line up first; the middle of a jumble is nothing.

The mode is the most common value, the size most people wear: 12 rands appears three times, so the mode is 12. The mode is the only middle that works for words: the most common way to school is walking.

From a frequency table the mean needs the counts: size times count for each row, 12 plus 35 plus 48 plus 35 plus 16 is 146, shared among 25 learners, 5.84. The mode is the biggest count, size 6. The median is the 13th learner in line: the first 3 wear size 4, the next 7 take you to 10, the next 8 take you to 18, so the 13th wears size 6.

Backwards: if the mean of five numbers is 20, the five add up to 100; four of them make 80, so the fifth is 20. Two classes with means 60 and 75 do not have a combined mean of 67.5 unless they are the same size; 30 learners at 60 and 20 at 75 give 3300 marks over 50 learners, which is 66. The questions for this section: share out, middle of the line, most common, and the 13th learner.

## Subtopic: The Giant Who Spoils the Average

Two classes can have the same middle and look completely different. Marks 48, 49, 50, 51, 52 and marks 30, 40, 50, 60, 70 both have a mean of 50, but the first class is bunched and the second is spread. The spread is the range, biggest minus smallest: 4 for the first, 40 for the second. Report the smallest and biggest too, the extremes; they tell you who needs help and who is far ahead.

Now the giant. In the tuck shop line, eleven learners spent between 5 and 25 rands and one spent 60. That 60 is an outlier, standing far from everyone else. Watch what it does. With the giant, the mean is 17.25 and the range is 55. Send the giant away and the mean drops to 13.36 and the range to 20. The median stays at 12 and the mode stays at 12. The giant pulls the mean towards himself and stretches the range, but the person in the middle of the line does not move.

That is why you choose the middle to fit the data. No giants and a fair spread: the mean is fine, like a class test. Giants or a lopsided spread: use the median, like incomes or house prices. Words or a most-common question: use the mode, like which shoe size to stock. Eight workers on R6 000 and a boss on R40 000 have a mean of R9 777.78 and a median of R6 000; the mean is true and misleading, the median tells you what a worker actually earns.

Always say which middle you used and why, naming the giant. Line them up, three middles, watch the giant. The final questions of the lesson are with you now: range and extremes, what the 60 does to each measure, and which middle fits which data.
