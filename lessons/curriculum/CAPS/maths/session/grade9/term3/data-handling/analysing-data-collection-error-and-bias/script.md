# Part 1 — Expert

Every number in a data report came from somewhere, and the somewhere can go wrong. Today we analyse the ways data collection, summarising and presentation introduce error and bias, so that a report can be judged on how its numbers were made and not only on what they say. We distinguish random error, the scatter of chance that shrinks as the sample grows, from systematic bias, a lean in one direction that no sample size cures. We examine bias in who was asked, who answered and how the question was put; we examine errors in recording, in choosing the summary statistic and in drawing the graph; and we practise auditing a published claim step by step. This is the critical stage of the data cycle, and it comes before reporting because an honest report names its own limitations.

## Subtopic: Random Error and Systematic Bias

Measure the same thing twice and you rarely get the same number. Ten learners each time the same 100 metre run with stopwatches and record 14.2, 14.5, 14.3, 14.4 and 14.1 seconds; the spread comes from reaction times and reading the watch, and it falls on both sides of the true time. This is random error: unpredictable in direction, and it averages out, so the mean of many readings is closer to the truth than any one reading. Sampling error is the random error of samples: two fair samples of 60 learners from the same school give slightly different percentages of walkers, 70 and 66, not because either is wrong but because chance picked different learners. Larger samples have smaller sampling error; a sample of 600 would land closer to the school's true figure than a sample of 60.

Bias is different. If every stopwatch was started late, every reading is too short, and averaging a thousand late readings gives a precisely wrong answer. Bias is systematic error, a lean in one direction built into the method, and more data does not remove it. A convenience sample of early arrivals under-counts long journeys however many early arrivals are asked; a leading question pushes every respondent the same way; a scale that reads 2 kilograms when empty adds 2 to every mass.

The two kinds call for different remedies. Random error is reduced by repeating measurements and taking the mean, and by increasing the sample size. Bias is reduced only by fixing the method: a fair sample, a neutral question, a calibrated instrument. A report should say which kind of error it faced and what it did about each. A result from a small fair sample is uncertain but honest; a result from a large biased sample is confident and wrong, and the second is the more dangerous.

Precision and accuracy name the difference. Precise means the readings agree closely with each other; accurate means they agree with the truth. The late stopwatches are precise and inaccurate. Five scattered readings with a correct mean are imprecise and accurate. Good data collection aims at both and reports both. The questions for this section are with you now: random error against bias, sampling error and sample size, which remedy fits which error, and precise against accurate.

## Subtopic: Bias in Collecting the Data

Bias enters through who is asked, who answers, and how the question is put. Selection bias: the sample is drawn so that some part of the population is over-represented. A radio station asks listeners to SMS yes or no on whether matric examinations should be scrapped, receives 4 000 replies and reports that 78 percent say yes. The sample is the station's listeners, not the public; only those who feel strongly enough to pay for an SMS reply, which is self-selection; and 4 000 of perhaps 850 000 listeners is under half a percent. A stratified survey of 1 000 adults chosen by the investigators found 41 percent in favour. The 4 000 is larger than the 1 000 and the 1 000 is the better number, because size does not cure selection bias.

Non-response bias: those who do not answer differ from those who do. A questionnaire about homework left in the pigeonholes is returned by 60 of 300 learners, and the 60 who bothered are likelier to be the organised ones. A high non-response rate is reported as a limitation, and a plan reduces it by collecting answers in class rather than waiting for returns.

Question bias: the wording pushes the answer. Do you agree that the tuck shop prices are far too high invites yes; given the cost of ingredients, are tuck shop prices reasonable invites yes to the opposite. A neutral wording asks how much the respondent spent and lets the data speak. Order matters too, since a question about load shedding asked straight after a question about rising prices draws angrier answers.

Interviewer and setting bias: the tuck shop owner asks customers at the counter whether they are satisfied and 45 of 50 say yes; an anonymous questionnaire completed in class finds 28 of 50 satisfied, which is 56 percent. People answer to please or avoid conflict with the person asking, and anonymity removes the pressure. Timing bias: surveying at the gate at 7 in the morning misses late arrivals; counting cars during the holidays misses the term-time traffic. Each of these is named in a critique with its direction: the counter survey is biased towards satisfaction, the SMS poll towards strong opinions among listeners. Your questions on this section are ready: the SMS poll against the stratified survey, non-response, leading wording, and the counter against the anonymous questionnaire.

## Subtopic: Errors in Summarising and Presenting Data

Correctly collected data can still be spoiled afterwards. Recording errors: a water meter reading of 350 litres entered as 35. Ten households' daily use was 280, 300, 310, 290, 350, 320, 305, 295, 330 and 320 litres, a sum of 3100 and a mean of 310 litres. With the 350 mistyped as 35, the sum is 2785 and the mean is 278.5, a drop of 31.5 litres from a single slipped digit. The check is to look for values that do not belong, since 35 litres is a tenth of every other household, and to go back to the source; an outlier is first a question about recording and only then a fact about the data.

Wrong summary statistic: a report says the average household income in a suburb is R18 000 a month, using the mean, when a few very high incomes pull it up and most households earn under R10 000; the median tells the typical household's story. The wrong measure is not a lie, but it misleads, and the critique names the measure that should have been used and why. Rounding too early: a percentage calculated from rounded intermediate figures drifts by a point or two; keep full precision until the final line.

Presentation errors return from the comparison lesson: a vertical axis that does not start at zero, a pie chart whose angles do not sum to 360, a histogram with unequal intervals, a comparison of counts between groups of different sizes, a graph of a chosen period. Each is a way of making correct numbers say something incorrect, and each is caught by the checks of the critical reading.

Then there is the gap between association and cause. A scatter plot shows that suburbs with more streetlights have less crime, and a councillor concludes that lights reduce crime. Perhaps they do, or perhaps wealthier suburbs afford both more lights and more security; the data alone cannot tell, and a report that claims cause from a scatter plot has overreached. The claim must be stated as association, with the alternative explanations named. The questions for this section are ready: the mistyped 35 and its effect on the mean, mean against median for incomes, the presentation errors, and streetlights and crime.

## Subtopic: Auditing a Report and the Error Museum

An audit works through a report in order and asks a fixed set of questions. Who is the population and who was actually sampled, by what method and how many. How was the data collected, with what instrument and what wording. What was measured, in what units, and were there recording checks. Which summary statistics are reported and are they the right ones for the data's shape. Are the graphs honest in scale, interval and comparison. Does the conclusion follow from the data, and is it stated with suitable caution. What limitations does the report itself admit.

Audit the SMS poll. Population claimed: South Africans. Sample: 4 000 self-selected listeners of one station. Method: paid SMS, no control over who replied or how often. Question: as broadcast, possibly leading. Summary: a single percentage, 78. Graph: none. Conclusion: that the public wants examinations scrapped, which does not follow. Limitations admitted: none. Verdict: the figure describes a self-selected group of listeners and says little about the public; the stratified 41 percent is the better estimate.

Audit the tuck shop survey. Population: the school's learners. Sample: 50 customers at the counter, a convenience sample of people who already buy there. Method: asked face to face by the owner, which pressures the answer. Summary: 45 of 50 satisfied, 90 percent. Verdict: biased upward by setting and selection; the anonymous 56 percent from a sample chosen across the school is the honest figure, and even that excludes learners who never buy because of the prices.

The error museum, five exhibits. Exhibit one: believing a large biased sample over a small fair one, 4 000 over 1 000. Exhibit two: treating an outlier as data without checking the recording, the 35 litres. Exhibit three: the mean reported for skewed incomes with no median. Exhibit four: a cause claimed from an association, streetlights and crime. Exhibit five: a report with no stated limitations, which is a report that has not been audited by its own author.

Layout for marks: for each question asked, name the source of error or bias, state its direction, up or down, give the evidence from the report, and propose the fix, a better sample, neutral wording, anonymity, a recording check, a different statistic or an honest graph. A critique that names a bias without its direction, or without a fix, is half an answer. The questions for this section are with you now: the audit questions, the two audits, and the five exhibits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

## Subtopic: Noise and Lean

Numbers go wrong in two ways, and they need two different fixes.

Noise is the wobble. Time the same runner five times and you get 14.2, 14.5, 14.3, 14.4, 14.1: sometimes a bit high, sometimes a bit low, no pattern. Noise is on both sides of the truth, so it cancels when you average, and the more readings you take the closer the average gets. Samples have noise too: two fair groups of 60 from the same school give 70 percent and 66 percent of walkers, both fine, just chance. A bigger sample has less noise.

Lean is the tilt. If every stopwatch is started late, every time is too short, and averaging a thousand of them gives you a very exact wrong answer. Lean is built into the method: whoever you asked, how you asked, what you measured with. A bigger sample does not fix lean; it just gives you more of the same tilt. Only fixing the method fixes lean: a fair sample, a neutral question, a scale that reads zero when empty.

So when you see a result, ask two questions. How much noise, meaning how small was the sample and how scattered were the readings? And which way does it lean, meaning what in the method would push the answer up or down? A small fair sample is wobbly but honest. A huge tilted sample is confident and wrong, and that is the dangerous one.

Two words for the two ideas: precise means the readings agree with each other; accurate means they agree with the truth. Late stopwatches are precise and wrong. Your questions on this section are ready: noise cancels, lean does not, bigger sample fixes which, and precise against accurate.

## Subtopic: Who Asked, Who Answered, How They Asked

Lean usually comes from three places.

Who answered. A radio station asks listeners to SMS yes or no about scrapping matric exams. Four thousand reply, 78 percent say yes. But who replied? Only listeners of that station, only the ones who cared enough to pay for an SMS, and 4 000 out of maybe 850 000 listeners. People who pick themselves are not a fair sample; the ones who feel strongly shout loudest. A proper survey of 1 000 adults chosen by the researchers found 41 percent. The smaller, chosen sample beats the bigger, self-chosen one. Same problem when a questionnaire is left in pigeonholes and 60 of 300 come back: the organised learners answered, the rest did not, and the 60 lean organised.

How they asked. Do you agree that tuck shop prices are far too high gets yes. Given the cost of ingredients, are the prices reasonable gets yes too, the other way. Both lean. The fair question asks how much you spent and lets the numbers decide. Even the order leans: ask about load shedding right after asking about prices and people are already cross.

Who asked. The tuck shop owner, at the counter, asks customers if they are happy: 45 of 50 say yes, because you do not insult the person serving you. An anonymous form filled in class says 28 of 50, which is 56 percent. Anonymity takes the pressure off. And when you asked matters: at the gate at 7 you miss the late arrivals, who are the ones who live furthest away.

Name the lean and its direction every time: the counter survey leans towards happy; the SMS poll leans towards strong opinions; the gate survey leans towards short journeys. The questions for this section: self-chosen samples, leading wording, the person asking, and naming the direction.

## Subtopic: Check the Sums and the Picture

Even good data can be spoiled on the way to the report.

A slipped digit. Ten households use 280, 300, 310, 290, 350, 320, 305, 295, 330, 320 litres a day: total 3100, mean 310. Type the 350 as 35 and the total is 2785, the mean 278.5, down by 31.5 litres because of one missing zero. The giveaway is the value that does not belong: 35 litres is a tenth of everyone else. Any value that stands far off is first a question about the recording, and only after checking is it a fact.

The wrong middle. A suburb's average income is reported as R18 000 a month, but that is the mean, and a few very rich households pulled it up while most earn under R10 000. The median tells you what a typical household earns. Not a lie, but misleading, and the fix is to name the right measure.

A crooked picture. A side scale that starts above zero, a pie that adds to 362, bands of different widths, raw counts compared between a big school and a small one, a graph that shows only the good months. The checks from the comparison lesson catch all of them.

Together is not because. Suburbs with more streetlights have less crime, so lights cut crime? Maybe, or maybe richer suburbs can afford both lights and security guards. A dot cloud shows two things moving together and cannot say which, if either, causes the other. Say association, and name the other explanation.

So audit a report like this: who was sampled and how, how the question was put, what was measured and checked, which middle was used, whether the picture is honest, whether the conclusion fits, and what limits the report admits. Name each problem, say which way it leans, give the evidence, and propose the fix. Noise and lean, who asked and answered and how, sums and pictures. The final questions of the lesson are with you now: the slipped digit, mean against median for incomes, together is not because, and the audit questions.
