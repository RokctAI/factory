### subtopic_1 — Organising Data: Ordered Lists, Frequency Tables and Two-Way Tables
**Assistant:** Why must I organise the data before calculating anything, and which organisation should I choose?

**Tutor:** Because every summary is defined on the organised data, and the organisation also shows you the shape before a single number is calculated. The ordered list is the universal first step: the twelve tuck shop values, written as 5, 8, 10, 10, 12, 12, 12, 15, 18, 20, 25, 60, reveal the smallest and largest, the cluster between 8 and 20 and the isolated 60, and the median is read straight from it. A frequency table suits data with few distinct values and many repeats, such as shoe sizes: a row for each size and a count beside it, with the counts summed to confirm the total of 25. When the data has many distinct values, group it into class intervals of equal width that do not overlap, such as 0 to under 1 kilometre, 1 to under 2, and tally each value into exactly one interval. A stem-and-leaf display is a frequency table that keeps every value: for the ten marks, the stems 2, 3 and 4 carry the leaves 3; 1, 5, 5, 8; and 0, 2, 4, 5, 7, so the ordering and the clustering are both visible. A two-way table organises by two criteria at once, such as gender and mode of transport, with row totals of 30 boys and 30 girls, column totals of 33, 21 and 6, and a grand total of 60 that checks both sets of totals; it lets you compare groups, for example 12 of 30 girls using taxis against 9 of 30 boys. The choice follows the data and the question: a single numerical variable with repeats wants a frequency table, one with spread wants intervals or a stem-and-leaf, and two categorical variables want a two-way table.

### subtopic_2 — Mean, Median and Mode
**Assistant:** How do I find the median when there is an even number of values, and how do I find the mean from a frequency table?

**Tutor:** For the median, order the values and locate the middle position. With an odd count n the median is the single value at position n plus 1 over 2, so an eleven-value list has its median at the sixth value. With an even count there are two middle values, at positions n over 2 and n over 2 plus 1, and the median is their mean; for the twelve tuck shop values the sixth and seventh are 12 and 12, so the median is 12, and for the ten marks the fifth and sixth are 38 and 40, so the median is 39. Writing one of the two middle values instead of their mean is a common error. For the mean from a frequency table, each value must be counted as many times as it occurs, so multiply each value by its frequency, add these products, and divide by the total frequency. For the shoe sizes the products are 12, 35, 48, 35 and 16, their sum is 146, and the total frequency is 25, so the mean is 5.84; adding the five sizes and dividing by 5 gives 6 and is wrong because it ignores the counts. The mode is the value with the highest frequency, size 6, and the median is the 13th of the 25 ordered values, found from cumulative frequencies of 3, 10 and 18 to lie in size 6. Reversing the mean is a matter of the sum: a mean of 20 over five values means a sum of 100, and if four values total 80 the fifth is 20. Combining groups also goes through the sum: 30 learners with mean 60 and 20 with mean 75 have total marks of 1800 plus 1500, which is 3300, over 50 learners, a combined mean of 66, not the 67.5 that averaging the two means would suggest.

### subtopic_3 — Range, Extremes and Outliers
**Assistant:** What exactly is an outlier, and why does it change some measures but not others?

**Tutor:** An outlier is a value that lies far from the rest of the data; the 60 in the tuck shop list is one because the next largest value is 25 and the cluster sits between 8 and 20. Before anything else, check whether it is genuine or a recording error, such as 6 entered as 60, since an error is corrected while a genuine value is kept and reported. The effect on each measure follows from how the measure is built. The mean adds every value, so the outlier's full size enters the sum: with the 60 the mean is 17.25, and without it the eleven remaining values total 147 and the mean is 13.36. The range is the maximum minus the minimum, so an outlier at either end changes it directly, from 55 to 20 here. The median depends only on the position of the middle, not on how far away the end values are, so removing the 60 leaves the median at 12, and the mode, the most frequent value, is likewise unaffected at 12. Two data sets illustrate why dispersion must be reported alongside the centre: 48, 49, 50, 51, 52 and 30, 40, 50, 60, 70 share a mean and median of 50 but have ranges of 4 and 40. The extremes, the minimum and maximum, deserve reporting in their own right, because the lowest mark identifies who needs help and the highest water reading identifies a possible leak. A summary that gives the mean alone hides both the spread and the outlier.

### subtopic_4 — Choosing the Right Measure and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** How do I decide which measure to recommend, and how do I write the justification so it earns full marks?

**Tutor:** Match the measure to the shape of the data and the purpose of the summary, then state both the measure and the reason in terms of the actual data. The mean is appropriate when the data is roughly symmetrical without outliers, because it uses every value, so a class's test marks are fairly summarised by their mean. The median is appropriate when there are outliers or the data is skewed, because it is not pulled, which is why incomes, house prices and spending are reported by their medians. The mode is appropriate for categorical data or when the most common value is the point, as when a shoe shop stocks the modal size. The salary example shows the stakes: eight workers on R6 000 and a manager on R40 000 give a mean of R9 777.78 and a median and mode of R6 000, so an advertisement quoting the mean is true and misleading, and the honest report gives the median and mentions the single high salary. A full-marks justification reads like this: the median of 12 rands, because the value 60 is an outlier that pulls the mean up to 17.25 while most learners spent between 8 and 20 rands. The error museum lists the ways marks are lost: a median taken from the unordered list; an even-count median given as one of the two middle values; a frequency-table mean that ignores the frequencies; a combined mean found by averaging means of different-sized groups; and a mean reported for skewed data without mention of the outlier. The layout writes the ordered list, states n, shows the sum and division for the mean, shows the middle positions for the median, gives the mode with its frequency, gives the extremes and range with units, names any outlier and its effect, and ends with the recommended measure and reason.
