### subtopic_1 — The Shape of a Data Report
**Assistant:** Why does the order of a data report matter so much, and what goes in each part?

**Tutor:** Because the order is the argument: each part supplies what the next one needs, and a reader follows the reasoning from question to answer without having to trust the author. The report opens with the question exactly as it was posed, naming the variable, the group and the period, so that the reader knows what would count as an answer. The method follows, giving the population, the sample size and how the sample was drawn, and the instrument and setting of collection; for the transport investigation that is 300 Grade 9 learners, 60 sampled by taking every 15th name on each of three class lists, and an anonymous two-item questionnaire completed during register on a Tuesday. The organised data comes next, the two-way table of mode by gender and a double bar graph with a key, because every later number must be traceable to it. Then the summary statistics with the reason for choosing them, the findings as sentences with numbers, the predictions with their uncertainty, the limitations with the direction of each possible bias, and the recommendation that answers the original question in proportion to the evidence. Traceability is the test of the whole: a reader who doubts any sentence in the findings should be able to put a finger on the table entry it rests on within seconds. Length is not the measure, since each part can be two or three sentences, but completeness is, because a report missing its method or its limitations has a hole where the reader's trust should be.

### subtopic_2 — Conclusions and Comparisons Backed by Numbers
**Assistant:** How do I write a finding so that it earns full marks, and how strongly may I word it?

**Tutor:** A finding is a claim fused to the number that supports it, with wording matched to the strength of that number. Walking is the most common way to school is a claim; 33 of 60 learners, which is 55 percent, walk is the number; together they are a finding, and either alone is not. Comparisons use the four moves from the comparison lesson and respect the sizes of the groups: girls are more likely than boys to use a taxi, 12 of 30 girls, which is 40 percent, against 9 of 30 boys, which is 30 percent; boys are more likely to walk, 18 of 30, which is 60 percent, against 15 of 30, which is 50 percent; car use is equal at 3 of 30 in each group. Percentages are written beside the counts even though both groups are 30, so that the comparison would still hold if the groups were unequal. The summary statistic is chosen for the data's shape and the choice is explained: for the tuck shop spending the median of 12 rands is the typical amount because the outlier of 60 pulls the mean to 17.25 while most learners spent between 8 and 20, and the mean and the range of 55, or 20 without the outlier, are reported alongside with that explanation. Wording follows the numbers. Fifty-five percent against 35 and 10 supports a clear statement; 40 percent against 30 is a difference of three learners in thirty and supports somewhat more likely, since another sample could narrow it. Hedging words such as slightly, somewhat and appears to are not timidity; they are the accurate size of the claim.

### subtopic_3 — Predictions, Extremes and Outliers
**Assistant:** How do I make a prediction from a sample or a trend without overclaiming, and what do I do with an outlier in the report?

**Tutor:** A prediction scales the sample's result to the population or extends the trend's pattern forward, and in both cases it carries the word about and names its basis and its doubt. From 33 of 60 walking, which is 55 percent, the report predicts that about 55 percent of the 300 Grade 9 learners, which is about 165, walk, about 105 use taxis and about 30 come by car; a sample of 60 cannot fix the population figure to the learner, and writing 165 without about claims a precision the data lacks. Extending to the whole school of 1200 adds the caution that only Grade 9 was sampled and other grades may travel differently. From the electricity graph, use rose by 60 kilowatt hours from May to June, from 420 to 480, so if the trend continued July would be about 540; the report states the basis, the recent trend, and the doubt, that winter use peaks and the rise may slow or reverse. Extremes are reported in their own right, since the furthest traveller, the heaviest water user and the lowest mark answer real questions and are not errors to be hidden. An outlier is reported with its treatment: the 60 rands was checked against the questionnaire, found to be genuine, kept, and its effect on the mean stated, which is why the median was chosen as the typical value. Silently dropping an outlier alters the data; silently including it lets it distort the summary; the honest report does neither. When an outlier proves to be an error, such as 35 litres checked against the meter and corrected to 350, the report says that it was found, checked and corrected and that the mean was recalculated, which builds the reader's trust rather than undermining it.

### subtopic_4 — Reporting Error, Bias and Limitations, and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** What belongs in the limitations section, and how do I write the final recommendation?

**Tutor:** The limitations section names each source of error or bias, states the direction in which it might push the findings, and says what was done to reduce it; it is the evidence that the author has audited the work and the reader's guide to how much weight the findings bear. For the transport report: the sample came from Grade 9 only, so predictions for the whole school assume other grades travel similarly, which may not hold in either direction; the questionnaire was completed on one Tuesday, so absent learners, who may include those travelling furthest, were missed, pushing the walking percentage slightly upward; random sampling error means another sample of 60 could land several points from 55 percent; and the anonymous closed questionnaire and the stratified sample across three classes reduced response and selection bias. For the tuck shop report: spending was self-reported from memory, which under-reports small purchases, so true amounts may be slightly higher; the sample of 12 is small, so the median is uncertain; the outlier was kept and its effect reported. The recommendation then answers the original question at the size the evidence allows: most Grade 9 learners walk, girls are somewhat more likely than boys to use taxis, traffic planning should assume about 165 walkers in the grade, and the survey should be repeated across all grades before planning for the whole school. The error museum shows the five failures a marker looks for: a finding without its number; a prediction without its uncertainty; a mean for the spending data with the outlier unmentioned or silently removed; a conclusion larger than the data, such as a reason for girls' taxi use that was never asked; and a missing limitations section or one that lists biases without their directions.
