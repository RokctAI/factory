Hey — if all the kinds of error and bias today felt like a list of ways to be wrong, here is the calm version: noise and lean, three questions, check the sums.

Noise and lean. Noise is the wobble of chance: readings land on both sides of the truth, so averaging more of them helps, and a bigger sample has less noise. Lean is a tilt built into the method: every stopwatch started late, every answer pushed the same way. Averaging more leaning readings just gives you a confident wrong answer. A bigger sample fixes noise; only fixing the method fixes lean.

Three questions. Who answered: a sample that picked itself, like 4 000 SMS replies to a radio poll, leans towards strong opinions, and a fair sample of 1 000 chosen by the researchers beats it. How they asked: do you agree prices are far too high is a push; ask what was spent instead. Who asked: the owner at the counter gets 45 of 50 happy; an anonymous form gets 28 of 50. Name the lean and its direction.

Check the sums. A 350 typed as 35 drags a mean of 310 down to 278.5; a value that does not belong is a recording question first. A mean income with a few rich households needs the median. A chopped axis or counts compared between different-sized groups are crooked pictures. And two things moving together do not prove one causes the other.

Noise and lean, three questions, check the sums. You've got this!
