### subtopic_1 — Reading a Graph Critically
**Assistant:** What does it mean to read a graph critically rather than just reading it?

**Tutor:** Reading a graph takes values and trends from it; reading it critically first checks whether the graph deserves to be trusted and whether it answers the question being argued. The check begins with the equipment: a title that says what, who and when; both axes labelled with their variables and units; a scale with equal steps whose starting point is visible; and a key wherever two series share the axes. A missing unit or an unlabelled axis is noted as a defect, not glossed over. Then the reading itself is stated with care: the electricity graph shows 320 kilowatt hours in January and 480 in June, a rise of 160, which is 50 percent of the January value, flat for three months and then climbing, steepest from May to June. The critical part looks for distortion. A vertical axis that starts at 300 without a marked break makes June's point sit six times as high as January's and suggests a severalfold increase where there was a half; unequal spacing of months on the time axis bends a straight trend; a three-dimensional pie makes the front slice look larger; unequal histogram intervals make a wide interval look busier; and a graph showing only the three rising months hides the fall before them. Finally the graph is matched to the claim. A graph of how many learners recycle at two schools answers a question about counts, and a claim about which school is more environmentally aware is a question about proportions that needs the school sizes; a graph of mean marks locates the centres, and a claim that one class is better needs the spread and the extremes too. When the graph and the claim do not match, saying so is the critical reading.

### subtopic_2 — Comparing Two Data Sets With Summary Statistics
**Assistant:** Class B has the higher mean. Why can't I just say Class B did better?

**Tutor:** Because a comparison on one number ignores most of what the data says, and in this case the one number barely differs. Class A's marks of 23, 31, 35, 35, 38, 40, 42, 44, 45 and 47 give a sum of 380 and a mean of 38.0, a median of 39 from the middle pair 38 and 40, a mode of 35, extremes of 23 and 47 and a range of 24. Class B's marks of 30, 33, 36, 37, 38, 39, 40, 41, 43 and 49 give a sum of 386 and a mean of 38.6, a median of 38.5 from the pair 38 and 39, no mode, extremes of 30 and 49 and a range of 19. The centres are nearly the same, with B's mean higher by 0.6 and A's median higher by 0.5, so the two measures of centre do not even agree on which class is ahead, and the honest statement is that the typical marks are similar. The spreads differ more: B's range of 19 against A's 24 makes B the more consistent class, A's lowest mark of 23 sits seven below B's lowest of 30, and B's highest of 49 is two above A's 47. So the comparison reads: similar centres, B more consistent, A with a weaker tail. Sample size tempers the conclusion further, since ten learners per class is small and a difference of 0.6 could easily reverse with one different learner, so nothing about the teaching follows. When sets differ in size, compare proportions rather than counts: 240 recyclers among 800 learners is 30 percent and 150 among 400 is 37.5 percent, so the school with fewer recyclers has the higher share.

### subtopic_3 — Comparing Two Data Sets With Graphs
**Assistant:** Which graph should I use to compare two sets, and what must be the same for the comparison to be fair?

**Tutor:** The graph follows the data type, as always, with the two sets sharing one set of axes and one scale. Two categorical sets across the same categories take a double bar graph with a key, as boys and girls did for transport. Two quantities over the same period take two broken lines on one graph. Two numerical sets of marks take a back-to-back stem-and-leaf display with shared stems. For the rainfall, Durban's 130, 120, 110, 70, 50 and 30 millimetres and Cape Town's 15, 15, 20, 50, 80 and 95 are plotted on the same axes, months evenly spaced and rainfall from zero; Durban's line falls steadily from the top left, Cape Town's rises from the bottom left, and they cross between April and May, with totals of 510 and 275. The crossing is the finding: opposite seasonal patterns, summer rain in Durban and winter rain in Cape Town. For the two classes, stems 2, 3 and 4 run down the centre with Class A's leaves to the left and Class B's to the right; A has a lone leaf on stem 2 and leaves on every stem while B has nothing on stem 2 and everything on stems 3 and 4, which shows A's straggler and B's tighter cluster at a glance. Fairness rests on two things. The scales must be identical, because two graphs with different vertical scales make a smaller value look larger and cannot be compared by eye. And when the groups differ in size, the bars show percentages rather than counts. Every comparison graph carries a title naming both sets and a key that tells them apart.

### subtopic_4 — Writing a Comparison and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** What should a written comparison look like, and how cautious should the conclusion be?

**Tutor:** It has four moves, each with numbers in it, and the conclusion is as cautious as the sample sizes and the size of the differences demand. First, state what is being compared and with which measures. Second, compare the centres with specific values: A's mean 38.0 and median 39 against B's mean 38.6 and median 38.5. Third, compare the spreads with specific values, including the extremes: B's range of 19 against A's 24, A's lowest of 23 against B's 30, B's highest of 49 against A's 47. Fourth, conclude in proportion to the evidence: the classes performed similarly on the typical mark, B was more consistent and A had a weaker tail, and with ten learners each these differences are too small to say anything about the teaching. For the rainfall, the moves give totals of 510 and 275, a Durban line falling from 130 to 30 and a Cape Town line rising from 15 to 95 with a crossing between April and May, opposite seasonal patterns, and the caution that six months favour Durban but a full year is needed to compare annual rainfall. The error museum holds the five failures: comparing counts from groups of different sizes instead of percentages; comparing centres alone and declaring a winner on a difference of 0.6; comparing graphs on different scales or reading a truncated axis as if it began at zero; concluding more than the data supports, such as a verdict on teaching from ten marks; and a comparison without numbers that says higher and more spread without saying by how much. The layout for marks is a side-by-side table of the summary statistics, a comparison graph on shared axes with title and key, and the four moves written out with values quoted and the caution stated.
