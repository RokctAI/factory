# Part 1 — Expert

The data cycle ends with a report, and the report is where the marks are won or lost, because it is the only part of the investigation a reader ever sees. Today we write the report: its fixed order from question to recommendation, its conclusions and comparisons backed by numbers, its predictions scaled to the population with honest uncertainty, its choice of summary statistics, its handling of extremes and outliers, and its statement of error, bias and limitations. We build one complete report from the transport and tuck shop investigations of this term and a second from the electricity graph, and we audit each sentence for the two faults that sink reports: claims without numbers and claims larger than the data. A report that a stranger could check from the tables and graphs is the standard.

## Subtopic: The Shape of a Data Report

A data report has a fixed order, and the order is the argument. First the question, exactly as posed, with its variable, group and period. Second the method: population, sample size and how the sample was drawn, how the data was collected and with what instrument. Third the data, organised: a frequency table or two-way table, and the graph that fits the data type. Fourth the summary statistics chosen for the data's shape, with the reason for the choice. Fifth the findings: conclusions and comparisons stated with numbers. Sixth any predictions, scaled to the population with their uncertainty. Seventh the limitations: sources of error and bias, and what they might do to the findings. Eighth a recommendation or answer to the original question, no bigger than the evidence.

Here is the opening of the transport report. Question: how do Grade 9 learners at our school travel to school each morning, and does this differ between boys and girls. Population: 300 Grade 9 learners. Sample: 60 learners, 20 from each of three classes by every 15th name on the class list. Method: a two-item closed questionnaire completed anonymously during register on a Tuesday. Data: the two-way table, boys walking 18, by taxi 9, by car 3; girls walking 15, by taxi 12, by car 3; totals 33, 21 and 6. Graph: a double bar graph of mode by gender with a key.

Every number in the report traces back to the table, and every claim in the findings points to a number. A reader who doubts a sentence can check it against the table in a few seconds, and that traceability is what distinguishes a report from an opinion.

Length is not the measure of a report; completeness is. Each of the eight parts can be a few sentences, and a report that omits the method or the limitations has a hole in its argument however long its findings run. The questions for this section are with you now: the eight parts in order, the opening of the transport report, and why each number must trace back to a table.

## Subtopic: Conclusions and Comparisons Backed by Numbers

A conclusion states what the data shows, in words, with the number that shows it. Walking is the most common way to school: 33 of the 60 learners sampled, which is 55 percent, walk. A conclusion without its number is an assertion; a number without its sentence is a table entry. Both together make a finding.

Comparisons follow the four moves of the comparison lesson, with the groups' sizes respected. Girls are more likely than boys to use a taxi: 12 of 30 girls, which is 40 percent, against 9 of 30 boys, which is 30 percent. Boys are more likely to walk: 18 of 30, which is 60 percent, against 15 of 30, which is 50 percent. Car use is the same at 3 of 30, which is 10 percent, in each group. Because each group has 30 learners the counts and percentages tell the same story, but the percentages are written anyway, so that the comparison would survive unequal groups.

The summary statistics are chosen for the data's shape and the choice is explained. For the tuck shop spending, the median of 12 rands is reported as the typical amount, because the value of 60 is an outlier that pulls the mean up to 17.25 while most learners spent between 8 and 20 rands; the mean is reported alongside with that explanation, and the range of 55 with the note that it is 20 without the outlier. For the test marks of the two classes, means and medians are both reported because the data is roughly symmetrical and they agree.

Conclusions are worded to match their strength. Walking is the most common mode is strongly supported, since 55 percent is well clear of the others. Girls are more likely to use taxis is supported but modest, since the difference is 3 learners in 30 and a different sample might narrow it. A conclusion is never stronger than the number under it, and hedging words, slightly, somewhat, appears to, are not weakness but accuracy. Your questions on this section are ready: a finding as sentence plus number, the three transport comparisons with percentages, the choice of median for spending, and matching the wording to the strength.

## Subtopic: Predictions, Extremes and Outliers

A prediction extends the sample's result to the population or the trend's pattern to the future, and it must carry its uncertainty. From 33 of 60 walking, which is 55 percent, we predict that about 55 percent of the 300 Grade 9 learners, which is about 165, walk to school; about 35 percent, which is about 105, use taxis; and about 10 percent, which is about 30, come by car. The word about is part of the prediction. A sample of 60 cannot pin the population figure to the learner, and a report that says 165 learners walk has claimed a precision it does not have. If the prediction is extended to the whole school of 1200, the caution grows, because the sample was drawn from Grade 9 only and other grades may travel differently.

Predictions from a trend extend a broken-line graph. The household's electricity use rose by 60 kilowatt hours from May to June, from 420 to 480; if the trend continued, July would be about 540. The prediction is reported with its basis, the recent trend, and its caution, that winter use peaks and the rise may slow or reverse in August. A trend prediction is only as good as the assumption that the pattern continues, and the report says so.

Extremes and outliers have a role in the report of their own. The extremes, minimum and maximum, are reported because they answer real questions: the learner who travels furthest, the household that uses most water, the lowest mark in the class. They are not errors to be hidden. An outlier is reported with what was done about it: the 60 rands was checked against the questionnaire, found to be a genuine answer, kept in the data, and its effect on the mean stated, which is why the median was chosen as the typical value. A report that silently drops an outlier has altered the data; a report that includes it without comment has let it distort the summary; the honest report does neither.

When an outlier is found to be an error, the correction is reported too: a water reading of 35 litres was checked against the meter and corrected to 350, and the mean recalculated. The reader learns both that the error occurred and that it was fixed, which builds rather than undermines trust. The questions for this section are ready: the predictions of about 165, 105 and 30, the July forecast with its caution, and the three honest ways to report an outlier.

## Subtopic: Reporting Error, Bias and Limitations, and the Error Museum

The limitations section names the sources of error and bias in the investigation, states the direction in which each might push the findings, and says what was done to reduce it. For the transport report: the sample was drawn from Grade 9 only, so predictions for the whole school assume other grades travel similarly, which may not hold. The questionnaire was completed on a single Tuesday, so learners absent that day, who may include those who travel furthest, were missed, pushing the walking percentage slightly up. Random sampling error means a different sample of 60 could give a walking percentage several points away from 55. The questionnaire was anonymous and closed, which reduced response bias, and the stratified sample across the three classes reduced selection bias.

For the tuck shop report: spending was self-reported from memory, which tends to under-report small purchases, so the true amounts may be slightly higher. The sample of 12 is small, so the median is uncertain. The outlier of 60 was kept and its effect reported.

A limitations section is not a confession of failure; it is the evidence that the author has audited the work and the reader's guide to how much weight the findings bear. A report without limitations is the one to distrust.

The recommendation closes the report by answering the question in proportion to the evidence. Most Grade 9 learners walk, with girls more likely than boys to use taxis; the school's traffic planning should assume about 165 walkers in the grade and should repeat the survey across all grades before planning for the whole school.

The error museum, five exhibits. Exhibit one: a finding without its number, most learners walk. Exhibit two: a prediction without its uncertainty, 165 learners walk to school. Exhibit three: a mean reported for the spending data with no mention of the outlier, or the outlier silently removed. Exhibit four: a conclusion larger than the data, girls prefer taxis because they feel unsafe walking, which the data never asked. Exhibit five: a report with no limitations section, or a limitations section that lists biases without their directions.

Layout for marks: the eight parts in order with headings; every finding as a sentence with its number and its source table; percentages beside counts in comparisons; predictions with about and their basis; the chosen statistic with its reason and the outlier's treatment; limitations with directions; and a recommendation that answers the question asked. The questions for this section are with you now: the limitations with their directions, the recommendation, and the five exhibits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through telling the story in order, every claim with its number and its caution, and the weather forecast rule — plain words, same rules.

## Subtopic: Tell the Story in Order

A data report is a story told in a fixed order, and if you skip a chapter the reader cannot follow. Chapter one: the question you set out to answer, exactly as you asked it. Chapter two: how you got the data, who the whole group was, how many you asked and how you picked them, and what you used to ask. Chapter three: the data, tidied into a table and drawn as the right graph. Chapter four: the middles and spreads you chose, and why those ones. Chapter five: what you found, in sentences with numbers. Chapter six: what you predict for the whole group, with the word about. Chapter seven: what could have gone wrong and which way it would push things. Chapter eight: your answer to the question, no bigger than the evidence.

The transport story begins like this. Question: how do Grade 9 learners get to school, and do boys and girls differ. Group: 300 Grade 9s. Sample: 60, twenty from each class, every 15th name. Tool: two tick-box questions, anonymous, during register. Data: boys 18 walk, 9 taxi, 3 car; girls 15 walk, 12 taxi, 3 car. Graph: a double bar graph with a key.

The rule of the story is that every number in your sentences must be findable in your table, so anyone who doubts you can check in seconds. That is what makes it a report and not just your opinion with decoration.

Short is fine. Each chapter can be two or three sentences. Missing a chapter is not fine, because a story without the how or without the what-could-go-wrong has a hole in it. Your questions on this section are ready: the eight chapters in order, the start of the transport story, and every number findable in the table.

## Subtopic: Claim, Number, Caution

Every finding is three things stuck together: a claim, the number that backs it, and a caution the right size.

Claim: walking is the most common way to school. Number: 33 of 60, which is 55 percent. Caution: this is a sample of Grade 9, so other grades might differ.

Claim: girls use taxis more than boys. Number: 12 of 30 girls, 40 percent, against 9 of 30 boys, 30 percent. Caution: the gap is three learners in thirty, so another sample could shrink it. Write the percentages even when the groups are the same size, so the comparison still works if they were not.

For the typical tuck shop spend, the claim is that a typical learner spent about 12 rands. The number is the median, 12. The caution explains why not the mean: one learner spent 60, a giant who pulls the mean up to 17.25, so the median is the honest middle, and the mean is given alongside with that explanation. Spread: the range is 55 with the giant and 20 without, and both are said.

Match your words to your numbers. Fifty-five percent against 35 and 10 is clear, so say clearly. Forty against 30 is a lean, so say somewhat more likely. Words like slightly and appears to are not you being timid; they are you being exact.

The giant gets a sentence of his own. Was the 60 checked? Yes, against the form, and it was real, so it stayed in, and its effect was reported. If it had been a slip, say a 6 written as 60, you would say it was found, checked and corrected, and that the mean was recalculated. Never quietly delete, never quietly include. The questions for this section: claim plus number plus caution, the taxi comparison in percentages, median with the reason, and the giant's own sentence.

## Subtopic: The Weather Forecast Rule

A prediction is a forecast, and the weather service never says it will rain; it says 70 percent chance of rain. Your predictions follow the same rule: say about, and say what the forecast rests on.

From 55 percent of the sample walking, predict that about 55 percent of the 300 Grade 9s walk, which is about 165; about 105 use taxis; about 30 come by car. Not 165 exactly, because 60 learners cannot pin down 300 to the learner. Stretching to the whole school of 1200 needs an extra warning, because only Grade 9 was asked.

From a trend, forecast the next step and say why it might not happen. Electricity went from 420 in May to 480 in June, up 60; if that keeps going, July is about 540. But winter peaks, so the rise may slow, and the report says so.

Then the chapter nobody wants to write and every marker looks for: what could have gone wrong. Say each thing and which way it pushes. Only Grade 9 was asked, so whole-school predictions assume other grades are similar. Absent learners on the Tuesday may be the long-distance ones, pushing the walking percentage up a little. A different sample of 60 could land a few points away. Spending was from memory, which forgets small buys, so true spending may be a bit higher. And say what you did right: anonymous forms, a sample across all three classes.

Finish by answering the question at the size the evidence allows: most Grade 9s walk, girls somewhat more likely to use taxis, plan for about 165 walkers in the grade, and survey the other grades before planning for the whole school. Story in order, claim-number-caution, the forecast rule. The final questions of the lesson are with you now: about 165, the July forecast and its warning, the what-could-go-wrong chapter with directions, and the right-sized answer.
