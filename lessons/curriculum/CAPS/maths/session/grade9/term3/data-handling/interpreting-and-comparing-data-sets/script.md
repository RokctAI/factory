# Part 1 — Expert

A graph or a table is an argument, and reading it critically means checking the argument before accepting it. Today we read graphs for what they actually show rather than what they appear to show, and we compare two sets of data on the same issue using both summary statistics and graphs drawn to the same scale. Two classes wrote the same test; two cities recorded rainfall over the same six months; two schools counted their recyclers. In each case the question is the same: how are the two sets alike, how do they differ, and what can fairly be concluded. The skill examiners want is a comparison that uses numbers, mentions both centre and spread, and states its conclusion with the right amount of caution. Next lesson we turn to the sources of error and bias behind the numbers; today we compare the numbers themselves.

## Subtopic: Reading a Graph Critically

Before reading any value from a graph, read its equipment. The title says what, who and when. The axis labels give the variables and their units. The scale shows the steps and where the axis begins. The key, if there is one, says which series is which. A graph with an unlabelled axis or an unstated unit cannot be read with confidence, and saying so is part of a critical reading.

Then read the values and the trend. The household electricity graph from last lesson shows 320 kilowatt hours in January and 480 in June, a rise of 160, which is 50 percent of the January figure. The trend is flat for three months and then rising, and the steepest segment is May to June. Reading a value means stating it with its unit; reading a trend means naming its direction and where it changes.

Now read for distortion. If the vertical axis of that graph started at 300 rather than zero, the June bar or point would sit at six times the height of the January one, and a glance would suggest use had multiplied several times over when it rose by half. A truncated axis without a marked break is the commonest distortion. Others are a horizontal axis with unequal gaps between the time points, a pie chart drawn in three dimensions so that the front slice looks larger, a histogram with unequal intervals, and a graph that shows a carefully chosen period, the three rising months only, and leaves out the fall before them.

Ask what question the graph answers and whether it is the question being discussed. A graph of total recyclers at two schools answers how many; a claim that one school is more environmentally aware is a question of proportion and needs the school sizes. A graph of the mean mark answers where the centre is; a claim that one class is better needs the spread and the extremes as well. Reading critically means matching the graph to the claim and noticing when they do not match. It also means asking who made the graph and why: a company's sales graph and an advertiser's price comparison are drawn to persuade, and the checks above are how a reader keeps the persuasion honest. The questions for this section are with you now: the equipment to check, reading a value and a trend from the electricity graph, the truncated axis, and matching graph to claim.

## Subtopic: Comparing Two Data Sets With Summary Statistics

Two classes of ten wrote the same test out of 50. Class A scored 23, 31, 35, 35, 38, 40, 42, 44, 45 and 47. Class B scored 30, 33, 36, 37, 38, 39, 40, 41, 43 and 49. The raw lists invite a glance and a guess; the comparison needs numbers.

Class A: the sum is 380 and the mean is 38.0; the median is the mean of the fifth and sixth values, 38 and 40, which is 39; the mode is 35; the extremes are 23 and 47 and the range is 24. Class B: the sum is 386 and the mean is 38.6; the median is the mean of 38 and 39, which is 38.5; there is no mode, since every mark appears once; the extremes are 30 and 49 and the range is 19.

Now compare. The centres are close: B's mean is higher by 0.6 and A's median is higher by 0.5, so neither class is clearly ahead on the typical mark. The spreads differ: A's range of 24 is larger than B's 19, so A's marks are more spread out, and A's lowest mark of 23 is seven below B's lowest of 30, while B's highest of 49 is two above A's 47. B is the more consistent class; A has both a weaker tail and a slightly lower top.

A fair comparison uses the same measures for both sets, reports centre and spread, and refers to specific values. It also respects the sizes: ten learners each is a small sample, so a difference of 0.6 in the mean is not evidence that one class is taught better, and the conclusion says the classes performed similarly, with B more consistent. A comparison that says B is better because 38.6 is bigger than 38.0 has read the numbers without understanding them. The two measures of centre do not even agree on which class leads, since A's median is the higher one, and that disagreement is itself evidence that the centres are effectively tied.

When the two sets have different sizes, compare proportions, not counts. School X has 240 recyclers among 800 learners, which is 30 percent; School Y has 150 among 400, which is 37.5 percent. X has more recyclers, Y has the higher proportion, and a claim that Y is more environmentally aware rests on the percentage, not the count. Your questions on this section are ready: the two classes' means, medians and ranges, what the comparison can and cannot conclude, and the two schools' percentages.

## Subtopic: Comparing Two Data Sets With Graphs

Graphs compare two sets when they share one set of axes and one scale. A double bar graph places the two groups side by side category by category, as the boys and girls did for transport last lesson, with a key. Two broken lines on one graph compare two quantities over the same period. A back-to-back stem-and-leaf display shares the stems and puts one class's leaves to the left and the other's to the right.

Rainfall, in millimetres, for six months in two cities. Durban: January 130, February 120, March 110, April 70, May 50, June 30. Cape Town: January 15, February 15, March 20, April 50, May 80, June 95. Plotted as two broken lines on the same axes, the Durban line falls steadily from a high start and the Cape Town line rises steadily from a low start, and they cross between April and May. The six-month totals are 510 for Durban and 275 for Cape Town. The graph shows two opposite seasonal patterns: summer rainfall in Durban, winter rainfall in Cape Town, with the crossing marking the change of season. Neither line alone tells this story; the comparison does.

The two classes on a back-to-back stem-and-leaf. Stems 2, 3 and 4 run down the middle. Class A's leaves on the left: for stem 2, 3; for stem 3, 8, 5, 5, 1 reading outward; for stem 4, 7, 5, 4, 2, 0. Class B's leaves on the right: for stem 2, nothing; for stem 3, 0, 3, 6, 7, 8, 9; for stem 4, 0, 1, 3, 9. The display shows A spread across all three stems with a lone mark in the twenties, and B concentrated in the thirties and forties, the same conclusion as the ranges but visible at once.

For graphs to compare fairly, the scales must match: two broken lines drawn on separate axes with different vertical scales cannot be compared by eye, and two bar graphs with different scales make a smaller count look larger. When groups differ in size, the bars show percentages. A comparison graph gets a title that names both sets and a key that distinguishes them. The questions for this section are ready: the two rainfall lines and their crossing, the back-to-back stem-and-leaf, and why the scales must match.

## Subtopic: Writing a Comparison and the Error Museum

A written comparison has a fixed shape. State what is being compared and the measures used. Compare the centres with specific values. Compare the spreads with specific values, including the extremes. Note any outliers or unusual features. Draw a conclusion that answers the question asked, with caution proportionate to the sample sizes and the size of the differences.

Here is one for the two classes. Class A and Class B each had ten learners write the same test out of 50. The classes have similar centres: A's mean is 38.0 and median 39; B's mean is 38.6 and median 38.5. B's marks are more consistent, with a range of 19 against A's 24; A's lowest mark of 23 is well below B's lowest of 30, while B's highest of 49 slightly exceeds A's 47. Neither class is clearly stronger on the typical mark; B is more consistent and A has a weaker tail. With only ten learners in each class, these differences are small and should not be taken as evidence about the teaching.

Here is one for the rainfall. Over January to June, Durban received 510 millimetres and Cape Town 275. Durban's monthly rainfall fell from 130 to 30, and Cape Town's rose from 15 to 95, with the two crossing between April and May. The cities have opposite seasonal patterns, Durban wetter in summer and Cape Town in winter, so the comparison of totals over six months favours Durban but a comparison over a full year would be needed to compare annual rainfall.

The error museum, five exhibits. Exhibit one: comparing counts from groups of different sizes, 240 recyclers against 150, instead of 30 percent against 37.5 percent. Exhibit two: comparing centres only, declaring B better because 38.6 exceeds 38.0, with no mention of spread. Exhibit three: comparing graphs drawn on different scales, or reading a truncated axis as if it started at zero. Exhibit four: a conclusion larger than the data, such as a judgement about teaching from ten marks. Exhibit five: a comparison with no numbers in it, saying one set is higher and more spread without saying by how much.

Layout for marks: a table of the summary statistics for both sets side by side, a comparison graph on shared axes with title and key, then the written comparison in the fixed shape, centres, spreads, features, conclusion, with values quoted and the caution stated. The questions for this section are with you now: the shape of a written comparison, the two worked comparisons, and the five exhibits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through reading the whole picture before believing it, two classes with the same middle and a different spread, and two lines on one graph — plain words, same rules.

## Subtopic: Read the Whole Picture

A graph is somebody telling you something, and before you believe it you check how they told it. Read the title: what, who, when. Read the labels on both axes and their units. Look at where the side scale starts and how big its steps are. Find the key if there are two shades. If any of that is missing, say so; a graph without units is a picture, not evidence.

Then read it properly. The electricity graph says 320 in January and 480 in June, a rise of 160, which is half of 320, so use went up by 50 percent. Flat for three months, then climbing, steepest from May to June. A value gets its unit; a trend gets its direction and its turning point.

Then look for tricks. If the side scale starts at 300 instead of 0, June towers over January and the rise looks like a multiplication rather than a half. That is a chopped axis, the oldest trick there is. Watch too for months spaced unevenly along the bottom, a pie drawn in 3D so the front slice bulges, bands of different widths in a histogram, and a graph that shows only the months that make the point.

Finally ask whether the graph answers the question actually being argued. A graph of how many learners recycle at two schools cannot say which school cares more unless you know how big each school is. A graph of average marks cannot say which class is better without the spread. Your questions on this section are ready: the checks before reading, reading the electricity graph, the chopped axis, and the graph that answers a different question.

## Subtopic: Same Middle, Different Spread

Two classes of ten wrote the same test out of 50. Class A: 23, 31, 35, 35, 38, 40, 42, 44, 45, 47. Class B: 30, 33, 36, 37, 38, 39, 40, 41, 43, 49. Looking at the lists tells you almost nothing; working out the same measures for both tells you a lot.

Middles. Class A adds to 380, mean 38.0; the two in the middle are 38 and 40, median 39. Class B adds to 386, mean 38.6; the two in the middle are 38 and 39, median 38.5. The middles are nearly the same: B's mean is up by 0.6, A's median is up by 0.5. Call it a draw.

Spreads. Class A runs from 23 to 47, range 24. Class B runs from 30 to 49, range 19. B is tighter. A's weakest learner, at 23, is seven below B's weakest at 30, and B's strongest, at 49, is two above A's strongest at 47. So B is the steadier class and A has a straggler.

Say it fairly. The classes did about the same on the typical mark; B was more consistent; A had one low mark. Do not say B is better because 38.6 beats 38.0; with ten learners each, a gap of 0.6 is nothing. And never compare raw counts from groups of different sizes: 240 recyclers out of 800 is 30 percent, 150 out of 400 is 37.5 percent, so the smaller school has fewer recyclers and a bigger share of them. The questions for this section: the same measures for both, the draw in the middles, the tighter spread, and 30 percent against 37.5.

## Subtopic: Two Lines on One Graph

To compare two things over time, draw them on the same picture with the same scale and a key. Durban's rain, in millimetres, January to June: 130, 120, 110, 70, 50, 30. Cape Town's: 15, 15, 20, 50, 80, 95. One line slides down from top left, the other climbs from bottom left, and they cross between April and May. Totals: Durban 510, Cape Town 275. The crossing is the story: Durban's rain is a summer thing, Cape Town's is a winter thing, and the seasons change hands in autumn.

The two classes can share a picture too. Draw the stems 2, 3, 4 down the middle, put Class A's units on the left and Class B's on the right: a back-to-back stem-and-leaf. A has a lonely 3 on the 2 stem and leaves on every stem; B has nothing on the 2 stem and everything packed into the 3 and 4 stems. You can see the straggler and the tighter class without a single calculation.

Two rules for comparison pictures. Same scale, or the eye is fooled: two graphs with different side scales make a small number look big. Percentages, not counts, when the groups are different sizes.

Then write the comparison in four moves. Say what you compared and with which measures. Compare the middles with the actual numbers. Compare the spreads with the actual numbers, including the lowest and highest. Finish with a conclusion no bigger than the data allows: similar middles, B steadier, ten learners is too few to judge the teaching. For the rain: Durban wetter so far, opposite seasons, a full year needed to compare properly. Read the whole picture, same middle different spread, two lines on one graph. The final questions of the lesson are with you now: the crossing lines, the back-to-back display, the two rules, and the four moves.
