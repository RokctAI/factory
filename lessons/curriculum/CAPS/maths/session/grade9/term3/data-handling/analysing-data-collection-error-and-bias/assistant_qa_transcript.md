### subtopic_1 — Random Error and Systematic Bias
**Assistant:** If a bigger sample makes the result more reliable, why does the lesson say a large sample can still be wrong?

**Tutor:** Because a larger sample reduces only one of the two kinds of error. Random error is the scatter of chance: five stopwatch readings of 14.2, 14.5, 14.3, 14.4 and 14.1 seconds fall on both sides of the true time, and their mean is closer to it than any single reading; two fair samples of 60 learners give 70 and 66 percent of walkers because chance picked different learners, and a sample of 600 would land closer to the school's true figure. More data shrinks this scatter. Systematic bias is a lean in one direction built into the method: stopwatches all started late give times that are all too short, and the mean of a thousand of them is precisely wrong; a convenience sample of early arrivals under-counts long journeys however many early arrivals are asked; a leading question pushes every respondent the same way. More data from a biased method gives more of the same lean, with a smaller scatter around the wrong value, which makes the wrong answer look more trustworthy. The remedies therefore differ. Random error is reduced by repeating measurements, averaging, and increasing the sample; bias is reduced only by changing the method, with a fair sample, a neutral question or a calibrated instrument. The vocabulary of precision and accuracy keeps the two apart: precise readings agree with each other, accurate readings agree with the truth, and the late stopwatches are precise and inaccurate. A report should say which errors it faced and what it did about each, and a reader should prefer a small fair sample to a large biased one.

### subtopic_2 — Bias in Collecting the Data
**Assistant:** How do I identify the biases in a survey, and how do I say which way they push the result?

**Tutor:** Work through who was asked, who answered, and how the question was put, and for each bias you find, state the direction it pushes. Selection bias comes from how the sample was drawn: a radio SMS poll samples one station's listeners rather than the public, and only those who cared enough to pay for a reply, which is self-selection; 4 000 replies from perhaps 850 000 listeners are a self-chosen half a percent, and the result of 78 percent leans towards the strong opinions of that audience, while a stratified survey of 1 000 adults chosen by the investigators found 41 percent and is the better estimate despite being smaller. Non-response bias comes from who declines: a questionnaire left in pigeonholes and returned by 60 of 300 learners leans towards the organised learners who bothered, and collecting answers in class reduces it. Question bias comes from wording and order: do you agree the prices are far too high pushes towards yes, given the cost of ingredients are the prices reasonable pushes the other way, and a question about load shedding asked straight after one about rising prices draws angrier answers; the neutral form asks how much was spent. Interviewer and setting bias comes from who asks and where: the tuck shop owner asking at the counter gets 45 of 50 satisfied because customers avoid offending the person serving them, while an anonymous form completed in class gets 28 of 50, which is 56 percent, so the counter survey leans towards satisfaction. Timing bias comes from when: a gate survey at seven misses the late arrivals who travel furthest, so it leans towards short journeys. A complete critique names the bias, its direction and its fix.

### subtopic_3 — Errors in Summarising and Presenting Data
**Assistant:** The data was collected fairly. What can still go wrong between the raw numbers and the report?

**Tutor:** Four things, and each has a check. Recording errors slip in when a value is written or typed: ten households' daily water use of 280, 300, 310, 290, 350, 320, 305, 295, 330 and 320 litres has a sum of 3100 and a mean of 310, but with the 350 typed as 35 the sum becomes 2785 and the mean 278.5, a drop of 31.5 litres from one missing digit. The check is to scan for values that do not belong, since 35 is a tenth of every other household, and to return to the source before treating an outlier as data. The wrong summary statistic misleads without lying: a suburb's mean income of R18 000 is pulled up by a few very high earners while most households earn under R10 000, and the median describes the typical household; the critique names the measure that should have been used and the reason, the skew. Rounding too early shifts percentages by a point or two, so full precision is kept until the final line. Presentation errors are the crooked pictures from the comparison lesson: an axis not starting at zero, pie angles not summing to 360, unequal histogram intervals, counts compared across groups of different sizes, and a cherry-picked period. Finally the leap from association to cause: suburbs with more streetlights show less crime on a scatter plot, and concluding that lights reduce crime ignores the possibility that wealthier suburbs afford both lighting and security. The honest statement reports an association and names the alternative explanation.

### subtopic_4 — Auditing a Report and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** When a question asks me to analyse a report's data collection and summaries, what structure should my answer follow?

**Tutor:** Follow the audit list in order and, for every problem found, give four things: the name of the error or bias, its direction, the evidence from the report, and the fix. The audit asks who the population is and who was actually sampled, by what method and how many; how the data was collected, with what instrument and wording; what was measured, in what units, and whether recording was checked; which summary statistics are reported and whether they suit the data's shape; whether the graphs are honest in scale, intervals and comparisons; whether the conclusion follows and is suitably cautious; and what limitations the report admits. Applied to the SMS poll: population claimed, the public; sample, 4 000 self-selected listeners of one station; method, paid SMS with no control over who replied or how often; wording, as broadcast and possibly leading; summary, a single percentage of 78; conclusion, that the public wants examinations scrapped, which does not follow; limitations admitted, none; verdict, the stratified 41 percent is the better estimate. Applied to the tuck shop: sample of 50 customers at the counter, a convenience sample of existing buyers; asked face to face by the owner, pressuring the answer upward; 45 of 50 satisfied, 90 percent; verdict, biased upward by selection and setting, with the anonymous 56 percent from a sample across the school the honest figure, and even that excluding learners who never buy. The error museum shows what an audit catches: a large biased sample preferred to a small fair one; an outlier accepted without checking the recording; a mean for skewed incomes with no median; a cause claimed from an association; and a report with no stated limitations. A critique that names a bias without its direction or its fix earns half the marks.
