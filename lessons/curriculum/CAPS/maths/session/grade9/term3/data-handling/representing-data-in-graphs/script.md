# Part 1 — Expert

Organised data becomes a graph so that its shape can be seen at a glance, and Grade 9 draws and reads six kinds: the bar graph and the double bar graph, the histogram with given intervals and with intervals you choose yourself, the pie chart, the broken-line graph and the scatter plot. Each kind fits a kind of data, and choosing the wrong graph for the data is as serious as a wrong calculation. Today we construct each graph from the data of the last two lessons, learn the conventions that make a graph readable, title, labelled axes with units, an even scale starting at zero, a key where two series share the axes, and we practise the arithmetic that pie charts and histograms require. Reading and comparing the graphs critically is next lesson's work; today we draw them correctly.

## Subtopic: Bar Graphs, Double Bar Graphs and Pie Charts

Categorical data is represented by a bar graph or a pie chart. The sixty learners' modes of transport, walk 33, taxi 21 and car 6, make a bar graph with the categories along the horizontal axis, one bar per category, gaps between the bars because the categories are separate, and frequency on the vertical axis with an even scale from zero. The tallest bar is the mode. A bar graph can be drawn with horizontal bars if the category names are long, and the convention is otherwise unchanged.

A double bar graph compares two groups across the same categories. From the two-way table, boys walk 18, taxi 9, car 3, and girls walk 15, taxi 12, car 3. Each category gets two bars side by side, one shaded for boys and one for girls, with a key saying which is which. The pairs make the comparison immediate: more boys than girls walk, more girls than boys take taxis, and the car bars are equal. The two groups must be of comparable size for the comparison to be fair, 30 and 30 here; if they were not, percentages would be graphed instead of counts.

A pie chart shows each category as a slice of a whole, so it suits data where the parts add up to a meaningful total. The angle of each slice is the category's fraction of the total multiplied by 360 degrees. Walk: 33 over 60 times 360, which is 198 degrees. Taxi: 21 over 60 times 360, which is 126 degrees. Car: 6 over 60 times 360, which is 36 degrees. The three angles add to 360, which is the check. As percentages the slices are 55, 35 and 10 percent, and both the angle and the percentage are usually calculated before drawing. The slices are drawn with a protractor from the centre, each labelled with its category and percentage, and the chart gets a title.

A pie chart cannot show frequencies directly, only shares, and it becomes unreadable with more than about six categories; a bar graph has neither limitation. A pie chart also cannot compare two groups unless two charts are drawn with their totals stated. The questions for this section are with you now: the transport bar graph, the double bar graph with its key, the three pie angles with their check, and when a pie chart is the wrong choice.

## Subtopic: Histograms With Given and Own Intervals

Numerical data that has been grouped into class intervals is represented by a histogram. Distances to school for 30 learners were grouped as 0 to under 1 kilometre, 8 learners; 1 to under 2, 11 learners; 2 to under 3, 6 learners; 3 to under 4, 3 learners; and 4 to under 5, 2 learners. The frequencies add to 30. The intervals go on the horizontal axis as a continuous number line, the frequencies on the vertical axis, and the bars touch, because the intervals run into each other with no gap in the data between them. The touching bars are what distinguishes a histogram from a bar graph, and a learner who leaves gaps has drawn the wrong graph.

The histogram's shape is the data's shape. The distances pile up in the 1 to under 2 interval and tail off towards 5 kilometres, a right-skewed shape typical of distances and incomes. The modal interval is 1 to under 2 kilometres. The histogram does not show individual values, so the median and mean cannot be read exactly from it, only estimated.

When the intervals are not given, you choose them, and the choice follows rules. Find the range of the data: distances from 0.2 to 4.8 kilometres have a range of 4.6. Choose between five and eight intervals of equal width that cover the range with round boundaries, here five intervals of width 1 starting at 0. Make the intervals non-overlapping with clear boundaries, 0 to under 1 and 1 to under 2 rather than 0 to 1 and 1 to 2, so that a distance of exactly 1 kilometre belongs to one interval only. Tally each value into its interval and check that the tallies add to the total.

Too few intervals hide the shape, two intervals make a histogram into two blocks; too many leave most intervals with one or no learners and the shape dissolves into spikes. Equal widths matter: unequal intervals make a wider interval look more frequent than it is, and Grade 9 histograms always use equal widths. Marks out of 50 for a class would be grouped as 0 to 9, 10 to 19, 20 to 29, 30 to 39 and 40 to 50, five intervals, with the last widened by one mark to include 50. Your questions on this section are ready: the distance histogram with touching bars, its modal interval and shape, and the rules for choosing intervals.

## Subtopic: Broken-Line Graphs and Scatter Plots

Data collected over time is represented by a broken-line graph. A household's electricity use in kilowatt hours over six months was 320 in January, 300 in February, 310 in March, 350 in April, 420 in May and 480 in June. Time goes on the horizontal axis at equal spacing, the quantity on the vertical axis, each month is plotted as a point, and consecutive points are joined by straight segments. The segments show the trend: roughly level from January to March, then rising steeply into winter as heaters go on. The line is called broken because it bends at every point; it does not claim to know the values between the months.

The vertical axis of a broken-line graph should start at zero unless a clear break is marked, because a scale starting at 300 would make the rise from 320 to 480 look like a leap from nothing to everything. The slope of each segment shows the rate of change: the steepest segment, May to June, is the month of fastest increase, 60 kilowatt hours.

A scatter plot shows two numerical variables measured on the same individuals, to see whether they are related. Eight learners' hours of study for a test and their marks out of 100 were plotted as points: one hour and 35, two hours and 42, two hours and 50, three hours and 55, four hours and 60, five hours and 68, six hours and 75, seven hours and 80. Each learner is one dot, hours on the horizontal axis and marks on the vertical axis, and the dots are not joined. The dots rise from left to right, which is a positive relationship: more hours tend to go with higher marks. Dots falling from left to right would be a negative relationship, and dots scattered with no slope would show no relationship.

A scatter plot shows association, not cause. Hours and marks rise together, but the plot alone cannot say whether studying raised the marks or whether confident learners both studied more and scored higher; that caution belongs in every scatter plot conclusion. A dot far from the pattern, a learner who studied seven hours and scored 40, would be an outlier and worth investigating. The questions for this section are ready: the electricity graph and its steepest segment, the axis starting at zero, the study scatter plot and its direction, and association against cause.

## Subtopic: Choosing and Labelling a Graph, and the Error Museum

The data type chooses the graph. Categorical data, such as transport mode or home language: a bar graph, or a pie chart if the shares of a whole are the point, or a double bar graph to compare two groups. Numerical data grouped in intervals, such as distances or marks: a histogram. A quantity measured over time: a broken-line graph. Two numerical variables on the same individuals: a scatter plot. A question that asks you to choose and justify wants the graph named and the data type given as the reason: a histogram, because distance is continuous numerical data grouped into intervals.

Every graph carries the same equipment. A title that says what, who and when. Both axes labelled with the variable and its unit, kilometres or kilowatt hours or percent. An even scale on each axis, with equal steps and starting at zero for frequencies and quantities. A key when two series share the axes. Categories or intervals clearly marked. Points plotted accurately with a sharp pencil, and bars drawn with a ruler. In an examination the title and labels carry marks of their own.

Draw the pie chart's arithmetic before the chart: a table with category, frequency, fraction, angle and percentage, with the angles summed to 360 as a check. Draw the histogram's frequency table before the histogram, with the tallies summed to the total. Draw the scatter plot's pairs as a two-column table before plotting.

The error museum, five exhibits. Exhibit one: a histogram with gaps between the bars, or a bar graph with the bars touching. Exhibit two: a pie chart whose angles do not add to 360, usually from rounding each angle up, or a pie chart drawn by guessing the slices. Exhibit three: a vertical axis that starts above zero without a marked break, exaggerating differences. Exhibit four: a double bar graph with no key, or comparing counts from groups of very different sizes. Exhibit five: a scatter plot with the dots joined up, or a conclusion that one variable causes the other.

Layout for marks: name the graph and the data type that justifies it, write the frequency or angle table with its check, draw with ruler and protractor, add the title, axis labels with units, scale and key, and finish with one sentence stating what the graph shows. The questions for this section are with you now: the data type to graph type matching, the equipment every graph carries, and the five exhibits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through towers and slices, bars that touch, and lines through time and clouds of dots — plain words, same rules.

## Subtopic: Towers and Slices

When your data is words, walk, taxi, car, you build towers or cut a pie. Towers are a bar graph: one tower per word, height equal to the count, towers standing apart with gaps because walk and taxi are separate things. Sixty learners: a walk tower of 33, a taxi tower of 21, a car tower of 6. The tallest tower is the mode.

To compare boys with girls, build two towers for every word, side by side, one shade for boys and another for girls, and put a key in the corner saying which shade is which. Boys 18, 9, 3 and girls 15, 12, 3: the walk pair leans to the boys, the taxi pair leans to the girls, and the car pair is level. That is a double bar graph, and it is only fair when the two groups are about the same size, 30 and 30 here.

A pie is for shares of a whole. Each slice is the word's share of 360 degrees. Walk is 33 out of 60, and 33 over 60 times 360 is 198 degrees. Taxi: 21 over 60 times 360 is 126. Car: 6 over 60 times 360 is 36. Add them: 198 plus 126 plus 36 is 360, so the pie closes. As percentages: 55, 35 and 10. Work out every angle before you pick up the protractor, and label each slice with its word and percentage.

Pies have limits. They show shares, not counts; they get crowded past six slices; and you cannot compare two groups in one pie. Towers can do all of that. Your questions on this section are ready: towers with gaps, two towers per word with a key, the three slice angles that add to 360, and when a pie is the wrong tool.

## Subtopic: Bars That Touch

When your data is numbers in bands, like distances to school in 0 to under 1 kilometre, 1 to under 2, and so on, you draw a histogram, and the one rule people forget is that the bars touch. They touch because the bands run straight into each other on a number line, with no gap in the numbers between 0.99 and 1.

Thirty learners: 8 in the first band, 11 in the second, 6 in the third, 3 in the fourth, 2 in the fifth. Check: 8 plus 11 plus 6 plus 3 plus 2 is 30. Draw the number line along the bottom, mark 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, and raise a bar over each band to its count, shoulder to shoulder. The tallest bar, 1 to under 2, is the modal band. The shape leans: a big pile near the school and a long tail of learners who live far away.

If nobody gives you the bands, make them. Find the smallest and biggest values, 0.2 and 4.8, so the range is 4.6. Pick five to eight bands of the same width with round edges, here five bands of width 1 from 0 to 5. Write the edges so no value fits two bands: 0 to under 1, then 1 to under 2, not 0 to 1 and 1 to 2. Tally every value into one band and check the tallies add to the total.

Too few bands and the picture is two blocks; too many and it is a row of lonely spikes. Bands of different widths lie, because a wide band looks busier than it is, so keep them equal. For marks out of 50, the bands 0 to 9, 10 to 19, 20 to 29, 30 to 39 and 40 to 50 work, with the last one stretched a mark to catch the 50. The questions for this section: why the bars touch, the 30-learner histogram, and how to make your own bands.

## Subtopic: Lines Through Time and Clouds of Dots

When your data is one thing measured month after month, draw a broken line. A household's electricity: January 320, February 300, March 310, April 350, May 420, June 480 kilowatt hours. Months along the bottom, evenly spaced; units up the side, starting at zero; a dot for each month; straight lines joining dot to dot. The line is flat through the first three months and then climbs into winter. The steepest piece, May to June, is a jump of 60, the fastest rise. Start the side scale at zero, or a rise from 320 to 480 looks like a rocket.

When your data is two numbers about each person, like hours studied and the mark earned, draw a cloud of dots, a scatter plot. One dot per learner: one hour and 35, two hours and 42, two hours and 50, three and 55, four and 60, five and 68, six and 75, seven and 80. Hours across, marks up, and do not join the dots; they are different people. The cloud slopes upward: more hours go with higher marks, a positive relationship. A cloud sloping down would be negative; a cloud with no slope means no relationship.

The cloud shows two things moving together, not one causing the other. Studying may lift marks, or confident learners may both study more and score more; the dots alone cannot tell. Say that in your conclusion. A dot off on its own, seven hours and 40, is an outlier worth a question.

Pick the graph from the data: words, towers or a pie; numbers in bands, bars that touch; one thing over time, a broken line; two numbers per person, a cloud of dots. Then dress it: title, labelled axes with units, even scale from zero, a key if two groups share the picture, and one sentence saying what it shows. Towers and slices, bars that touch, lines and clouds. The final questions of the lesson are with you now: the electricity line and its steepest piece, the study cloud and its slope, together but not because, and matching data to graph.
