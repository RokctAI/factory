# Part 1 — Expert

Data handling begins before any number is written down, with a question worth answering and a plan for collecting data that can answer it. Today we learn to pose questions on social, economic and environmental issues precisely enough that data can settle them, to distinguish a population from a sample and to choose a sample that represents the population fairly, and to select and justify sources and methods of collecting data, from questionnaires and observation to the published statistics of Statistics South Africa. These are the first stages of the data cycle: pose, collect, then organise, represent, interpret and report in the lessons that follow. A badly posed question or a biased sample cannot be rescued by any amount of later arithmetic, which is why this lesson comes first.

## Subtopic: Posing a Question That Data Can Answer

A statistical question is one whose answer varies from person to person or place to place and is settled by collecting data, not by looking up a single fact. How tall is the tallest learner in the school is not a statistical question; what is the distribution of heights of Grade 9 learners in the school is. Do people like load shedding is too vague; how many hours of electricity did households in our ward lose in the last week, and how did they cope, is a question data can answer.

Good questions name the variable, the group and the period. Social: what proportion of learners in our school walk more than two kilometres to school each day. Economic: what is the average weekly taxi fare paid by learners in Grade 9, and how has it changed since last year. Environmental: how many litres of water does each household in our street use per day, and how much of the school's waste is recycled each week. Each question identifies what is measured, who is measured, and when. A question that fails to name one of the three is not yet ready: how much do learners spend has no period and no group, and two learners answering it may be describing a week and a month, or a school and a province.

The variable determines the kind of data. Categorical data sorts into named groups: mode of transport, home language, whether a household recycles. Numerical data is measured or counted: fare in rands, height in centimetres, number of siblings. Numerical data is discrete when it is counted in whole units, number of siblings or taxis per day, and continuous when it is measured on a scale and can take any value, height, mass, time or water in litres. The kind of data decides the later representation: categorical data suits a bar graph or pie chart, numerical data a histogram or broken-line graph.

A question also fixes the unit and the precision in advance. Water in litres per household per day, fares in rands to the nearest rand, distance to the nearest 100 metres. Deciding this before collection prevents the common failure of a column with some answers in kilometres and some in minutes. The questions for this section are with you now: statistical against non-statistical questions, naming variable, group and period, and categorical, discrete and continuous data.

## Subtopic: Populations and Samples

The population is the whole group the question is about: every learner in the school, every household in the ward, every taxi on the route. A census collects data from every member of the population, as Statistics South Africa did in Census 2022 for the whole country. A sample is a part of the population chosen to stand in for it, and most investigations use a sample because a census is too slow, too expensive or impossible.

A sample is useful only when it is representative, meaning that it resembles the population in the ways that matter for the question. A school has 1200 learners and we survey 60 of them, which is 5 percent. If the 60 are all Grade 12 learners, or all members of the soccer team, or all the learners who happened to be near the tuck shop, the sample is biased: it over-represents one part of the population and the answer will lean that way. A convenience sample, whoever is easiest to reach, is the commonest source of bias. Volunteers are a second source: when only the learners who feel strongly reply, the sample is self-selected and the strong feelings are over-represented, which is why a notice saying answer if you want to produces a different result from a sample chosen by the investigator.

Random sampling gives every member of the population an equal chance of selection, by drawing names from a hat or using random numbers on the class register. Systematic sampling takes every nth name on a list: with 1200 learners and 60 wanted, every 20th learner on the alphabetical register. Stratified sampling divides the population into groups, strata, and samples each in proportion; with 300 learners in each of four grades, 15 from each grade gives 60 in total and guarantees every grade is heard. Each method is justified by naming the bias it avoids.

Sample size matters as well as method, and the two are separate questions: a large convenience sample is still biased, and a small random sample is still fair but imprecise, so a good plan says both how many and how chosen. Ten learners cannot represent 1200 reliably; sixty can give a reasonable picture; six hundred would be better but costly. A sample's result is used to estimate the population's: if 42 of 60 sampled learners say they walk to school, which is 70 percent, we estimate that about 840 of the 1200 do, with the understanding that the true number is near 840, not exactly 840. Your questions on this section are ready: population against sample, census, convenience bias, the three fair methods with the 1200 and 60, and the estimate of 840.

## Subtopic: Sources and Methods of Collecting Data

Data is primary when you collect it yourself and secondary when someone else collected it and you use their records. Primary methods include questionnaires, interviews, observation, measurement and experiment. Secondary sources include Statistics South Africa's census and labour force surveys, municipal records of water and electricity use, the South African Weather Service's rainfall records, school registers and company reports.

The method is chosen to fit the question and justified in writing. To find how learners travel to school, a short questionnaire to a stratified sample is quick and direct. To find how many cars pass the school gate between 7 and 8 in the morning, observation with a tally sheet is more reliable than asking people. To find each household's daily water use, measurement from meter readings beats memory. To find the youth unemployment rate in the province, no school project can survey the province; the Quarterly Labour Force Survey from Statistics South Africa is the appropriate secondary source, and citing it is the justification.

Questionnaires need design. Questions must be clear, neutral and answerable. Do you agree that the tuck shop prices are far too high is a leading question that pushes the answer; how much did you spend at the tuck shop this week, with ranges to tick, is neutral. Closed questions with set options are fast to count and compare; open questions give richer answers that are harder to summarise. Options must not overlap: 0 to 10 rands and 10 to 20 rands leaves a learner who spent exactly 10 rands with two boxes; 0 to 9 and 10 to 19 does not. A pilot run on five people finds the confusing questions before the real survey. Anonymity matters for sensitive questions, such as spending or household income; respondents answer more honestly when no name is attached, and the plan should say that the questionnaire is anonymous.

Secondary data must be checked for source, date and definition. Who collected it, when, and exactly what they counted: unemployment has an official definition and an expanded one, and the two rates differ by around ten percentage points, so a report must say which it used. A reliable source is identified and dated in the report. The questions for this section are ready: primary against secondary, matching a method to each question, the leading question, overlapping ranges, and checking a source.

## Subtopic: Planning an Investigation and the Error Museum

A plan sets out the question, the population, the sample and how it is drawn, the method of collection with the instrument, the variable with its unit and type, and the time and place of collection. Here is one. Question: how far do Grade 9 learners at our school travel to school each morning, and by what means. Population: the 300 Grade 9 learners. Sample: 45 learners, 15 from each of the three Grade 9 classes, chosen by every 20th name on each class list. Method: a questionnaire of four closed questions, piloted on five learners first. Variables: distance to the nearest 100 metres, continuous numerical; mode of transport, categorical. Collection: during register on Tuesday, with the questionnaire completed anonymously.

A plan is judged by whether its data could answer the question and whether the sample could mislead. Forty-five learners drawn across all three classes can speak for 300; forty-five drawn from one class cannot. A distance question answered in minutes cannot be averaged with one answered in kilometres. A question asked only of learners who arrive early will miss the learners who travel furthest and arrive late, a timing bias worth noting in the plan.

The error museum, five exhibits. Exhibit one: a vague question, do learners like school transport, with no variable, group or period. Exhibit two: a convenience sample, the first 20 learners through the gate, presented as representative of the school. Exhibit three: a leading question or overlapping response options in a questionnaire. Exhibit four: a sample percentage reported as if it were the exact population figure, 70 percent of the sample becoming exactly 840 learners with no acknowledgement of uncertainty. Exhibit five: secondary data used without source, date or definition, such as an unemployment rate with no indication of which rate.

Layout for marks: state the question with variable, group and period; name the population and the sample with its size and method; justify the method of collection against the alternatives; describe the instrument and its pilot; name the variables with units and types; and note any likely bias and how the plan reduces it. The questions for this section are with you now: the elements of a plan, the Grade 9 transport plan, and the five exhibits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through a question you can actually count, tasting the soup, and knowing where the numbers come from — plain words, same rules.

## Subtopic: Ask a Question You Can Actually Count

Data handling starts with a question, and the question has to be one you can count or measure. Is load shedding bad is an opinion. How many hours of power did the homes in our street lose last week is a question you can count, house by house, and add up.

A countable question says three things: what you are measuring, who you are measuring, and when. What: hours without power. Who: the homes in our street. When: last week. Change any one and you have a different question, so pin all three down before you start.

Decide what kind of answer you will get. Some answers are names of groups: walk, taxi, bus, car. That is categorical data, and you count how many in each group. Some answers are numbers: a fare of 14 rands, a height of 162 centimetres. That is numerical data. Numbers you count in whole steps, like number of brothers and sisters, are discrete. Numbers you measure on a scale, like height or litres of water, are continuous, because they can land anywhere, 162.5 or 162.48.

Fix the unit before you ask. Distance to school in kilometres to one decimal place, fares to the nearest rand, water in litres per day. Otherwise you end up with a page where half the answers say 2 kilometres and half say 20 minutes, and nothing can be added up. Your questions on this section are ready: opinion against countable, what, who and when, and categorical, discrete and continuous.

## Subtopic: Tasting the Soup: Samples and Populations

You do not drink the whole pot to find out if the soup needs salt; you taste a spoonful. The pot is the population, every single person or thing your question is about. The spoonful is the sample, the part you actually ask or measure. Statistics South Africa tasted the whole pot in Census 2022, every household in the country, which took years and a great deal of money. A school project tastes a spoonful.

A spoonful only works if you stir first. If all the salt has sunk to the bottom and you taste the top, you get the wrong answer. If you ask only the Grade 12s, or only the soccer team, or only the learners standing near you, your spoonful is unstirred, and the answer leans their way. That is a biased sample, and the laziest kind, whoever is nearest, is called a convenience sample. Letting people volunteer is nearly as bad, because only the ones who care most will bother, and the spoonful tastes of nothing but them.

Three ways to stir. Random: every learner has the same chance, names from a hat or random numbers on the register. Systematic: every 20th name on the list, so 1200 learners give 60. Stratified: split the school into its grades and take the same share from each, 15 from each of four grades of 300 for a total of 60, so no grade is left out. Say which one you used and why.

A bigger spoon helps too: 10 learners tell you little about 1200, 60 tell you a fair amount. Then scale up carefully. If 42 of your 60 walk to school, that is 70 percent, so about 840 of the 1200 probably walk. About, not exactly; a spoonful is an estimate. The questions for this section: the pot and the spoon, stirring, the three fair methods, and about 840.

## Subtopic: Where the Numbers Come From

You can collect numbers yourself or use numbers somebody else collected. Your own are primary data: a questionnaire, an interview, standing at the gate with a tally sheet, reading the water meter, running an experiment. Somebody else's are secondary data: Statistics South Africa, the municipality's water records, the Weather Service's rainfall, the school register.

Match the method to the question, and be ready to say why the other methods would have been worse. How learners get to school: a short questionnaire. How many cars pass the gate between 7 and 8: stand there and tally, because people's memories of traffic are useless. How much water a household uses: read the meter, do not ask. Youth unemployment in the province: you cannot survey a province, so use Statistics South Africa's labour force survey and say so.

If you write a questionnaire, keep it fair. Do you agree the tuck shop is a rip-off pushes people to say yes; how much did you spend at the tuck shop this week, tick a range, does not. Make the ranges not overlap: 0 to 9, 10 to 19, 20 to 29, so nobody fits two boxes. Try it on five people first and fix whatever confused them.

When you borrow numbers, check who collected them, when, and exactly what they counted. Unemployment has a strict definition and a broader one about ten percentage points apart, and your report has to say which one it is quoting. Write down the source and the date.

Then write the plan: the question with what, who and when; the pot and the spoonful with its size and how you stirred; the method and why; the instrument and its pilot; the units; and the bias you are watching for. Countable question, stirred spoonful, honest source. The final questions of the lesson are with you now: primary against secondary, matching methods, the fair questionnaire, and the plan.
