### subtopic_1 — Posing a Question That Data Can Answer
**Assistant:** How do I tell whether a question is a statistical question, and how do I fix one that is not?

**Tutor:** A statistical question is one whose answer varies across the members of a group and is settled by collecting data from them; a question with a single fixed answer, such as how tall the tallest learner is, or a matter of opinion with no variable attached, such as whether load shedding is bad, is not one. To fix a vague question, supply the three missing parts: the variable to be measured or counted, the group it is measured on, and the period or occasion. Do learners spend too much at the tuck shop becomes how much, in rands to the nearest rand, did each Grade 9 learner spend at the tuck shop during the past week; now every learner can give an answer, the answers can be compared and summarised, and the question of too much can be addressed with the numbers rather than asserted. The variable then tells you what kind of data to expect. Categorical data sorts into named groups, such as mode of transport or home language, and is counted group by group. Numerical data is counted or measured: discrete when it comes in whole steps, like siblings or taxis per day, and continuous when it comes from a scale and can take any value, like height, mass, time or litres of water. The type decides the later representation, bar graphs and pie charts for categorical data, histograms and line graphs for numerical data, and it decides what summary makes sense, since a mean of transport modes is meaningless. Fixing the unit and precision in the question itself, kilometres to one decimal place or rands to the nearest rand, prevents a mixed column later.

### subtopic_2 — Populations and Samples
**Assistant:** Why not just survey everyone, and if I must sample, how do I choose a sample I can defend?

**Tutor:** Surveying everyone is a census, and it is the gold standard when it is possible: Statistics South Africa did it for the whole country in Census 2022, at enormous cost over several years. For a school project a census of 1200 learners is slow and a census of a province is impossible, so a sample stands in for the population, and the sample is defensible when every part of the population had a fair chance to be included. Convenience sampling, taking whoever is nearest, fails this test: the first learners through the gate live nearby or are driven, so a transport survey of them under-reports long journeys; volunteers fail it too, since only those who feel strongly reply. Three methods pass. Random sampling gives every member an equal chance, by drawing names or using random numbers on the register. Systematic sampling takes every nth name on a list, and with 1200 learners and 60 wanted, n is 20. Stratified sampling divides the population into groups that might differ, here the four grades of 300, and samples each in proportion, 15 per grade, so that no grade is missing and each is represented by its fair share. The justification names the bias avoided: stratifying by grade guards against a sample dominated by one grade. Size matters alongside method; 60 of 1200 is 5 percent and gives a reasonable picture where 10 would not. When the result is scaled up, 42 of 60 being 70 percent gives an estimate of about 840 of 1200, and the word about is part of the answer, because a sample estimates and does not count.

### subtopic_3 — Sources and Methods of Collecting Data
**Assistant:** How do I decide between collecting data myself and using data someone else has published?

**Tutor:** Ask whether you can reach the population with your own resources and whether a reliable record already exists. Data you collect yourself is primary, through a questionnaire, an interview, observation with a tally sheet, measurement from a meter or ruler, or an experiment. Data someone else collected is secondary, such as Statistics South Africa's census and Quarterly Labour Force Survey, a municipality's water and electricity records, the Weather Service's rainfall figures or the school register. Match the method to the question and write the justification. How learners travel to school suits a short questionnaire to a stratified sample. How many cars pass the gate between seven and eight suits observation with a tally, because counting beats memory. Household water use suits meter readings, because measurement beats estimates. Youth unemployment in the province cannot be surveyed by a school, so the labour force survey is the right source, cited with its date. A questionnaire needs design: questions must be neutral rather than leading, so that do you agree the prices are far too high is replaced by how much did you spend this week; closed questions with set options are quick to count while open questions are richer but harder to summarise; response ranges must not overlap, so 0 to 9 and 10 to 19 rather than 0 to 10 and 10 to 20; sensitive questions are answered anonymously; and a pilot on five people catches confusing wording. Secondary data is checked for who collected it, when, and under what definition, since the official and expanded unemployment rates differ by around ten percentage points and a report must say which it quotes.

### subtopic_4 — Planning an Investigation and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** What must a written plan for an investigation contain, and what are the mistakes that sink one?

**Tutor:** A plan has six parts, and a marker looks for each. First the question, with its variable, group and period. Second the population, named and if possible counted. Third the sample, with its size and the method of selection, justified by the bias it avoids. Fourth the method of collection and the instrument, a questionnaire, tally sheet or meter, with a pilot where an instrument is designed. Fifth the variables with their units and types. Sixth the time and place of collection and the biases being watched. For the transport investigation: how far, to the nearest 100 metres, and by what means do the 300 Grade 9 learners travel to school; a sample of 45, 15 from each of three classes by every 20th name on each list; a four-item closed questionnaire piloted on five learners; distance as continuous numerical data and mode as categorical; completed anonymously during register on a Tuesday, noting that asking only early arrivals would miss long journeys. The error museum displays what sinks a plan: a vague question with no variable, group or period; a convenience sample passed off as representative; a leading question or overlapping options on the questionnaire; a sample percentage reported as an exact population count, 70 percent becoming precisely 840 learners; and secondary data quoted without source, date or definition. The layout for marks states each of the six parts in order, justifies the sample and the method against the alternatives, and names the likely bias and how the plan reduces it.
