### subtopic_1 — Reading the Axes and the Story
**Assistant:** How do I decide which quantity belongs on the horizontal axis?

**Tutor:** The horizontal axis carries the independent variable, which is the quantity that is chosen, counted or that simply passes, and the vertical axis carries the dependent variable, which is the quantity that responds to it. Time is almost always independent, because temperature depends on the time of day rather than the other way around, so time runs along the bottom and temperature up the side. The number of kilometres a taxi travels is independent and the fare depends on it. The number of items bought is independent and the total cost depends on it. A useful test is to complete the sentence "this depends on that": the thing after "on" goes on the horizontal axis. This matches the table work from last week, where the input x was listed first and the output y second; the graph puts x across and y up, so the ordered pair 14 semicolon 28 is plotted above 14:00 and level with 28 degrees. Once the axes are identified, read the units and the scale before anything else, because a block that represents 5 degrees rather than 1 degree changes every reading. A description earns marks only when it quotes readings from both axes with their units, so the sentence "the temperature reached 28 degrees Celsius at 14:00" is an answer while "it got hot in the afternoon" is not.

### subtopic_2 — Linear or Non-Linear, Increasing, Decreasing or Constant
**Assistant:** What is the difference between a graph being constant and a graph being linear?

**Tutor:** Constant describes the direction of a section: the dependent variable does not change as the independent variable moves on, so the graph is horizontal. Linear describes the shape: the graph is a straight line, meaning the dependent variable changes by the same amount for each unit step of the independent variable. A horizontal line is therefore both constant and linear, with a change of zero per step, but most linear graphs are not constant, because they rise or fall at a steady rate. The taxi fare of R20 plus R12 per kilometre is linear and increasing: each kilometre adds exactly R12, which is the constant difference you saw in last week's tables. The distance-from-home graph of the walk to school has three linear sections: increasing from 0 to 10 minutes, constant from 10 to 15 minutes while the learner stands in the shop, and increasing again from 15 to 25 minutes. A constant distance means the learner is stationary, not moving backwards; a return home would appear as a decreasing section heading towards zero. Non-linear graphs curve because the step keeps changing: the ball's height rises fast, slows and falls, and a cooling cup of tea drops steeply at first and then flattens. In a description, name the shape word, the direction word and the interval for each section, and compare steepness where two linear graphs appear, because the steeper line changes faster.

### subtopic_3 — Maximum, Minimum and Turning Points
**Assistant:** Why does an answer about a maximum need both a value and a time?

**Tutor:** Because the maximum is a point on the graph, and a point is an ordered pair with two coordinates: the value of the dependent variable, which is how high the graph reaches, and the value of the independent variable, which is when or where it reaches it. On the Bloemfontein temperature graph the maximum is 28 degrees Celsius and it occurs at 14:00; the minimum is 8 degrees Celsius at 05:00. If the question asks what the maximum temperature was, the answer is 28 degrees; if it asks when the temperature was highest, the answer is 14:00; if it asks for the maximum without qualification, both are given. A turning point is where the direction changes, from decreasing to increasing at a minimum or from increasing to decreasing at a maximum, and the ball's height graph has one turning point at its maximum height of 20 metres after 2 seconds. Two cautions matter for marks. First, not every extreme is at a turning point: a graph that rises throughout the interval drawn has its maximum at the right-hand end, as the taxi fare does at R140 after 10 kilometres. Second, the steepest part of a graph is not its highest part. The temperature rises fastest around 09:00, where the curve is steepest, but it is highest at 14:00, where the curve is momentarily flat at the top. Fastest change and largest value are different readings.

### subtopic_4 — Discrete or Continuous and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** How do I decide whether to join the points on a graph?

**Tutor:** Ask whether the quantity on the horizontal axis can take values between the plotted points. If it can, the quantity is continuous and the points are joined, because there is a genuine value of the dependent variable at every intermediate point: time, distance, temperature and height are continuous, so a graph of fare against kilometres is a line and a graph of a ball's height against time is a curve. If it cannot, the quantity is discrete and the points are left as separate dots: the number of passengers in a taxi, the number of SMSs sent or the number of tickets bought can only be whole numbers, so a graph of fare collected against passengers at R15 each is the dots 1 semicolon 15, 2 semicolon 30, 3 semicolon 45 and 4 semicolon 60. Joining them would assert that two and a half passengers paid R37,50, which describes nothing real, and it is the first exhibit in the error museum. The other exhibits are errors of reading rather than drawing: treating a horizontal section of a distance-time graph as travelling backwards, giving a value when a time was asked, calling the steepest section the maximum, and describing a graph with no readings at all. Each is avoided by the same discipline: decide discrete or continuous first, then for every section write a direction word, an interval, a value and a unit, and for every extreme write both the value and when it occurs.
