# Part 1 — Expert

Today we measure a straight line. Every linear graph has three features that an examiner can ask for by name: the y-intercept, where it crosses the vertical axis; the x-intercept, where it crosses the horizontal axis; and the gradient, which measures how steep it is and in which direction. Each feature is a number with a method behind it, each can be read from a graph or calculated from an equation, and all three live inside the standard form y equals mx plus c, which is how Grade 10 writes every straight line. The anchor line is y equals 2x plus 1, and the context is the taxi fare of R20 plus R12 per kilometre.

## Subtopic: The y-Intercept

The y-intercept is the point where the line crosses the y-axis. Every point on the y-axis has x equal to 0, so the y-intercept is found by substituting x equals 0 into the equation. For y equals 2x plus 1, x equals 0 gives y equals 1, so the y-intercept is the point 0 semicolon 1. Some questions want the point; others want only the value, 1. Give what is asked, and if in doubt give the point.

In the equation y equals mx plus c, the y-intercept is c, because the mx term vanishes at x equals 0. y equals 3x minus 6 has y-intercept negative 6. y equals negative 2x plus 4 has y-intercept 4. y equals one half x plus 3 has y-intercept 3. Reading c straight off the equation is legitimate once the equation is in the form y equals something, with y alone on the left; if a question gives 2y equals 4x plus 6, divide through by 2 first to get y equals 2x plus 3, and the y-intercept is 3, not 6.

In context the y-intercept is the starting value, what the dependent variable is when the independent variable is zero. The taxi fare 20 plus 12k has y-intercept 20: the flag fee charged before a single kilometre is driven. A tank draining from 500 litres has y-intercept 500. A phone contract with a R99 monthly fee plus charges has y-intercept 99. Interpreting the y-intercept in a sentence about the context is a standard mark.

A line through the origin has y-intercept 0, and its equation has no constant term: y equals 3x crosses the vertical axis at 0 semicolon 0. Direct proportion from Term 1, such as cost equals 15 times the number of items, always gives a line through the origin, because zero items cost nothing. A line that does not pass through the origin is not a direct proportion, however straight it is, and the taxi fare with its R20 flag fee is the standard example.

On a drawn graph, read the y-intercept where the line meets the vertical axis, using the vertical scale carefully. A line that crosses one and a half blocks above the origin on a scale of 2 per block has y-intercept 3. The questions for this section are with you now: substituting x equals 0, reading c from the standard form, and the y-intercept as the starting value.

## Subtopic: The x-Intercept

The x-intercept is where the line crosses the x-axis, and every point on the x-axis has y equal to 0. So substitute y equals 0 and solve the resulting equation for x with the inverses from two lessons ago. For y equals 2x plus 1: 0 equals 2x plus 1, so 2x equals negative 1 and x equals negative one half. The x-intercept is the point negative one half semicolon 0. A fractional x-intercept is normal and should not be rounded.

For y equals 3x minus 6: 0 equals 3x minus 6, so 3x equals 6 and x equals 2. The point 2 semicolon 0. For y equals negative 2x plus 4: 0 equals negative 2x plus 4, so 2x equals 4 and x equals 2. Two different lines can share an x-intercept, as these do, while having different y-intercepts and gradients.

The two intercepts are often the quickest two points for drawing a line, which is next lesson's business, and they are the two places where the graph answers a yes-or-no question about the context. On the tank-draining graph, the x-intercept is the time when the tank is empty, the moment the volume is zero. On a profit graph, the x-intercept is the break-even point, where profit is exactly zero.

A horizontal line such as y equals 4 has no x-intercept at all, because y is never zero; it runs parallel to the floor. A line through the origin has both intercepts at the same point, 0 semicolon 0. These special cases appear in tests precisely because learners expect every line to have two separate intercepts.

Keep the two intercepts straight by their zero. The y-intercept has x equal to 0 and sits on the vertical axis. The x-intercept has y equal to 0 and sits on the horizontal axis. Learners regularly substitute x equals 0 when hunting for the x-intercept, because the name suggests x; remember instead that the x-intercept is where the OTHER variable, y, is zero. Writing each intercept as a full ordered pair, with the zero visible, prevents the swap. Your questions on this section: substituting y equals 0 and solving, a fractional intercept, and keeping the two intercepts apart.

## Subtopic: The Gradient

The gradient measures steepness as a ratio: the vertical change divided by the horizontal change between any two points on the line, often said as rise over run. Take y equals 2x plus 1 and the points 0 semicolon 1 and 2 semicolon 5. The rise is 5 minus 1, which is 4. The run is 2 minus 0, which is 2. The gradient is 4 over 2, which is 2. Choose two other points, 1 semicolon 3 and 3 semicolon 7: rise 4, run 2, gradient 2 again. The gradient of a straight line is the same between any two of its points, which is what makes it a property of the line.

The formula: gradient m equals y two minus y one, over x two minus x one, for any two points x one semicolon y one and x two semicolon y two. The subtraction must go the same way on top and bottom. From 3 semicolon 3 to 0 semicolon negative 3: 3 minus negative 3 is 6 on top, 3 minus 0 is 3 below, gradient 2. Reversing both subtractions gives negative 6 over negative 3, still 2. Reversing only one gives negative 2, which is wrong.

In y equals mx plus c the gradient is m, the coefficient of x. y equals 2x plus 1 has gradient 2: for each step of 1 to the right, the line rises 2. y equals negative 2x plus 4 has gradient negative 2: for each step right, the line falls 2. A positive gradient means increasing, a negative gradient means decreasing, and a gradient of 0 means horizontal, a constant graph. y equals one half x plus 3 has gradient one half: a gentle line rising 1 for every 2 across.

In context the gradient is the rate. The taxi fare 20 plus 12k has gradient 12, which is R12 per kilometre, the rate the fare grows. A tank draining at 25 litres per minute has gradient negative 25. The unit of a gradient is the vertical unit per horizontal unit, and quoting it, rand per kilometre or litres per minute, is part of a full answer. Lines with the same gradient are parallel: y equals 2x plus 1 and y equals 2x minus 3 never meet. The questions for this section are ready: rise over run between two points, the sign of the gradient, and the gradient as a rate with units.

## Subtopic: Reading Features From a Graph and the Error Museum

A line is drawn through 0 semicolon negative 3 and 3 semicolon 3. Read all three features. The y-intercept is where it crosses the vertical axis: negative 3, at the point 0 semicolon negative 3. The gradient from the two points: rise 3 minus negative 3, which is 6; run 3 minus 0, which is 3; gradient 2. The equation is therefore y equals 2x minus 3, and the x-intercept comes from y equals 0: 2x equals 3, x equals 1,5. Reading it off the graph confirms the line crosses halfway between 1 and 2.

The same three features can be read from a table of ordered pairs without a picture. For the pairs 0 semicolon negative 3, 1 semicolon negative 1, 2 semicolon 1 and 3 semicolon 3, the constant difference of 2 is the gradient, the pair with x equal to 0 gives the y-intercept negative 3, and the x-intercept is where y would be 0, between x equals 1 and x equals 2, at 1,5. Table, equation and graph are three views of one line.

Reading a gradient from a graph means picking two points where the line passes exactly through grid intersections, so the rise and run are whole numbers. Picking a point between grid lines and estimating introduces error. If the line passes through 1 semicolon negative 1 and 2 semicolon 1 as well, the rise is 2 and the run is 1, and the gradient is again 2.

The error museum, five exhibits. Exhibit one: run over rise, giving one half instead of 2. Exhibit two: a positive gradient reported for a line that falls from left to right; a falling line always has a negative gradient, and the sign must be checked against the picture. Exhibit three: the intercepts swapped, reporting the x-intercept as negative 3 because the number was read at the wrong axis. Exhibit four: reading c as the y-intercept from 2y equals 4x plus 6 without dividing through, giving 6 instead of 3. Exhibit five: computing a gradient from one point, dividing its y by its x, which gives the gradient only for lines through the origin and is otherwise wrong.

Layout for marks: for the y-intercept write x equals 0 and the value; for the x-intercept write y equals 0, the equation, and the solution; for the gradient write the two points, the rise, the run and the ratio, and then the sign check against the direction of the line. A final line y equals mx plus c with the two numbers in place summarises the whole analysis. The questions for this section are with you now: all three features from two points, choosing grid-intersection points, and the five exhibits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same three features again through a taxi meter, a staircase, and the floor and the wall of a room — plain words, same rules.

## Subtopic: Where the Line Crosses the Wall

Picture the vertical axis as a wall on the left and the horizontal axis as the floor. A straight line crosses the wall once and the floor once, and how steep it is between them is the third thing. Start with the wall. The point where the line touches the wall is the y-intercept, and at that point you have not moved along the floor at all, so x is 0.

A taxi charges R20 just to get in, then R12 a kilometre. Before you have gone anywhere, zero kilometres, the meter already says 20. That is the y-intercept: the starting fee. On the graph, the line hits the wall at 20. In the equation fare equals 12k plus 20, the 20 is the number without a letter next to it, the one that stays when k is zero.

For y equals 2x plus 1, put x equal to 0 and you get 1. The line crosses the wall at 1, the point 0 semicolon 1. For y equals 3x minus 6, the wall crossing is negative 6, below the floor. The rule: the number on its own at the end of y equals something is the y-intercept, as long as the equation starts with y on its own. If it says 2y, halve everything first.

On a drawn graph, follow the line to the wall and read the number there, checking what one block is worth. Your questions on this section are ready: x is zero at the wall, the starting fee, and the number on its own in the equation.

## Subtopic: Steps Up for Each Step Across

The gradient is how steep the line is, and the way to measure it is a staircase. Pick two points where the line goes exactly through a grid corner. From the first to the second, count how many you go up, then how many you go across. Gradient is up divided by across. For y equals 2x plus 1, from 0 semicolon 1 to 2 semicolon 5: up 4, across 2, gradient 4 over 2, which is 2. Every step across, the line climbs 2. Pick different corners, 1 semicolon 3 and 3 semicolon 7: up 4, across 2, still 2. A straight line has the same steepness everywhere, which is why one number describes it.

Up first, then across. Across over up gives a half, and that is the wrong way round. Say it as a rhythm: rise over run, up over across.

Downhill lines have negative gradients. y equals negative 2x plus 4 goes down 2 for every step across, gradient negative 2. If the line falls as you read left to right, the gradient is negative, full stop. A flat line has gradient 0: no climb at all.

The gradient is also the rate in the story. The taxi line climbs 12 for every kilometre across: gradient 12, which is R12 per kilometre. A tank draining 25 litres every minute has gradient negative 25. Give the unit when the story has one. And two lines with the same gradient are parallel, like two taxis with the same per-kilometre rate but different starting fees: the lines never cross. The questions for this section: up over across, downhill means negative, and gradient as the rate.

## Subtopic: Where the Line Hits the Floor

The floor is the horizontal axis, and when the line hits the floor, the height is zero: y is 0. So the x-intercept is found by putting y equal to 0 and working out x. For y equals 3x minus 6: 0 equals 3x minus 6, undo the minus 6 by adding 6, 3x is 6, undo the times 3, x is 2. The line hits the floor at 2, the point 2 semicolon 0.

For y equals 2x plus 1: 0 equals 2x plus 1, take 1 off both sides, 2x is negative 1, so x is negative a half. The floor crossing is to the left of the wall, at negative 0,5. Fractions are fine; do not round to zero or to negative 1.

A table shows it too. Pairs 0 semicolon negative 3, 1 semicolon negative 1, 2 semicolon 1: the outputs cross from negative to positive between x equals 1 and x equals 2, so the floor crossing is in there, and the equation pins it to 1,5.

Here is the trap: the x-intercept is where y is zero, not where x is zero. The name says x because that is the axis it sits on. Think floor: on the floor, the height is zero. Write every intercept as a full pair with the zero showing, 2 semicolon 0 for the floor and 0 semicolon negative 6 for the wall, and you will not swap them.

In the story, the floor crossing answers a question. A tank draining has its x-intercept when the volume is zero: the tank is empty. A business's profit line hits the floor at break-even.

Now all three together for a line through 0 semicolon negative 3 and 3 semicolon 3. Wall: negative 3. Staircase: up 6, across 3, gradient 2. Equation: y equals 2x minus 3. Floor: 0 equals 2x minus 3, x is 1,5. Wall, staircase, floor: y-intercept, gradient, x-intercept. The final questions of the lesson are with you now: y is zero on the floor, the fraction that is not rounded, and all three features from two points.
