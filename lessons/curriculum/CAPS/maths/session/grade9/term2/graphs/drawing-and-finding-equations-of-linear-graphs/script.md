# Part 1 — Expert

Today the straight line goes both ways. Given an equation such as y equals 2x minus 1, we draw its graph by three methods: a table of values, the two intercepts, and the gradient-intercept method that reads m and c straight from y equals mx plus c. Given a drawn line, we recover its equation by reading the y-intercept and calculating the gradient. This is the last lesson of the graphs topic and the one most directly examined, because a question can give you either the picture or the formula and expect the other. The anchor equation is y equals 2x minus 1; the anchor picture is the line through 0 semicolon negative 3 and 3 semicolon 3.

## Subtopic: Drawing a Line From a Table of Values

A straight line is fixed by two points, but we plot three, because the third is a check: if it does not lie on the line through the other two, there is an arithmetic error. For y equals 2x minus 1, choose x equals negative 1, 0 and 1. Substitute with brackets: 2 times the bracket negative 1, minus 1, is negative 3; at 0, negative 1; at 1, 1. The table reads negative 1 semicolon negative 3, 0 semicolon negative 1, 1 semicolon 1. Plot the three points, confirm they line up, lay a ruler through them and draw the line, extending past the points with arrowheads at both ends, since the equation holds for every x.

Choosing x values close to zero keeps the arithmetic small and the points on a small grid. If the coefficient is a fraction, choose x values that clear it: for y equals one half x plus 3, use x equals negative 2, 0 and 2, giving 2, 3 and 4, all whole numbers.

Drawing two lines on the same axes is a standard question, and the table method handles it cleanly: one table per line, three points each, two ruled lines, each labelled. Where the lines cross is the point whose coordinates satisfy both equations at once, an idea Grade 10 develops into solving simultaneous equations graphically. For now, reading the crossing point accurately is the skill.

Label the line with its equation written beside it, because a test often asks for two or three lines on the same axes and an unlabelled line cannot be credited. Label the axes, choose a uniform scale that fits all the y values, and mark the scale on both axes.

The table method always works, for any linear equation, and is the fallback when the other methods feel unsafe. Its cost is three substitutions; its benefit is the built-in check. The constant difference in the table, 2 for each step of 1 in x here, is the gradient, which connects this method to the next two. The questions for this section are with you now: three points with one as a check, choosing x values wisely, and labelling the line with its equation.

## Subtopic: Drawing Using Intercepts and the Gradient

The dual-intercept method plots the two points where the line crosses the axes. For y equals negative 2x plus 4: the y-intercept, at x equals 0, is 4, the point 0 semicolon 4; the x-intercept, at y equals 0, solves 0 equals negative 2x plus 4, giving x equals 2, the point 2 semicolon 0. Plot both, draw the line through them. Two substitutions, two points, and the line falls from left to right as its negative gradient says it must. Check with a third point if time allows: x equals 1 gives y equals 2, and 1 semicolon 2 lies on the line.

The dual-intercept method fails for a line through the origin, because both intercepts are the same point 0 semicolon 0. For y equals 3x, plot the origin and one more point, such as 1 semicolon 3.

The gradient-intercept method uses the form y equals mx plus c directly. Start at the y-intercept c on the vertical axis. Then use the gradient m as a staircase: for each 1 across to the right, go m up, or m down if m is negative. For y equals 2x minus 1, start at 0 semicolon negative 1, go 1 across and 2 up to 1 semicolon 1, again to 2 semicolon 3. For y equals negative 2x plus 4, start at 0 semicolon 4, go 1 across and 2 down to 1 semicolon 2, again to 2 semicolon 0. For a fractional gradient like one half, go 2 across and 1 up, reading the fraction as rise over run.

Two special lines. y equals 3 is horizontal through 0 semicolon 3, gradient 0, every point having y equal to 3. x equals negative 2 is vertical through negative 2 semicolon 0, every point having x equal to negative 2; it is not of the form y equals mx plus c and has no gradient in the Grade 9 sense. Both appear in tests because learners try to plot them as sloping lines. Your questions on this section: the two intercepts as two points, the staircase from c, and horizontal and vertical lines.

## Subtopic: Finding the Equation From a Drawn Line

Reverse the process. A line is drawn through 0 semicolon negative 3 and 3 semicolon 3. The equation has the form y equals mx plus c, and two numbers are needed. Read c where the line crosses the y-axis: negative 3. Calculate m from two clear points: rise 3 minus negative 3, which is 6; run 3 minus 0, which is 3; m equals 2. Equation: y equals 2x minus 3. Verify with a point not used: at x equals 1 the line passes through 1 semicolon negative 1, and 2 times 1 minus 3 is negative 1.

A line through negative 2 semicolon 0 and 0 semicolon 4: c is 4, read at the y-axis. Gradient: rise 4 minus 0 is 4, run 0 minus negative 2 is 2, m equals 2. Equation: y equals 2x plus 4. Both lines have gradient 2, so they are parallel, and the picture shows two lines that never meet.

A falling line through 0 semicolon 5 and 5 semicolon 0: c is 5; rise 0 minus 5 is negative 5, run 5 minus 0 is 5, m equals negative 1. Equation: y equals negative x plus 5. The negative sign is read off the picture as well as the calculation: the line falls, so m must be negative.

The verification point is not optional in an examination answer. Reading c and calculating m both involve the picture, and a picture can be misread by one grid block; substituting a third point into the finished equation is the only step that proves the equation matches the line, and markers look for it.

Horizontal and vertical lines have the simplest equations. A horizontal line through 0 semicolon 3 is y equals 3. A vertical line through negative 2 semicolon 0 is x equals negative 2. Learners who try to force these into y equals mx plus c either get m equals 0, which is correct for the horizontal line and gives y equals 3, or divide by zero for the vertical line, which is a sign to write x equals a number instead.

Layout for marks: state c with the point it was read from, state the two points used for m, show rise over run with the subtraction, write the equation, and verify with a third point. The questions for this section are ready: c from the axis, m from two points, and the sign check against the picture.

## Subtopic: Lines Through Two Points and the Error Museum

Sometimes the y-intercept is not visible on the given graph, or two points are given with neither on the y-axis. The gradient is still rise over run, and c is found by substituting one of the points into y equals mx plus c. A line passes through 1 semicolon 5 and 3 semicolon 9. Gradient: rise 9 minus 5 is 4, run 3 minus 1 is 2, m equals 2. Substitute 1 semicolon 5 into y equals 2x plus c: 5 equals 2 plus c, so c equals 3. Equation: y equals 2x plus 3. Check with the other point: 2 times 3 plus 3 is 9.

A line through 2 semicolon 1 and 4 semicolon negative 3: rise negative 3 minus 1 is negative 4, run 4 minus 2 is 2, m equals negative 2. Substitute 2 semicolon 1: 1 equals negative 4 plus c, so c equals 5. Equation: y equals negative 2x plus 5. Check: at x equals 4, negative 8 plus 5 is negative 3.

The error museum, six exhibits. Exhibit one: plotting only two points from a table and drawing through an arithmetic error that a third point would have caught. Exhibit two: the staircase stepped in the wrong direction for a negative gradient, drawing y equals negative 2x plus 4 rising. Exhibit three: reading c from a graph whose y-axis is not at x equals 0, for instance when the axes have been drawn with the vertical axis at the left edge of a grid that starts at x equals 1. Exhibit four: m calculated as run over rise. Exhibit five: y equals 3 drawn as a line through the origin with gradient 3. Exhibit six: an unlabelled line on axes with two or three lines, so the marker cannot tell which is which.

Every exhibit is caught by a verification point: substitute a third point from the picture into the equation, or a third x value into the table, and the mismatch appears. Layout for marks: for drawing, the table or the two points written down, the plotted points, the ruled line with arrowheads and its label; for finding the equation, c and its point, m with its two points and the subtraction, the equation, and a verification. The questions for this section are with you now: c from a point when the intercept is not visible, the two-point method end to end, and the six exhibits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lines again through three dots and a ruler, a staircase that starts on the wall, and reading a line backwards into its formula — plain words, same rules.

## Subtopic: Three Dots and a Ruler

The safest way to draw any straight line from its formula is three dots and a ruler. Pick three easy x values, usually negative 1, 0 and 1. Feed each into the machine. For y equals 2x minus 1: negative 1 goes in and negative 3 comes out; 0 goes in and negative 1 comes out; 1 goes in and 1 comes out. Three dots: negative 1 semicolon negative 3, 0 semicolon negative 1, 1 semicolon 1. Hall then lift for each one. Lay a ruler along them and draw, going past the end dots with little arrows, because the formula works for every x, not just the three you picked.

Why three and not two? Two dots always line up, so two dots cannot tell you if one of them is wrong. Three dots either line up, which means all three are right, or one sits off, which means go back and check that one. The third dot is free insurance.

If the formula has a fraction, pick x values that kill it. For y equals a half x plus 3, use negative 2, 0 and 2: half of them is a whole number, and the dots are 2, 3 and 4.

Write the formula next to the line when you are done. In a test there might be three lines on one grid, and a line without its name gets no marks even if it is perfect. Label the axes, choose an even scale that fits your biggest and smallest y, and you are finished. Your questions on this section are ready: three easy x values, the third dot as insurance, and the formula written beside the line.

## Subtopic: Start on the Wall, Step the Staircase

A faster way, once you trust y equals mx plus c. The c is where the line touches the wall, the y-axis. The m is the staircase: how many up for each one across. So start on the wall at c, then step the staircase to make more dots.

y equals 2x minus 1: the wall number is negative 1, so start at 0 semicolon negative 1. The staircase is 2: one across, two up, to 1 semicolon 1. Again: 2 semicolon 3. Three dots, ruler, done. y equals negative 2x plus 4: start on the wall at 4. Staircase negative 2: one across, two DOWN, to 1 semicolon 2. Again: 2 semicolon 0. The line falls, because the staircase is negative. If your line rises when m is negative, you stepped the wrong way.

A fraction staircase is read as up over across. m equals a half means one up for two across. Start at 3 for y equals a half x plus 3, go two across and one up to 2 semicolon 4.

Two odd lines. y equals 3 has no x at all: every point has height 3, so it is a flat line through 0 semicolon 3. x equals negative 2 has no y: every point is 2 to the left, so it is a straight-up line through negative 2 semicolon 0. Do not try to give either of them a staircase. Flat line for y equals a number; upright line for x equals a number.

Another two-dot shortcut: find where the line crosses the wall and where it crosses the floor, plot both, join. For y equals negative 2x plus 4, the wall is 4 and the floor is where 0 equals negative 2x plus 4, so x is 2. Two dots: 0 semicolon 4 and 2 semicolon 0. The questions for this section: wall then staircase, negative means step down, and the flat and upright lines.

## Subtopic: Read the Line Backwards

Now someone gives you the picture and wants the formula. A formula y equals mx plus c needs two numbers, and the picture gives you both. Wall first: where does the line touch the y-axis? That is c. Then the staircase: pick two grid corners the line goes exactly through, count up, count across, divide. That is m.

A line through 0 semicolon negative 3 and 3 semicolon 3. Wall: negative 3. Staircase from the first dot to the second: up 6, across 3, m is 2. Formula: y equals 2x minus 3. Check with a third corner the line passes through, 1 semicolon negative 1: 2 times 1 minus 3 is negative 1. It fits.

A line through negative 2 semicolon 0 and 0 semicolon 4. Wall: 4. Staircase: up 4, across 2, m is 2. Formula: y equals 2x plus 4. Same staircase as the last line, so the two lines are parallel; the picture shows them never meeting. A line falling from 0 semicolon 5 to 5 semicolon 0: wall 5, staircase down 5 for across 5, m is negative 1. Formula: y equals negative x plus 5. Falling line, negative m, every time.

Two lines on one grid: do each one separately, wall then staircase, and label each formula next to its own line. Where the lines cross is the one point that fits both formulas, and reading it off carefully is a mark on its own.

If the wall crossing is not on the picture, use a dot instead. A line through 1 semicolon 5 and 3 semicolon 9: staircase up 4, across 2, m is 2. Now put one dot into y equals 2x plus c: 5 equals 2 plus c, so c is 3. Formula: y equals 2x plus 3. Check with the other dot: 2 times 3 plus 3 is 9.

A flat line through 3 on the wall is just y equals 3. An upright line through negative 2 on the floor is just x equals negative 2. Three dots and a ruler, wall then staircase, read the line backwards, and always check with one more dot. The final questions of the lesson are with you now: c from the wall or from a dot, m from two corners, and the one-more-dot check.
