# Part 1 — Expert

Today we draw. Reading graphs was last week; this lesson revises the Cartesian plane itself, plotting ordered pairs accurately in all four quadrants, and sketching a global graph from a story so that its shape says what the story says. Accuracy marks in Grade 9 are lost on small things: a swapped coordinate, an uneven scale, an unlabelled axis, dots joined that should have stayed apart. We fix those habits now, because next lesson's straight lines and every graph in Grade 10 are drawn on exactly this plane with exactly these rules.

## Subtopic: The Cartesian Plane and Its Quadrants

The Cartesian plane is two number lines at right angles. The horizontal one is the x-axis, positive to the right and negative to the left. The vertical one is the y-axis, positive upwards and negative downwards. They cross at the origin, the point 0 semicolon 0. Both axes are labelled with their letter at the positive end, and every few units carry a number so the scale can be read.

The axes divide the plane into four quadrants, numbered anticlockwise from the top right. Quadrant one, top right, has x positive and y positive. Quadrant two, top left, has x negative and y positive. Quadrant three, bottom left, has both negative. Quadrant four, bottom right, has x positive and y negative. Knowing the sign pattern lets you check a plotted point at a glance: negative 3 semicolon 2 must sit in quadrant two, top left, and if it does not, a coordinate has been misread.

The two coordinates are read against the two axes in turn, and the quadrant is a check rather than a method: a point in quadrant four has gone right and down, so its x must be positive and its y negative, and a learner who reads 4 semicolon negative 2 and finds the pencil in quadrant two has swapped a sign. The origin itself is the only point with both coordinates zero.

Points on the axes belong to no quadrant. 4 semicolon 0 is on the x-axis; 0 semicolon negative 5 is on the y-axis. Those are the intercepts from last lesson, and their zero tells you which axis they sit on.

The scale is chosen before anything is drawn. Look at the largest and smallest values to be plotted, in both x and y, and choose a number of units per block that fits them on the page with room to spare. The scale on the x-axis need not match the scale on the y-axis, but each axis must be uniform: every block on one axis represents the same number of units. A scale that goes 0, 1, 2, 5, 10 is not a scale; it is a list, and every point plotted on it is in the wrong place. The questions for this section are with you now: the signs in each quadrant, points on the axes, and a uniform scale.

## Subtopic: Plotting Ordered Pairs

An ordered pair x semicolon y is plotted by moving x units horizontally from the origin, right for positive and left for negative, then y units vertically, up for positive and down for negative. 3 semicolon 2: three right, two up, quadrant one. Negative 2 semicolon 4: two left, four up, quadrant two. Negative 3 semicolon negative 1: three left, one down, quadrant three. 4 semicolon negative 2: four right, two down, quadrant four. The horizontal move always comes first because x is written first.

Mark each point with a small, precise cross or dot and label it with its coordinates, or with a capital letter if the question names it. A blob the size of a grid block earns no accuracy mark because the marker cannot tell which intersection it means. Use a sharp pencil.

Plotting a table of ordered pairs from last week: y equals 2x plus 1 gave negative 2 semicolon negative 3, negative 1 semicolon negative 1, 0 semicolon 1, 1 semicolon 3 and 2 semicolon 5. Plot all five. They fall in quadrants three, three, on the y-axis, one and one, and they line up along a straight line, which is the constant difference of 2 made visible. If one point sits off the line, it has been misplotted or miscalculated; check its coordinates before drawing anything through it.

Mirror images are a useful check. The point 3 semicolon 2 reflected in the y-axis is negative 3 semicolon 2, directly opposite across the vertical axis; reflected in the x-axis it is 3 semicolon negative 2, directly below. Plotting a point and its mirror images shows at once whether the signs have been handled correctly, and Term 4 will use exactly this idea for reflections in transformation geometry.

Negative coordinates are where most plotting errors happen. Negative 2 semicolon 4 and 2 semicolon negative 4 are different points in different quadrants; so are negative 2 semicolon 4 and 4 semicolon negative 2. Read the sign with the number, move in the sign's direction, and check the quadrant against the sign pattern before marking. Your questions on this section: across then up, a precise mark with a label, and checking the quadrant from the signs.

## Subtopic: Drawing a Global Graph From a Story

A global graph shows the shape of a relationship without necessarily having numbers on every point. The axes are labelled with the two quantities and their units, the independent one horizontal, and the shape of the line is chosen to match the story section by section, using last week's words in reverse: increasing is a line going up to the right, decreasing goes down, constant is horizontal, linear is straight and non-linear is curved.

A bath is filled at a steady rate for 10 minutes, someone sits in it for 20 minutes, and then it drains in 5 minutes. Horizontal axis: time in minutes, 0 to 35. Vertical axis: volume of water in litres. The graph rises in a straight line from the origin for 10 minutes, runs horizontal for the next 20, and falls in a straight line to zero over the last 5. The draining section is steeper than the filling section, because the same volume leaves in half the time. Every feature of the story is a feature of the picture.

A ball thrown upwards: height against time rises, slows, turns at the maximum and falls, in a smooth symmetrical curve, never a pointed peak, because the ball does not change direction instantly. A cup of tea cooling: a curve that falls steeply at first and flattens towards room temperature, never crossing it. Bacteria doubling: a curve that starts low and grows steeper, the geometric pattern drawn.

A journey can be sketched the same way. A taxi drives from Mamelodi to Pretoria station at a steady speed, waits at the rank, then returns. Distance from Mamelodi against time: a straight line up, a horizontal section at the rank, and a straight line down to zero. If the return trip is faster, its line is steeper.

Sketching is not careless drawing. Label both axes with quantity and unit, mark the key values from the story on the axes, 10, 30 and 35 minutes for the bath, and make the direction, steepness and curvature of each section match. Marks are given for each section being the right shape and for the key points being in the right place relative to each other. The questions for this section are ready: a section for each part of the story, labelled axes with key values, and smooth curves where the story is smooth.

## Subtopic: Drawing From a Table, Joining and the Error Museum

When the graph is to be drawn from a table of values, plot every pair first and only then decide how to join them. If the independent variable is continuous and the pattern is linear, lay a ruler along the points and draw a straight line through them, extending it a little past the first and last point and adding an arrowhead if the relationship continues. If the pattern is non-linear, join the points with a smooth freehand curve, never with a ruler, because straight segments between points on a curve misrepresent the shape. For y equals x squared minus 1 with the pairs negative 2 semicolon 3, negative 1 semicolon 0, 0 semicolon negative 1, 1 semicolon 0 and 2 semicolon 3, the curve is a symmetrical U with its lowest point at 0 semicolon negative 1.

If the independent variable is discrete, the points are left as dots. The fare collected against passengers at R15 each is plotted at 1 semicolon 15, 2 semicolon 30, 3 semicolon 45 and 4 semicolon 60 and not joined, however tempting the straight line through them looks.

The error museum, six exhibits. Exhibit one: swapped coordinates, plotting 2 semicolon 5 at five across and two up. Exhibit two: a non-uniform scale, where the y-axis reads 0, 5, 10, 20, 50 at equal spacings; every point is then misplaced. Exhibit three: joining discrete points. Exhibit four: a ruler used on a parabola, making a pointed V instead of a smooth U. Exhibit five: unlabelled axes, so the marker cannot tell what was plotted. Exhibit six: a point plotted off the line without being checked, when the table says it must be on the line.

Layout for marks: axes drawn with a ruler, labelled with letters and with quantity and unit where there is a story, uniform scales written in, each point marked precisely and labelled, the joining decision made and made correctly, and a title if the question asks for one. A graph drawn this way earns full accuracy marks even when the content is unfamiliar, because the standards are the same for every graph in the FET phase. The questions for this section are with you now: plot everything then join, ruler or smooth hand or neither, and the six exhibits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same drawing again through a hallway and a staircase, a bath with somebody in it, and a rule about rulers — plain words, same rules.

## Subtopic: Across the Hall, Then Up the Stairs

The grid is a building. The x-axis is a hallway running left and right along the ground floor, and the y-axis is a lift going up and down. The origin is the front door, where you start every time. A pair like 3 semicolon 2 is directions: walk 3 along the hall to the right, then go up 2 floors. Always the hall first, then the lift, because x is written first.

Say the directions out loud as you walk them: three right, two up. The words stop you from swapping them, because the hall always comes before the lift in the sentence.

Negative numbers just change direction. Negative 2 semicolon 4: walk 2 to the LEFT, then up 4. Negative 3 semicolon negative 1: 3 left, then 1 DOWN into the basement. 4 semicolon negative 2: 4 right, 2 down. The four corners of the building are the four quadrants: top right is both positive, top left is left-and-up, bottom left is both negative, bottom right is right-and-down. Before you mark a point, glance at the two signs and ask which corner it should be in. If your pencil is in the wrong corner, you misread a sign.

Mark the point with a small sharp cross exactly on the grid corner, and write its name next to it. A big blob could be any of four corners, and the marker will not guess for you.

Plot the five pairs from y equals 2x plus 1: negative 2 semicolon negative 3, negative 1 semicolon negative 1, 0 semicolon 1, 1 semicolon 3, 2 semicolon 5. Two in the basement on the left, one on the lift itself, two upstairs on the right. They line up like beads on a string, because the rule adds 2 every time. If one bead is off the string, go back and check its directions. Your questions on this section are ready: hall first then lift, signs choose the corner, and a small sharp cross with a name.

## Subtopic: Sketch the Story

A global graph is a picture of a story, and you draw it one chapter at a time. First draw the two axes and write what each one measures, with units: time in minutes along the bottom, litres of water up the side. Then read the story and draw each part with last week's five words: up for increasing, down for decreasing, flat for constant, straight if it is steady and bendy if it is not.

The bath. Tap runs steadily for 10 minutes: a straight line going up from the front door. Somebody sits in it for 20 minutes: flat, from 10 to 30 minutes, because the water is not changing. Then the plug is pulled and it drains in 5 minutes: a straight line going down to zero, from 30 to 35 minutes, and steeper than the filling line, because the same water leaves in half the time. Mark 10, 30 and 35 on the time axis so the chapters are the right lengths.

A ball thrown up: a smooth hill, not a pointed roof, because the ball slows down before it turns. Tea cooling: a slide that is steep at first and flattens out, never reaching the floor. Bacteria doubling: a slide the other way, starting flat and getting steeper.

The picture does not need a number on every point. It needs labelled axes, the key times marked, and each chapter the right shape and the right steepness compared with the others. That is what the marks are for. The questions for this section: axes with units first, one chapter at a time, and steeper means faster.

## Subtopic: Dots, Then a Ruler or a Smooth Hand

When you have a table, plot every dot first. Do not join anything until all the dots are on the grid, because a dot that sits off the pattern is telling you that you made a slip, and once a line is drawn you will not see it.

Then decide how to join, and there are three choices. Straight line: if the dots line up and the thing along the bottom can be a fraction, like time or kilometres, lay a ruler along them and draw one line, going a little past the first and last dot. Smooth curve: if the dots bend, like y equals x squared minus 1 making a U with its bottom at 0 semicolon negative 1, put the ruler away and draw a smooth freehand curve through every dot. A ruler on a curve makes a pointed V, and a pointed V is wrong. No join at all: if the thing along the bottom comes in whole pieces, like passengers, leave the dots as dots.

Extending a straight line a little past the end dots, with a small arrowhead, says that the rule carries on beyond the table. For a story that stops, such as a bath that is drained at 35 minutes, the line stops too, at the point where the story ends.

A scale must be even. If one block is 5 litres at the bottom of the axis, one block is 5 litres all the way up. Writing 0, 5, 10, 20, 50 at equal spaces is not a scale, and every dot on it is in the wrong place. Choose the scale before you draw, by looking at your biggest and smallest numbers.

One more habit: a title or caption when the question asks for one, and the letter of each named point written beside its cross. These are small, and they are the marks that separate a neat 5 out of 5 from a 3 out of 5 with the same dots.

The checklist for every graph: ruler-straight axes with letters, quantity and unit; an even scale written in; each dot a small sharp cross with its name; the joining decision; and a quick look that every dot is where its two signs say it should be. Hall then lift, chapter by chapter, dots then decide. The final questions of the lesson are with you now: plot everything before joining, the three joining choices, and an even scale.
