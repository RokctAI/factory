# Part 1 — Expert

Today we read graphs before we draw them. A global graph is a picture of a whole relationship, and the Grade 9 skill is to describe it in precise words: linear or non-linear, increasing or decreasing or constant, where it reaches a maximum or a minimum, and whether the quantity is discrete or continuous. These words are examined directly, usually with a graph of a real situation such as a temperature through a day, water draining from a tank or a taxi fare against passengers. Every description must be anchored to a reading from the axes, with units, because a sentence without a number is not yet an answer.

## Subtopic: Reading the Axes and the Story

A graph has two axes, and the first job is to read what each one measures. The horizontal axis carries the independent variable, the quantity that is chosen or that simply passes: time, number of kilometres, number of items. The vertical axis carries the dependent variable, the quantity that responds: temperature, cost, height. Read the labels and the units first, then the scale: what does one grid block represent? A graph of temperature in Bloemfontein over a day might run from midnight to midnight on the horizontal axis, with each block two hours, and from 0 to 30 degrees Celsius vertically, with each block 5 degrees.

A single point on the graph is an ordered pair read from both axes. The point above 14:00 and level with 28 says that at two in the afternoon the temperature was 28 degrees. Reading it needs a ruler held vertically from the time and horizontally from the value. A description that says "it was hot in the afternoon" is not a reading; "the temperature reached 28 degrees Celsius at 14:00" is.

The story is the sequence of readings in words. Temperature fell from 15 degrees at midnight to a low of 8 degrees at 05:00, rose steadily through the morning to 28 degrees at 14:00, and fell again to 16 degrees by midnight. Each clause has a direction, a value and a time. That is the standard of a full-marks interpretation.

Scales are not always one block to one unit, and misreading the scale is the quietest way to lose a mark. If each vertical block is 5 degrees, a point one and a half blocks above 20 reads 27,5, not 21,5. Count the blocks between two labelled values, divide, and write the value of one block in the margin before reading any point. The same care applies to a horizontal axis that starts at 06:00 rather than at midnight, or to a vertical axis that does not start at zero, which can make a small change look dramatic.

Independent and dependent are also the x and y of the table work you did last week. The table of ordered pairs listed x first; the graph puts x across and y up. So the ordered pair 14 semicolon 28 is the afternoon point. Reading a graph is reading a table that has been drawn. The questions for this section are with you now: which variable goes on which axis, reading a point with units, and turning readings into a sentence.

## Subtopic: Linear or Non-Linear, Increasing, Decreasing or Constant

Linear means the graph is a straight line, and a straight line means the dependent variable changes by the same amount for each unit of the independent variable, the constant difference from the table lesson. A taxi fare of R20 plus R12 per kilometre is linear: every extra kilometre adds exactly R12. Non-linear means curved: the change is not the same each step. A ball thrown upwards rises fast, slows, stops and falls, and its height-against-time graph is a curve.

Increasing means the graph rises as you read from left to right: as the independent variable grows, so does the dependent one. Decreasing means it falls from left to right. Constant means it is horizontal: the dependent variable does not change at all while the independent variable moves on. A tank being filled is increasing; a tank draining is decreasing; a full tank with the taps closed is constant.

A graph can be all three in different sections, and the examiner expects each section to be named with its interval. A learner walks to school, stops at a shop, then walks on. The distance-from-home graph increases from 0 to 10 minutes, is constant from 10 to 15 minutes while the learner is in the shop, and increases again from 15 to 25 minutes. Constant distance does not mean the learner vanished; it means the learner stood still.

A cooling cup of tea is a non-linear decreasing graph: it loses heat fast at first, when the gap between tea and room is large, and more slowly later, so the curve is steep on the left and flattens to the right without ever quite reaching room temperature. A population of bacteria doubling every hour is non-linear increasing, the geometric pattern from Term 1 drawn as a curve that gets steeper. Naming the direction and whether the rate is speeding up or slowing down describes a curve completely.

Steepness carries information. A steeper increasing line means a faster increase: two taxis with fares R20 plus R12 per kilometre and R20 plus R15 per kilometre both increase, but the second line is steeper, and the steepness is the rate per kilometre. Next lesson this steepness is measured and called the gradient. For now, compare lines by eye and say which increases faster. Your questions on this section: straight versus curved, the three directions, and a graph that changes direction in sections.

## Subtopic: Maximum, Minimum and Turning Points

A maximum is the highest value the dependent variable reaches, and a minimum is the lowest. On the temperature graph the maximum is 28 degrees and the minimum is 8 degrees. Two pieces of information go with each: the value itself, read from the vertical axis, and when it happens, read from the horizontal axis. The maximum temperature was 28 degrees Celsius, and it occurred at 14:00. Giving only the time or only the value is half an answer.

A turning point is where a graph changes from increasing to decreasing or from decreasing to increasing. The temperature graph turns at 05:00 from falling to rising, which is where the minimum is, and turns at 14:00 from rising to falling, which is where the maximum is. The ball's height graph turns once, at its highest point: it rises to a maximum height of 20 metres after 2 seconds and then falls. Not every maximum is at a turning point: a graph that increases steadily over its whole domain has its maximum at the right-hand end.

Distinguish the maximum of the graph from the steepest part of the graph. The temperature is rising fastest at about 09:00, where the graph is steepest, but the temperature is highest at 14:00, where the graph is flat for a moment at the top of its curve. Fastest increase and largest value are different questions, and confusing them costs the mark.

Where there is no turning point, the extremes are at the ends. The taxi fare graph over 0 to 10 kilometres has its minimum of R20 at 0 kilometres and its maximum of R140 at 10 kilometres, simply because the line never stops rising within the interval that was drawn.

In context, a maximum or minimum often answers the question the graph was drawn for. A shop's daily sales graph has a maximum at the time of day to put more staff on the tills; a reservoir's level has a minimum in the week before the rains, which is when restrictions bite. Interpreting is naming the extreme, placing it in time, and saying what it means for the people in the story. The questions for this section are ready: value and time for each extreme, turning points, and highest versus steepest.

## Subtopic: Discrete or Continuous and the Error Museum

A quantity is continuous if it can take any value in between, including fractions and decimals. Time, temperature, height and distance are continuous: there is a temperature at every instant and a height at every moment of the ball's flight, so the graph is a connected line or curve. A quantity is discrete if it can only take separate values, usually whole numbers. The number of passengers in a taxi is discrete: there can be 3 or 4, never 3,5. The cost of sending SMSs is discrete in the number of messages. A discrete graph is a set of separate dots that should not be joined, because a joined line would claim a value for 3,5 passengers that does not exist.

The distinction sits on the horizontal axis. For the fare against kilometres travelled, the kilometres are continuous and the graph is a line. For the total fare collected against the number of passengers at R15 each, the passengers are discrete and the graph is dots at 15, 30, 45, 60. Both are linear in pattern; one is a line, the other is a row of dots on a line.

The error museum, five exhibits. Exhibit one: joining the dots on a passengers-against-fare graph, inventing fares for half a passenger. Exhibit two: reading a constant section of a distance-time graph as "the learner went backwards"; horizontal means stationary. Exhibit three: answering "when was the temperature highest" with 28 degrees, giving the value when the time was asked. Exhibit four: calling the steepest part of the graph the maximum. Exhibit five: describing a graph without any reading from the axes, such as "it goes up then down", which earns no marks because it could describe a thousand graphs.

Layout for marks: for every description, a direction word, an interval or time, a value and a unit. For every extreme, the value and when. For every graph, a decision about discrete or continuous before describing its shape. Interpretations that follow this layout earn full marks even on unfamiliar contexts, because the words are the same whatever the story. The questions for this section are with you now: dots or a line, the five exhibits, and the four parts of a full-marks description.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same graph-reading again through a day in Bloemfontein, a walk to school with a stop at the shop, and a taxi filling up one passenger at a time — plain words, same rules.

## Subtopic: Every Graph Tells a Story

A graph is a story with the time or the count along the bottom and the thing that changes up the side. Before you say anything about the shape, read the two labels and their units out loud. Bottom: time of day, in hours. Side: temperature, in degrees Celsius. Now you know what story it is: how warm Bloemfontein was through one day.

Pick a point and read it both ways. Go along the bottom to 14:00, go up to the graph, go across to the side: 28. So at two in the afternoon it was 28 degrees. Write it with the unit and the time, and you have a reading. Every sentence you write about a graph should contain at least one reading like that, because "it got hot" could be any day anywhere, while "28 degrees at 14:00" is this graph.

Check the scale before you trust a reading. If one block up the side is 5 degrees, then half a block is 2,5 degrees, not 0,5. Count the blocks between two numbers you can see, work out what one block is worth, and write it down in the margin.

Tell the whole story from left to right. It started at 15 degrees at midnight. It cooled down to 8 degrees at 05:00, the coldest moment. It warmed all morning to 28 degrees at 14:00, the hottest moment. It cooled again to 16 degrees by midnight. Four sentences, each with a direction, a number and a time. That is a full-marks answer, and it is just reading.

Last week's tables are the same thing before drawing. Each column of a table is one dot on the graph: the input across, the output up. So reading a graph is reading a table that someone drew for you. Your questions on this section are ready: labels and units first, read a point both ways, tell the story left to right.

## Subtopic: Up, Down, Flat, Straight or Bendy

Five words describe the shape of any part of a graph. Up means increasing: as you read to the right, the line climbs. Down means decreasing: the line falls. Flat means constant: the line is horizontal and the thing being measured is not changing. Straight means linear: the same step every time, like a taxi adding R12 every kilometre. Bendy means non-linear: the step keeps changing, like a ball that shoots up fast, slows, and falls.

Here is a walk to school. Distance from home along the side, time along the bottom. For the first 10 minutes the line goes up: walking away from home. From 10 to 15 minutes it is flat: the learner is in the shop buying a vetkoek, not moving. From 15 to 25 minutes it goes up again: walking on. Flat does not mean going back. It means standing still. Going back would be a line coming down towards zero.

Straight lines can be steep or gentle, and steeper means faster. Two taxis: one adds R12 a kilometre, one adds R15. Both lines go up and both are straight, but the R15 line is steeper, because each kilometre adds more. Next lesson you will measure the steepness and call it the gradient. For now, just say which one climbs faster and why.

A cup of tea cooling is down and bendy: it drops fast at first and then slower and slower, flattening out towards room temperature. Bacteria doubling every hour is up and bendy: slow at first, then steeper and steeper. For a bendy graph, say the direction and whether it is speeding up or slowing down, and you have described it.

Describe a graph section by section, and for each section give the word and the interval: increasing from 0 to 10 minutes, constant from 10 to 15 minutes, increasing from 15 to 25 minutes. The questions for this section: the five shape words, flat means still, and steeper means faster.

## Subtopic: Dots or a Line, and Where the Peak Is

Some things come in whole pieces and some things flow. Passengers in a taxi come in whole pieces: 1, 2, 3, 4, never 3 and a half. So a graph of fare collected against passengers is a row of separate dots: 15, 30, 45, 60 rand. Do not join them. A line between the dots would say that 2 and a half passengers paid R37,50, and there is no such passenger. Things that come in pieces are called discrete, and their graphs are dots.

Time, temperature, distance and height flow. There is a temperature at every instant, a height at every moment of the ball's flight. Those graphs are joined lines or curves. Things that flow are called continuous. Decide dots or line by asking: can the thing along the bottom be a fraction? Kilometres can, so the fare-against-kilometres graph is a line. Passengers cannot, so dots.

Now the peak. The highest point of a graph is the maximum, the lowest is the minimum, and each one needs two numbers: how high, and when. The temperature's maximum was 28 degrees, at 14:00. The ball's maximum height was 20 metres, after 2 seconds. If the question asks when, answer with the time. If it asks what, answer with the value. If it asks for the maximum, give both.

And do not confuse the top of the hill with the steepest slope. The temperature climbs fastest at about 09:00, when the line is steepest. It is highest at 14:00, when the line is flat at the very top. Steepest is about speed of change; highest is about size. Dots or a line, up down flat straight bendy, how high and when. The final questions of the lesson are with you now: deciding dots or a line, the two numbers for a peak, and highest versus steepest.
