### subtopic_1 — The y-Intercept
**Assistant:** Why is the y-intercept found by setting x equal to zero rather than y?

**Tutor:** Because the y-intercept is the point where the line crosses the y-axis, and every point on the y-axis has an x-coordinate of zero; the axis is the set of all points that have not moved left or right from the origin. Substituting x equals 0 into the equation therefore asks what y is at the exact moment the line passes the vertical axis. For y equals 2x plus 1 that gives y equals 1 and the point 0 semicolon 1. In the standard form y equals mx plus c the term mx disappears when x is zero, which is why the constant c is the y-intercept and can be read directly, provided the equation has y alone on the left; an equation such as 2y equals 4x plus 6 must be divided through by 2 first, giving y equals 2x plus 3 and a y-intercept of 3, not 6. In context the y-intercept is the value of the dependent variable before the independent variable has started, which is why a taxi fare of 20 plus 12k has a y-intercept of 20, the flag fee, and why a direct proportion such as cost equals 15 times the number of items passes through the origin with y-intercept zero. On a drawn graph the y-intercept is read where the line meets the vertical axis, with the scale checked first. A full answer gives the point, the value and, where a story is attached, a sentence that says what the starting value means.

### subtopic_2 — The x-Intercept
**Assistant:** Why does finding the x-intercept turn into solving an equation?

**Tutor:** Because the x-intercept is where the line crosses the x-axis, every point of which has y equal to 0, and substituting y equals 0 leaves x inside the equation still wrapped in the operations of the rule. Unwrapping those operations is exactly the inverse method from two lessons ago. For y equals 3x minus 6, substituting gives 0 equals 3x minus 6; adding 6 to both sides gives 3x equals 6 and dividing by 3 gives x equals 2, so the x-intercept is the point 2 semicolon 0. For y equals 2x plus 1 the equation 0 equals 2x plus 1 gives x equals negative one half, and that fraction is the correct answer; rounding it to 0 or to negative 1 moves the point off the line. Finding the y-intercept, by contrast, is a calculation rather than an equation, because substituting x equals 0 leaves only numbers on the right. The confusion to avoid is substituting x equals 0 when the x-intercept is wanted, because the name suggests x; the x-intercept is where the other variable, y, is zero, since the line is at height zero when it meets the horizontal axis. Writing each intercept as a full ordered pair with its zero visible, 2 semicolon 0 and 0 semicolon negative 6, keeps the two apart. Two special cases are worth knowing: a horizontal line such as y equals 4 has no x-intercept, and a line through the origin has both intercepts at 0 semicolon 0.

### subtopic_3 — The Gradient
**Assistant:** Why does it not matter which two points I choose to calculate the gradient?

**Tutor:** Because a straight line has the same steepness everywhere, so the ratio of vertical change to horizontal change between any two of its points is always the same number. On y equals 2x plus 1, the points 0 semicolon 1 and 2 semicolon 5 give a rise of 4 and a run of 2, ratio 2; the points 1 semicolon 3 and 3 semicolon 7 also give rise 4 and run 2, ratio 2; and the points 0 semicolon 1 and 1 semicolon 3 give rise 2 and run 1, ratio 2 again. Any pair works, which is why the gradient is a property of the line rather than of a particular pair. What does matter is doing the subtraction in the same order on top and bottom. From 3 semicolon 3 to 0 semicolon negative 3, the rise is 3 minus negative 3, which is 6, and the run is 3 minus 0, which is 3, giving 2; reversing both gives negative 6 over negative 3, still 2; reversing only one gives negative 2 and is wrong. Choosing points where the line passes exactly through grid intersections keeps the rise and run whole and avoids estimation errors. The sign carries meaning: a positive gradient is a rising line, a negative gradient a falling one, and zero is horizontal. In y equals mx plus c the gradient is m, and in context it is the rate with its unit, R12 per kilometre for the taxi or negative 25 litres per minute for a draining tank. Lines with equal gradients are parallel.

### subtopic_4 — Reading Features From a Graph and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** What is the quickest reliable way to get all three features from a drawn line?

**Tutor:** Read the y-intercept where the line meets the vertical axis, calculate the gradient from two grid-intersection points, write the equation in the form y equals mx plus c, and then solve y equals 0 for the x-intercept, checking it against where the line visibly crosses the horizontal axis. For a line through 0 semicolon negative 3 and 3 semicolon 3, the y-intercept is negative 3, the gradient is 3 minus negative 3 over 3 minus 0, which is 6 over 3, so 2, the equation is y equals 2x minus 3, and setting y to zero gives 2x equals 3, so the x-intercept is 1,5, which matches the line crossing halfway between 1 and 2. The same three features can be read from a table of ordered pairs: the constant difference is the gradient, the pair with x equal to 0 gives the y-intercept, and the x-intercept is where the outputs would pass through zero. The error museum collects the slips that cost marks: dividing run by rise to get one half instead of 2; reporting a positive gradient for a line that falls from left to right; swapping the intercepts by reading a number at the wrong axis; taking c from 2y equals 4x plus 6 without dividing through; and computing a gradient from a single point by dividing its y by its x, which is correct only for lines through the origin. The protection against all five is a layout that writes x equals 0 for the y-intercept, y equals 0 for the x-intercept, and the two points, the rise, the run and the ratio for the gradient, followed by a sign check against the picture.
