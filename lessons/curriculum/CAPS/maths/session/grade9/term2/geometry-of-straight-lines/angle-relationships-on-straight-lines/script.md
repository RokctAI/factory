# Part 1 — Expert

Today we leave algebra for geometry, and the first thing to notice is that the algebra comes with us. The geometry of straight lines rests on a handful of angle facts: angles on a straight line add to 180 degrees, angles around a point add to 360 degrees, vertically opposite angles are equal, and when two parallel lines are cut by a transversal, corresponding angles are equal, alternate angles are equal and co-interior angles add to 180 degrees. Each fact has a name, each name is a reason that must be written in brackets after every statement, and the examiner marks the reason as strictly as the number. By the end you will state each relationship precisely and recognise it in a diagram from any angle.

## Subtopic: Angles on a Straight Line and Around a Point

A straight line is a half-turn, and a half-turn measures 180 degrees. So two or more angles that sit next to each other on a straight line, sharing a vertex and filling the line, add up to 180 degrees. The reason is written as angles on a straight line, abbreviated to angles on a str line. If one angle is 115 degrees, its neighbour is 180 minus 115, which is 65 degrees. Two angles that add to 180 degrees are called supplementary, whether or not they touch.

Angles that meet at a point and fill the whole turn add to 360 degrees; the reason is angles around a point. Three angles meeting at a point measure 90 degrees, 140 degrees and x: then x is 360 minus 230, which is 130 degrees. Angles that add to 90 degrees are complementary, and perpendicular lines, marked with a small square at the corner, create four right angles of 90 degrees each; the reason for a 90 is given lines perpendicular, or simply the right-angle mark.

Algebra enters immediately. Two angles on a straight line are x and 2x plus 30. Then x plus 2x plus 30 equals 180, so 3x equals 150 and x equals 50. The angles are 50 degrees and 130 degrees, and they add to 180, which is the check. The equation line must carry the reason: 3x plus 30 equals 180, angles on a str line. Without the reason, the equation is unsupported and the mark is lost.

Adjacent angles on a straight line can be more than two. If a line is cut by two rays from the same point into angles of 40 degrees, x and 65 degrees, then x is 180 minus 105, which is 75 degrees, with the same reason. The rule is about filling the half-turn, however many pieces do it.

The layout for every geometry statement in Grade 9 is: the statement, an equals sign, the value or expression, and the reason in brackets. Angle ABC equals 65 degrees, angles on a str line. This habit is the one that carries into Grade 10 Euclidean geometry, where the reasons are the marks. The questions for this section are with you now: 180 on a line, 360 around a point, perpendicular means 90, and the reason in brackets.

## Subtopic: Vertically Opposite Angles

When two straight lines cross, four angles are formed at the intersection. The two that sit opposite each other, sharing only the vertex, are vertically opposite angles, and they are equal. The reason is written vert opp angles. If one of the four is 70 degrees, the one directly opposite is also 70 degrees, and the other two are each 110 degrees, because each is on a straight line with a 70.

Why are they equal? Call the four angles a, b, c and d going round. a and b lie on a straight line, so a plus b equals 180. b and c lie on a straight line too, so b plus c equals 180. Both a and c are therefore 180 minus b, and so a equals c. That two-line argument is the proof, and a Grade 9 learner can be asked to reproduce it. Equal angles are marked in a diagram with the same number of small arcs.

Vertically opposite angles give equations when expressions are involved. One angle is 3x minus 10 and its vertically opposite partner is 2x plus 20. Then 3x minus 10 equals 2x plus 20, vert opp angles, so x equals 30 and each angle is 80 degrees. The two remaining angles are 100 degrees each, angles on a str line. Check: 80 plus 100 is 180.

The four angles at a crossing therefore come in two equal pairs, and the two pairs are supplementary to each other: knowing any one of the four fixes all four. That is why a question about two crossing lines usually gives one angle and asks for the other three, and why writing the three reasons, vert opp angles twice and angles on a str line once, earns three marks for what feels like one idea.

Two cautions. Vertically opposite angles need two straight lines crossing; two angles that merely look opposite at a corner where one line stops are not vertically opposite. And vertically opposite angles are equal, not supplementary; learners who write 180 minus 70 for the opposite angle have confused the two relationships. The questions for this section are ready: the X made by two crossing lines, the two-line proof, and equal versus supplementary.

## Subtopic: Parallel Lines Cut by a Transversal

Two lines are parallel if they never meet, and parallel lines are marked with matching arrowheads in a diagram. A transversal is a third line that crosses both. Eight angles are formed, four at each crossing, and they fall into three named pairs.

Corresponding angles are in the same position at each crossing: both above the parallel and to the right of the transversal, for instance. They are equal. The shape they make with the lines is an F, possibly rotated or reflected. Reason: corresp angles, followed by the names of the parallel lines, as in corresp angles, AB parallel to CD.

Alternate angles lie between the parallels, on opposite sides of the transversal. They are equal. The shape is a Z, or an N when the diagram is rotated. Reason: alt angles, AB parallel to CD.

Co-interior angles lie between the parallels, on the same side of the transversal. They add to 180 degrees. The shape is a C or a U. Reason: co-int angles, AB parallel to CD. These are the only pair of the three that are supplementary rather than equal, and that is the most common slip in the topic.

Example. Parallel lines AB and CD are cut by a transversal, and one angle at the top crossing is 125 degrees. Its corresponding angle at the bottom crossing is 125 degrees, corresp angles, AB parallel to CD. Its alternate angle is 125 degrees, alt angles. Its co-interior partner is 180 minus 125, which is 55 degrees, co-int angles. Every other angle in the figure is now either 125 or 55 by angles on a straight line or vertically opposite angles.

Any one of the eight angles fixes the other seven, because each is related to the given angle by one of the three pairs, by vertically opposite angles or by angles on a straight line. In a test, a diagram with one given angle and letters for the rest is a request to work round the figure, writing one statement and one reason per letter.

The converses are also true and examinable: if a pair of corresponding or alternate angles is equal, or a pair of co-interior angles is supplementary, then the two lines are parallel. The reason is then written the other way round, for instance AB parallel to CD, alt angles equal. The questions for this section are with you now: F, Z and C, which pairs are equal and which add to 180, and the converse.

## Subtopic: Naming, Notation and the Error Museum

Angles are named with three letters, the vertex in the middle: angle ABC is the angle at B between the arms BA and BC. A single letter, angle B, is acceptable only when exactly one angle sits at B. Where several angles share a vertex, a question may label them with a small number, angle B one and angle B two, or with a letter such as x. Parallel lines carry matching arrowheads; equal angles carry matching arcs; a right angle carries a small square. Reading these marks is the first step in every problem, because a relationship that the marks do not support cannot be used.

The reason vocabulary, written in brackets after each statement: angles on a str line; angles around a point; vert opp angles; corresp angles, with the parallel lines named; alt angles, with the parallel lines named; co-int angles, with the parallel lines named; given, for an angle the question supplied. Reasons are not optional and are not decorative: in the marking guideline each statement earns its mark only with its reason.

The error museum, five exhibits. Exhibit one: using a parallel-line reason when the lines have no arrowheads and are not stated to be parallel; two lines that look parallel in a sketch may not be. Exhibit two: writing alt angles for a pair that are actually co-interior, and so claiming equality where the sum should be 180. Exhibit three: stating that angles are equal because they look equal, which is not a reason. Exhibit four: naming an angle with the vertex at the wrong end, angle BAC when the angle is at B. Exhibit five: a correct value with no reason, which forfeits the method mark.

Layout for marks: identify the parallel lines and the transversal and say which is which; name each angle with three letters; write each statement as angle, equals, value, reason; when an equation is needed, write the equation with its reason, solve it with the inverses from earlier this term, then state the angle size with its unit. The questions for this section are with you now: three-letter names, the reason vocabulary, and the five exhibits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same angle facts again through a half-turn and a full turn, a pair of scissors, and three letters of the alphabet that live between parallel lines — plain words, same rules.

## Subtopic: Half a Turn Is 180

Stand facing a wall. Turn all the way round until you face it again: that is a full turn, 360 degrees. Turn halfway, so your back is to the wall: a half-turn, 180 degrees. A straight line is a half-turn drawn on paper. So any angles sitting side by side along a straight line, meeting at the same point, must add up to 180, because together they make the half-turn. If one angle on the line is 115 degrees, the one next to it is 180 take away 115, which is 65 degrees. Reason, in brackets: angles on a straight line.

Angles meeting at a point and going all the way round make the full turn, so they add to 360. Three angles at a point are 90, 140 and a mystery: the mystery is 360 take away 230, which is 130. Reason: angles around a point. A right angle is a quarter-turn, 90 degrees, and it gets a little square in the corner. Two lines that meet at a right angle are perpendicular.

When the angles have letters in them, the half-turn gives you an equation. Two angles on a line are x and 2x plus 30. Together they are the half-turn: x plus 2x plus 30 equals 180. Sort the fruit: 3x plus 30 equals 180. Undo buttons: 3x is 150, x is 50. The angles are 50 and 130, and 50 plus 130 is 180, so it checks.

Every line you write gets a reason in brackets. Not because the teacher is fussy, but because the reason is the mark. Angle equals 65 degrees, angles on a straight line. Say the fact you used, every time. Your questions on this section are ready: the half-turn, the full turn, the quarter-turn, and the reason in brackets.

## Subtopic: Scissors and the X

Open a pair of scissors. The two blades and the two handles make four angles at the pivot, like an X. The angle between the blades and the angle between the handles are opposite each other, and they are always the same size: open the blades wider and the handles open wider by exactly the same amount. Those are vertically opposite angles, and the rule is that they are equal. Reason: vert opp angles.

Why? Each blade angle and its neighbouring handle angle sit on a straight line, so they add to 180. The two neighbours of any angle are both 180 take away that angle, so they are the same size as each other, and the one across from it is too. If one angle in the X is 70, the one opposite is 70, and the two others are 110 each.

Letters in the X make an equation. One angle is 3x minus 10, the opposite one is 2x plus 20. Opposite means equal: 3x minus 10 equals 2x plus 20. Take 2x off both sides: x minus 10 equals 20, so x is 30. Each of those two angles is 80, and the other two are 100 each.

Two warnings. Opposite angles are EQUAL, not adding to 180; do not take 70 away from 180 for the opposite one. And you need two full lines crossing to make the X. A corner where one line stops is not an X, and there is nothing vertically opposite there. The questions for this section: scissors, the X, equal not supplementary, and two full lines.

## Subtopic: F, Z and C — The Parallel Line Alphabet

Two railway tracks are parallel: they never meet, and in a diagram they get matching arrowheads. A road crossing both tracks is a transversal. At each crossing there are four angles, eight in total, and three letters of the alphabet tell you how they pair up.

F: corresponding angles. Look for an F shape made by one track, the road, and the other track. The two angles tucked into the F's corners, in the same position at each crossing, are equal. The F might be upside down or back to front; it is still an F. Reason: corresp angles, then name the parallel lines.

Z: alternate angles. Look for a Z, or an N if the picture is turned. The two angles inside the Z's corners, between the tracks and on opposite sides of the road, are equal. Reason: alt angles, and name the lines.

C: co-interior angles. Look for a C or a U. The two angles inside the C, between the tracks and on the same side of the road, add to 180. This is the odd one out: not equal, but a half-turn together. Reason: co-int angles, and name the lines.

Some people remember it as: F and Z are twins, C is a couple that adds up. Any memory aid works, as long as the reason you write uses the proper name, because the marker reads the brackets, not your finger.

Suppose one angle at the top crossing is 125. Its F partner is 125, its Z partner is 125, and its C partner is 180 take away 125, which is 55. Every other angle in the picture is 125 or 55 from there, by the half-turn or the X. If you want to know whether the angles equal or add up, draw the letter with your finger: F and Z mean equal, C means 180.

One more X trick: if the four angles are given as letters, you only need one number to find the rest. Opposite twins are equal, and neighbours add to 180, so one number unlocks all four.

Two last habits. Check the arrowheads before you use any of the three letters; no arrowheads, no parallel reasons. And name angles with three letters, the corner letter in the middle. Half-turn, full turn, scissors, F, Z, C, and a reason in brackets every time. The final questions of the lesson are with you now: which letters mean equal, which letter means 180, and arrowheads before reasons.
