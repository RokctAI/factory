### subtopic_1 — Multi-Step Problems on Intersecting Lines
**Assistant:** Why is finding x not the end of an angle problem?

**Tutor:** Because x is a device for finding angles, not an angle itself, and the question almost always asks for the angles. In the figure where 2x and x plus 30 are adjacent on a straight line, the equation 3x plus 30 equals 180, with the reason angles on a str line, gives x equals 50, but the angles are 2 times 50, which is 100 degrees, and 50 plus 30, which is 80 degrees. The vertically opposite partners are then 100 degrees and 80 degrees by vert opp angles, so the four angles are 100, 80, 100 and 80, and they add to 360 degrees, which is the check that the whole chain is sound. A learner who writes x equals 50 and stops has done the algebra and missed the geometry, and the marking guideline typically allocates separate marks to the sizes of the angles with their reasons. The same discipline applies to figures with three lines through a point, where two adjacent angles of 40 and 65 degrees give a third of 75 degrees on the same half-turn, and the three opposite partners follow by vertically opposite angles, with all six summing to 360 degrees. The layout is a chain: one angle per line, each as angle equals value with the reason in brackets, any equation written with its reason before it is solved, substitution back shown, units on every angle, and a final check line. Angles labelled with letters are found in the order they can be reached, not alphabetically.

### subtopic_2 — Algebra With Parallel Lines
**Assistant:** How do I know whether to set two expressions equal or add them to 180?

**Tutor:** By naming the pair of angles before writing anything. Corresponding angles, the F shape, and alternate angles, the Z or N shape, are equal when the lines are parallel, so the two expressions are set equal: for corresponding angles 3x minus 15 and 2x plus 25, the equation is 3x minus 15 equals 2x plus 25, with the reason corresp angles and the parallel lines named, giving x equals 40 and angles of 105 degrees each. For alternate angles 4x and 2x plus 50, the equation 4x equals 2x plus 50 gives x equals 25 and angles of 100 degrees. Co-interior angles, the C or U shape, add to 180 degrees, so the expressions are summed: for 2x plus 10 and x plus 20 the equation is 3x plus 30 equals 180, with the reason co-int angles, giving x equals 50 and angles of 110 and 70 degrees, which add to 180 as the check. Choosing the wrong form is the costliest slip in the topic; setting the co-interior expressions equal gives x equals 10 and two angles of 30 degrees, which cannot be co-interior because they do not add to 180. When one of the pair is a number rather than an expression, the same reason applies with the number on the right: 3x minus 15 equals 105 for a corresponding pair gives x equals 40. Once x is known, the remaining angles of the figure follow by one relationship each, and the answer is the chain to the angle asked for.

### subtopic_3 — Figures With Two Transversals
**Assistant:** How does a two-transversal figure show that the angles of a triangle add to 180?

**Tutor:** By building the triangle between two parallel lines and moving two of its angles up to the third vertex with alternate angles. Let AB and CD be parallel, with E on AB and two transversals EF and EG meeting CD at F and G, so that EFG is a triangle with its base on CD and its apex on AB. Suppose the base angles at F and G are 50 and 60 degrees. The angle at E between EA and EF is alternate to the 50 at F, both lying between the parallels on opposite sides of EF, so it is 50 degrees, alt angles, AB parallel to CD. The angle at E between EB and EG is alternate to the 60 at G, so it is 60 degrees by the same reason. Those two angles and the apex angle FEG lie along the straight line AB at E, so angle FEG is 180 minus 50 minus 60, which is 70 degrees, angles on a str line. The three angles of the triangle are therefore 50, 60 and 70, and their sum of 180 was produced by the argument rather than assumed. That is the standard proof of the angle sum of a triangle, which Term 3 uses as a tool, and it shows how the parallel-line facts sit underneath the rest of the geometry. The method for any two-transversal figure is the same: work round the figure one relationship at a time, writing each angle with its reason, and let the picture fill in.

### subtopic_4 — Proving Lines Parallel and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** What is the difference between using parallel lines and proving them?

**Tutor:** Direction. When lines are given as parallel, by arrowheads or a statement, the relationships run forwards: corresponding and alternate angles are equal and co-interior angles add to 180, and each may be used as a reason to find an angle. When a question asks whether lines are parallel, nothing about the lines may be assumed, and the relationships run backwards as converses: if a pair of alternate or corresponding angles is equal, or a pair of co-interior angles adds to 180, then the lines are parallel, and the reason is written as the lines being parallel because of that angle fact. With alternate angles of 65 and 65, the statement is PQ parallel to RS, alt angles equal. With co-interior angles of 110 and 70, the sum 180 gives PQ parallel to RS, co-int angles supplementary. With co-interior angles of 110 and 65, the sum is 175, so the lines are not parallel, and that answer with its arithmetic is complete and correct. Sometimes the pair you need must first be built, for example by taking the vertically opposite angle of a given 65 to obtain an interior angle that is alternate to another 65; that preliminary step uses only the straight-line and vertically-opposite facts, which need no parallel assumption. The error museum for this lesson is short: using a parallel-line rule inside a proof that the lines are parallel, which is circular; stopping at x; writing the co-interior equation as an equality; using an angle before it has been found; and leaving reasons off lines that felt obvious. A proof is laid out as the claim, the pair built with reasons, the comparison with arithmetic, and the conclusion with its converse reason.
