### subtopic_1 — Angles on a Straight Line and Around a Point
**Assistant:** Why must the reason be written after every statement, even when the number is right?

**Tutor:** Because in geometry the marks are awarded for justified statements, not for correct numbers, and the marking guideline typically gives one mark for the statement and one for the reason, or withholds the mark entirely when the reason is missing. An unknown angle of 65 degrees next to a 115 on a straight line is correct, but the statement that earns credit is angle equals 65 degrees, angles on a straight line, because the reason tells the marker which fact was applied and shows that the value was derived rather than guessed. The same applies when algebra is involved: 3x plus 30 equals 180 is an equation with no standing until the bracket says angles on a straight line, which is the fact that makes the equation true. The reason vocabulary for this section is short: angles on a str line for a half-turn of 180 degrees, angles around a point for a full turn of 360 degrees, and given or the right-angle mark for a 90 produced by perpendicular lines. Two angles that add to 180 are called supplementary and two that add to 90 are complementary, and these words may appear in questions, but they are descriptions rather than reasons; the reason names the configuration in the diagram. Building the statement-reason habit now is what makes Grade 10 Euclidean geometry manageable, because there every line of a proof is marked this way.

### subtopic_2 — Vertically Opposite Angles
**Assistant:** How do I prove that vertically opposite angles are equal rather than just stating it?

**Tutor:** Use the straight-line fact twice and compare. Label the four angles at the crossing a, b, c and d in order around the point. The angles a and b are adjacent on one of the straight lines, so a plus b equals 180 degrees, angles on a str line. The angles b and c are adjacent on the other straight line, so b plus c equals 180 degrees, angles on a str line. Both equations give the same thing when rearranged: a equals 180 minus b and c equals 180 minus b, so a equals c. The same argument with b and d shows that b equals d. That is the complete proof, three lines long, and a Grade 9 question can ask for it directly. The proof also explains the structure of every crossing: the four angles form two equal pairs, and each pair is supplementary to the other, so one given angle fixes all four. If one angle is 70 degrees, its vertically opposite partner is 70 degrees, vert opp angles, and the remaining two are each 110 degrees, angles on a str line. With expressions, equality gives the equation: 3x minus 10 equals 2x plus 20, vert opp angles, so x equals 30 and the angles are 80, 80, 100 and 100 degrees. The two errors to avoid are subtracting from 180 for the opposite angle, which confuses equal with supplementary, and applying the reason at a corner where one line ends, since vertically opposite angles exist only where two full straight lines cross.

### subtopic_3 — Parallel Lines Cut by a Transversal
**Assistant:** How do I tell alternate angles from co-interior angles when both are between the parallel lines?

**Tutor:** By which side of the transversal they are on. Both pairs lie between the two parallel lines, in the interior, but alternate angles are on opposite sides of the transversal and co-interior angles are on the same side. The letter shapes make the distinction visible: alternate angles sit in the corners of a Z or an N, which crosses from one side to the other, while co-interior angles sit inside a C or a U, which stays on one side. The consequence is the important part. Alternate angles are equal, alt angles with the parallel lines named, while co-interior angles add to 180 degrees, co-int angles with the lines named. Corresponding angles, the third pair, are in the same position at each crossing, one interior and one exterior, make an F, and are equal. So of the three pairs only co-interior angles are supplementary, and writing alt angles for a co-interior pair claims equality where a sum is required, which is the most frequent error in the topic. For a given angle of 125 degrees, the corresponding and alternate partners are 125 degrees and the co-interior partner is 55 degrees. All three relationships depend on the lines actually being parallel, shown by matching arrowheads or stated in the question; without that, none of the three reasons may be used. The converses hold too: equal alternate or corresponding angles, or supplementary co-interior angles, prove that two lines are parallel, and the reason is then written as the lines being parallel because of the angle relationship.

### subtopic_4 — Naming, Notation and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** What is the correct way to name an angle, and why does it matter?

**Tutor:** An angle is named with three letters, one from each arm and the vertex in the middle, so angle ABC is the angle at B between the arms BA and BC. The middle letter is the vertex, and that is the whole convention. It matters because a figure usually has several angles at or near the same point, and a two-letter or wrongly ordered name is ambiguous: angle BAC is at A, not at B, and a marker cannot award a mark for a correct value attached to the wrong angle. A single letter such as angle B is acceptable only when exactly one angle sits at B. When several angles share a vertex, questions label them with subscripts or small numbers, angle B one and angle B two, or with a letter such as x, and those labels are used exactly as given. Reading the diagram's marks comes before naming anything: matching arrowheads mean parallel lines and unlock the three parallel-line reasons; matching arcs mean equal angles; a small square means 90 degrees. The error museum collects what happens when the conventions are skipped: a parallel reason used on lines with no arrowheads, alt angles written for a co-interior pair, angles declared equal because they look equal, a vertex named at the wrong end, and a correct value with no reason. The layout that earns full marks names each angle with three letters, writes statement, equals, value and reason on one line, writes any equation with its reason before solving it, and ends with the angle size and its unit.
