# Part 1 — Expert

Today the angle facts from last lesson become a method for solving problems. A geometry problem gives a figure with a few angles marked, some as numbers and some as expressions, and asks for the rest. The solution is a chain: each new angle is found from angles already known, by one named relationship, with its reason, until the target is reached. Problems with algebra add one step, forming and solving an equation, and problems that ask you to prove two lines parallel reverse the logic, using the angles to earn the arrowheads rather than the arrowheads to earn the angles. The marks are in the chain, and every link must be written.

## Subtopic: Multi-Step Problems on Intersecting Lines

Two straight lines cross. One angle is marked 2x and the angle next to it along one line is marked x plus 30. Find all four angles. The two marked angles are adjacent on a straight line, so 2x plus x plus 30 equals 180, angles on a str line. Then 3x equals 150 and x equals 50. Now substitute back: 2x is 100 degrees and x plus 30 is 80 degrees. The angle vertically opposite the 100 is 100 degrees, vert opp angles, and the angle vertically opposite the 80 is 80 degrees, vert opp angles. Four angles, 100, 80, 100, 80, and they add to 360, which is the check.

The order matters. Finding x is not the end: the question asked for angles, and a learner who stops at x equals 50 has answered a question that was not asked. Substitute back, state each angle with its unit, and give the reason that produced it.

A three-line figure. Three straight lines pass through one point, making six angles. Two adjacent ones are 40 degrees and 65 degrees. The third angle on that side of a straight line is 180 minus 105, which is 75 degrees, angles on a str line. The three angles on the other side are the vertically opposite partners: 40, 65 and 75, vert opp angles. All six add to 360, angles around a point, which is the check.

A chain can be checked at the end as well as built at the start. The four angles at any crossing add to 360 degrees, the angles along any straight line add to 180 degrees, and the eight angles at two crossings of a transversal with parallel lines come in two sizes that add to 180. If the finished figure violates any of these, a link in the chain is wrong, and the check has earned its place in the layout.

Layout for marks: a numbered or lettered chain, one angle per line, each line in the form angle equals value with the reason in brackets, equations with their reason before being solved, and a final check line. Where a figure's angles are labelled with letters a, b, c, state them in the order in which they can be found, not in alphabetical order; a cannot be found before the angle it depends on. The questions for this section are with you now: forming an equation from adjacent angles, substituting back, and the order of the chain.

## Subtopic: Algebra With Parallel Lines

Parallel lines AB and CD are cut by a transversal. A pair of corresponding angles is marked 3x minus 15 and 2x plus 25. Corresponding angles are equal, so 3x minus 15 equals 2x plus 25, corresp angles, AB parallel to CD. Subtract 2x: x minus 15 equals 25, so x equals 40. Each angle is 3 times 40 minus 15, which is 105 degrees, and 2 times 40 plus 25, also 105. The agreement of the two is the check that x is right.

A pair of alternate angles is marked 4x and 2x plus 50. Equal, alt angles, so 4x equals 2x plus 50, 2x equals 50, x equals 25, and each angle is 100 degrees.

A pair of co-interior angles is marked 2x plus 10 and x plus 20. These add to 180, co-int angles, so 3x plus 30 equals 180, 3x equals 150, x equals 50. The angles are 110 degrees and 70 degrees, and 110 plus 70 is 180, the check. Notice that the equation for co-interior angles has 180 on the right, while the equations for corresponding and alternate angles have the two expressions equal to each other. Choosing the wrong form is the single most expensive slip, and it is avoided by naming the pair out loud before writing anything.

A question may give one expression and one number instead of two expressions. If a corresponding angle to 3x minus 15 is marked 105 degrees, the equation is 3x minus 15 equals 105, corresp angles, and x is 40. The reason is identical; only the right-hand side changes.

Once x is known, every angle in the figure follows. With the co-interior pair at 110 and 70, the eight angles at the two crossings are four of 110 and four of 70, each found by one of vertically opposite angles, angles on a straight line, corresponding or alternate angles. A question that asks for a specific angle, say angle labelled y on the far side of the figure, wants the chain from the given expressions to y, not a list of everything. Your questions on this section: equal for F and Z, 180 for C, and the chain from x to the angle asked for.

## Subtopic: Figures With Two Transversals

Two transversals make a richer figure, and the method is unchanged: work round the figure one relationship at a time. Parallel lines AB and CD, with two transversals from a single point E on AB meeting CD at F and G. The angles at F and G on the inside of the figure are 50 degrees and 60 degrees. Find the angle FEG at E.

The angle at E between EA and EF is alternate to the 50 at F, since they lie between the parallels on opposite sides of transversal EF: 50 degrees, alt angles, AB parallel to CD. The angle at E between EB and EG is alternate to the 60 at G: 60 degrees, alt angles, AB parallel to CD. Those two angles and angle FEG together fill the straight line AB at E, so angle FEG equals 180 minus 50 minus 60, which is 70 degrees, angles on a str line.

The same figure can be labelled with letters instead of numbers, and the chain is written in the same order: the two alternate angles at E first, each with its reason, then the straight line at E for the third.

Look at what has been shown. The three angles of triangle EFG are 50, 60 and 70, and they add to 180. The argument did not assume that; it produced it from alternate angles and a straight line. This is the standard proof that the angles of a triangle sum to 180 degrees, and Term 3 will use that fact constantly. The parallel-line facts are the foundation under it.

A second two-transversal figure: parallel lines with two transversals crossing between them, forming an X between the parallels. One angle at the top crossing is 125 degrees. Its alternate angle at the bottom of the X is 125 degrees; the angle vertically opposite that is 125 degrees; and the acute angles at the X are 55 degrees, angles on a str line. Each step is one relationship, and the figure fills in. The questions for this section are ready: alternate angles into a triangle, the straight line at the apex, and working round two crossings.

## Subtopic: Proving Lines Parallel and the Error Museum

The converse problems give angles and ask whether two lines are parallel. The logic runs backwards: if a pair of alternate angles is equal, or a pair of corresponding angles is equal, or a pair of co-interior angles adds to 180, then the lines are parallel. The reason is written as the lines being parallel because of the angle fact, for instance AB parallel to CD, alt angles equal.

Two lines PQ and RS are cut by a transversal. The alternate angles are 65 degrees and 65 degrees. Statement: PQ is parallel to RS, alt angles equal. One line is enough. If instead the co-interior angles are 110 degrees and 70 degrees: 110 plus 70 is 180, so PQ is parallel to RS, co-int angles supplementary. If the co-interior angles are 110 degrees and 65 degrees: 110 plus 65 is 175, not 180, so PQ is not parallel to RS, and that negative answer, with its arithmetic shown, earns the marks.

Sometimes the angles given are not the pair you need, and a preliminary step builds the pair. If a 65 at the top crossing is given on the outside of the figure, find its vertically opposite angle, 65, which is inside, and then compare with the 65 at the bottom as alternate angles. The chain is: vertically opposite angles, then the converse.

The error museum, five exhibits. Exhibit one: using a parallel-line relationship to find an angle in a question that asked you to prove the lines parallel; that is circular, assuming what must be shown. Exhibit two: stopping at x equals 50 when the angles were asked for. Exhibit three: writing the co-interior equation as 2x plus 10 equals x plus 20 instead of summing to 180. Exhibit four: a chain with a missing link, where an angle is used before it has been found. Exhibit five: reasons omitted on the easy lines because they felt obvious; the marker does not share that feeling.

Layout for marks in a proof: state what is to be proved, build the needed pair with reasons, show the equality or the sum with the arithmetic, and conclude with the parallel statement and its converse reason. For a find-the-angle problem: the chain, the substitution back, the units, the check. The questions for this section are with you now: the three converses, the preliminary step that builds the pair, and the five exhibits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same problem-solving again through a chain of dominoes, letters inside the F, Z and C, and a detective proving two roads parallel — plain words, same rules.

## Subtopic: Find One, Then the Next

An angle problem is a row of dominoes. You cannot knock over the last one directly; you knock over the first, which knocks the second, and so on until the one you want falls. Each domino is one angle found from one fact, with the fact written in brackets.

Two lines cross. One angle is 2x, the one beside it on the line is x plus 30. First domino: beside each other on a line means a half-turn, so 2x plus x plus 30 equals 180, angles on a straight line. Sort and undo: 3x is 150, x is 50. Second domino: put 50 back in. 2x is 100, x plus 30 is 80. Third and fourth dominoes: the X rule, opposite angles equal, so the other two are 100 and 80. Check: 100 plus 80 plus 100 plus 80 is 360, a full turn.

The trap is stopping at x equals 50. x is not an angle; it is the key to the angles. The question asked for angles, so put x back and write each angle with its degree sign. A key is useless if you never open the door.

Three lines through one point make six angles, like a sliced pizza. Two slices are 40 and 65. The third slice on that half is 180 take away 105, which is 75, because the three fill a half-turn. The other three slices are the opposite twins: 40, 65, 75. All six add to 360.

If a problem has both numbers and letters, do the number dominoes first; they are free. Then the letter ones with an equation, and then put x back.

Write the dominoes in the order they fall, one per line, each with its reason. If the figure labels the angles a, b, c, find them in the order you can, not in alphabetical order. Your questions on this section are ready: dominoes in order, put x back, and the full-turn check.

## Subtopic: Letters Inside the F, Z and C

When the angles between parallel lines have letters in them, the alphabet from last lesson writes the equation for you. Find the letter shape first. Say it out loud. Then write the equation that goes with it.

F or Z: the two angles are twins, so set the two expressions equal. Corresponding angles 3x minus 15 and 2x plus 25: 3x minus 15 equals 2x plus 25. Take 2x off both sides: x minus 15 is 25, so x is 40. Each angle is 105. Alternate angles 4x and 2x plus 50: 4x equals 2x plus 50, so 2x is 50 and x is 25. Each angle is 100.

C: the two angles are a couple that adds to 180, so add the expressions and set the sum to 180. Co-interior angles 2x plus 10 and x plus 20: 2x plus 10 plus x plus 20 equals 180, so 3x plus 30 is 180, 3x is 150, x is 50. The angles are 110 and 70, and 110 plus 70 is 180. If you had set them equal instead, you would get x equal to 10 and angles of 30 and 30, which do not add to 180 and cannot be co-interior.

So the rule is: F and Z, equals sign between the two expressions. C, plus sign between them and 180 on the right. Say the letter, then write the equation. Then put x back and finish every angle the question asked for, reason by reason. The questions for this section: say the letter first, equals for twins, plus and 180 for the couple.

## Subtopic: Prove It Parallel, or Prove It Not

Now the detective case. Two roads cross a third, and nobody has told you whether the two roads are parallel. No arrowheads. Your job is to decide, using only the angles. The clues are the same three letters, run backwards: if the Z angles are equal, the roads are parallel. If the F angles are equal, parallel. If the C angles add to 180, parallel. If none of those holds, not parallel.

Case one: alternate angles 65 and 65. Equal Z twins, so the roads are parallel. Write: PQ is parallel to RS, alternate angles equal. Case two: co-interior angles 110 and 70. 110 plus 70 is 180, so parallel: PQ is parallel to RS, co-interior angles add to 180. Case three: co-interior angles 110 and 65. 110 plus 65 is 175, not 180. Not parallel, and that is a complete, correct answer with the arithmetic written down.

Sometimes the clue angles are not the ones you were given, and you have to build them. A 65 on the outside of the top crossing is not alternate to anything, but its opposite twin through the X is 65 on the inside, and that one IS alternate to the 65 at the bottom. One domino first, then the proof.

Which clue to use depends on which angles you have. Both inside the roads on opposite sides of the crossing line: Z. Same position at both crossings: F. Both inside on the same side: C. Pick the letter that matches the pair in front of you.

The one thing a detective must never do is assume the answer. If you use an F, Z or C rule to find an angle before the roads have been proved parallel, you have used the thing you were supposed to prove. Build the pair with the half-turn and the X, then compare, then conclude.

The whole topic in one breath: dominoes in order, say the letter before writing the equation, put x back, give every angle its unit and its reason, and prove parallel by running the letters backwards. The final questions of the lesson are with you now: the three backwards clues, building the pair before comparing, and never assuming what you must prove.
