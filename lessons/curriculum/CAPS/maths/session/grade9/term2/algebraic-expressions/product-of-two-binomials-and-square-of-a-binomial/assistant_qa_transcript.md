### subtopic_1 — Multiplying Two Binomials With the Grid
**Assistant:** Why does multiplying two binomials give four products rather than just two?

**Tutor:** Because the distributive property applies twice. The bracket x plus 3 times the bracket x plus 5 means the whole of the first bracket multiplies the second. Distribute the second bracket over the two terms of the first: x times the bracket x plus 5, plus 3 times the bracket x plus 5. Each of those is last lesson's skill, a monomial times a binomial, and each produces two products. Two lots of two is four: x squared, 5x, 3x and 15. Multiplying only x by x and 3 by 5 is the error of pairing firsts with firsts and lasts with lasts, and it skips the two cross products entirely. The grid makes the count visible: a two-by-two table with the terms of one bracket across the top and the other down the side has four cells, and every cell must be filled. The area model explains why it is true rather than just a rule: a rectangle with sides x plus 3 and x plus 5 is made of a square of side x, a 3 by x strip, a 5 by x strip and a 3 by 5 corner, and the total area is the sum of the four pieces. Substituting x equals 1 confirms it: the rectangle is 4 by 6, area 24, and x squared plus 8x plus 15 gives 1 plus 8 plus 15, also 24.

### subtopic_2 — The Square of a Binomial
**Assistant:** Why is the square of x plus 5 not simply x squared plus 25?

**Tutor:** Because squaring means multiplying the bracket by itself, and that is a product of two binomials with four products, not two. The bracket x plus 5 times the bracket x plus 5 gives x squared, 5x, 5x and 25. The two cross products are identical, so they add to 10x, and the expansion is x squared plus 10x plus 25. Squaring does not distribute over addition: the square of a sum is not the sum of the squares. A numerical test settles it. With x equals 1 the bracket is 6, and 6 squared is 36; x squared plus 25 gives 26, so the shortcut is off by exactly the missing 10x. The area picture makes the missing piece visible: a square tile of side x plus 5, cut at the 5 mark both ways, has a large x by x square, a small 5 by 5 square and two rectangles each x by 5. The rectangles are the middle term. The general identity is the bracket a plus b, all squared, equals a squared plus 2ab plus b squared, and with a minus between the terms only the middle sign changes: a squared minus 2ab plus b squared. The constant term of a squared binomial is always positive, so the bracket x minus 4, all squared, is x squared minus 8x plus 16, never minus 16.

### subtopic_3 — The Difference of Two Squares as a Product
**Assistant:** Why does x plus 7 times x minus 7 have only two terms?

**Tutor:** Because the two cross products are equal in size and opposite in sign, so they cancel exactly. Expand in full: x times x is x squared; x times negative 7 is negative 7x; 7 times x is positive 7x; 7 times negative 7 is negative 49. The negative 7x and positive 7x add to zero, leaving x squared minus 49. This happens whenever the two brackets contain the same two terms with opposite signs between them, which is why the identity is written as the bracket a plus b times the bracket a minus b equals a squared minus b squared. It is important not to confuse it with the square of a difference: the bracket x minus 7, all squared, has the same sign in both brackets, so the cross products are both negative 7x and they add to negative 14x rather than cancelling, giving x squared minus 14x plus 49. One has three terms and a positive constant; the other has two terms and a negative constant. Substituting x equals 1 distinguishes them: the square gives 36, the sum-times-difference gives 8 times negative 6, which is negative 48, matching x squared minus 49 at x equals 1. Recognising x squared minus 49 as a product of a sum and a difference is exactly the factorising skill next lesson requires.

### subtopic_4 — Mixed Expansions and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** In a mixed expansion, why keep the second product inside a bracket before subtracting?

**Tutor:** Because a minus sign in front of a product applies to every term of that product, and the only way to be certain every sign flips is to expand the product fully inside its own bracket first, then distribute the minus on a separate line. Take the bracket x plus 3 times the bracket x plus 5, minus the bracket x minus 2, all squared. Expand the first: x squared plus 8x plus 15. Expand the second and keep it in a bracket: minus the bracket x squared minus 4x plus 4. Now distribute the minus: minus x squared plus 4x minus 4. Collect: the x squared terms cancel, 8x plus 4x is 12x, 15 minus 4 is 11, so the result is 12x plus 11. The common failure is to write the second expansion without a bracket and flip only its first sign, producing minus x squared minus 4x plus 4, which collects to 4x plus 19 and is wrong. Substitution at x equals 1 exposes it: the original is 24 minus 1, which is 23; 12 plus 11 is 23, while 4 plus 19 is also 23, so for this particular question try x equals 2 instead: the original is 35 minus 0, which is 35; 12x plus 11 gives 35; 4x plus 19 gives 27. A check at two values is never wasted when the x squared terms cancel. Layout for marks: each product on its own line, the second inside a bracket, the minus distributed on a fresh line, then collect.
