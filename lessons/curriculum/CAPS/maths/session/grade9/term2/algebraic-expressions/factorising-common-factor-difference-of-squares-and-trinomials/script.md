# Part 1 — Expert

Today we run last lesson backwards. Expanding takes brackets and produces terms; factorising takes terms and rebuilds the brackets. Grade 9 CAPS asks for three kinds of factorising: taking out a common factor, recognising a difference of two squares, and splitting a trinomial of the form x squared plus bx plus c, including the case where a number in front of x squared is a common factor that comes out first. Every factorised answer can be checked by expanding it, so there is never a reason to submit a wrong one. This is the skill that solves equations in two lessons' time and that Grade 10 uses in every algebra question.

## Subtopic: Taking Out a Common Factor

A common factor is anything that divides every term. Take 6x squared plus 9x. The coefficients 6 and 9 share the highest common factor 3. The variable parts x squared and x share x. So the highest common factor of the two terms is 3x. Write it in front of a bracket, and inside the bracket write what is left when each term is divided by 3x: 6x squared divided by 3x is 2x; 9x divided by 3x is 3. Result: 3x times the bracket 2x plus 3. Check by expanding: 3x times 2x is 6x squared, 3x times 3 is 9x. Correct.

The highest common factor matters. 6x squared plus 9x also equals 3 times the bracket 2x squared plus 3x, and that is true, but the bracket still contains a common factor x, so it is not fully factorised and loses a mark. The instruction factorise fully means the bracket must have nothing left to take out.

With two variables: 4a squared b minus 6ab squared. The coefficients share 2; the a parts share a; the b parts share b. Highest common factor 2ab. Inside the bracket: 4a squared b divided by 2ab is 2a; negative 6ab squared divided by 2ab is negative 3b. Result: 2ab times the bracket 2a minus 3b. The sign of the second term rides into the bracket.

With a negative first term, you may take out a negative common factor: negative 3x squared minus 12x is negative 3x times the bracket x plus 4. Both forms, with or without the negative outside, are accepted, but the signs inside must agree with the choice made outside. Expand to confirm: negative 3x times x is negative 3x squared; negative 3x times 4 is negative 12x. The questions for this section are with you now: finding the highest common factor, dividing each term by it, and expanding to check.

## Subtopic: The Difference of Two Squares

Last lesson, the bracket x plus 7 times the bracket x minus 7 expanded to x squared minus 49. Reverse it: x squared minus 49 factorises as the bracket x plus 7 times the bracket x minus 7. The pattern has three requirements. Two terms. A minus between them. Both terms are perfect squares. x squared is x squared, and 49 is 7 squared. Then a squared minus b squared is the bracket a plus b times the bracket a minus b.

9x squared minus 4: two terms, a minus, 9x squared is the square of 3x, 4 is the square of 2. Result: the bracket 3x plus 2 times the bracket 3x minus 2. 16 minus y squared: the square of 4 minus the square of y, so the bracket 4 plus y times the bracket 4 minus y. The order of the two brackets does not matter, but the sign pattern does: one plus, one minus.

A sum of two squares does not factorise in Grade 9. x squared plus 49 stays as it is. Learners who write the bracket x plus 7 times the bracket x plus 7 should expand it: x squared plus 14x plus 49, which is not the original. The middle term appears, because squaring a binomial always produces one.

Common factor first, always. 2x squared minus 50 is not a difference of two squares as it stands, because 2 and 50 are not perfect squares. Take out the 2: 2 times the bracket x squared minus 25. Now the bracket is a difference of two squares: 2 times the bracket x plus 5 times the bracket x minus 5. Three factors. Writing the bracket 2x plus 10 times the bracket x minus 5 expands correctly, but the first bracket still contains a common factor 2, so it is not fully factorised. Order of operations for factorising: common factor, then look for a pattern in what remains. Your questions on this section: the three requirements for a difference of two squares, why a sum of squares does not factorise, and the common factor that comes out first.

## Subtopic: Factorising Trinomials x Squared Plus bx Plus c

Last lesson's expansion of the bracket x plus 3 times the bracket x plus 5 gave x squared plus 8x plus 15, and the constant 15 was 3 times 5 while the middle coefficient 8 was 3 plus 5. To factorise x squared plus 8x plus 15, hunt for two numbers whose product is 15 and whose sum is 8. The factor pairs of 15 are 1 and 15, 3 and 5. Only 3 and 5 add to 8. Result: the bracket x plus 3 times the bracket x plus 5. Expand to check: four products, x squared, 5x, 3x, 15, collecting to the original.

Signs tell you where to look. x squared minus 3x minus 10: product negative 10, sum negative 3. A negative product means one number is positive and one negative. Pairs for 10: 1 and 10, 2 and 5. The pair with a difference of 3 is 2 and 5, and the bigger one takes the sign of the middle term, so negative 5 and positive 2. Result: the bracket x plus 2 times the bracket x minus 5. Check: 2 times negative 5 is negative 10, 2 plus negative 5 is negative 3.

x squared minus 8x plus 16: product positive 16, sum negative 8. A positive product with a negative sum means both numbers are negative. Negative 4 and negative 4. Result: the bracket x minus 4 times the bracket x minus 4, written as the bracket x minus 4, all squared. This is a perfect square trinomial, the square of a binomial from last lesson.

x squared plus 2x minus 24: product negative 24, sum positive 2. Pairs for 24: 1 and 24, 2 and 12, 3 and 8, 4 and 6. Difference of 2: 4 and 6. The bigger takes the plus: positive 6 and negative 4. Result: the bracket x plus 6 times the bracket x minus 4.

Not every trinomial factorises with whole numbers. x squared plus 3x plus 5 has product 5 and sum 3, and the only pairs for 5 are 1 and 5, which add to 6. In Grade 9 that trinomial is simply left as it is; Grade 10 gives you other tools for it. Recognising when the hunt has no answer is part of the skill, and it saves time in a test.

Layout for marks: write the product and the sum you are looking for, list the pair you chose, write the two brackets, and expand in the margin if time allows. A substitution check also works: at x equals 1, x squared minus 3x minus 10 is negative 12, and the bracket 1 plus 2 times the bracket 1 minus 5 is 3 times negative 4, also negative 12. The questions for this section are ready: the product-and-sum hunt, the sign rules, and the perfect square trinomial.

## Subtopic: Trinomials With a Common Factor and the Error Museum

Grade 9 does not factorise trinomials where the number in front of x squared cannot be removed; that is Grade 10. But when the coefficient of x squared is a common factor of all three terms, take it out first and then factorise the trinomial that remains. 3x squared minus 3x minus 18: every term divides by 3, giving 3 times the bracket x squared minus x minus 6. Now factorise the bracket: product negative 6, sum negative 1; the numbers are negative 3 and positive 2. Result: 3 times the bracket x minus 3 times the bracket x plus 2.

2x squared plus 16x plus 30: common factor 2, leaving x squared plus 8x plus 15, which is the anchor trinomial. Result: 2 times the bracket x plus 3 times the bracket x plus 5. Learners who try to factorise 2x squared plus 16x plus 30 directly, hunting for two numbers with product 30 and sum 16, find 15 and 1 and write a bracket with 2x in it that does not expand correctly. The common factor first rule removes the difficulty entirely.

The decision procedure for any factorising question. Step one: is there a common factor? Take it out. Step two: how many terms remain in the bracket? Two terms with a minus between squares is a difference of two squares. Three terms is a trinomial, hunt for product and sum. Step three: expand to check.

The error museum. Exhibit one: x squared minus 49 written as the bracket x minus 7, all squared. Expanding gives x squared minus 14x plus 49, which has a middle term the original does not. Exhibit two: x squared plus 8x plus 15 written as the bracket x plus 15 times the bracket x plus 1, because 15 times 1 is 15; but 15 plus 1 is 16, not 8, and the expansion shows 16x. Exhibit three: 6x squared plus 9x written as 3 times the bracket 2x squared plus 3x, true but not fully factorised. Exhibit four: x squared minus 3x minus 10 written as the bracket x minus 2 times the bracket x plus 5, the right numbers with the wrong signs; expanding gives plus 3x.

Every exhibit is exposed by expansion, which is why expansion was last lesson and factorising is this one. A factorised answer that you have not expanded is a guess; a factorised answer that you have expanded is a fact. The questions for this section are with you now: the common factor before the trinomial, the three-step decision procedure, and the four exhibits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same three kinds of factorising again through what every bag shares, a two-number game, and a shape-spotting habit — plain words, same rules.

## Subtopic: Pull Out What Every Term Shares

Picture a row of bags: 6x squared in one, 9x in the other. Ask what is inside every single bag. Both have a 3, because 6 and 9 are both in the three times table. Both have an x. So 3x is in every bag. Pull it out and put it in front: 3x times the bracket. What is left in each bag? 6x squared with 3x removed is 2x; 9x with 3x removed is 3. Answer: 3x times the bracket 2x plus 3. Multiply it back out to check: 6x squared plus 9x. It works.

Pull out everything that is shared, not just some of it. If you only pull the 3, you get 3 times the bracket 2x squared plus 3x, and there is still an x sitting in both bags that you left behind. The marker sees the leftover x and takes a mark. Fully factorised means nothing shared is left inside.

Two letters: 4a squared b minus 6ab squared. Shared: a 2, an a, a b. Pull out 2ab. Left behind: 2a in the first bag, negative 3b in the second. Answer: 2ab times the bracket 2a minus 3b. The minus stays with its term.

Think of it as a taxi fare. Four friends each pay a fare that includes the same R3 base charge, so you can say three times the bracket, and the bracket holds whatever differs. Factorising is noticing the shared part and naming it once. Your questions on this section are ready: spotting what every term shares, what is left behind, and checking by multiplying back.

## Subtopic: The Two-Number Game

A trinomial like x squared plus 8x plus 15 came from two brackets, x plus something and x plus something else. The game is to find the two somethings. The clue is in the last two numbers. The two somethings multiply to the end number, 15, and add to the middle number, 8. List pairs that multiply to 15: 1 and 15, 3 and 5. Which pair adds to 8? 3 and 5. Answer: the bracket x plus 3 times the bracket x plus 5. Last lesson you expanded exactly this and got x squared plus 8x plus 15, so it checks.

Signs are the only tricky part, and there are three cases. Both signs plus, like x squared plus 8x plus 15: both numbers positive. Last sign minus, like x squared minus 3x minus 10: the two numbers have different signs, so you need a pair with a DIFFERENCE equal to the middle number. Pairs for 10: 1 and 10, 2 and 5. Difference 3: 2 and 5. The bigger one copies the middle sign, so negative 5 and positive 2. Answer: the bracket x plus 2 times the bracket x minus 5. Last sign plus but middle minus, like x squared minus 8x plus 16: both numbers negative. Negative 4 and negative 4. Answer: the bracket x minus 4, all squared.

A product and a sum are two clues, and you need both. Many pairs multiply to 15; only one adds to 8. Many pairs add to 8; only one multiplies to 15. The pair that satisfies both clues is the answer, and there is never more than one such pair.

Say the two conditions out loud before you hunt: multiply to this, add to that. Write both numbers down. Then expand the brackets in the margin to be sure. For x squared plus 2x minus 24: multiply to negative 24, so different signs, difference 2. Pairs: 4 and 6. Bigger copies the plus: 6 and negative 4. The bracket x plus 6 times the bracket x minus 4. The questions for this section: multiply to this, add to that, and the three sign cases.

## Subtopic: Spot the Shape, Then Check

Before any factorising, ask two questions in order. First, is there something every term shares? Pull it out. Second, how many terms are left in the bracket, two or three?

Two terms with a minus in the middle, and both are squares: that is the vanishing-middle pair from last lesson. x squared minus 49 is x squared minus 7 squared, so the answer is the bracket x plus 7 times the bracket x minus 7. 9x squared minus 4 is 3x squared minus 2 squared, so the bracket 3x plus 2 times the bracket 3x minus 2. Two terms with a PLUS in the middle do not factorise. x squared plus 49 stays as it is; leave it alone and move on.

Three terms: play the two-number game.

Here is why the shared-part step comes first. 2x squared minus 50 does not look like a square minus a square, because 2 and 50 are not squares. Pull out the 2: 2 times the bracket x squared minus 25. Now it is a square minus a square. Answer: 2 times the bracket x plus 5 times the bracket x minus 5. Same with 3x squared minus 3x minus 18: pull out 3, leaving x squared minus x minus 6, then play the game, multiply to negative 6 and add to negative 1, which gives negative 3 and positive 2. Answer: 3 times the bracket x minus 3 times the bracket x plus 2.

Then the check. Every factorised answer can be multiplied back out, and if it does not give you the original, it is wrong. x squared minus 49 as the bracket x minus 7 squared? Multiply back: x squared minus 14x plus 49. Not the original. Busted. x squared plus 8x plus 15 as the bracket x plus 15 times the bracket x plus 1? Multiply back: 16x in the middle. Busted. Shared part out, count the terms, spot the shape, multiply back. The final questions of the lesson are with you now: the two questions you ask first, the shape of a square minus a square, and multiplying back to check.
