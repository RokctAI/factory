### subtopic_1 — Taking Out a Common Factor
**Assistant:** How do I know I have taken out the highest common factor and not just a factor?

**Tutor:** Look inside the bracket after you have factorised. If any number or variable still divides every term in the bracket, you have not taken out the highest common factor, and the question is not finished. For 6x squared plus 9x, writing 3 times the bracket 2x squared plus 3x is a true statement, but inside the bracket both 2x squared and 3x still contain x, so the full answer is 3x times the bracket 2x plus 3, and now the bracket's terms share nothing. A systematic approach: find the highest common factor of the coefficients using the Term 1 prime factorisation skill, then for each variable take the lowest power that appears in every term. For 4a squared b minus 6ab squared, the coefficients 4 and 6 share 2, the lowest power of a is a, and the lowest power of b is b, so the highest common factor is 2ab and the bracket holds 2a minus 3b. Then expand: 2ab times 2a is 4a squared b and 2ab times negative 3b is negative 6ab squared, which is the original. The expansion check costs a few seconds and protects both the method mark and the answer mark.

### subtopic_2 — The Difference of Two Squares
**Assistant:** Why does x squared plus 49 not factorise when x squared minus 49 does?

**Tutor:** Because the pattern that produces a two-term answer, the bracket a plus b times the bracket a minus b, always produces a minus between the two squares. Expanding it gives a squared minus ab plus ab minus b squared, and the cross products cancel only because they have opposite signs; the surviving constant is negative b squared. There is no pair of brackets with whole-number terms that expands to a squared plus b squared. If you try the bracket x plus 7 times the bracket x plus 7, the cross products are both positive 7x and add to 14x, giving x squared plus 14x plus 49, which is a perfect square trinomial and not the original. If you try the bracket x minus 7 times the bracket x minus 7, you get x squared minus 14x plus 49. The middle term always appears when the signs match. So in Grade 9 a sum of two squares is left as it is, and recognising that is a mark, not a failure. The requirements for the difference of two squares are strict: exactly two terms, a minus between them, and both terms perfect squares, such as x squared and 49, or 9x squared and 4. If a common factor stops the terms being perfect squares, as in 2x squared minus 50, take it out first and the pattern often appears in the bracket.

### subtopic_3 — Factorising Trinomials x Squared Plus bx Plus c
**Assistant:** How do the signs of b and c tell me which two numbers to look for?

**Tutor:** The constant c is the product of the two numbers and the coefficient b is their sum, so the sign of c tells you whether the numbers share a sign and the sign of b tells you which sign dominates. If c is positive, the numbers have the same sign, and then b decides: positive b means both positive, as in x squared plus 8x plus 15 with 3 and 5; negative b means both negative, as in x squared minus 8x plus 16 with negative 4 and negative 4. If c is negative, the numbers have different signs, and their sum is really a difference, so you look for a factor pair of c whose difference is the size of b, then give the larger number the sign of b. For x squared minus 3x minus 10, the pairs for 10 are 1 and 10, 2 and 5; the pair with difference 3 is 2 and 5, and since b is negative the 5 is negative, giving the bracket x plus 2 times the bracket x minus 5. For x squared plus 2x minus 24, the pair 4 and 6 has difference 2 and the 6 is positive, giving the bracket x plus 6 times the bracket x minus 4. Writing the product and sum you are hunting for, then the pair you chose, earns the method mark even before the brackets are written, and expanding the brackets confirms the signs.

### subtopic_4 — Trinomials With a Common Factor and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Why must the common factor come out before I factorise the trinomial?

**Tutor:** Because the two-number method is built for trinomials whose x squared coefficient is 1, and taking out the common factor is what reduces a Grade 9 trinomial to that form. In 3x squared minus 3x minus 18 every term divides by 3, giving 3 times the bracket x squared minus x minus 6, and the bracket now factorises with the usual hunt: product negative 6, sum negative 1, numbers negative 3 and positive 2, so the full answer is 3 times the bracket x minus 3 times the bracket x plus 2. If you try to factorise the original directly you need two numbers with product negative 18 and sum negative 3 and a bracket containing 3x, and the pairs that satisfy the arithmetic do not fit the brackets without a method you only meet in Grade 10. The decision procedure is always the same: common factor first; then count the terms in the bracket, two terms with a minus between squares being a difference of two squares and three terms being a trinomial; then expand to check. The error museum shows what happens when a step is skipped. Writing x squared minus 49 as the bracket x minus 7 squared ignores the term count; writing x squared plus 8x plus 15 as the bracket x plus 15 times the bracket x plus 1 satisfies the product but not the sum; writing the bracket x minus 2 times the bracket x plus 5 for x squared minus 3x minus 10 has the right numbers with the signs reversed. Each one is exposed in one line of expansion.
