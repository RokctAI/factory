### subtopic_1 — Simplifying a Single Algebraic Fraction
**Assistant:** What exactly is the difference between cancelling a factor and cancelling a term?

**Tutor:** A factor is something that multiplies the whole of an expression; a term is something that is added to or subtracted from the rest of it. A fraction bar divides the whole numerator by the whole denominator, and division undoes multiplication, so only a factor of the whole numerator can be cancelled against a factor of the whole denominator. In x squared plus 8x plus 15 over x plus 3, the 8x is a term of the numerator; it is added to the other pieces, so the x inside it does not multiply the whole top and cannot be cancelled with anything below. Once the numerator is factorised as the bracket x plus 3 times the bracket x plus 5, the bracket x plus 3 does multiply the whole top, and it cancels with the identical bracket on the bottom, leaving x plus 5. The numerical version is the clearest test: 10 plus 5 over 5 is 15 over 5, which is 3, while cancelling the added 5 would give 10. Substitution exposes a wrong cancellation every time. At x equals 2 the original fraction is 35 over 5, which is 7, and x plus 5 is also 7; the illegal cancellation giving x squared plus 5 would give 9. So the procedure is fixed: factorise the top fully, factorise the bottom fully, cancel only whole factors that appear in both, and keep a bracket such as x plus 3 over x exactly as it is, because x is not a factor of x plus 3.

### subtopic_2 — Multiplying and Dividing Algebraic Fractions
**Assistant:** When dividing algebraic fractions, why do I flip the second fraction and not the first?

**Tutor:** Because dividing by a quantity is the same as multiplying by its reciprocal, and the quantity you are dividing by is the second fraction, the divisor. With numbers, a half divided by a quarter asks how many quarters fit into a half; the answer is 2, and you get it by multiplying a half by 4 over 1, the flipped quarter. Flipping the first fraction instead would compute a different question entirely. For the bracket x plus 1 over 4 divided by x squared plus x over 8, flip the divisor to get 8 over x squared plus x, then multiply. Factorise after flipping: x squared plus x is x times the bracket x plus 1. Now the bracket x plus 1 cancels across the two fractions and 4 goes into 8 to leave 2, so the result is 2 over x. The check confirms it: at x equals 3 the original is 1 divided by 12 over 8, which is 1 divided by 1,5, giving two thirds, and 2 over 3 is two thirds. The order of operations is important for marks: flip first, then factorise everything, then cancel across both tops and both bottoms, then write the product of what remains. Flipping after partly factorising is where learners lose track, and flipping the wrong fraction produces an answer that no substitution check will rescue.

### subtopic_3 — Adding and Subtracting Algebraic Fractions
**Assistant:** Why does the second numerator need a bracket when I subtract algebraic fractions?

**Tutor:** Because the minus sign between the fractions applies to the whole of the second fraction, and once the fractions are written over a common denominator that minus applies to the whole of the second numerator, not just its first term. For the bracket x plus 1 over 3 minus the bracket x minus 2 over 4, the lowest common denominator is 12, so the first fraction becomes 4 times the bracket x plus 1 over 12 and the second becomes 3 times the bracket x minus 2 over 12. The combined numerator is 4x plus 4 minus the bracket 3x minus 6. Distributing the minus flips both signs inside: minus 3x plus 6. Collecting gives x plus 10, so the answer is the bracket x plus 10 over 12. Dropping the bracket and writing 4x plus 4 minus 3x minus 6 gives x minus 2, which is the most common wrong answer for this question. Substitution at x equals 2 settles it: the original is 1 minus 0, which is 1; the bracket x plus 10 over 12 gives 12 over 12, which is 1; the wrong answer gives zero. The same discipline applies with variables in the denominator, such as 3 over 2x plus 5 over 3x, where the LCD is 6x and the numerators become 9 and 10, giving 19 over 6x, and the denominator is never added to itself.

### subtopic_4 — Simplifying Mixed Expressions and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** How should I choose a value for the substitution check on a fraction question?

**Tutor:** Choose a small value that keeps the arithmetic quick and that does not make any denominator in the original expression zero. For x squared minus 4 over x minus 2 plus 3x, the denominator x minus 2 is zero at x equals 2, so that value is excluded; x equals 3 works well: the fraction is 5 over 1, plus 9, giving 14, and the simplified 4x plus 2 also gives 14. Avoid x equals 0 when a variable sits in a denominator, as in 2 over x, and avoid x equals 1 when the question has terms that could coincidentally agree at 1, as sometimes happens with squared brackets. If the two results disagree, the error is in one of the usual places: a term cancelled instead of a factor, as in the exhibit that turned x squared plus 8x plus 15 over x plus 3 into x squared plus 5; the wrong surviving bracket, as in x squared minus 9 over x minus 3 written as x minus 3 instead of x plus 3; the wrong fraction flipped in a division; or a second numerator subtracted without its bracket. A check at a second value is worthwhile when the first value happens to make both sides zero. The layout that earns full marks shows every fraction in factorised form, the cancelling strokes, the stated LCD where fractions are added, the bracket around the second numerator, and the final answer on its own line, with the check written in the margin.
