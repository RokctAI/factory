### subtopic_1 — Multiplying a Polynomial by an Integer
**Assistant:** Why does the multiplier have to reach every term? Why is 3(2x − 5) not just 6x − 5?

**Tutor:** Because the bracket is a single quantity and multiplying a quantity by 3 means tripling all of it, not part of it. Put a number in for x and the point is immediate: at x equals 4 the bracket is 8 minus 5, which is 3, and three times that is 9. The correct expansion 6x minus 15 gives 24 minus 15, which is 9. The incomplete expansion 6x minus 5 gives 19, which is wrong, and it is wrong because the negative 5 inside the bracket was supposed to be tripled too. This is the distributive property — a times the quantity b plus c equals a b plus a c — which you revised with integers in Term 1 as the reason 7 times 98 can be computed as 7 times 100 minus 7 times 2. Nothing changes when the parts contain letters. In marking, the first line of an expansion is expected to show each product separately — 3 times 2x and 3 times negative 5 — and the method mark is awarded for that line; a student who writes 6x minus 5 has shown that the multiplier was applied to one term, which is a conceptual error about what a bracket means. The habit that prevents it is to count: two terms in the bracket, two products on the line, and if the counts differ a term has been left behind.

### subtopic_2 — Multiplying a Polynomial by a Monomial
**Assistant:** When I multiply 2x by 3x^2 inside a bracket, how do I keep track of the exponents and the coefficients at once?

**Tutor:** Treat each product as a tiny monomial multiplication, and do it in the same two steps every time: coefficients first, then each variable's exponents. For 2x times 3x squared, the coefficients are 2 and 3, product 6; the variable x has exponents 1 and 2, sum 3; the product is 6x cubed. Move to the next term of the bracket and repeat: 2x times negative x is negative 2 for the coefficient and exponent 1 plus 1, giving negative 2x squared. Then 2x times 4 has coefficient 8 and exponent 1, giving 8x. Writing the three results side by side gives 6x cubed minus 2x squared plus 8x, and because the exponents differ nothing collects. The reason this works is that multiplication is associative and commutative, so 2 times x times 3 times x times x can be regrouped as 2 times 3 times x times x times x, which is what the two-step rule computes. When two variables appear, as in negative 3ab times 2a minus 5b, run the exponent step once for each letter: a gets 1 plus 1, b stays at 1 for the first product, and so on. A check at x equals 2 is worth the few seconds because an exponent error — writing 6x squared for 2x times 3x squared — passes at x equals 1 and fails at 2.

### subtopic_3 — Dividing a Polynomial by an Integer or a Monomial
**Assistant:** Why does 4x over 4x give 1 and not 0? There is nothing left after dividing.

**Tutor:** There is something left: the number 1, which is what any non-zero quantity divided by itself equals. Twelve over twelve is 1, not 0, and 4x over 4x is the same statement with a variable inside. The confusion arises because students picture division as "cancelling" — crossing out the 4x above and below — and when everything is crossed out they see an empty space and write 0 or nothing. The empty space means 1, because cancelling is really dividing both parts by 4x, and 4x divided by 4x is 1. The practical consequence in the anchor, 12x cubed minus 8x squared plus 4x over 4x, is that the answer has three terms, 3x squared minus 2x plus 1, and dropping the 1 produces 3x squared minus 2x, which is a different polynomial. The multiplication check exposes the omission at once: 4x times 3x squared minus 2x gives 12x cubed minus 8x squared, and the 4x that was in the original has vanished, so the answer was incomplete. I recommend that check for every division in this lesson precisely because it catches this error and the first-term-only error without any substitution. One more habit: count the terms. Three terms above the bar, divided by a monomial, must give three terms below, unless two of them combine, which they cannot when their degrees differ.

### subtopic_4 — Checking by Substitution and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** If I always check by substitution, do I still need to be careful with the method? The check will catch mistakes.

**Tutor:** The check will tell you a mistake exists; it will not fix it, and in an examination a correct answer with a wrong first line still loses the method mark. The check is a safety net, not a replacement for the method, and there are three reasons to keep both. First, marks: the memorandum awards marks for the expansion line that shows every product with its sign, and for the collection line, independently of the final answer. A student who writes a wrong first line and then adjusts the answer to pass a check has an inconsistent script and will be marked down for it. Second, reliability: substitution at a single value can be fooled — x equals 1 does not distinguish 2x from 2x squared, and x equals 0 hides every term with a variable — so the check must be chosen with the error in mind, and that requires understanding the method. Third, speed: a careful first line takes a few seconds; a failed check followed by a hunt for the error takes much longer. So the order of work is method first, laid out term by term; then a check at x equals 2 when exponents are involved, or the multiply-back check for a division; and only then move on. Treat a failed check as information about where to look — the size of the mismatch usually points at a sign or an exponent — and not as a licence to skip the method next time.
