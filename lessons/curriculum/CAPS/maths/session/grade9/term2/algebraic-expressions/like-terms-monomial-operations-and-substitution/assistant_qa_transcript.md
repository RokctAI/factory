### subtopic_1 — Adding and Subtracting Like Terms
**Assistant:** Why does a minus in front of a bracket flip every sign and not just the first one?

**Tutor:** Because the minus sign in front of a bracket is a multiplication of the entire bracket by negative 1, and multiplication by a number distributes to every term inside — that is the distributive property you revised with integers. Minus the bracket x squared plus 4x minus 1 means negative 1 times x squared, plus negative 1 times 4x, plus negative 1 times negative 1, which is minus x squared, minus 4x, plus 1. The bracket is a single quantity, and subtracting a quantity means subtracting all of it. A number example makes this concrete: 10 minus the bracket 2 plus 3 minus 1 is 10 minus 4, which is 6; distributing correctly gives 10 minus 2 minus 3 plus 1, which is also 6; flipping only the first sign gives 10 minus 2 plus 3 minus 1, which is 10, and is wrong. The partial flip is the single most frequent error in Grade 9 algebra and it costs the method mark, not merely the answer. Two protections are worth building in. First, remove the bracket on its own line, writing every term with its resolved sign before collecting anything. Second, substitute x equals 1 into the original and the result; a partial flip changes the value and the check exposes it immediately.

### subtopic_2 — Multiplying and Dividing Monomials
**Assistant:** Why do exponents add when I multiply but coefficients multiply? It feels inconsistent.

**Tutor:** It is consistent once you see what each part of a monomial is. The coefficient is an ordinary number, and when two monomials are multiplied their coefficients are simply two numbers being multiplied — 3 times 4 is 12, nothing more. The exponent is not a number being operated on; it is a count of how many copies of the variable are multiplied together. x squared is two x's and x cubed is three x's, so x squared times x cubed is five x's in a row, which we write as x to the power 5. The exponents add because counts of identical factors add when you put the factors side by side. So "coefficients multiply, exponents add" is really one rule — multiply everything — applied to two different kinds of object: numbers multiply as numbers, and repeated factors accumulate by count. Division is the same rule reversed: coefficients divide as numbers, and pairs of identical factors above and below the fraction bar cancel, so the counts subtract. When the rule feels slippery, write the factors out: 3x squared times 4x cubed is 3 times x times x times 4 times x times x times x, and regrouping gives 12 and five x's. The written-out form is slow but it is never wrong, and after a few repetitions the shortcut becomes trustworthy.

### subtopic_3 — Substitution With Fractions and Decimals
**Assistant:** Should I substitute a fraction as a fraction or convert to a decimal first?

**Tutor:** Either, provided you finish in the form the question asks for and you are honest about exactness. A fraction such as one half or three quarters converts to a terminating decimal, 0,5 or 0,75, and the arithmetic is identical in value, so you may choose whichever you handle more reliably — the anchor gives 0 both ways, and negative three quarters gives thirty-five eighths or 4,375, the same number. The caution is with fractions like one third, whose decimal 0,333 recurring must be rounded; substituting the rounded decimal gives an approximate answer, and if the question wants an exact value, only the fraction route provides it. My general advice is to stay in fractions when the question is in fractions, and in decimals when it is in decimals, because the memorandum will be written that way and your answer will be compared to it directly. Whatever the form, the two non-negotiable habits are the same: write the substituted value in brackets, so that a negative is squared as a whole and a minus times a negative becomes a plus, and apply the exponent to the variable before multiplying by the coefficient — 2x squared at one half is 2 times one quarter, not the square of 1. A quick cross-check between the fraction and the decimal route, when both are easy, catches arithmetic slips at no cost.

### subtopic_4 — Mixed Simplification and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** How does checking with x = 1 tell me where the mistake is, and not just that there is one?

**Tutor:** It tells you where by the size and sign of the mismatch, and by what the common errors do to the value. Suppose you simplified 3x squared minus 2x plus 5 minus the bracket x squared plus 4x minus 1 and obtained 2x squared plus 2x plus 4. At x equals 1 the original is 2 and your answer is 8. A difference of 6 is suspicious: the terms you may have mishandled are 4x and negative 1, and flipping both of them changes the value by 2 times 4 minus 2 times 1 at x equals 1, which is 6. The check has pointed at the bracket. Suppose instead you wrote 3x times 2x as 5x squared in a mixed simplification: at x equals 2 the true product is 24 and yours is 20, and a product that is too small by exactly one copy of the variable squared says the coefficients were added rather than multiplied. With practice you learn the signatures: a partial sign flip shifts the value by twice the mishandled terms, an exponent error produces a value wildly off at x equals 2 but perhaps correct at x equals 1, which is why I recommend checking at 2 whenever exponents are involved. The check cannot name the line for you, but it narrows the search to one or two candidates, and it always tells you whether to search at all.
