# Part 1 — Expert

Today factorising earns its keep. An algebraic fraction is a fraction whose numerator or denominator contains a variable, and the only way to simplify one is to factorise top and bottom and cancel the factors they share. We then multiply, divide, add and subtract algebraic fractions using exactly the rules you know for numerical fractions, with factorising doing the work that prime factorisation did for numbers in Term 1. The one rule that governs everything: you cancel factors, never terms. Every simplification can be checked by substituting a value, so a wrong cancellation never needs to reach the marker.

## Subtopic: Simplifying a Single Algebraic Fraction

Take x squared plus 8x plus 15, all over x plus 3. The temptation is to cancel the x on top with the x on the bottom, or the 15 with the 3. Both are wrong, because the numerator is a sum, and a fraction bar divides the whole of the top by the whole of the bottom. Factorise the numerator: the bracket x plus 3 times the bracket x plus 5. Now the fraction is a product of two brackets over the bracket x plus 3, and the bracket x plus 3 is a factor of the whole numerator, so it cancels with the denominator. Result: x plus 5. Check at x equals 2: the original is 35 over 5, which is 7; x plus 5 is 7.

6x squared plus 9x, all over 3x. Factorise the top: 3x times the bracket 2x plus 3. The 3x cancels. Result: 2x plus 3. Last lesson's common factor skill, used immediately.

2x squared minus 8, all over 4x plus 8. Top: common factor 2, then a difference of squares: 2 times the bracket x plus 2 times the bracket x minus 2. Bottom: 4 times the bracket x plus 2. Cancel the bracket x plus 2, and cancel 2 from the 2 and the 4. Result: the bracket x minus 2 over 2. Check at x equals 3: the original is 10 over 20, which is one half; the answer is 1 over 2.

x squared minus 9, all over x squared minus 3x. Top: the bracket x plus 3 times the bracket x minus 3. Bottom: x times the bracket x minus 3. Cancel the bracket x minus 3. Result: the bracket x plus 3 over x. Nothing more cancels: the x on the bottom is a factor of the denominator, but x is not a factor of the whole numerator, because x plus 3 is a sum.

Notice that the same fraction can look simplified and not be. x squared plus 8x plus 15 over x plus 3 has no visible common factor until the top is factorised, and a learner who stops there scores nothing. Conversely, the bracket x plus 3 over x looks as if the x should cancel, and it must not. Appearances are not the test; factors are.

The procedure is always the same. Factorise the numerator fully. Factorise the denominator fully. Cancel any factor that appears in both. Write what remains. The only values of x that are excluded are those that make a denominator zero, and Grade 10 will make much of that; for now, be aware that x plus 3 over x cannot be evaluated at x equals 0. The questions for this section are with you now: factorising before cancelling, factors versus terms, and the substitution check.

## Subtopic: Multiplying and Dividing Algebraic Fractions

Multiplying fractions: top times top, bottom times bottom, but factorise and cancel before multiplying so the arithmetic stays small. x squared minus 4, over 3x, times 6x squared over the bracket x plus 2. Factorise: the first numerator is the bracket x plus 2 times the bracket x minus 2. Now look across both fractions for common factors. The bracket x plus 2 on the first top cancels with the bracket x plus 2 on the second bottom. 3x on the first bottom cancels into 6x squared on the second top, leaving 2x. What remains: the bracket x minus 2 times 2x, written 2x times the bracket x minus 2. Check at x equals 3: the original is 5 over 9 times 54 over 5, which is 6; the answer is 6 times 1, also 6.

Dividing fractions: multiply by the reciprocal of the second fraction, exactly as with numbers. The bracket x plus 1 over 4, divided by x squared plus x over 8. Flip the second fraction and multiply: the bracket x plus 1 over 4 times 8 over x squared plus x. Factorise x squared plus x as x times the bracket x plus 1. Cancel the bracket x plus 1; cancel 4 into 8, leaving 2. Result: 2 over x. Check at x equals 3: the original is 1 divided by 12 over 8, which is 1 divided by 1,5, which is two thirds; 2 over 3 matches.

A longer one: x squared plus 5x plus 6, over x squared plus 2x, times x over the bracket x plus 3. Factorise: the bracket x plus 2 times the bracket x plus 3 over x times the bracket x plus 2, times x over the bracket x plus 3. Everything cancels: the result is 1. That is a legitimate answer, and learners who expect something longer sometimes undo correct cancelling to manufacture one.

Layout for marks: write every fraction in factorised form on one line, show the cancelling with neat strokes, write the result. Flipping the wrong fraction when dividing, or flipping before factorising and then losing track, are the two common slips; flip first, then factorise everything, then cancel. Your questions on this section: factorise before multiplying, flip the second fraction when dividing, and cancel across the fractions.

## Subtopic: Adding and Subtracting Algebraic Fractions

Adding fractions requires a common denominator, and the lowest common denominator is found exactly as the LCM of the denominators was found in Term 1. The bracket x plus 1 over 3, minus the bracket x minus 2 over 4. The LCD of 3 and 4 is 12. Rewrite each fraction over 12: multiply the first top and bottom by 4, the second by 3. Now the numerator is 4 times the bracket x plus 1, minus 3 times the bracket x minus 2. Expand carefully, keeping the second product in a bracket because of the minus: 4x plus 4 minus the bracket 3x minus 6, which is 4x plus 4 minus 3x plus 6. Collect: x plus 10. Result: the bracket x plus 10 over 12. Check at x equals 2: the original is 1 minus 0, which is 1; the answer is 12 over 12, also 1.

With variables in the denominator: 3 over 2x plus 5 over 3x. The LCD of 2x and 3x is 6x. The first fraction becomes 9 over 6x; the second becomes 10 over 6x. Add the numerators: 19 over 6x. Numerators add; denominators do not.

The bracket x plus 2 over x, minus 1 over x. Same denominator already, so subtract the numerators: x plus 2 minus 1 is x plus 1. Result: the bracket x plus 1 over x. Do not cancel the x on top with the x on the bottom: x plus 1 is a sum, and x is not a factor of it.

The error that costs the most marks is subtracting only the first term of the second numerator. For the anchor, writing 4x plus 4 minus 3x minus 6 gives x minus 2, which is wrong; the check at x equals 2 gives zero instead of 1 and exposes it. The minus applies to the whole of the second numerator, which is why it is kept in a bracket until the sign is distributed. The questions for this section are ready: the lowest common denominator, rewriting each fraction, and the bracket around the second numerator.

## Subtopic: Simplifying Mixed Expressions and the Error Museum

Examination questions mix a fraction with ordinary terms. Simplify x squared minus 4, over x minus 2, plus 3x. Simplify the fraction first: the bracket x plus 2 times the bracket x minus 2 over the bracket x minus 2, which is x plus 2. Then add: x plus 2 plus 3x is 4x plus 2. Check at x equals 3: the fraction is 5 over 1, plus 9, giving 14; 4x plus 2 at 3 is 14.

Another: 2x plus 6, over x squared plus 3x. Top: 2 times the bracket x plus 3. Bottom: x times the bracket x plus 3. Cancel the bracket. Result: 2 over x. Learners who cancel the 6 with the 3 or the 2x with the x squared before factorising get nonsense, because none of those are factors of the whole expression.

The error museum, four exhibits. Exhibit one: x squared plus 8x plus 15 over x plus 3 simplified to x squared plus 5 by cancelling the 8x with the x and the 15 with the 3. Terms were cancelled, not factors. At x equals 2 the original is 7 and the exhibit gives 9. Exhibit two: x squared minus 9 over x minus 3 written as x minus 3. The factorisation is the bracket x plus 3 times the bracket x minus 3, and the surviving factor is x plus 3, not x minus 3. Exhibit three: the bracket x plus 1 over 4, divided by x squared plus x over 8, with the first fraction flipped instead of the second, giving x squared over 2 times the bracket x plus 1 squared, which is wrong. Only the divisor is flipped. Exhibit four: the bracket x plus 1 over 3 minus the bracket x minus 2 over 4 written as the bracket x plus 1 minus x minus 2 over 12, with the numerators not multiplied up and the minus not distributed.

Every exhibit falls to the same check: pick a value, substitute into the original and into the answer, compare. Choose a value that does not make any denominator zero and that keeps the arithmetic small; x equals 2 or x equals 3 is usually right. Layout for marks: factorised forms written out, cancelling shown, the LCD stated, the second numerator in a bracket, the final answer on its own line. The questions for this section are with you now: a fraction mixed with terms, the four exhibits, and choosing a value for the check.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same fraction rules again through a pizza that is cut into slices, a rule about what you are allowed to cross out, and the flip you already know from ordinary fractions — plain words, same rules.

## Subtopic: Cross Out Factors, Never Terms

Here is the whole lesson in a number. 15 over 5 is 3, because 15 is 3 times 5 and the 5s cross out. Now look at 10 plus 5, over 5. The answer is 3 as well, because 10 plus 5 is 15. But if you cross out the 5 on top with the 5 on the bottom you get 10, which is wrong. You can only cross out something that multiplies the WHOLE top and the WHOLE bottom. The 5 in 10 plus 5 is added, not multiplied, so it is not a factor and it cannot be crossed out.

That is why factorising comes first. x squared plus 8x plus 15, over x plus 3. Nothing can be crossed out yet, because the top is a sum. Factorise the top with last lesson's two-number game: the bracket x plus 3 times the bracket x plus 5. Now the top is a multiplication, and the bracket x plus 3 multiplies the whole top. Cross it out with the bottom. Answer: x plus 5. Try x equals 2: the original is 35 over 5, which is 7, and 2 plus 5 is 7.

6x squared plus 9x over 3x. Factorise the top: 3x times the bracket 2x plus 3. Cross out 3x. Answer: 2x plus 3.

A quick way to remember the rule: a fraction bar is a division sign, and you can divide a multiplication by cancelling one of its factors, but you cannot divide only one part of a sum and leave the rest alone.

The rhythm: factorise the top, factorise the bottom, cross out whole brackets or whole common factors that appear on both, write what is left. If the top is still a sum after factorising and nothing matches, nothing crosses out, and that is a fine answer too. Your questions on this section are ready: the 10 plus 5 over 5 trap, factorising before crossing out, and the check with a number.

## Subtopic: Flip the Second One

Multiplying fractions you already know: tops times tops, bottoms times bottoms. With letters, factorise everything first and cross out anything that appears on any top and any bottom, even across the two fractions. x squared minus 4 over 3x, times 6x squared over the bracket x plus 2. Factorise the first top: the bracket x plus 2 times the bracket x minus 2. The bracket x plus 2 appears on a top and a bottom: cross out. 3x and 6x squared: 3x goes into 6x squared twice, leaving 2x. Left over: 2x times the bracket x minus 2. Try x equals 3: the original is 5 over 9 times 54 over 5, which is 6; the answer gives 6 times 1, which is 6.

Dividing fractions you also already know: flip the second one and multiply. Half divided by a quarter is a half times four over one, which is 2. The bracket x plus 1 over 4, divided by x squared plus x over 8. Flip the second: 8 over x squared plus x. Factorise it: 8 over x times the bracket x plus 1. Multiply: the bracket x plus 1 crosses out, 4 into 8 leaves 2. Answer: 2 over x.

The rule to say out loud: flip the SECOND one, the one you are dividing by, and only after flipping do you factorise and cross out. Flipping the first one gives a different, wrong answer. Keep dividing by a half in your head: dividing by a half doubles things, so the half is what flips to become 2. The questions for this section: factorise then cross out across both fractions, flip only the second, and the half-divided-by-a-quarter check.

## Subtopic: Same Slices Before You Add

You cannot add thirds and quarters until the slices are the same size. A third is four twelfths; a quarter is three twelfths; together, seven twelfths. Algebraic fractions are no different. The bracket x plus 1 over 3, minus the bracket x minus 2 over 4. Twelfths again. The first fraction becomes 4 times the bracket x plus 1, over 12. The second becomes 3 times the bracket x minus 2, over 12. Now subtract the tops, and keep the second top in its bag because of the minus: 4x plus 4, minus the bag 3x minus 6. Open the bag with every sign flipped: minus 3x plus 6. Collect: x plus 10. Answer: the bracket x plus 10 over 12. Try x equals 2: the original is 1 minus 0, which is 1, and 12 over 12 is 1.

With letters on the bottom: 3 over 2x plus 5 over 3x. The smallest bottom that both 2x and 3x fit into is 6x. 3 over 2x becomes 9 over 6x; 5 over 3x becomes 10 over 6x. Add the tops: 19 over 6x. Bottoms stay as they are; only tops add.

The slip that costs the most: forgetting to multiply the tops up when you change the bottoms, or opening the bag with only the first sign flipped. Both are caught by trying a number. If the original and your answer disagree at x equals 2, go back to the bag.

Three habits for every fraction question: factorise before you cross out anything; flip only the second fraction when dividing; get the same bottom before adding, and keep the second top in a bag. Then try a number. The final questions of the lesson are with you now: same-size slices, tops change when bottoms change, and the bag around the second top.
