# Part 1 — Expert

Today an equation gets a second variable. y equals 2x plus 1 is not solved for one value of x; it describes a relationship, and for every x you choose there is a y that goes with it. Substituting a run of x values produces a table of ordered pairs, each written in the form x semicolon y, and those pairs are the points that next week's graphs are drawn from. This lesson is the bridge between the equations you have just solved and the Cartesian plane: substitution forwards fills the table, solving backwards finds a missing x, and reading the differences in the table tells you whether the rule is linear.

## Subtopic: Substituting Into an Equation in Two Variables

Take y equals 2x plus 1. Choose x equals negative 2, negative 1, 0, 1 and 2, which is the standard run for Grade 9. Substitute each with brackets, as the substitution lesson taught. x equals negative 2: 2 times the bracket negative 2, plus 1, is negative 4 plus 1, which is negative 3. x equals negative 1: negative 2 plus 1, negative 1. x equals 0: 1. x equals 1: 3. x equals 2: 5. The table has two rows, x across the top and y below, and reads negative 3, negative 1, 1, 3, 5.

Each column is an ordered pair. The pair is written with x first and y second, separated by a semicolon in South African notation: negative 2 semicolon negative 3, negative 1 semicolon negative 1, 0 semicolon 1, 1 semicolon 3, 2 semicolon 5. The order matters: 2 semicolon 5 says x is 2 and y is 5, and 5 semicolon 2 is a different point that does not satisfy this equation, because 2 times 5 plus 1 is 11, not 2.

Every ordered pair in the table is a solution of the equation. Check one: 1 semicolon 3 gives 2 times 1 plus 1, which is 3. True. An equation in two variables has infinitely many solutions, and the table shows five of them. In Term 1 you met this as an input-output machine with a rule; the table of ordered pairs is the same idea with the standard notation that graphs will use.

Why five values and why that run? Five columns are enough to show a pattern and few enough to compute in a test, and a run centred on zero shows what happens on both sides of the origin, which matters for the squared rules later in the lesson. If a question specifies different x values, use those; if it gives none, negative 2 to 2 is the convention, and a table with x equals 0 in it always gives the constant term of a linear rule directly.

Negative coefficients demand brackets. y equals 5 minus 3x at x equals negative 2: 5 minus 3 times the bracket negative 2 is 5 minus negative 6, which is 11. Without the bracket a learner writes 5 minus 6 and gets negative 1. Layout for marks: the substitution line showing the bracket, the arithmetic line, the value, and then the pair. The questions for this section are with you now: the standard run of x values, brackets around negatives, and the x-first order of a pair.

## Subtopic: Tables for Linear and Non-Linear Rules

y equals 3x minus 2 over the same run: at negative 2, negative 8; at negative 1, negative 5; at 0, negative 2; at 1, 1; at 2, 4. Look at the differences between consecutive y values: 3, 3, 3, 3. A constant difference in y for a constant step in x is the signature of a linear rule, and the difference equals the coefficient of x. Next week this will be called the gradient.

y equals x squared minus 1: at negative 2, 4 minus 1 is 3; at negative 1, 0; at 0, negative 1; at 1, 0; at 2, 3. The differences are negative 3, negative 1, 1, 3. Not constant, so not linear. The table is symmetrical about x equals 0, because squaring a negative gives the same as squaring the positive, and the smallest value is in the middle. That is the parabola shape the graphs lesson will draw.

y equals 24 over x, for x equals 1, 2, 3, 4, 6: 24, 12, 8, 6, 4. As x grows, y shrinks, and the product of each pair is always 24. This is the inverse relationship from the ratio lesson in Term 1, and x equals 0 is left out of the table because division by zero is undefined.

A table can also be built for a rule with fractions or decimals, and the brackets rule still applies. y equals one half x plus 3 at x equals negative 2 is one half times the bracket negative 2, plus 3, which is negative 1 plus 3, giving 2; at x equals 2 it is 4. The differences are 1 each time for a step of 2 in x, which is still constant, so the rule is still linear even though the y values do not go up in whole-number jumps of the coefficient.

The Term 1 pattern language applies directly: a constant difference means a linear rule, a constant ratio means a geometric pattern, and neither means something else. Reading the differences before drawing anything tells you what kind of graph to expect, and an examiner who asks "is the relationship linear" wants the differences shown as evidence. Your questions on this section: constant differences for linear rules, the symmetry of a squared rule, and why x equals 0 is excluded from 24 over x.

## Subtopic: Finding x When y Is Given

A table sometimes has a gap in the x row. y equals 3x minus 2, and y equals 10: what is x? Substitute y and solve the resulting one-variable equation with last lesson's inverses. 10 equals 3x minus 2. Add 2: 12 equals 3x. Divide by 3: x equals 4. The pair is 4 semicolon 10. Check: 3 times 4 minus 2 is 10.

y equals 2x plus 1 with y equals 15: 15 equals 2x plus 1, so 2x equals 14 and x equals 7. The pair 7 semicolon 15.

With a squared rule there may be two answers. y equals x squared minus 1 with y equals 8: 8 equals x squared minus 1, so x squared equals 9, and x equals 3 or x equals negative 3. Two pairs, 3 semicolon 8 and negative 3 semicolon 8, and both belong in the table, which is the symmetry again.

In context: a taxi fare is 20 plus 12k rand for k kilometres. The table for k equals 1 to 5 reads 32, 44, 56, 68, 80. If a passenger paid R92, substitute: 92 equals 20 plus 12k, so 12k equals 72 and k equals 6. The pair 6 semicolon 92 extends the table by one column and the pattern holds: 80 plus 12 is 92.

Backwards questions sometimes appear with a decimal or a fraction. y equals 2x plus 1 with y equals 6: 2x equals 5, so x equals 2,5. A non-integer x is a perfectly good answer and appears as a point between the grid lines when the graph is drawn. Do not round it to 3; 2 times 3 plus 1 is 7, not 6, and the check fails.

The two directions use the same equation. Given x, substitute and simplify to find y. Given y, substitute and solve to find x. Forwards is arithmetic; backwards is an equation. Marks are lost when learners substitute the given value into the wrong variable, for instance replacing x with 10 and reporting 28 as the answer to "find x when y is 10". Read which variable is given. The questions for this section are ready: substitute the given y and solve, two answers for a squared rule, and extending a context table.

## Subtopic: From Table to Rule and the Error Museum

The reverse question: a table is given and the rule is asked for. x reads 0, 1, 2, 3 and y reads 5, 8, 11, 14. The differences in y are 3, 3, 3, so the rule is linear with coefficient 3. At x equals 0, y is 5, so the constant is 5. Rule: y equals 3x plus 5. Verify with a column not used to build it: x equals 3 gives 9 plus 5, which is 14.

If the table does not include x equals 0, substitute a known pair to find the constant. x reads 2, 3, 4 and y reads 7, 10, 13. Difference 3, so y equals 3x plus something; at x equals 2, 6 plus something is 7, so the something is 1. Rule: y equals 3x plus 1. Check at x equals 4: 13.

Now the error museum, five exhibits. Exhibit one: substituting x equals negative 2 into 2x plus 1 without a bracket and writing 2 minus 2 plus 1, which is 1 instead of negative 3. Exhibit two: writing the pair as y first, 5 semicolon 2, when x is 2 and y is 5; the point lands in the wrong place on the plane. Exhibit three: finding x when y is 10 by substituting 10 for x. Exhibit four: assuming a rule is linear from two columns only; two points always have a constant difference, and the third column is the test. Exhibit five: in y equals x squared minus 1 with y equals 8, giving x equals 3 only and losing the negative root.

Each exhibit is caught by checking the completed pair in the original equation and by reading the question for which variable is given. Layout for marks: a table with x on top and y below, brackets shown in each substitution, pairs written x first, differences written beneath the table when linearity is asked, and the backwards equation solved line by line. The questions for this section are with you now: rule from a table with and without x equals 0, and the five exhibits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same tables again through a machine that turns inputs into outputs, running the machine backwards, and a pattern-spotting habit — plain words, same rules.

## Subtopic: The Machine Makes the Table

Picture a machine with a rule printed on it: double, then add 1. That is y equals 2x plus 1. Feed in a number, the machine does the rule, and a number comes out. Feed in the standard five: negative 2, negative 1, 0, 1, 2. Out come negative 3, negative 1, 1, 3, 5. Write the inputs along the top row of a table and the outputs underneath. Done. That table is the whole relationship, five snapshots of it.

Each column of the table is a pair, written in brackets with a semicolon: input first, output second. 2 semicolon 5 means in went 2, out came 5. Never swap them. 5 semicolon 2 would say in went 5 and out came 2, and the machine does not do that: 5 doubled plus 1 is 11.

Negatives into the machine: always put them in a bracket. Double negative 2 is 2 times the bracket negative 2, which is negative 4, then add 1, negative 3. Without the bracket people write 2 minus 2 plus 1 and get 1, which is a different column of a different table.

Why bother with a table at all? Because next week each column becomes a dot on a grid, and five dots are enough to see the shape of the whole relationship. The table is the list of dots before they are drawn.

Change the machine, change the table. Rule: three times, then take away 2. Inputs negative 2 to 2, outputs negative 8, negative 5, negative 2, 1, 4. Rule: square, then take away 1. Outputs 3, 0, negative 1, 0, 3. Same inputs every time, so the only thing that changes the outputs is the rule, and the only job is to do the rule carefully to each input. Your questions on this section are ready: inputs along the top, outputs underneath, input first in the pair, and brackets for negatives.

## Subtopic: Running the Machine Backwards

Sometimes you know what came out and need to know what went in. The machine is three times, then take away 2, and out came 10. Run it backwards with last lesson's undo buttons, in reverse order: undo the take away 2 by adding 2, giving 12; undo the three times by dividing by 3, giving 4. In went 4. Check forwards: 4 times 3 is 12, take away 2 is 10. The pair is 4 semicolon 10.

Machine double then add 1, out came 15. Undo the add 1: 14. Undo the double: 7. In went 7.

Squaring machines run backwards give two answers. Square then take away 1, out came 8. Undo the take away 1: 9. Undo the square: 3, but also negative 3, because negative 3 squared is 9 too. Two inputs give 8, so two pairs: 3 semicolon 8 and negative 3 semicolon 8.

A story machine: a taxi costs R20 to get in and R12 per kilometre, so the machine is twelve times, then add 20. Feed in 1 to 5 kilometres and the fares come out: 32, 44, 56, 68, 80. Someone paid R92. Backwards: take off the 20, leaving 72; divide by 12, giving 6. They travelled 6 km.

Backwards does not always give a whole number. Double then add 1, out came 6: undo the add 1 to get 5, undo the double to get 2,5. In went 2 and a half. That is fine; the machine takes any number, not only whole ones.

The trap: reading which number you were given. "Find x when y is 10" means 10 came OUT. If you feed 10 IN by mistake you get 28, and that is the wrong direction. Read the question, decide forwards or backwards, then go. The questions for this section: undo buttons in reverse to find the input, two inputs for a squaring machine, and forwards or backwards.

## Subtopic: Spot the Pattern, Write the Rule

Here is a table with the rule hidden: inputs 0, 1, 2, 3, outputs 5, 8, 11, 14. Look at how the outputs jump. 5 to 8 is 3, 8 to 11 is 3, 11 to 14 is 3. Same jump every time means the machine multiplies by that jump: times 3. Now what else does it do? At input 0, times 3 gives 0, but the output is 5, so the machine adds 5. Rule: times 3, add 5, or y equals 3x plus 5. Check with a column you did not use: input 3, times 3 is 9, add 5 is 14. Matches.

If input 0 is not in the table, use any column to find the add-on. Inputs 2, 3, 4, outputs 7, 10, 13. Jumps of 3, so times 3. Input 2 times 3 is 6, and the output is 7, so add 1. Rule: y equals 3x plus 1. Check at input 4: 12 plus 1 is 13.

Jumps that are all the same mean a straight-line rule, and next week that straight line will actually be drawn, with the jump called the gradient. Jumps that change, like 3, 0, negative 1, 0, 3 for the squaring machine, mean a curve. Look at the jumps before you decide anything, and look at at least three columns, because any two columns have a constant jump by themselves.

A story table works the same way. Fares 32, 44, 56, 68, 80 for 1 to 5 kilometres: the jumps are all 12, so the rule is times 12, and at 1 kilometre the fare is 32, so the add-on is 20. Fare equals 12k plus 20, which is exactly the taxi machine.

Four habits for every table question. Brackets around negative inputs. Input first in every pair. Decide forwards or backwards from which number is given. Check the jumps before calling a rule linear. Then check one pair in the original equation. The final questions of the lesson are with you now: same jumps mean times that number, the add-on from any column, and three columns before you decide.
