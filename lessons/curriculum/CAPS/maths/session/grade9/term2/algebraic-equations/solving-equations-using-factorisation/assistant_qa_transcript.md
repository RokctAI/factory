### subtopic_1 — A Product of Factors Equal to Zero
**Assistant:** Why can I split a product equal to zero into two equations but not a product equal to six?

**Tutor:** Because zero is the only number with the property that a product equal to it forces one factor to be it. The integer multiplication rules from Term 1 say that a positive times a positive, a negative times a negative, and a positive times a negative all give non-zero results, so two non-zero numbers can never multiply to zero. If the bracket x minus 2 times the bracket x plus 7 equals 0, at least one bracket is therefore zero, and each case gives a linear equation: x minus 2 equals 0, so x equals 2; or x plus 7 equals 0, so x equals negative 7. The word or is essential, because the equation is satisfied when either case holds, and x cannot take both values at once. A product equal to 6 carries no such information. The factors could be 1 and 6, 2 and 3, negative 2 and negative 3, 12 and a half, or infinitely many other pairs, so writing x minus 2 equals 6 chooses one pair out of infinitely many with no justification and gives a wrong answer. The correct treatment of the bracket x minus 2 times the bracket x plus 7 equals 6 is to expand, move the 6 across to make the right side zero, and factorise afresh. The habit to form is to look at the right-hand side before anything else: if it is zero and the left is a product, split; otherwise, rearrange first.

### subtopic_2 — Solving Trinomial Equations by Factorising
**Assistant:** Why do the solutions have the opposite sign to the numbers in the brackets?

**Tutor:** Because each solution comes from setting a factor equal to zero and solving that small linear equation, and solving x plus 3 equals 0 requires subtracting 3 from both sides, which gives x equals negative 3. The number in the bracket is added to x; the solution is the value that cancels it, so the sign flips. For x squared plus 8x plus 15 equals 0, the factorised form is the bracket x plus 3 times the bracket x plus 5 equals 0, and the solutions are x equals negative 3 or x equals negative 5. For x squared minus 3x minus 10 equals 0, the factors are the bracket x plus 2 and the bracket x minus 5, so the solutions are negative 2 and positive 5. Reading the bracket numbers directly as the solutions is one of the most common Grade 9 errors and it reverses every sign; the substitution check catches it at once, since 3 squared plus 8 times 3 plus 15 is 48, not zero. A perfect square trinomial such as x squared minus 8x plus 16 equals 0 factorises to the bracket x minus 4, all squared, and both factors give x equals 4, so there is a single repeated solution. Writing the two small equations explicitly, x plus 3 equals 0 and x plus 5 equals 0, earns the method mark and makes the sign flip visible rather than remembered.

### subtopic_3 — Rearranging to Zero First
**Assistant:** What exactly goes wrong when I divide both sides of x squared equals 5x by x?

**Tutor:** Division by a variable is only valid if that variable is not zero, so dividing by x silently assumes x is non-zero and removes the possibility that it is. In x squared equals 5x, the value x equals 0 satisfies the equation, because 0 squared is 0 and 5 times 0 is 0. Dividing both sides by x gives x equals 5, which is also a solution, but the solution x equals 0 has been discarded, and the examiner deducts for the missing root. The correct method is to subtract 5x from both sides so the right side is zero, giving x squared minus 5x equals 0, then take out the common factor x to get x times the bracket x minus 5 equals 0, and apply the zero-product rule: x equals 0 or x equals 5. The same principle governs every equation in this lesson: the zero-product rule works only when one side is zero, so x squared equals 49 becomes x squared minus 49 equals 0 and factorises as a difference of two squares to give 7 or negative 7; x squared plus 6x equals negative 8 becomes x squared plus 6x plus 8 equals 0 and gives negative 2 or negative 4; and x times the bracket x plus 3 equals 10 is expanded and rearranged to x squared plus 3x minus 10 equals 0 before it is factorised, giving negative 5 or 2. A numerical common factor, as in 2 times the bracket x plus 5 times the bracket x minus 5 equals 0, never produces a solution, because 2 is never zero.

### subtopic_4 — Equations in Context and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** When a context problem gives two solutions, how do I decide which to keep?

**Tutor:** Substitute each solution back into the original equation first, to confirm it is a genuine solution, and then test it against the meaning of the variable in the story. For the rectangle whose length is 3 cm more than its width and whose area is 40 square centimetres, the equation w times the bracket w plus 3 equals 40 rearranges to w squared plus 3w minus 40 equals 0 and factorises to the bracket w plus 8 times the bracket w minus 5 equals 0, giving w equals negative 8 or w equals 5. Both satisfy the equation, since 64 minus 24 minus 40 and 25 plus 15 minus 40 are both zero, but w is a width in centimetres and a negative length does not exist, so w equals negative 8 is rejected and the rejection is written with its reason: width cannot be negative. The answer is a width of 5 cm and a length of 8 cm, checked by 5 times 8 being 40. When the context does not forbid a value, both solutions are reported. For a number whose square plus itself is 12, n squared plus n minus 12 equals 0 gives n equals negative 4 or n equals 3, and both are legitimate numbers, so both stay. The marks in a context question are spread across setting up the equation, solving it, interpreting both solutions, and writing the final answer with units, so a silent omission of the negative root loses the interpretation mark even when the final number is right.
