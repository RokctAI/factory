# Part 1 — Expert

Today the factorising from two lessons ago solves equations that inverses cannot. An equation such as x squared plus 8x plus 15 equals 0 has x in two places with different powers, so there is no single chain of undo buttons that isolates it. Instead we use one fact about zero: if two quantities multiply to give zero, at least one of them is zero. Factorise the expression into a product, set each factor equal to zero, and solve the resulting linear equations. That is the whole method, it produces two solutions as a rule, and it is the Grade 9 entry point to quadratic equations in Grade 10.

## Subtopic: A Product of Factors Equal to Zero

Start with an equation already in factorised form: the bracket x minus 2 times the bracket x plus 7 equals 0. Two quantities multiply to give zero. The only way a product can be zero is if one of the factors is zero, because any non-zero number times any non-zero number is non-zero; that is the integer multiplication rule from Term 1. So either x minus 2 equals 0, giving x equals 2, or x plus 7 equals 0, giving x equals negative 7. Two solutions. Check each in the original: at x equals 2 the first bracket is 0 and the product is 0; at x equals negative 7 the second bracket is 0 and the product is 0.

3x times the bracket x minus 4 equals 0. Factors: 3x and x minus 4. 3x equals 0 gives x equals 0; x minus 4 equals 0 gives x equals 4. Do not forget the x equals 0 solution. 3 is also a factor, but 3 equals 0 is impossible, so it contributes nothing.

The zero is essential. The bracket x minus 2 times the bracket x plus 7 equals 6 cannot be split the same way: two numbers multiplying to 6 could be 1 and 6, 2 and 3, negative 1 and negative 6, or infinitely many other pairs, so knowing the product is 6 tells you nothing about either factor. Writing x minus 2 equals 6 is the error that exhibits this misunderstanding. Only a product equal to zero pins down a factor.

Write the solutions with the word or between them: x equals 2 or x equals negative 7. The word and would claim x is both at once, which is impossible. In Grade 10 these solutions will be called roots and the equation a quadratic, and the method you learn today is the one used first in every such question. The questions for this section are with you now: why zero and only zero splits a product, the x equals 0 solution, and writing or between solutions.

## Subtopic: Solving Trinomial Equations by Factorising

x squared plus 8x plus 15 equals 0. The left side is the anchor trinomial from the factorising lesson: the bracket x plus 3 times the bracket x plus 5. So the bracket x plus 3 times the bracket x plus 5 equals 0, and the zero-product rule gives x plus 3 equals 0 or x plus 5 equals 0, hence x equals negative 3 or x equals negative 5. Check x equals negative 3: 9 minus 24 plus 15 is 0. Check x equals negative 5: 25 minus 40 plus 15 is 0. Both work.

Notice the sign flip between the factors and the solutions. The factor x plus 3 gives the solution negative 3. Learners who read the solutions straight off the brackets as 3 and 5 have skipped the step x plus 3 equals 0, and the substitution check exposes it: 9 plus 24 plus 15 is 48, not 0. Write the small linear equation every time.

x squared minus 3x minus 10 equals 0. Factorise: product negative 10, sum negative 3, numbers 2 and negative 5, so the bracket x plus 2 times the bracket x minus 5 equals 0. Solutions: x equals negative 2 or x equals 5. Check x equals 5: 25 minus 15 minus 10 is 0.

x squared minus 8x plus 16 equals 0. Factorise: the bracket x minus 4, all squared, equals 0. Both factors are the same, so there is one solution, x equals 4, and it is sometimes called a repeated solution. A perfect square trinomial always gives a single answer.

Layout for marks: the equation with zero on the right, the factorised form, the two linear equations side by side or on consecutive lines, the two solutions joined by or, and a substitution check for at least one. The method marks are for the factorisation and for setting each factor to zero; the accuracy marks are for the solutions. Your questions on this section: the sign flip from factor to solution, the repeated solution, and the substitution check.

## Subtopic: Rearranging to Zero First

The zero-product rule only works when one side is zero, so any equation not already in that form is rearranged first. x squared equals 5x. The temptation is to divide both sides by x and write x equals 5. That loses a solution. Instead subtract 5x from both sides: x squared minus 5x equals 0. Factorise with a common factor: x times the bracket x minus 5 equals 0. Solutions: x equals 0 or x equals 5. Both check: 0 equals 0 and 25 equals 25. Dividing by x assumed x was not zero, and discarded exactly the case where it was.

x squared equals 49. Subtract 49: x squared minus 49 equals 0. Difference of two squares: the bracket x plus 7 times the bracket x minus 7 equals 0, so x equals negative 7 or x equals 7. This is the same answer inspection gave two lessons ago, now with a method behind it that shows both roots without having to remember them.

x squared plus 6x equals negative 8. Add 8 to both sides: x squared plus 6x plus 8 equals 0. Factorise: product 8, sum 6, numbers 2 and 4, so the bracket x plus 2 times the bracket x plus 4 equals 0. Solutions: x equals negative 2 or x equals negative 4. Check x equals negative 2: 4 minus 12 is negative 8.

x times the bracket x plus 3 equals 10. Expand: x squared plus 3x equals 10. Subtract 10: x squared plus 3x minus 10 equals 0. Factorise: product negative 10, sum 3, numbers 5 and negative 2, so the bracket x plus 5 times the bracket x minus 2 equals 0. Solutions: x equals negative 5 or x equals 2. Check x equals 2: 2 times 5 is 10. A product equal to 10 was useless until it became a product equal to 0.

2x squared minus 50 equals 0. Common factor first: 2 times the bracket x squared minus 25 equals 0, then 2 times the bracket x plus 5 times the bracket x minus 5 equals 0. The factor 2 cannot be zero, so x equals negative 5 or x equals 5. The sequence is always: zero on one side, factorise fully, each factor to zero, solve, check. The questions for this section are ready: why dividing by x loses a solution, moving everything to one side, and the common factor that is never zero.

## Subtopic: Equations in Context and the Error Museum

A rectangle has a length 3 cm more than its width and an area of 40 square centimetres. Let the width be w; the length is w plus 3; the area is w times the bracket w plus 3, which equals 40. Expand and rearrange: w squared plus 3w minus 40 equals 0. Factorise: product negative 40, sum 3, numbers 8 and negative 5, so the bracket w plus 8 times the bracket w minus 5 equals 0. Solutions: w equals negative 8 or w equals 5. A width cannot be negative, so w equals 5 cm and the length is 8 cm. Check: 5 times 8 is 40. The equation produced two solutions and the context rejected one; both steps must be written.

A number squared plus the number itself is 12. Let the number be n: n squared plus n equals 12, so n squared plus n minus 12 equals 0, the bracket n plus 4 times the bracket n minus 3 equals 0, and n is negative 4 or 3. Here both answers are valid: 16 minus 4 is 12 and 9 plus 3 is 12. Without a context that forbids one, both are reported.

The error museum, five exhibits. Exhibit one: x squared equals 5x divided by x to give x equals 5 alone; the solution x equals 0 is lost. Exhibit two: the bracket x minus 2 times the bracket x plus 7 equals 6 split into x minus 2 equals 6; only a product equal to zero can be split. Exhibit three: x squared plus 8x plus 15 equals 0 answered as x equals 3 or 5, reading the brackets without solving x plus 3 equals 0. Exhibit four: x squared equals 49 answered as x equals 7 only. Exhibit five: the rectangle answered as w equals 5 or negative 8 with no rejection of the negative width; the mark for interpreting the context is lost.

Each exhibit falls to substitution and to reading the context. A proposed solution that does not make the original equation true is not a solution, and a solution that makes the original equation true but describes an impossible width is rejected with a written reason. Layout for marks: zero on one side, factorised form, each factor set to zero, both solutions with or, the check, and the sentence that rejects or accepts each solution in context. The questions for this section are with you now: rejecting a solution for a reason, keeping both when there is no reason, and the five exhibits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same equation-solving again through the one trick zero can do, a rule about getting zero on one side first, and a habit of checking both answers against the story — plain words, same rules.

## Subtopic: Zero's One Trick

Here is something only zero can do. If I tell you two numbers multiply to make 12, you know nothing about either one: 3 and 4, 2 and 6, 1 and 12, negative 3 and negative 4, 24 and a half. But if I tell you two numbers multiply to make ZERO, you know something for certain: one of them is zero. There is no other way to get zero out of a multiplication. That is the trick, and it is the whole lesson.

So when an equation says the bracket x minus 2 times the bracket x plus 7 equals 0, it is two things multiplying to make zero. One of them must be zero. Either x minus 2 is 0, so x is 2, or x plus 7 is 0, so x is negative 7. Two answers, joined with the word or, because x cannot be both at once. Check them: put 2 in and the first bracket is zero, so the whole thing is zero. Put negative 7 in and the second bracket is zero.

3x times the bracket x minus 4 equals 0. Two things multiplying to zero: 3x and x minus 4. 3x is zero when x is zero. x minus 4 is zero when x is 4. Answers: 0 or 4. People forget the zero answer because it feels like nothing; it is an answer, and it is worth a mark.

Watch the trap: the bracket x minus 2 times the bracket x plus 7 equals 6. Not zero. Two things multiplying to 6 could be anything, so you cannot say x minus 2 is 6. Zero's trick only works for zero. Your questions on this section are ready: why only zero splits a multiplication, the answer that is zero, and the word or.

## Subtopic: Zero on One Side, Then the Two-Number Game

Most equations do not arrive in the shape two things multiplying to zero. You build that shape in two moves. Move one: get zero on one side by moving everything across. Move two: factorise the other side with the two-number game, so it becomes two brackets multiplying.

x squared plus 8x plus 15 equals 0. Zero is already there. Two-number game: multiply to 15, add to 8, so 3 and 5. The bracket x plus 3 times the bracket x plus 5 equals 0. Now zero's trick: x plus 3 is 0, so x is negative 3; or x plus 5 is 0, so x is negative 5. Notice the signs flipped: the bracket says plus 3, the answer is negative 3. Do not read the answers straight off the brackets. Write the little equation and solve it.

x squared equals 5x. Everyone wants to divide by x and say x is 5. Do not. Move the 5x across: x squared minus 5x equals 0. Pull out the shared x: x times the bracket x minus 5 equals 0. Zero's trick: x is 0, or x is 5. Two answers, and dividing by x would have thrown away the zero.

x squared plus 6x equals negative 8. Move the negative 8 across: x squared plus 6x plus 8 equals 0. Two-number game: multiply to 8, add to 6, so 2 and 4. The bracket x plus 2 times the bracket x plus 4 equals 0. x is negative 2 or negative 4. Check negative 2: 4 minus 12 is negative 8, matches.

x times the bracket x plus 3 equals 10. Looks like a product, but it equals 10, not zero, so open it up: x squared plus 3x equals 10. Move the 10: x squared plus 3x minus 10 equals 0. Two-number game: multiply to negative 10, add to 3, so 5 and negative 2. x is negative 5 or 2. Check 2: 2 times 5 is 10. The questions for this section: zero across first, never divide by x, and solving the little equations rather than reading the brackets.

## Subtopic: Check Both, Keep the Ones That Make Sense

Every factorised equation gives you two answers, and your job is not finished until you have checked both and decided whether both belong.

Check means put the answer back into the original equation. x squared minus 3x minus 10 equals 0 gives the bracket x plus 2 times the bracket x minus 5 equals 0, so x is negative 2 or 5. Put 5 in: 25 minus 15 minus 10 is 0. Put negative 2 in: 4 plus 6 minus 10 is 0. Both pass. Both stay.

Sometimes the two answers are the same. x squared minus 8x plus 16 equals 0 is the bracket x minus 4, all squared, equals 0. Both brackets say x is 4. One answer, and that is fine.

Sometimes the story throws one out. A rectangle is 3 cm longer than it is wide and has area 40. Width w, length w plus 3, so w times the bracket w plus 3 equals 40. Open it: w squared plus 3w equals 40. Zero across: w squared plus 3w minus 40 equals 0. Two-number game: multiply to negative 40, add to 3, so 8 and negative 5. w is negative 8 or 5. A width of negative 8 cm does not exist, so write that down and reject it: w is 5 cm, length 8 cm, and 5 times 8 is 40. The rejection sentence is worth a mark; silently dropping the negative is not the same thing.

And sometimes both answers are real. A number squared plus the number is 12: n squared plus n minus 12 equals 0, the bracket n plus 4 times the bracket n minus 3 equals 0, n is negative 4 or 3. Both work: 16 minus 4 is 12, 9 plus 3 is 12. No story forbids a negative number, so both stay.

Five moves for every one of these: zero on one side, factorise, each bracket to zero, solve the little equations, check both and decide. The final questions of the lesson are with you now: checking both answers, the repeated answer, and rejecting one with a written reason.
