### subtopic_1 — Solving by Inspection
**Assistant:** Why does x squared equals 49 have two solutions but x cubed equals 27 only one?

**Tutor:** Because squaring a negative number produces a positive result while cubing a negative number produces a negative result. Seven squared is 49, and negative 7 squared is negative 7 times negative 7, which is also 49 by the integer sign rules, so both 7 and negative 7 satisfy x squared equals 49 and both must be written. For x cubed equals 27, 3 cubed is 27, but negative 3 cubed is negative 3 times negative 3 times negative 3, which is negative 27, so negative 3 does not satisfy the equation and 3 is the only solution. The general rule is that an even power hides the sign of its base, so an equation with an even power and a positive right-hand side has two solutions, while an odd power keeps the sign, so an equation with an odd power has exactly one solution and that solution carries the sign of the right-hand side. x cubed equals negative 8 therefore has the single solution negative 2. Examiners deduct a mark whenever the negative root of a square is omitted, and the habit of writing plus or minus the root is one Grade 10 relies on heavily. Inspection is the right method for these because the squares and cubes are known from Term 1, but the check is still written: 7 squared is 49, negative 7 squared is 49.

### subtopic_2 — Additive and Multiplicative Inverses
**Assistant:** Why must the inverses be applied in reverse order, and what happens if they are not?

**Tutor:** Because the operations were applied to x in a particular order, and undoing them is like unwrapping a parcel: the last layer added is the first layer removed. In 3x minus 5 equals 16, x was first multiplied by 3 and then 5 was subtracted; the subtraction is the outer layer, so it is undone first by adding 5 to both sides, giving 3x equals 21, and the multiplication is undone last by dividing both sides by 3, giving x equals 7. Reversing that order is not wrong mathematically, but it is costly. Dividing both sides by 3 first gives x minus five thirds equals sixteen thirds, and then adding five thirds gives twenty-one thirds, which is 7, so the answer is the same, but the working runs through fractions that invite arithmetic slips and that markers find hard to follow. The reverse-order rule keeps every step in whole numbers for as long as possible. The other half of the method is applying each inverse to both sides, because an equation asserts that two quantities are equal and only an identical change to both keeps them equal. The additive inverse of subtracting 5 is adding 5, and the multiplicative inverse of multiplying by 3 is dividing by 3; naming the inverse used on each line is what earns the method marks.

### subtopic_3 — Equations With Brackets, Fractions and Exponents
**Assistant:** When I clear fractions by multiplying by the LCD, why must every term be multiplied?

**Tutor:** Because multiplying both sides of an equation by a number means multiplying the whole of each side, and each side is a sum of terms, so every term in it is multiplied by the distributive law. In x over 2 minus x over 3 equals 2, the lowest common denominator of 2 and 3 is 6. Multiplying the left side by 6 multiplies both fraction terms: 6 times x over 2 is 3x and 6 times x over 3 is 2x. Multiplying the right side by 6 turns the 2 into 12. The equation becomes 3x minus 2x equals 12, so x equals 12, and the check confirms it: 6 minus 4 is 2. The common error is to multiply only the fractions, leaving the right side as 2, which gives x equals 2 and fails the check immediately, because 1 minus two thirds is a third, not 2. The same principle governs brackets and exponents. A bracket is expanded by multiplying every term inside it, so 2 times the bracket x minus 3 is 2x minus 6 and not 2x minus 3. For an exponent equation such as 3 to the power of the bracket x plus 1 equals 81, both sides are written with base 3, since 81 is 3 to the fourth, and the whole exponent x plus 1 is set equal to 4, giving x equals 3; forgetting the plus 1 and writing x equals 4 is the equivalent slip.

### subtopic_4 — Setting Up Equations From Words and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** In a word problem, how do I avoid answering with the wrong quantity?

**Tutor:** By making the first step a written sentence that names the letter and says exactly what it stands for, and making the last step a return to the question to see which quantity was asked for. In the age problem, writing let s be the sister's age in years fixes what s means. The equation s plus 2s plus 3 equals 27 solves to s equals 8, which is the sister's age. If the question asks for Sipho's age, the answer is 2 times 8 plus 3, which is 19, and the final line should read Sipho is 19 years old, with the unit. A learner who writes 8 as the answer has solved the equation correctly and answered a different question. The same discipline applies to the rectangle: w equals 5 is the width, and if the length is asked for, it is w plus 3, which is 8 cm. A check against the story rather than against the equation catches most of these slips, because the story says the ages add to 27, and 8 plus 19 is 27, while 8 plus 8 is not. The other exhibits in the error museum are caught by substituting the solution into the original equation: subtracting 5 instead of adding it, dividing by 2 instead of negative 2, distributing to only the first term in a bracket, and dropping the negative root of a square all produce values that make the original equation false.
