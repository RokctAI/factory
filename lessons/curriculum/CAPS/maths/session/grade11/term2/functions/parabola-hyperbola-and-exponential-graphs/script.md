# Part 1 — Expert

Grade ten introduced the parabola, the hyperbola and the exponential graph in their simplest positions. Grade eleven hands each of them a full set of controls: the number a that stretches and flips, the number p that slides left and right, and the number q that slides up and down. This session masters those controls across all three families, then closes with the average gradient — the first honest step towards the calculus of grade twelve.

## Subtopic: The Parabola in Turning-Point Form

The grade eleven parabola is written y equals a, times x plus p, all squared, plus q — turning-point form, because the turning point can be read straight off it. The bracket is smallest when it equals zero, at x equals minus p, and there y equals q. So the turning point sits at minus p, q, and the axis of symmetry is the vertical line x equals minus p.

The three controls separate cleanly. The a outside decides shape and width: positive a opens upwards with a minimum, negative a opens downwards with a maximum, and a bigger size of a makes a narrower graph. The p inside the bracket slides the graph horizontally — and note the direction carefully: x plus one slides left by one, x minus one slides right by one, opposite to the sign you see. The q outside slides the graph vertically, exactly as written.

Work a full example: y equals two, times x minus one, all squared, minus eight. Turning point: one, minus eight — a minimum, since a is positive two. Axis of symmetry: x equals one. The y-intercept comes from x equals zero: two times one, minus eight, is minus six. The x-intercepts come from y equals zero: two times the bracket squared equals eight, the bracket squared equals four, so x minus one is two or minus two, giving x equals three or x equals minus one. Notice the symmetry check: three and minus one average to one, the axis — intercepts always straddle the axis equally, and that check catches sign errors for free.

Domain and range complete the reading. The domain is all real x, always, for every parabola. The range starts at the turning point: y greater than or equal to minus eight here, because the minimum is minus eight and the arms rise forever.

Pause here — the questions for this section are with you now. They read turning points from the form, slide graphs in both directions, and one exploits the symmetry check.

## Subtopic: The Hyperbola and Its Asymptotes

The hyperbola is y equals a over x plus p, plus q, and its personality comes from what it cannot do. The denominator cannot be zero, so x never equals minus p: that missing x-value is a vertical asymptote, a wall the graph approaches but never touches. And the fraction a over something can never itself be zero, so y never equals q: a horizontal asymptote. The two asymptotes cross at minus p, q — the centre of the hyperbola — and the two branches live in opposite corners of that cross.

Read an example: y equals three over x minus two, plus one. Vertical asymptote: x equals two. Horizontal asymptote: y equals one. With a positive, the branches occupy the top-right and bottom-left corners of the cross; a negative a would flip them into the other two corners.

Intercepts are ordinary substitution. The y-intercept, at x equals zero: three over minus two, plus one, which is minus one comma five plus one — minus zero comma five. The x-intercept, at y equals zero: three over x minus two equals minus one, so x minus two equals minus three, giving x equals minus one. Both intercepts sit on the branch in the bottom-left corner, and a quick sketch of the cross with two intercepts pinned down is a complete, mark-earning graph.

Domain and range are the asymptotes restated: x is any real number except two, and y is any real number except one. Writing them without the exceptions is the standard dropped mark of this section.

One more reading skill: the axes of symmetry of the hyperbola are the two diagonal lines through the centre with gradients one and minus one — for our example, y equals x minus one, and y equals minus x plus three. Substitute the centre, two, one, into both to confirm each passes through it.

Stop for this section's questions now — asymptotes first, corners second, intercepts third, and never hand in a domain without its exception.

## Subtopic: The Exponential Graph Raised and Shifted

The exponential is y equals a times b to the power x plus p, plus q, with b positive and not one. Its skeleton: for b greater than one the graph climbs ever faster to the right; for b between zero and one it decays. The power b to anything is always strictly positive, so a times that power never reaches zero — meaning y never reaches q. The horizontal asymptote y equals q is the exponential's floor when a is positive, or its ceiling when a is negative. There is no vertical asymptote: the domain is all real x, always.

The controls behave as before: p slides horizontally, opposite to its sign; q slides vertically; a stretches, and its sign decides whether the graph lives above or below the asymptote.

Work the example: y equals two times three to the power x plus one, minus six. Asymptote: y equals minus six. Since a is positive two and the base three exceeds one, the graph climbs, hugging the asymptote on the far left and rising steeply to the right. The y-intercept, at x equals zero: three to the power one is three, times two is six, minus six — zero. So the graph passes through the origin. The x-intercept, at y equals zero: two times three to the power x plus one equals six, so three to the power x plus one equals three, and matching exponents on the common base gives x plus one equals one, x equals zero. Both intercepts are the same point, the origin — a coincidence built into the example, and a reminder that both intercept calculations run through the same equation-solving from term one.

The range: y strictly greater than minus six. Strictly — the graph never lands on its asymptote, so the inequality carries no equals sign. Compare that with the parabola's range, which does include its turning point: the parabola touches its extreme value, the exponential only approaches its floor. Examiners test exactly that distinction.

The questions for this section arrive now — find the asymptote from q, solve intercepts with common bases, and mind the strict inequality in the range.

## Subtopic: Average Gradient and the Slope of a Curve

A straight line has one gradient everywhere. A curve does not — it steepens and flattens as you move — so grade eleven asks a humbler question: what is the average gradient between two points on a curve? The answer: the gradient of the straight line joining them, change in y over change in x, exactly as in analytical geometry.

Compute one on the parabola y equals x squared. Between x equals one, where y is one, and x equals three, where y is nine: the average gradient is nine minus one, over three minus one — eight over two, four. Geometrically, the line through those two points on the curve has gradient four; the curve itself starts shallower than four at the left point and finishes steeper at the right, and the average smooths that journey into one number.

Now the idea that opens the door to grade twelve. Fix the left point at x equals one and drag the right point closer. Between one and two: four minus one over one — three. Between one and one comma five: two comma two five minus one, over zero comma five — two comma five. Between one and one comma one: one comma two one minus one, over zero comma one — two comma one. The averages are marching towards two. The gradient of the curve at the point x equals one — the steepness of the curve itself, right there — is the number these averages approach: two. Grade twelve will name this limit the derivative; grade eleven only asks you to feel the approach, and occasionally to compute a table exactly like this one.

Two exam disciplines. First, average gradient needs y-values you compute from the function — substitute carefully before subtracting. Second, do not confuse the average gradient between two points with the gradient at a point: the first is a straight-line calculation you can always do; the second is a limit, and questions signal it with phrases like the gradient of the curve at.

The final questions of this part are with you now — two substitutions, one subtraction, one division, and watch the averages close in on the true steepness.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same three graphs with their controls, seen as a tent on a field, fences you may never touch, and tollgates on the highway — same rules, same answers.

## Subtopic: Sliding the Tent

Think of the basic parabola as a tent pitched with its lowest point at the town square — the origin. The equation's three controls are three instructions to the crew. The q is a crane: plus q lifts the whole tent q metres up, minus q lowers it, shape untouched. The p is a horizontal push — with one trap: the push goes opposite to the sign. X plus one in the bracket pushes the tent one to the left; x minus one pushes it one to the right. Read the bracket as the x-value that makes it zero — that is where the tent's lowest point now stands — and the trap never catches you.

The a is the fabric tension. A bigger a pulls the walls steep and narrow; a small fraction slackens them wide and shallow. A negative a flips the tent upside down entirely — the lowest point becomes a highest point, a minimum becomes a maximum.

So y equals two, times x minus one, squared, minus eight, reads as a work order: push the tent one to the right, drop it eight, tighten the fabric by a factor of two. The lowest point lands at one, minus eight, exactly the turning point the formula promises. Every reading question on the parabola is just running the work order in your head.

And the beautiful bonus of any tent: it is symmetric. The two places where the tent's walls cross the ground — the x-intercepts, minus one and three in our example — sit at equal distances either side of the centre pole at x equals one. If your two intercepts do not average to the pole, one of them is wrong. That single check audits half the parabola questions in the paper.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on this section come to you now. Read each equation as a work order: push, lift, tension.

## Subtopic: The Fences You Never Touch

The hyperbola and the exponential each come with fencing — lines the graph approaches forever but never, ever touches. Those are the asymptotes, and finding them is always the first move.

The hyperbola has two fences making a cross. The vertical fence stands where the denominator would divide by zero — at x equals two for three over x minus two, plus one. No graph can exist on that line, because no answer exists there. The horizontal fence lies at the height the fraction can never produce: the fraction part is never exactly zero, so y is never exactly the plus one at the end. The two fences cross at the centre, and the two branches of the hyperbola curl into opposite corners of the cross, like two dogs in the far corners of a yard. Positive a: top-right and bottom-left. Negative a: the other pair.

The exponential has just one fence, a floor or a ceiling at the height q. Three to any power is always some positive amount — it can be tiny, a fraction of a fraction, but never zero and never negative. So two times three to a power sits strictly above zero, and the whole graph of our example sits strictly above its floor of minus six, sliding along it to the left, soaring away to the right.

Why care so much about fences? Because domain and range are just fences written as sentences. The hyperbola: x is anything except the vertical fence, y is anything except the horizontal one. The exponential: x is anything at all, y is strictly beyond the floor — strictly, greater-than with no equals, because approaching is not arriving. The tent, by contrast, genuinely touches its lowest point, so the parabola's range does carry the equals sign. Touch versus approach — that one distinction is a recurring mark in Paper 1.

Your questions for this part are up now. Fences first, corners second, and keep approach and touch strictly apart.

## Subtopic: Average Speed Between Two Tollgates

A car on the highway passes one tollgate at nine in the morning and another, one hundred and eighty kilometres on, at eleven. Average speed: distance over time, ninety kilometres per hour. Nobody claims the car did exactly ninety the whole way — it slowed at the toll, overtook a truck, sped on the open stretch. The average is one honest number summarising a varying journey.

Average gradient is precisely that idea for a curve. Between two points on a graph, it is the change in height over the change in x — the gradient of the straight line joining the two points, as if you drew a taut string between them. On y equals x squared, from x equals one to x equals three, the heights are one and nine: string gradient eight over two, four. The curve is lazier than four at the start and fiercer than four at the end; four is the honest summary.

Now bring the tollgates closer. Keep the first at x equals one and move the second to two, then one comma five, then one comma one. The string gradients read three, then two comma five, then two comma one. See them marching: three, two and a half, two comma one... they are closing in on two. Bring the second gate close enough and the string gradient sits as near to two as you please. That number two is the speedometer reading at the instant of x equals one — the curve's own steepness at a single point, revealed as the limit of averages between ever-closer gates. Grade twelve builds all of calculus on this one move; grade eleven just wants you to watch it happen and compute the table.

Keep the two questions separate in your mind the way the road does. Average speed between two gates: always computable, two substitutions and a division. Speedometer at an instant: a limit, the destination of shrinking averages. Exam phrasing tells you which is wanted — between the points means the string; at the point means the speedometer.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: substitute both heights before subtracting, divide by the gap in x, and when the gates creep together, name the number the averages are approaching.
