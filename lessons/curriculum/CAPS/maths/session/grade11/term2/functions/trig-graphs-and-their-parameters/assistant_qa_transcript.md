**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! First, the one I heard four times: how are we supposed to know all the anchor points without a calculator?

**Tutor:** You already know them — they are the special angles from term one wearing graph clothing, and there are only five per wave that matter. For sine on one period: zero at zero degrees, maximum one at ninety, zero at one eighty, minimum minus one at two seventy, zero again at three sixty. For cosine, the same five heights starting from the peak: one, zero, minus one, zero, one, at the same five angles. Notice you are not memorising coordinates; you are remembering one sentence per graph — sine starts in the middle climbing, cosine starts at the top — and the five quarter-marks of the period follow. For tangent, three anchors per period carry everything: zero at zero, one at forty-five, and the wall at ninety. Every transformed graph is these anchors pushed through the controls: multiply the heights by a, add q to them, divide the angle positions by k, slide them by the shift. So the sketching skill is not artistic and not about plotting twenty points — it is five anchor points, transformed correctly, joined smoothly. Practise drawing each mother graph freehand in under thirty seconds, anchors labelled. Once your hand owns the three skeletons, every exam sketch is a two-minute job.

***

**Bianca:** Next: when an equation has both a k and a p, like sine of two x minus sixty, people get completely different sketches. What goes wrong?

**Tutor:** What goes wrong is the order of reading, because with both controls inside the bracket, the shift is no longer the number you see — and CAPS knows it, which is why the syllabus limits sketches to two parameters at a time. But the question deserves a full answer, since interpretation questions can still show the form. The safe method is the same bracket-zero habit, applied carefully. Take sine of two x minus sixty. Ask: where does the inside equal zero? Two x minus sixty is zero at x equals thirty. So the wave behaves at thirty the way plain sine behaves at zero — the true shift is thirty degrees right, not sixty. The error is reading the visible sixty as the shift; the k of two has compressed the shift along with everything else. If you prefer a rule: factorise the inside first. Sine of two x minus sixty is sine of two, times x minus thirty — and in factorised form the shift reads correctly as thirty, with the k standing cleanly outside. So: factorise the bracket, read k as the speed, read the factored shift as the start, and then sketch in the usual order — shape, speed, shift, stretch. Your examination sketches will pair a with p, or a with k, where this trap cannot arise; but the bracket-zero habit answers even the combined form, which is exactly why it is the habit to build.

***

**Bianca:** This one comes from the sketch questions: examiners keep asking for the range of something like two sine x plus one. Why do they care so much about range here?

**Tutor:** Because the range is where all three of this lesson's ideas are audited in one line — and because it is the wave property that survives into real applications. Computing it forces the right mental model. The bare sine swings from minus one to one. The a of two scales that swing: minus two to two. The q of one lifts it: minus one to three. So the range is minus one to three, written with square brackets because a wave genuinely touches its peak and its floor — unlike the exponential from the previous lesson, which only approaches its asymptote. A learner who writes minus two to two forgot the lift; one who writes zero to three added instead of shifting; one who writes strict inequalities has confused touching with approaching. One line, three misconceptions tested — an examiner's dream. There is also a forward-looking reason: in applications, the range is the physical statement — the highest and lowest tide, the loudest and softest sound, the maximum and minimum temperature over a day. The pairing to memorise: range from q minus the size of a, to q plus the size of a — middle line minus and plus the amplitude — and always square brackets for sine and cosine waves.

***

**Bianca:** Last one: is the k inside the bracket the same idea as the p and q shifts, or something genuinely new?

**Tutor:** Genuinely new, and seeing why deepens the whole controls picture. The p and q controls are translations — they pick the graph up and set it down elsewhere, unchanged in shape. Every point moves by the same amount, and distances between features are preserved: the period of sine of x minus thirty is still three sixty, and the amplitude of sine x plus one is still one. The a and k controls are scalings — they stretch or compress the graph, changing its proportions. The a scales vertically, multiplying every height; the k scales horizontally, dividing every width, which is why the period becomes three sixty over k. The complete grammar is a two-by-two table: vertical translation q, horizontal translation p, vertical scaling a, horizontal scaling k. Grade ten gave you three of the four cells; grade eleven completes the table with k. And the same grammar governs every function family you know — the parabola, the hyperbola and the exponential all read a, p and q identically — trig graphs simply add the fourth cell, because a wave has a width worth scaling. Master the table once and you have mastered the transformations for every graph in the FET phase, including the ones grade twelve will add.
