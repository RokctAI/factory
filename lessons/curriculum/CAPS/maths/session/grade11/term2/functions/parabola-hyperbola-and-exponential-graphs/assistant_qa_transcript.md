**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! The big one first: why does the p work backwards? X plus one moves the graph LEFT, and it trips everyone.

**Tutor:** It feels backwards because you are reading the sign as an instruction to the graph, when it is really a question about the input. The bracket x plus one asks: what must x be for the machine inside to behave the way plain x squared behaved at zero? Answer: x must be minus one, because minus one plus one is zero. So the special moment of the graph — the tent pole, the turning point — happens at x equals minus one, which is one step to the left. In other words, the graph is not being pushed; the schedule of inputs is being shifted, and to get the old output early you need a smaller x. Here is the reading habit that makes the trap harmless: never interpret the sign at all. Instead, set the bracket equal to zero and solve — that x-value is where the graph's centre now lives. X minus one in the bracket: bracket zero at x equals one, so the graph sits one to the right. X plus three: bracket zero at minus three, so three to the left. Ten seconds, no memory rule, no direction error. The same habit reads the hyperbola's vertical asymptote — denominator zero — and the exponential's key input. One habit, three graph families.

**Bianca:** Next: how do we sketch a full hyperbola quickly in an exam? Some spend ten minutes plotting points and still lose marks.

**Tutor:** Point-plotting is the slow road, and the fast road has exactly four stops. Stop one: draw the fences. Vertical asymptote where the denominator dies — x equals two in our example — and horizontal asymptote at the tail value, y equals one. Draw them as dotted lines, and label them, because the labels carry marks. Stop two: choose the corners. Look at the sign of a: positive a puts the branches in the top-right and bottom-left corners of the cross; negative a uses the other pair. Stop three: pin the intercepts. Substitute x equals zero for the y-intercept, minus zero comma five, and y equals zero for the x-intercept, minus one — both computed in two lines each. Notice both landed in the bottom-left corner, which confirms the corner choice for that branch. Stop four: sweep the curves — each branch hugging both its fences, bending through any pinned point. That is a complete sketch: two dotted asymptotes, two labelled intercepts, two correctly placed branches. Four stops, under three minutes. And the marking reality: memos allocate marks to the asymptotes, the intercepts and the shape — not to the accuracy of individual plotted points — so the fast road is also the full-marks road.

**Bianca:** This one is subtle: why does the parabola's range use greater-than-or-equal-to, but the exponential's range is strictly greater-than? It looks inconsistent.

**Tutor:** It looks inconsistent but it is reporting an honest geometric difference between touching and approaching. The parabola physically reaches its minimum: at x equals one, our graph sits exactly at minus eight — there is a real point of the graph at that height, so the range must include it, and the equals sign says so. The exponential never reaches its floor. The graph of two times three to the power x plus one, minus six, gets closer and closer to minus six as x runs left — at large negative x the power is a millionth, then a billionth — but two times a positive number is still positive, so the total is always some hair above minus six. No point of the graph ever has y equal to minus six, so the range must exclude it: strictly greater than. The hyperbola plays the same game on both axes, which is why its domain and range both carry exceptions rather than inequalities. When you write a range, silently ask: does the graph own a point at this boundary value, or does it only lean towards it? Own it, include it; lean, exclude it. Examiners test the distinction precisely because it shows whether you see the graph or merely memorise formats.

**Bianca:** Last one: the average gradient table felt like busywork to some — the answers just crept towards two. What is the point of it?

**Tutor:** That creeping IS the point, and it is the most important thing in the lesson for your future self, because the table is grade twelve calculus happening in slow motion. Here is the genuine problem it solves. A curve's steepness changes at every point, so asking for the gradient of the curve at x equals one seems impossible — gradient needs two points, and you only have one. The table is the escape route: take a second point nearby, compute the honest two-point average, then drag the second point closer and watch the averages settle. Three, two comma five, two comma one — the numbers are converging on two, and no matter how much closer you drag the point, they keep homing on two. So we declare: the gradient of this curve at x equals one is two, defined as the number the averages approach. Next year that number gets a name — the derivative — and a shortcut for computing it without tables, and on it hang maximum and minimum problems, rates of change, and a third of Paper 1. Grade eleven plants the concept honestly so that grade twelve's shortcut means something. If you remember one sentence from today, keep this one: the steepness of a curve at a point is the limit of average gradients over shrinking intervals. Everything in calculus is that sentence, decorated.
