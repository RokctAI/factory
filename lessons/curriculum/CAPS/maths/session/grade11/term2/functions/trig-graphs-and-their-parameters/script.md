# Part 1 — Expert

The sine, cosine and tangent of term one were numbers attached to angles; this session turns them into graphs — waves that repeat forever — and then hands you the same controls you used on the parabola and the exponential: a stretch, a shift up, a shift sideways, and a brand-new control that squeezes the wave itself. The trig graphs carry heavy marks in Paper 1 and Paper 2 alike, and they are read, sketched and interrogated with a small fixed toolkit.

## Subtopic: The Mother Graphs — Sine, Cosine and Tangent

Build sine point by point on the interval from minus three sixty to three sixty degrees. At zero degrees, sine is zero. Rising to one at ninety, back through zero at one eighty, down to minus one at two seventy, and home to zero at three sixty. Join the points and the shape is a smooth wave. Its amplitude — the distance from the middle line to a peak — is one. Its period — the width of one complete wave — is three hundred and sixty degrees, after which it repeats identically, forwards and backwards, forever.

Cosine is the same wave with a different starting posture: it begins at its maximum. One at zero degrees, zero at ninety, minus one at one eighty, zero at two seventy, one at three sixty. Same amplitude, one; same period, three sixty. Lay the two graphs over each other and cosine is sine slid ninety degrees left — a fact that will become an exam question when horizontal shifts arrive.

Tangent is the rebel. At zero it is zero, at forty-five it is one, and as the angle approaches ninety, tangent explodes — recall the term one reason: cosine underneath goes to zero. The graph shoots up towards a vertical asymptote at ninety degrees, reappears from far below just past ninety, climbs through zero at one eighty, and repeats. Its period is one hundred and eighty degrees, not three sixty, and it has no amplitude — it takes every value from minus infinity to plus infinity in each branch. Asymptotes stand at ninety plus every multiple of one eighty: minus two seventy, minus ninety, ninety, two seventy on our interval.

For sketching, memorise each graph's five anchor points per period — the zeros, maxima and minima for sine and cosine, and the zero, the one at forty-five, and the asymptotes for tangent. Anchors first, smooth curve after: that is the whole craft.

Pause here — the questions for this section are with you now. They test the anchor points, the periods, and why tangent alone owns asymptotes.

## Subtopic: Amplitude and Vertical Shift — the Work of a and q

The equation y equals a sine x plus q carries two controls you already know from the other functions. The a multiplies every height: amplitude becomes the size of a. The q lifts the whole wave: the middle line moves from the x-axis up to the height q.

Read y equals two sine x plus one as a work order. Tension first: every height doubles, so the bare wave swings between two and minus two. Crane second: lift everything one unit. The wave now swings between three and minus one, around a middle line at height one. Maximum three, reached where sine peaks, at ninety degrees; minimum minus one, at two seventy. Range: from minus one to three, written with square brackets in your workbook because both extremes are genuinely reached — waves touch their peaks, like the parabola and unlike the exponential.

Two formulas fall out, worth keeping as reflexes. Amplitude equals the size of a — half the distance between maximum and minimum. Middle line equals q — the average of maximum and minimum. Examiners run this machine backwards constantly: from a sketched wave with maximum three and minimum minus one, the middle line is the average, one, so q is one; the amplitude is three minus one over... careful — half of the full swing: three minus minus one is four, half is two, so a is two. Reading a and q off a picture is the same two calculations every time.

A negative a flips the wave upside down: y equals minus sine x starts at zero but dives first instead of climbing. The flip changes where maxima happen — a question favourite — but never changes amplitude, which is a size and therefore positive.

Stop for this section's questions now — half the swing for amplitude, the average height for the middle line, and mind the first move of a flipped wave.

## Subtopic: Changing the Period — the Effect of k

The new control lives inside, against the angle: y equals sine of k x. It does not change heights at all — it changes how fast the wave completes a cycle. Sine of two x finishes a full wave when two x reaches three sixty, that is, when x reaches one eighty: the period halves. Sine of half x needs x to reach seven hundred and twenty degrees for a full cycle: the period doubles. The rule: new period equals the old period divided by the size of k — three sixty over k for sine and cosine, one eighty over k for tangent.

Work the standard trio. Y equals cosine two x: period three sixty over two, one hundred and eighty degrees — two full waves fit where one used to. Y equals sine of half x: period seven twenty — a lazy half-wave on the standard interval. Y equals tangent two x: period one eighty over two, ninety degrees — and the asymptotes compress with everything else, landing at forty-five plus multiples of ninety. Asymptotes obey the squeeze exactly like anchor points.

Sketching with k is anchor arithmetic. Take the mother anchors and divide every x-coordinate by k. Cosine two x has its maximum at zero, first zero at forty-five, minimum at ninety, zero at one thirty-five, maximum at one eighty — the cosine skeleton at half spacing. Plot the compressed anchors, then curve smoothly.

One warning, the most profitable in the topic: k changes the period only. Amplitude untouched, middle line untouched. When a question shows a wave with maximum one and two full cycles in three sixty degrees, the equation is sine two x or cosine two x with a equal to one — resist the reflex that a faster wave must also be a taller one. The controls are independent, and reading them independently is precisely what the marks reward.

The questions for this section arrive now — divide the period by k, compress the anchors, and leave the heights alone.

## Subtopic: Horizontal Shifts and Combined Sketches

The final control is the horizontal shift: y equals sine of x plus p. As with every bracket in this course, the visible sign points the wrong way: x minus thirty shifts the wave thirty degrees right, x plus forty-five shifts it forty-five left. The bracket-zero habit settles it: sine of x minus thirty behaves at thirty the way plain sine behaves at zero — so the wave now starts its climb at thirty degrees.

Sketch y equals sine of x minus thirty on zero to three sixty. Slide every anchor thirty right: zero-crossing climbing at thirty, maximum at one twenty, falling zero at two ten, minimum at three hundred. The piece pushed past three sixty re-enters on the left if the interval demands it — the wave has no beginning or end, only the window we draw.

Now the co-function fact from term one becomes visible geometry: cosine x equals sine of x plus ninety. The sine wave slid ninety left is exactly cosine — the two mother graphs are one wave in two positions, and examiners ask for exactly that translation in words: cosine is sine shifted ninety degrees to the left.

CAPS caps the machinery at two parameters at a time, so the summit sketch looks like y equals two cosine of x minus sixty. Run the work order in the reliable sequence: shape first — cosine skeleton; shift second — every anchor sixty right: maximum at sixty, zero at one fifty, minimum at two forty, zero at three thirty, maximum again at four twenty, which sits beyond the window; stretch third — double all heights. Final wave: peaks at two, dips to minus two, maximum at sixty, minimum at two forty. Intercept check by calculation: at x equals zero, two cosine of minus sixty is two times a half — one. The sketch must cross the y-axis at one, and it does. Anchors, shift, stretch, check — the four-step order that never fails.

The final questions of this part are with you now — bracket zero locates the shift, and the four-step order turns any two-control equation into a clean sketch.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same waves from the clinic and the taxi rank — a heartbeat monitor, a fast-forward button, and a song started late — same controls, same sketches.

## Subtopic: The Heartbeat on the Screen

Sit in a clinic and watch the heart monitor: a spike, a dip, and again, and again — the same shape sliding past forever. That is what a trigonometric graph is: a repeating wave. Two numbers describe any such wave completely. How TALL it swings — the amplitude, measured from the calm middle line to a peak, never from bottom to top. And how LONG one heartbeat takes — the period, the width of one complete repeat.

Sine is the calmest possible wave: middle line on the axis, amplitude one, period three sixty degrees. Cosine is the identical heartbeat, caught at a different moment — it starts photographed at the peak instead of at the middle. Tangent is the one that does not belong on a heart monitor: every one eighty degrees it sprints from the deepest depths to the highest heights and smacks into a wall it never touches — the asymptote — then starts again from the bottom. No amplitude, because there is no peak; just walls every one eighty.

Now the two controls that never leave you. Multiplying outside — the a — is the volume knob: two sine x swings twice as high. Adding outside — the q — is carrying the whole monitor up a shelf: two sine x plus one swings around a middle line at one, from minus one up to three. Read any wave backwards with two questions: where is the calm middle — that is q, the average of top and bottom; and how far is the peak from the middle — that is a, half of the total swing. Top three, bottom minus one: middle one, swing four, half-swing two — the wave is two sine x plus one. A picture became an equation in two divisions.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on this section come to you now. Middle first, half-swing second, and tangent has walls instead of peaks.

## Subtopic: The Fast-Forward Button

The k inside the bracket is the fast-forward button on the wave. Sine of two x is the same song played at double speed: every beat arrives in half the time, so a full wave fits into one eighty degrees instead of three sixty. Sine of half x is slow motion: one lazy wave stretched over seven twenty. The rule in one line: new period is the old period divided by k. And everything inside the wave obeys the button together — for tangent two x, even the walls rush closer, standing every ninety degrees instead of every one eighty, with the first at forty-five.

Here is what the fast-forward button never touches: the volume. Play a song at double speed and it is not louder — just faster. Sine two x still swings exactly between one and minus one. This is the single most tested misunderstanding on trig graphs: a wave with two cycles crammed into three sixty has k equal to two, and that says nothing whatsoever about its height. Speed and volume are different knobs.

Counting waves is the practical skill. Look at a sketch window of three sixty degrees. One complete wave: k is one. Two complete waves: k is two — the period has halved to one eighty. Half a wave: k is a half. So the reading recipe for any wave picture is now three steps: middle line gives q, half-swing gives a, and waves-per-window gives k. Three knobs, three readings, each blind to the others.

Your questions for this part are up now. Divide the period, count the cycles in the window, and never let speed pretend to be volume.

## Subtopic: The Same Song, Started Later

The last control does not change the song at all — not its speed, not its volume. It changes when the song starts. Sine of x minus thirty is the same wave, pressed play thirty degrees late: every peak, every dip, every crossing arrives thirty degrees to the right. Sine of x plus forty-five started forty-five early — everything sits to the left. The bracket seems to point the wrong way, so refuse to read the sign at all: ask instead where the bracket equals zero. At x equals thirty, the late song is at its beginning — so the wave starts its climb at thirty. That question settles every shift in this course, for every function family.

One famous pair of songs is secretly identical. Start sine ninety degrees early — sine of x plus ninety — and listen: it opens at its peak, falls to zero, dips to its lowest, returns. That is cosine, note for note. Cosine is not a second song; it is sine with an early start. When an exam asks how cosine relates to sine, the full answer is one sentence: shift sine ninety degrees to the left.

The summit sketch combines two knobs, and the order of work keeps it calm. Two cosine of x minus sixty: first hum the plain cosine skeleton; then start it sixty late — peak at sixty, zero at one fifty, low point at two forty, zero at three thirty; then turn the volume to two — peaks at two, dips at minus two. Finish with the safety check every musician performs: play the first note. At x equals zero the equation gives two cosine of minus sixty — two times a half, one. Your curve must cross the y-axis at height one. If the sketch and the calculation disagree, the sketch is wrong, and you have caught it before the marker did.

Volume, shelf, speed, start time — four knobs, and every trig graph in the examination is some two of them turned at once. Read them one at a time, in order, and no wave can confuse you again.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: bracket-zero finds the start time, cosine is sine started early, and always play the first note as your final check.
