Hey, it's Bianca — three graph families, three controls each, plus that average gradient table at the end. If it felt like the lesson was juggling too many balls, let me show you it was actually juggling one.

The one ball: every equation in this lesson is a work order on a basic shape. The a is tension — stretch, squash, or flip. The q is a crane — lift or lower. The p is a sideways push — and you never have to remember which way, because the bracket tells you itself: set it to zero, solve, and that x is where the centre of the action now stands. Turning point of the parabola, vertical fence of the hyperbola, key input of the exponential — all found by the same five-second move.

Then each family has one personality trait to respect. The parabola is symmetric: its two x-intercepts must average to the axis, which gives you a free error-check every time. The hyperbola lives inside its cross of fences: draw the two dotted asymptotes first, always. The exponential floats above or below its single fence and never lands on it — so its range is strictly greater-than, no equals sign, while the tent-shaped parabola genuinely touches its lowest point and keeps the equals.

The average gradient is not a new topic; it is analytical geometry visiting: two points, rise over run, done. The only new idea is watching those averages creep towards a single number as the points close in — and you do not have to master that yet, just recognise it.

Tonight, one exercise: take y equals two, times x minus one squared, minus eight. Find the turning point, the axis, both intercepts, and check the intercepts average to the axis. Then find the average gradient of x squared from one to three. Fifteen minutes, and every ball lands in your hand. You've got this!
