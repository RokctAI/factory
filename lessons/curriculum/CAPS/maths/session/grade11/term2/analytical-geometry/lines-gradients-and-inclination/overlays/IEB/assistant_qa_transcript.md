**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! First one: when a question gives three points and asks you to prove they are collinear, why is it not enough to show the two gradients are equal — why do people keep mentioning the shared point?

**Tutor:** Because equal gradients on their own only prove the two segments are parallel — and parallel segments can sit on two different lines, like the opposite sides of a parallelogram. Take PQ with gradient minus three quarters, and some other segment ST across the plane, also with gradient minus three quarters: same direction, different lines. What upgrades parallel to collinear is a shared point. If the gradient from P to Q equals the gradient from Q to R, both segments pass through the same point Q — two lines with the same direction through the same point must be one line, so all three points lie on it. That is why the written conclusion should always name the common point: gradient PQ equals gradient QR, with Q common, therefore P, Q and R are collinear. Leave out the common point and a careful marker can withhold the final mark, because the logic genuinely has a hole in it. The safe habit is to compute both gradients with the middle point appearing in both calculations — then the shared point is built into your working and the sentence writes itself.

**Bianca:** Next: someone found the c in their equation as a fraction — minus five quarters — and assumed they had made a mistake because it was not a whole number. Are fraction answers a warning sign?

**Tutor:** Not at all — fractions are the normal currency of this topic, and treating them as evidence of error causes more damage than the fractions ever could. Our own working pair produced c equals minus five quarters, and it is perfectly correct. Think about what c is: the height where the line crosses the y-axis. There is no reason on earth that height should be a whole number — lines cross axes wherever the geometry sends them. The reliable way to know whether your c is right is not its appearance but the check we built into the method: substitute the point you did not use. We found the line through P and Q using Q, so feed in P: minus three quarters times minus three is nine quarters, minus five quarters leaves four quarters, which is one — exactly P's y-coordinate. That ten-second substitution is a genuine proof that both your m and your c are correct, because only the true line passes through both points. So replace the instinct of does the answer look nice with the discipline of does the unused point land. One is superstition; the other is mathematics. Keep answers as fractions too — decimals invite rounding errors that the fraction never had.

**Bianca:** This one comes up every year: for inclination, the calculator gives minus thirty-six comma eight seven degrees for a negative gradient. Why is the calculator wrong, and what is the actual routine?

**Tutor:** The calculator is not wrong — it is answering a different question. Inverse tangent on a calculator always returns an angle between minus ninety and plus ninety, because that is the convention the machine is built on. Inclination has its own definition: the angle measured anticlockwise from the positive x-axis, between zero and one eighty. Those two conventions agree for rising lines, where the angle is acute, and disagree for falling lines, where the inclination must be obtuse but the calculator hands you a negative acute angle instead. The two answers differ by exactly one hundred and eighty degrees — one period of tangent — so they describe the same direction of line, but only one of them sits inside the definition. The routine that never fails: feed the calculator the size of the gradient only, never the sign. For gradient minus three quarters, inverse tangent of three quarters gives thirty-six comma eight seven — the reference angle. Then place it yourself: rising line, keep the acute answer; falling line, subtract from one eighty, giving one hundred and forty-three comma one three. Size from the machine, placement from the definition. If you write a negative inclination in an exam, the marker reads it as a definition error, not a calculator quirk — so let the sign of the gradient tell you which quadrant, and let one eighty minus do the rest.

**Bianca:** Last one: in the proofs — parallelogram, rectangle, right-angled triangle — how do you know which test to compute first, so you do not waste time calculating everything?

**Tutor:** Start from the definition of the shape, because the definition is a shopping list of exactly what must be shown — nothing more. A parallelogram is defined by both pairs of opposite sides parallel, so the shopping list is four gradients, compared in two pairs — no lengths, no right angles needed. A rectangle adds one right angle to that list, so it is the same four gradients plus a single corner test — one product equal to minus one; you do not need to test all four corners, because once the sides are parallel in pairs, one right angle forces the rest. A right-angled triangle is the reverse emphasis: three gradients, and you multiply the pair meeting at the suspected corner — choose the corner by looking at the sketch before calculating anything. A rhombus swaps the corner test for a length test, so there Pythagoras joins the list instead. The general discipline: write the shape's definition at the top of your answer, convert each phrase into one calculation — parallel means equal gradients, right angle means product minus one, equal sides means equal distance formulas — and then do only the calculations on the list. Learners who calculate everything and hope are not being thorough; they are spending time the exam does not refund. The definition is the map; follow it and every line of working earns something.
