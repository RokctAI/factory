**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! The first one comes up every year: does it matter which point you call the first one when calculating gradient? People get different signs and panic.

**Tutor:** The order does not matter — provided you keep the same order on top and bottom, and that proviso is where the panic comes from. Take our pair: A at minus two, three, and B at four, minus five. Going from A to B: the top is minus five minus three, minus eight; the bottom is four minus minus two, six; gradient minus four thirds. Going from B to A instead: the top is three minus minus five, eight; the bottom is minus two minus four, minus six; gradient eight over minus six — minus four thirds again. Both orders give the identical answer, because swapping the direction flips the sign of the top and the bottom together, and the two flips cancel. The error that actually produces wrong signs is mixing the orders: B's y minus A's y on top, but A's x minus B's x underneath. One flip with no partner, and the gradient comes out positive when it should be negative. So build the habit mechanically: write the two points side by side, draw an arrow from one to the other, and subtract along the arrow in both the numerator and the denominator. Then the sign takes care of itself, and a quick glance at a rough sketch — does the line fall to the right? — confirms it.

**Bianca:** Next: why does flip the fraction and switch the sign give the perpendicular gradient? It feels like a magic spell rather than mathematics.

**Tutor:** It is a rotation, and you can see it in the gradient triangle. A gradient of four thirds means: for every three blocks across, climb four blocks up — a little right-angled triangle with run three and rise four. Now rotate that whole triangle a quarter turn, ninety degrees, about the point where the two lines will cross. The rotation sends the run to where the rise was and the rise to where the run was — so the new triangle has run four and rise three, giving the fraction flipped: three over four. But the rotation also changes the direction of travel: a line that was climbing to the right now falls to the right, or the reverse, which switches the sign — three over four becomes minus three quarters if you started at plus four thirds. Flip and switch is just a quarter turn described in numbers. The product being minus one then follows by algebra: a over b, times minus b over a, is minus one, since the letters cancel. And that gives you the two-way tool: construct a perpendicular gradient by flip and switch, or test a suspected right angle by multiplying and watching for minus one. The only escape from the rule is the horizontal-vertical pair, where one gradient is zero and the other does not exist — quote the geometry there, not the product.

**Bianca:** This one divided the class: for the equation of a line, some were taught y minus y-one equals m times x minus x-one, and some use y equals m x plus c. Which should we use?

**Tutor:** Both are correct and both are accepted — they are the same fact dressed differently, so use whichever your hand runs to fastest, but understand what each is doing. The form y minus y-one equals m, times x minus x-one, is really the definition of gradient rearranged: it says the slope from the fixed point x-one, y-one to any point x, y on the line equals m. Substitute the known point and gradient, expand the bracket, and tidy — the c appears at the end by itself. The form y equals m x plus c does the same journey in the other order: write the frame with m already in place, substitute the known point to expose one equation in c, solve for c, then write the final line. Count the steps and they match; neither is faster in any real sense. Where the point-gradient form earns its keep is when the arithmetic is awkward — fractions like our minus four thirds — because it postpones all the fraction work to one tidy expansion instead of an equation you must solve. Where m x plus c earns its keep is interpretation: c is visible immediately as the y-intercept, which sketching questions want. My honest advice: practise both once, pick one as your default, and be able to read the other, because memos write their solutions in either.

**Bianca:** Last one: for inclination, when the gradient is negative, why can we not just give the calculator's negative angle as the answer?

**Tutor:** Because inclination is defined as an angle between zero and one hundred and eighty degrees, measured anticlockwise from the positive x-axis — and a negative number is simply outside the definition, the way a length of minus five metres is outside what length means. The calculator is not wrong; it is answering on its own convention. Feed it inverse tangent of minus four thirds and it returns minus fifty-three comma one three degrees, an angle swept clockwise below the axis. That describes the same slope of line, but the examination asks for the inclination, and the marker checks the range. The repair is the second-quadrant reduction from term one: tangent is negative for angles between ninety and one eighty, so the inclination of a falling line must sit there. Take the size of the gradient, four thirds, find its inverse tangent, fifty-three comma one three, and subtract from one eighty to get one twenty-six comma eight seven degrees. Notice the two answers differ by exactly one eighty — the calculator's angle and yours are the same line direction, one period of tangent apart, which is precisely why tangent's period being one eighty matters here. The safe routine: always feed the calculator the positive size, decide acute or obtuse from the sign of the gradient — rising means acute, falling means obtuse — and let one eighty minus do the placing. Size from the machine, placing from the definition.
