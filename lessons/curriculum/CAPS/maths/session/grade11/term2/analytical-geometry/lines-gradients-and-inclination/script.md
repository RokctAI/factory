# Part 1 — Expert

Analytical geometry does geometry with coordinates instead of a compass: every point is a pair of numbers, and every geometric fact becomes a calculation. Grade eleven consolidates the three grade ten instruments — distance, midpoint and gradient — then builds two new skills on top: writing the equation of any straight line from minimal information, and reading the angle of a line straight out of its gradient. Thirty marks of Paper 2 live here, and they are among the most learnable marks in the paper.

## Subtopic: Distance, Midpoint and Gradient — the Three Instruments

Everything in this topic starts from two points, so fix a working pair: A at minus two, three, and B at four, minus five.

Instrument one: distance. The horizontal gap between the points is four minus minus two — six. The vertical gap is minus five minus three — minus eight, size eight. Those two gaps are the legs of a right-angled triangle whose hypotenuse is the distance AB, so Pythagoras finishes it: six squared plus eight squared is thirty-six plus sixty-four, one hundred, and the square root gives ten. The distance formula — the root of the difference in x squared plus the difference in y squared — is nothing but Pythagoras wearing coordinates.

Instrument two: midpoint. The midpoint averages the coordinates: x is minus two plus four over two, which is one; y is three plus minus five over two, which is minus one. Midpoint: one, minus one. Averages, nothing more — but write both coordinates, since half the errors here are answers with only an x.

Instrument three: gradient. Gradient is the change in y divided by the change in x — rise over run. From A to B: minus eight over six, which simplifies to minus four thirds. Two disciplines protect the sign. Subtract in the same order on top and bottom — if B's y minus A's y is on top, then B's x minus A's x goes underneath. And interpret the result: a positive gradient climbs from left to right, a negative one falls; a horizontal line has gradient zero; a vertical line has no gradient at all, because the run is zero and division by zero is undefined.

Pause here — the questions for this section are with you now. They run all three instruments on fresh pairs of points, and one checks the vertical-line exception.

## Subtopic: Parallel, Perpendicular and Collinear

Gradient is the instrument that talks about direction, and three relationships between lines are read directly from it.

Parallel lines have equal gradients. Same steepness, same direction, never meeting — if one line has gradient minus four thirds, every line parallel to it has gradient minus four thirds too. The converse also holds, and riders use both directions: prove parallel by showing gradients equal, or import a known gradient because parallelism is given.

Perpendicular lines have gradients that multiply to minus one. The quick construction: flip the fraction and switch the sign. Perpendicular to minus four thirds is plus three quarters — and the check, minus four thirds times three quarters, is minus twelve twelfths, minus one. The exception pair stands outside the formula: a horizontal line, gradient zero, is perpendicular to a vertical line, which has no gradient — the product test cannot even be written there, so quote the geometry instead.

Collinear points all lie on one straight line, and the test is gradients again: A, B and C are collinear when the gradient of AB equals the gradient of BC. Test the point C at seven, minus nine against our pair. Gradient of AB is minus four thirds. Gradient of BC: minus nine minus minus five is minus four, over seven minus four, three — minus four thirds again. Equal gradients through the shared point B, so A, B and C sit on one line.

These three tests convert geometry vocabulary into two-line calculations, and examiners phrase whole questions in that vocabulary: show that ABCD is a parallelogram — opposite sides parallel, so two gradient pairs; show that the triangle is right-angled — one gradient product equal to minus one. Read the shape's definition, then choose the instrument.

Stop for this section's questions now — equal for parallel, product minus one for perpendicular, and equal gradients through a shared point for collinear.

## Subtopic: Finding the Equation of a Line

A straight line's equation is y equals m x plus c: gradient m, y-intercept c. Producing that equation from given information is the core skill of the topic, and every version reduces to the same two-step recipe: find m, then find c by substituting one known point.

Version one: two points given. Through A at minus two, three, and B at four, minus five, the gradient is minus four thirds, from the first section. Take the frame y equals minus four thirds x plus c and feed it either point — use B: minus five equals minus four thirds times four, plus c, so minus five equals minus sixteen thirds plus c, giving c equals sixteen thirds minus five, which is one third. The line: y equals minus four thirds x plus one third. Verify with the other point, A: minus four thirds times minus two is eight thirds, plus one third is nine thirds, three — correct, and the verification costs ten seconds.

Version two: one point and a parallel line. Through one, two, parallel to y equals three x minus four. Parallel means the gradient is inherited: m is three. Substitute the point: two equals three plus c, so c is minus one. The line: y equals three x minus one.

Version three: one point and a perpendicular line. Through two, minus one, perpendicular to y equals two x plus three. Flip and switch: m is minus a half. Substitute: minus one equals minus a half times two plus c, so minus one equals minus one plus c, and c is zero. The line: y equals minus a half x.

Two special cases refuse the recipe. A vertical line has no gradient, so its equation is simply x equals a constant — the x-coordinate of any point on it. A horizontal line is y equals a constant. Learners who force these through the m-and-c machinery stall; recognise them by sight.

The questions for this section arrive now — gradient first, one substitution for c, and always spend the ten seconds checking with the unused point.

## Subtopic: Inclination — the Angle Inside the Gradient

The inclination of a line is the angle it makes with the positive x-axis, measured anticlockwise, between zero and one hundred and eighty degrees. The bridge to trigonometry is one statement: the gradient equals the tangent of the inclination. Rise over run is opposite over adjacent — the gradient triangle is a tangent triangle.

For a rising line the angle is acute and the calculator's inverse tangent answers directly. Gradient one: inverse tangent of one is forty-five degrees. Gradient two: inverse tangent of two is sixty-three comma four three degrees.

For a falling line the gradient is negative, and the inclination must land between ninety and one eighty — the reduction machinery from term one takes over. Take gradient minus four thirds. Work with the size first: inverse tangent of four thirds is fifty-three comma one three degrees — the reference angle. Tangent is negative in the second quadrant, so the inclination is one eighty minus fifty-three comma one three: one hundred and twenty-six comma eight seven degrees. Never type the minus into the calculator and accept the negative angle it returns; inclination is defined between zero and one eighty, and the marker wants the obtuse angle, not its negative cousin.

The boundary cases confirm the definition. A horizontal line: gradient zero, inclination zero degrees. A vertical line: inclination ninety degrees, and tangent of ninety is undefined — matching the vertical line's missing gradient exactly. The definitions interlock perfectly with the trigonometry you already own.

Inclination also travels in the other direction. A line inclined at one hundred and thirty-five degrees has gradient tangent of one thirty-five, which reduces to minus tangent forty-five: minus one. And because parallel lines share a gradient, they share an inclination — while the angle between two lines can be attacked by comparing their inclinations, a favourite finishing question.

The final questions of this part are with you now — tangent links gradient to angle, negative gradients mean second-quadrant angles, and one eighty minus the reference angle closes it.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same toolkit laid over a town you can walk through — streets for a grid, ramps for gradients, and directions you could give a stranger — same formulas, same answers.

## Subtopic: The Town Grid

Lay a town over the coordinate plane: avenues running east-west, streets running north-south, and every corner named by two numbers — how far east, how far north. A point at minus two, three is two blocks west, three blocks north of the town square. That is all coordinates are: an address system.

Distance between two corners, as the crow flies, is Pythagoras on the blocks. From minus two, three, to four, minus five: you would walk six blocks east and eight blocks south. The crow cuts the diagonal: six squared plus eight squared is a hundred, so ten blocks direct. The distance formula is nothing to memorise blindly — it is the walk east, the walk south, and the diagonal shortcut.

The midpoint is where you would agree to meet halfway: average the east-west numbers, average the north-south numbers. Halfway between our two corners: one, minus one. Meeting a friend halfway means averaging both parts of the address, not just one.

And gradient is how steep the direct path feels: how much north you gain for every block east. Losing eight north over six east is a drop of four thirds per block — gradient minus four thirds. Downhill to the right when negative, uphill when positive, dead flat at zero. A perfectly north-south path has no east progress at all, so per block east means dividing by zero — that path simply has no gradient, and its equation is just its street name: x equals some number.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on this section come to you now. East first, north second, and let the crow check your Pythagoras.

## Subtopic: Ramps, Twins and Corners

Think of every line as a ramp. Two ramps with the same steepness are twins: start them at different places and they run side by side forever, never meeting. That is all parallel means, and it is why the test is simply equal gradients.

A perpendicular ramp is the twin's opposite: to turn a ramp through a perfect corner, swap how far you go across with how far you go up, and reverse the direction of the climb. Numerically that is flip the fraction, switch the sign: minus four thirds becomes plus three quarters. Multiply a gradient by its perpendicular partner and you always get minus one — that is the corner test, and it is how you prove a triangle right-angled without a protractor: multiply the gradients of the two sides meeting at the suspect corner and watch for minus one.

Collinear is the queue test: three people stand in a straight queue if the direction from the first to the second matches the direction from the second to the third. Same gradient from A to B as from B to C, through the shared middle point — one queue. If the gradients differ, the middle one has stepped out of line.

These three tests are the entire grammar of show that questions. Show it is a parallelogram: both pairs of opposite sides are twins. Show it is a rectangle: twins plus one corner test. Show the points lie on one line: the queue test. Translate the shape's name into ramps and corners, and the geometry becomes arithmetic.

Your questions for this part are up now. Twins run parallel, corners multiply to minus one, and queues keep one direction.

## Subtopic: Directions From a Landmark

An equation of a line is just directions for drawing it, compressed into y equals m x plus c: start c above the town square, and climb m for every block east. To write the directions yourself you need two facts: the steepness, and one corner the path passes through. Every question in this topic hands you those two facts in some disguise.

Two points given? The steepness comes from the gradient between them. A point and a parallel path? Steal the twin's steepness. A point and a perpendicular path? Flip and switch the other one's steepness. After that, one substitution: feed the known corner into y equals m x plus c and the starting height c falls out. Through two, minus one, perpendicular to a path of steepness two: new steepness minus a half; substitute to get c equals zero; directions: y equals minus a half x. Ten seconds more buys the check — walk the unused point through your equation and confirm it lands.

The last idea is the compass bearing of a path: its inclination, the angle it cuts with the eastward avenue, swept anticlockwise, somewhere from zero up to one eighty. The link to trigonometry is one sentence: steepness is the tangent of that angle, because rise over run is exactly opposite over adjacent. Steepness one means forty-five degrees. Steepness minus four thirds is a falling path, so its angle is obtuse: take the size, four thirds, get its inverse tangent, fifty-three comma one three, and subtract from one eighty — one twenty-six comma eight seven degrees. The falling path leans backwards, past the vertical of ninety, exactly as the second-quadrant tangent from term one promised.

Hold the two pictures together and the whole lesson fits in a pocket: an address system, ramps with twins and corners, and directions with a bearing. Every formula in the topic is one of those pictures written in symbols.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: steal or flip the steepness as the question demands, substitute one corner for c, and give every falling path its obtuse angle.
