**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! The most common one by far: in a big diagram, how do we even recognise that a quadrilateral is cyclic, before deciding whether we may use the theorems?

**Tutor:** Recognition is a checklist, not an instinct. A quadrilateral is cyclic when all four of its vertices lie on the circle — so put your pen on each corner in turn and confirm it sits on the circumference. If even one corner is the centre, or an intersection point of two chords inside the circle, or an external point where tangents meet, the quadrilateral is not cyclic and none of the cyclic theorems may be quoted for it. That is the trap in most mixed riders: a diagram might contain the quadrilateral OAPB from our tangent work, whose corners include the centre O and the external point P — its angles still add to three sixty like any quadrilateral, but opposite angles adding to one eighty is not available, because O and P are not on the circle. Meanwhile the same diagram might contain a genuine cyclic quadrilateral elsewhere, made of four circumference points. So the discipline is: before writing the reason opposite angles of cyclic quad, silently name the four points and check each one against the circle. And in the other direction, when a question asks you to prove points concyclic, that is your signal that the quadrilateral is not given as cyclic — you must earn it with one of the three converse tests before anything circular can be quoted.

**Bianca:** Next: the alternate segment keeps tripping people. Is there a foolproof way to see which angle equals which in the tangent-chord theorem?

**Tutor:** There is a mechanical procedure, and it works every time. The chord from the point of contact splits the circle into two segments, and the tangent line at that point carries two angles, one on each side of the chord. The rule: each tangent-chord angle pairs with the segment on the far side of the chord from itself. So put your pencil inside the tangent-chord angle you are given — say the sixty-five degrees — and then push the pencil across the chord into the region beyond it. Whatever arc bounds that far region is the home of the equal angle: any circumference angle standing on the same chord from that arc equals your sixty-five. The other tangent-chord angle, which is one eighty minus sixty-five because the tangent is a straight line, pairs with the other segment in exactly the same crossing fashion. The check that catches every error: the two tangent-chord angles add to one eighty, and the two angles they equal sit in opposite segments on the same chord — which also add to one eighty, since the four endpoints form a cyclic quadrilateral. If your pairing ever has the big tangent angle equal to the small inscribed angle, you paired across the wrong side. Cross the chord — that is the whole memory device.

**Bianca:** This one is practical: when a rider can be solved through the centre or through tangent-chord, which road should we take in the exam?

**Tutor:** Take the shorter road if you can see it, and either earns full marks — markers follow any valid chain of statements and reasons to the same answer. The real advice is about how you spend the diagram's information. Tangent-chord is usually shorter when the given angle sits between a tangent and a chord, or at the external point between two tangents, because the isosceles triangle plus one echo gets you across the circle in two or three lines. The centre road is usually shorter when the centre is drawn and radii are already marked, because the quadrilateral made by two radii and two tangents surrenders the central angle in one subtraction, and halving finishes it. What you should not do is start both roads and abandon each halfway — half chains earn only their completed links. Choose one, run it to the answer, and then, if time allows at the end of the paper, run the other road in rough as a private check. When both roads land on seventy, you can move on with certainty; when they disagree, you have caught an error the marker would otherwise have caught for you, at full price.

**Bianca:** Last one: why does proving that four points lie on a circle even matter? It feels like an answer without a purpose.

**Tutor:** It is one of the most consequential moves in the whole geometry course, because a circle is a bundle of guarantees, and proving four points concyclic activates every guarantee at once. Before the proof, the four points are strangers: you know nothing about the angles they make beyond the ordinary triangle facts. The moment they share a circle, an entire contract comes into force: every pair of angles on the same chord from the same side is equal, every opposite pair is supplementary, every exterior angle photocopies its interior opposite — and if a tangent shows up later, tangent-chord joins the contract too. Examiners structure long riders exactly this way: part a asks you to prove some quadrilateral cyclic, and parts b, c and d are unsolvable without quoting the theorems that the proof unlocked. So when you see prove that A, B, C, D are concyclic, read it as: the rest of this question runs on circle theorems, and here is the key. It also explains a marking subtlety: if you skip part a but use cyclic reasons in part b, those reasons quote a fact never established, and the memo penalises it. Geometry is honest bookkeeping — first earn the circle, then spend it.
