**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! First: how do you tell from a diagram whether a quadrilateral is actually cyclic — people keep quoting the opposite-angles theorem for shapes that just happen to be drawn near a circle.

**Tutor:** The test is brutally literal: all four vertices must sit on the circle itself, in order around it. Not near the circle, not three out of four — all four, on the curve. Diagrams are constructed to punish assumptions here, and the standard trap has one vertex pulled slightly inside the circle, often where two chords cross. A crossing point of two chords is almost never on the circle, yet it looks like a natural corner, and learners quote the theorem across it. So before quoting anything, do a four-touch check with your pen: touch each vertex and confirm it lies on the circumference. If the question has not drawn the circle but names one — points A, B, C and D lie on a circle — then the sentence itself is your licence, and you may quote freely. If neither the drawing nor the wording guarantees it, then the quadrilateral being cyclic is probably the thing you are meant to prove, using one of the three converse tests, and assuming it would be circular reasoning in the most literal sense. One more subtlety: the vertices must be in cyclic order — a crossed quadrilateral, where the sides intersect, does not obey the theorem even with all four points on the circle. Order the letters around the circle, touch all four, then quote.

***

**Bianca:** Next: the converse tests. A learner asks why proving one pair of opposite angles supplementary is enough — surely you should have to check both pairs?

**Tutor:** The second pair comes free, and the reason is ordinary quadrilateral arithmetic, no circle needed. The four interior angles of any quadrilateral total three hundred and sixty degrees — that is true of every four-sided figure, cyclic or not. Now suppose you have shown one opposite pair, say B plus D, equals one eighty. Subtract from the total: A plus C must equal three sixty minus one eighty — also one eighty. The other pair had no freedom left. So demanding both pairs would be demanding the same fact twice, and mathematics does not charge twice for one fact. This economy is worth internalising because it shapes exam strategy: when you hunt for a cyclic proof, you need only chase the one pair of opposite angles that the given information reaches most easily — or the single exterior-equals-interior-opposite equality, or one pair of equal angles on the same side of a segment. One clean equality, correctly reasoned, completes the proof. Where learners actually lose marks is the conclusion sentence: after establishing the equality, you must still write the claim and name the converse — quadrilateral is cyclic, converse, opposite angles supplementary. The equality is the evidence; the named converse is the verdict. Evidence without a verdict leaves the proof unfinished in the marker's eyes.

**Bianca:** This one had the class arguing: in the tangent-chord theorem, how do you know which angle in the circle equals the tangent-chord angle? Some picked angles on the same side of the chord and got everything wrong.

**Tutor:** The word doing all the work is alternate — the equal angle lives in the alternate segment, the region on the opposite side of the chord from your tangent-chord angle. Here is a mechanical routine that never fails. Draw the tangent and the chord from the point of contact, and mark the tangent-chord angle you care about. That angle, together with the chord, encloses one of the two segments — shade that segment lightly. The theorem's equal angles live in the unshaded segment: any point on that far arc, joined to the chord's two endpoints, sees the chord at exactly the tangent-chord angle. If your candidate vertex sits in the shaded region, you have not crossed the chord, and whatever equality you write will be false. Remember also that the tangent makes two angles with the chord, one on each side, summing to one eighty. Each has its own alternate segment: the fifty-eight-degree angle sends fifty-eight to the far arc, while its neighbour of one twenty-two serves the near arc. That symmetry is your built-in check — the two circle angles you produce should also sum to one eighty, because their vertices sit in opposite segments of the same chord. And the final condition people forget under pressure: the chord must spring from the exact point of contact. A chord starting elsewhere on the circle makes no tangent-chord angle, however convincing the diagram looks.

**Bianca:** Last one: the rider had two completely different solutions — the tangent-chord road and the angles-around-the-centre road — both giving sixty-four. In an exam, which road should we take, and is checking with the second road worth the time?

**Tutor:** Take the road you see first — the mark scheme accepts any valid chain of theorems with reasons, and there is no bonus for the elegant route over the plain one. The tangent-chord road took three lines: equal tangents, isosceles base angles of sixty-four, echo across the chord. The centre road also took three: two right angles from tangent perpendicular to radius, the angles of quadrilateral OAPB giving one twenty-eight at the centre, then halving. Same marks either way. Now, on checking: a full second solution costs two or three minutes, so I would not write both out for a routine question. But a mental check along the second road — thirty seconds, no writing — is some of the best time you will ever spend, and here is why. The two roads share no theorems. A wrong assumption on one road — a mis-identified isosceles triangle, a halving on the wrong arc — will almost never produce the same wrong answer on the other road, because the errors have nowhere to correlate. Agreement between independent routes is therefore powerful evidence, not just comfort. Where I do recommend writing the second road in full is when the question itself smells like a checkpoint — a final part worth many marks, where one early slip poisons everything after it. There, three minutes buys insurance on a whole question. And there is a deeper habit forming underneath: searching for the second road forces you to see the diagram through every theorem at once, and that sight is exactly the skill the hardest riders examine.
