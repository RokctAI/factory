# Part 1 — Expert

The previous session put angles inside the circle; this one completes the toolkit with the two remaining families: the cyclic quadrilateral, whose four corners all sit on the circle, and the tangent, the line that touches the circle exactly once. Between them they supply the theorems that dominate the hardest riders in Paper 2 — opposite angles adding to one eighty, the two equal tangents, and the tangent-chord angle that echoes into the alternate segment.

## Subtopic: Cyclic Quadrilaterals — Opposite Angles Add to One Eighty

A cyclic quadrilateral is a four-sided figure whose four vertices all lie on one circle. The theorem: the opposite angles of a cyclic quadrilateral are supplementary — each opposite pair adds to one hundred and eighty degrees.

The proof rides on the centre theorem. Call the quadrilateral ABCD and mark the centre O. The angle at B stands on the arc ADC, the arc that avoids B, so the central angle on that arc is twice B. The angle at D stands on the opposite arc, ABC, so its central angle is twice D. Those two central angles wrap the whole way around the centre between them — they sum to three hundred and sixty degrees. So twice B plus twice D is three sixty, and B plus D is one eighty. The same argument delivers A plus C.

Immediate use: if angle A is ninety-five degrees, angle C is eighty-five. One subtraction — but only when the quadrilateral is genuinely cyclic, all four corners on the circle. Quote the reason as: opposite angles of cyclic quadrilateral.

A companion theorem comes almost free. Extend side DC beyond C to a point E. The exterior angle BCE and the interior angle BCD lie on a straight line, so BCE equals one eighty minus BCD — and one eighty minus BCD is exactly the opposite interior angle A. So the exterior angle of a cyclic quadrilateral equals the interior opposite angle. In riders this is a shortcut worth two lines: spot the extended side, and transfer the angle straight across the diagram.

One warning about reading diagrams. The four vertices must lie on the circle in order around it — if one point sits inside or outside, or the quadrilateral crosses itself, the theorem does not apply. Check the four corners before quoting anything.

Pause here — the questions for this section are with you now. They work the supplementary pairs forwards and backwards, and one baits you with a quadrilateral that only has three corners on the circle.

## Subtopic: Proving a Quadrilateral Is Cyclic

Examiners love the reverse question: not use the circle, but prove one exists — show that four points are concyclic. Each theorem from the forward direction has a converse, and any one of the three converses settles it.

Test one: if one pair of opposite angles of a quadrilateral adds to one hundred and eighty degrees, the quadrilateral is cyclic. Note the economy — one pair suffices, because the angle sum of any quadrilateral is three sixty, so the second pair is then automatic.

Test two: if the exterior angle of a quadrilateral equals the interior opposite angle, the quadrilateral is cyclic — the converse of the exterior angle theorem.

Test three, the subtle one: if two points on the same side of a line segment subtend equal angles at that segment, then those two points and the segment's endpoints are concyclic. This is the converse of angles in the same segment: equal angles from the same side means all four points share a circle.

Strategy in a rider: when a question says prove that A, B, C and D lie on a circle, hunt for whichever test is closest to the given information. Given angles that add suspiciously towards one eighty, take test one. Given an extended side, take test two. Given two triangles standing on the same base with equal apex angles on the same side, take test three. Then write the conclusion with its reason, naming the test — for example: ABCD is cyclic, converse, opposite angles supplementary.

Why examiners prize this: proving a quadrilateral cyclic unlocks the circle's whole toolbox for the rest of the rider. The moment ABCD is cyclic, same-segment pairs and supplementary pairs all become quotable. Many long riders hinge exactly there — part a proves the circle exists, part b spends it.

Stop for this section's questions now — one pair adding to one eighty is enough, and each test is the converse of a theorem you already own.

## Subtopic: Tangents — the Perpendicular Radius and the Two Equal Tangents

A tangent touches the circle at exactly one point, the point of contact. The foundation fact is taken as an axiom in CAPS: the tangent is perpendicular to the radius drawn to the point of contact. No proof required — but it must be quoted, as tangent perpendicular to radius, every time it is used.

It immediately feeds calculations through Pythagoras. A point P sits thirteen units from the centre of a circle of radius five, and PT is the tangent from P touching at T. The radius OT meets the tangent at ninety degrees, so triangle OTP is right-angled at T, with hypotenuse OP. Tangent length squared equals thirteen squared minus five squared: one sixty-nine minus twenty-five, one forty-four. The tangent is twelve units long. Any question pairing a tangent with a distance to the centre is this triangle.

Now the theorem for two tangents. From a point outside the circle, two tangents can be drawn, one touching on each side. The theorem: they are equal in length. Proof by congruency again: let the tangents from P touch at A and B. Compare triangles OAP and OBP. OA equals OB, radii. OP is common — and it is the hypotenuse of both triangles, because the angles at A and B are ninety degrees, tangent perpendicular to radius. Right angle, hypotenuse, side: congruent, so PA equals PB. Quote it as: tangents from same point.

The equal tangents plant an isosceles triangle in the diagram, and it radiates results. If the angle at P between the two tangents is forty degrees, triangle PAB is isosceles with PA equal to PB, so its base angles are each half of one eighty minus forty: seventy degrees. File that seventy — the next theorem is about to catch it.

The questions for this section arrive now — perpendicular at the point of contact, Pythagoras for tangent lengths, and equal tangents making isosceles triangles.

## Subtopic: The Tangent-Chord Theorem and a Full Rider

The final theorem is the most striking: the angle between a tangent and a chord drawn from the point of contact equals the angle subtended by that chord in the alternate segment — the segment on the other side of the chord.

Set it up precisely. A tangent touches at A, and a chord AB leans away from it, cutting the circle into two segments. The angle between tangent and chord on one side equals the angle that AB subtends at any point in the segment on the other side. The proof is worth knowing in skeleton. Draw the diameter from A through the centre to a point C, and join C to B. The angle between the tangent and the diameter is ninety — tangent perpendicular to radius. So the angle between chord and diameter, angle BAC, is ninety minus x, where x is the tangent-chord angle. The angle ABC is ninety — angle in semicircle. The angle sum of triangle ABC then gives angle ACB equals x. But angle ACB sits in the alternate segment and stands on chord AB — and by same segment, so does every other angle on that arc. The echo is proved.

Use it once cleanly: tangent at A, chord AB, tangent-chord angle sixty-five degrees. Then any angle standing on AB from the alternate segment is sixty-five. Quote it as: tangent-chord.

Now the full rider, stitching the session together. PA and PB are tangents from P, touching at A and B, with angle APB equal to forty degrees. C is a point on the major arc AB. Find angle ACB. Line one: PA equals PB — tangents from same point. Line two: triangle PAB is isosceles, so angles PAB and PBA are each seventy degrees — base angles, and angle sum with forty. Line three: angle PAB is the angle between tangent PA and chord AB, so the angle in the alternate segment equals it: angle ACB equals seventy degrees — tangent-chord. Three moves, three reasons, and the answer surfaces on the far side of the circle.

Cross-check it with the earlier machinery, because good geometry always closes its loops. In quadrilateral OAPB the angles at A and B are ninety each, so the central angle AOB is three sixty minus ninety minus ninety minus forty: one hundred and forty. The circumference angle on the major arc is half of that — seventy. Same answer along a completely different road, which is exactly the confidence a rider should give you.

The final questions of this part are with you now — find the tangent-chord angle, cross the chord into the alternate segment, and remember that every theorem in this topic is happy to confirm the others.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same theorems from the roadside — a bicycle against a kerb, a goat on two ropes, and an echo across a river — same reasons, same marks, pictures first.

## Subtopic: Two Arcs That Share the Whole Circle

Why must opposite corners of a cyclic quadrilateral add to one eighty? Picture the four corners as four fence posts on a perfectly circular kraal wall. Stand at corner B and face the wall opposite you — your angle stands on the far arc, the one walking from A through D to C. Now stand at the opposite corner D and face the other way — your angle stands on the remaining arc, from A through B to C. Between the two of you, you have swept the entire wall: the two arcs together are the whole circle.

Remember the greedy centre from the last lesson: the centre claims double whatever any corner sees. So the centre's two claims — double B and double D — must together wrap the full three hundred and sixty degrees of the wall. Double B plus double D is three sixty, so B plus D is one eighty. The theorem is just the whole circle, shared between two opposite viewpoints, then halved.

And the exterior angle version is the same fact leaning on a straight line. Walk past corner C and keep going: the angle outside the kraal at C and the angle inside add to a straight one eighty. But the inside angle at C also pairs with A to one eighty. Two things that each complete C to one eighty must equal each other — so the outside angle at C is a photocopy of the angle at A, sent across the diagonal.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on this section come to you now. Two opposite corners, two arcs, one whole circle — that is the entire theorem.

## Subtopic: The Kerb and the Two Ropes

Lean a bicycle wheel against a straight kerb so it touches at exactly one point. The spoke running from the axle to that touching point stands dead square to the kerb — push the wheel along and that stays true at every touching point. That is the tangent axiom: the radius to the point of contact meets the tangent at ninety degrees, always. It costs nothing to use, but the reason must still be written: tangent perpendicular to radius.

That right angle is a Pythagoras machine. Stand thirteen paces from the axle of a round water tank of radius five, and stretch a rope from where you stand to touch the tank: rope, radius, and your line to the centre make a right-angled triangle, and the rope comes out at twelve — thirteen squared minus five squared, then the square root.

Now tie a goat outside a circular kraal, at one peg, with two ropes running to touch the kraal wall on either side. The theorem says the two ropes are exactly equal — always, no matter where the peg stands. The reason is the mirror symmetry through the peg and the centre: the line from peg to centre is a mirror, and each rope is the other one reflected. Equal ropes mean the peg and the two touching points form an isosceles triangle, and isosceles triangles pay out equal base angles on demand. Peg angle forty degrees? The two base angles split the remaining one forty: seventy each. Every two-tangent diagram hides this triangle, and finding it is usually the first mark.

Your questions for this part are up now. Square at the touch, Pythagoras on the rope, and two ropes from one peg are always twins.

## Subtopic: The Echo Across the Chord

The tangent-chord theorem sounds exotic, so picture it as an echo. The tangent is a wall running past the circle, touching it at one point. From that touching point a chord strikes off into the circle, making some angle with the wall — say sixty-five degrees. The theorem: shout that angle across the chord, and it echoes back, exact and undistorted, from every point on the far arc. Anyone standing in the other segment, looking at the same chord, sees sixty-five degrees.

Two details keep the echo honest. First, the echo crosses the chord — it lands in the alternate segment, the region on the other side of the chord from the angle you started with. A tangent-chord angle on the left of the chord echoes to the arc on the right, and the other tangent-chord angle, the one eighty minus sixty-five on the other side, echoes to the other arc. If your echo landed on the same side as the shout, you have crossed nothing and the answer is wrong. Second, the echo starts at the point of contact only — the chord must be drawn from exactly where the tangent touches.

Now hear the whole rider as a story. Two ropes from one peg, forty degrees between them. The isosceles triangle pays out seventy and seventy at the wall. Each seventy is a tangent-chord angle — a shout against the wall — so it echoes across the chord to the far arc: the spectator on the other side sees seventy degrees. Three sentences: twin ropes, isosceles payout, echo across. That is a five-mark rider, told at walking pace.

And the deepest habit of all: when your answer matters, walk a second road. The same seventy comes from the greedy centre — ninety plus ninety plus forty leaves one forty at the centre, and the far seat sees half, seventy. When two different theorems land on the same number, the number is safe.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: square at the touch, twins from the peg, and every shout against the wall echoes across the chord — same size, other segment.
