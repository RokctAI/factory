**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! The one asked most: do we really have to learn the proofs word for word, or is using the theorems enough?

**Tutor:** You must be able to produce the proofs, and the examination guidelines say so explicitly — a proof of one of the prescribed theorems can be asked directly, and it typically carries five or six marks. But word-for-word memorising is the wrong preparation, because a proof is a structure, not a poem. Take the centre-double theorem. Its structure is three moves: draw the line from the circumference point through the centre; spot the two isosceles triangles the radii create; apply the exterior angle theorem to each and add. If you own those three moves you can reconstruct the wording live, label the diagram however the paper labels it, and adapt when the examiner places the point in a slightly different position — which is exactly what a memorised script cannot do. The same applies to the chord theorem: its structure is one congruency argument, right angle, hypotenuse, side. My advice: for each prescribed proof, write down the skeleton in three or four short phrases on a summary card, practise expanding the skeleton into full lines twice, and test yourself a week later. Structure first, wording second — that order survives exam pressure.

**Bianca:** Next: how do you tell which arc an angle is standing on? The double-and-half theorem keeps going wrong for people because of this.

**Tutor:** Use the two-finger test, and it never goes wrong again. Put one finger on each arm of the angle, at the points where the arms meet the circle — those two points are the ends of the subtending chord. Now trace the circle from one finger to the other, going the way that does not pass through the angle's vertex. The piece you traced is the arc the angle stands on. Do the same for the other angle in the question. The theorem only links a centre angle and a circumference angle when the two traced arcs are the same piece of circle. This is where the reflex case stops being confusing: if the vertex at the circumference sits on the minor arc, then the arc it stands on is the major arc, and the centre angle it doubles is the reflex angle — the one going the long way round, more than one eighty. So a circumference angle of one hundred and ten degrees pairs with a reflex centre angle of two hundred and twenty, while an ordinary centre angle of one hundred and forty pairs with a seat angle of seventy on the other side. Shade the traced arc in pencil before halving anything; the ten seconds of shading is cheaper than the two marks the mix-up costs.

**Bianca:** This one surprised me: someone solved a whole rider correctly using angles, but got almost nothing because of the reasons column. What counts as an acceptable reason?

**Tutor:** The marking memo accepts short standard phrases, and the safest course is to use the phrasing your textbook and teacher use, because the memo lists those forms. For this lesson's theorems the standard reasons are: radii, for two sides of a triangle being equal because both run from the centre; line from centre perpendicular to chord, for a bisected chord; angle at centre equals two times angle at circumference, which may be abbreviated to angle at centre; angles in same segment, for two equal angles on the same chord and same side; and angle in semicircle, for the ninety on a diameter. General geometry reasons stay available too: base angles of isosceles triangle, exterior angle of triangle, angle sum of triangle, vertically opposite angles. Two habits protect you. First, one reason per statement, written on the same line — a paragraph of reasoning with reasons sprinkled somewhere near it forces the marker to guess which reason supports which claim, and markers do not guess in your favour. Second, quote the reason for the theorem you actually used, not a nearby one: writing angles in same segment when you used the centre-double theorem is a wrong reason and the mark is gone even though the number is right.

**Bianca:** Last one: any advice for the learner who freezes when the diagram is complicated — circles inside triangles, letters everywhere?

**Tutor:** Freezing comes from trying to see the whole diagram at once, and the cure is to shrink what you are searching for. A complicated circle diagram only ever contains a handful of standard shapes, so run a fixed checklist and tick them off physically. One: is there a centre? Then darken every radius, because each pair of radii is an isosceles triangle carrying two equal angles. Two: is there a diameter? Then write the ninety at the circumference immediately, even before you know whether you need it. Three: pick each chord in turn and ask who stands on it — two vertices on the same side means an equal pair by same segment. Four: transfer every angle you deduce onto the diagram in pencil as you find it, so the diagram accumulates knowledge and the next deduction becomes visible. Most riders fall in three to five such steps, and the steps are almost always forced — at each moment there is usually only one theorem that can fire. If you are truly stuck, work backwards one step: name the angle the question wants, and ask which triangle or which chord would deliver it, then hunt for that triangle's missing pieces. The freeze breaks the moment the task changes from solve this diagram to find the next single certainty.
