# Part 1 — Expert

Grade eleven geometry moves from triangles and parallelograms into the circle, and it raises the stakes: theorems must now be proved, not just used, and every statement in a rider must carry a reason. This session covers the first half of the circle work — the chord theorems, the angle at the centre, the angle in a semicircle, and angles in the same segment — plus the discipline of writing a rider solution that a marker can follow line by line.

## Subtopic: The Vocabulary of the Circle and the Chord Theorems

Definitions first, because reasons quote them. A radius runs from the centre to the circle; all radii of a circle are equal, and that single fact powers more riders than any theorem. A chord joins two points on the circle; the diameter is the chord through the centre, twice the radius. An arc is a piece of the circle itself, and a chord cuts the inside of the circle into two segments — the major and the minor.

Theorem one: the line drawn from the centre perpendicular to a chord bisects the chord. The proof is a congruency argument worth learning as a pattern. Let the centre be O, the chord AB, and M the foot of the perpendicular from O to AB. Compare triangles OMA and OMB. OA equals OB, both radii. OM is common to both. The angles at M are both ninety degrees. That is the right angle, hypotenuse, side condition — the triangles are congruent, so AM equals MB. Bisected.

The theorem runs in reverse too: the line from the centre to the midpoint of a chord is perpendicular to that chord. And a third relative completes the family: the perpendicular bisector of a chord passes through the centre — which is why a machinist can find the centre of a circular plate by drawing two chords and bisecting both.

The calculation this family feeds is pure Pythagoras. A chord of length sixteen sits in a circle of radius ten; how far is the chord from the centre? The perpendicular from the centre bisects the chord, giving half-lengths of eight. The radius to an endpoint is the hypotenuse: distance squared equals ten squared minus eight squared, one hundred minus sixty-four, thirty-six. The chord sits six units from the centre. Every chord-distance question is this triangle — half the chord, the distance, and a radius.

Pause here — the questions for this section are with you now. They test the three members of the chord family and the half-chord triangle, and one asks which line earns the name perpendicular bisector.

## Subtopic: The Angle at the Centre Is Double the Angle at the Circumference

The central theorem of the topic, in both senses. Take an arc AB, a point C anywhere on the rest of the circle, and the centre O. The theorem: the angle AOB at the centre equals twice the angle ACB at the circumference, when both stand on the same arc AB.

The proof deserves attention because it is examinable. Draw the line from C through O, extended to a point D beyond O. Triangle OAC is isosceles, since OA and OC are radii, so the base angles at A and C are equal — call each one x. The exterior angle of a triangle equals the sum of the interior opposite angles, so angle AOD equals two x. The same argument on triangle OBC, with base angles y, gives angle BOD equals two y. Add the two results: angle AOB equals two x plus two y, while angle ACB equals x plus y. Double, exactly as claimed. When C sits off to one side, the same construction subtracts instead of adds, and the doubling survives.

Using it is immediate: a central angle of one hundred and forty degrees over an arc means every circumference angle on that arc is seventy degrees. But watch the direction of the arc. If the angle at the centre is reflex — say two hundred and twenty degrees around the far side — then the circumference angle standing on that same far arc is half of two hundred and twenty, one hundred and ten degrees. The theorem never fails; learners fail it by halving the wrong central angle. Shade the arc the angles stand on before you halve anything.

One corollary drops out at once. Let the arc be a half-circle, so the chord is a diameter and the central angle is a straight line — one hundred and eighty degrees. Every angle at the circumference standing on a diameter is then half of one eighty: ninety degrees. The angle in a semicircle is a right angle, and its reason is written exactly that way: angle in semicircle. Spot it in reverse as well — if a chord subtends a right angle at the circumference, that chord must be a diameter, and it passes through the centre.

Stop for this section's questions now — halve the central angle standing on the correct arc, and treat every diameter as a right angle waiting to happen.

## Subtopic: Angles in the Same Segment

Fix a chord AB and stay on one side of it — one segment. Put two points, C and D, anywhere on that arc. The theorem: angle ACB equals angle ADB. Angles subtended by the same chord, on the same side, are equal — no matter where on the arc the vertex sits.

The proof is one line once the centre theorem exists: each of those angles equals half the central angle AOB standing on the same arc, so they equal each other. This is the standard structure of the topic — one heavyweight theorem, then corollaries that fall out of it cheaply.

In riders, this theorem is the great connector, because it transfers an angle from one triangle into another. If chords AC and BD cross at a point P inside the circle, then angle CAB and angle CDB both stand on chord CB from the same side, so they are equal; likewise angle ABD and angle ACD both stand on chord AD. Two pairs of equal angles connecting two overlapping triangles — that is often the entire skeleton of a rider.

The condition same side is not decoration. Points on opposite sides of the chord sit in different segments, and their angles are not equal — they are supplementary, a fact that arrives with cyclic quadrilaterals in the next session. For now, the discipline: before quoting the theorem, trace the chord with your pen and confirm both vertices sit on the same arc.

A worked chain to fix the habit. Chord AB subtends angle ACB equal to fifty-three degrees at C. Point D lies on the same arc as C. Then angle ADB is also fifty-three degrees — reason: angles in the same segment. If additionally AD crosses BC at P, the triangles APC and BPD... hold the thought and observe the angles: angle at P in the one triangle equals the angle at P in the other, vertically opposite, and the pair of same-segment angles then forces the third angles equal too — two triangles with matching angles, built almost for free.

The questions for this section arrive now — same chord, same side, equal angles, and always name the chord doing the subtending.

## Subtopic: Riders — Reading the Diagram and Writing Reasons

A rider is a puzzle assembled from the theorems, and it is marked statement by statement: every line of your solution is a claim plus a reason, and an unreasoned claim scores nothing even when true. The accepted reasons are short standard phrases: radii equal, line from centre perpendicular to chord, angle at centre is twice angle at circumference, angle in semicircle, angles in same segment, base angles of isosceles triangle, exterior angle of triangle, angles of triangle add to one eighty.

Strategy comes before writing. First, mark every radius the moment you see the centre — any two radii make an isosceles triangle, and isosceles triangles are angle factories. Second, hunt for a diameter, because it plants a ninety-degree angle at the circumference for free. Third, shade the arc under any central angle before halving or doubling. Fourth, chase the angles with a pencil on the diagram itself before committing to written lines.

Now a full worked rider. O is the centre. A, B and C lie on the circle, with angle AOB equal to one hundred degrees, standing on the minor arc AB, and C on the major arc. Find angle ACB and the base angles of triangle AOB. Line one: angle ACB equals fifty degrees — reason: angle at centre is twice angle at circumference. Line two: OA equals OB — reason: radii. Line three: triangle AOB is isosceles, so its base angles at A and B are equal — reason: base angles of isosceles triangle. Line four: those base angles sum with one hundred degrees to one eighty, so each is forty degrees — reason: angles of triangle add to one eighty. Four statements, four reasons, full marks.

Extend it one step, the way examiners do. Suppose BC is a diameter. Then angle BAC equals ninety degrees — angle in semicircle — and angle ABC follows from the angle sum of triangle ABC: one eighty minus ninety minus fifty, forty degrees. Notice what happened: each new line leaned on an earlier one. Riders are chains, and the marks lie in the links.

The final questions of this part are with you now — quote a reason for every statement, mark the radii first, and let each answer feed the next.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same circle, built from a folded chapati wrapper, a soccer field, and a stadium of seats — same theorems, same reasons, pictures first.

## Subtopic: Folding the Circle

Cut a perfect circle out of paper and fold it so the two ends of a straight crease-line meet. The fold line does two things at once: it cuts the chord exactly in half, and it crosses the chord at a perfect right angle. Fold along any other chord and the same happens. And here is the quiet miracle: every such fold line passes through the exact middle of the paper. Two folds, and their crossing point is the centre — found without a ruler, without measuring anything.

That folding fact is three theorems wearing one coat. The perpendicular from the centre to a chord cuts it in half. The line from the centre to a chord's halfway point stands perpendicular to it. The perpendicular cut-in-half line of any chord runs through the centre. One symmetry, three official sentences.

The sums that come from it are all the same triangle. A chord of sixteen in a circle of radius ten: the fold cuts the chord into two eights, the radius runs out to the chord's end, and the fold line, the half-chord and the radius make a right-angled triangle. Pythagoras: ten squared minus eight squared is thirty-six, so the fold line is six long. Whenever a question mentions a chord and a distance, draw that triangle before anything else — half the chord, mind, never the whole one. Using the full sixteen is the classic lost mark.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on this section come to you now. Fold the circle in your head, and remember: the triangle always uses half the chord.

## Subtopic: The Whole Stadium Sees the Same Game

A soccer pitch sits inside a circular stadium. The goal mouth is a chord — two posts on the circle. Now walk along the curved stand behind the halfway line, staying on one side of the goal-chord, and keep watching the goal. Here is the theorem you can feel: from every seat on that same curve, the goal mouth opens at exactly the same angle. Front of the arc, middle of the arc — same viewing angle. That is angles in the same segment: same chord, same side, equal angles, wherever the seat.

Now compare the stands with the centre spot. Stand at the exact centre of the circle and the goal opens at some angle; the theorem of the topic says every seat on the far curve sees it at exactly half of the centre's angle. Centre one hundred and forty, seats seventy. The centre is the greedy position — it always claims double.

And one seat is special. If the goal-chord runs straight through the centre — a full diameter, touchline to touchline — the centre's angle is a dead-straight one hundred and eighty. Half of that is ninety, so every seat on the curve sees a diameter at a perfect right angle. That is the angle in a semicircle, and it is the most quoted single fact in the whole topic: see a diameter, expect a ninety.

The only way to lose marks here is to halve the wrong side. The rule of the stadium: the seat and the centre must be watching the same arc. Shade the arc between the posts that both are actually facing, and the theorem never misfires.

Your questions for this part are up now. Same curve, same angle; the centre doubles it; a diameter makes it ninety.

## Subtopic: Solving a Rider Like a Case File

A rider hands you a diagram and one or two facts, and asks for an angle three steps away. Treat it as a case file, and work it the way an investigator works: from what must be true, one small certainty at a time.

Open the file the same way every time. Mark the centre and darken every radius — two radii always make an isosceles triangle, and an isosceles triangle donates a pair of equal base angles without being asked. Scan for a diameter, because a diameter plants a free ninety on the circle's edge. Then shade the arc under any centre angle, so the halving happens on the correct side.

Work the case we solved upstairs, in plain speech. The centre angle over the minor arc is one hundred. The seat on the far curve therefore sees fifty — the centre claims double, so the seat gets half. The two radii to the chord's ends make an isosceles triangle whose top angle is the one hundred; the remaining eighty degrees split equally, forty and forty. Three certainties, each hanging on the one before, and the case is closed.

The last habit is the one the marks actually attach to: say why, every single line. In this topic the why is always one of a short list — radii equal, centre angle is double, same segment, angle in semicircle, angles of a triangle. Write the claim, write the reason, move to the next link. A correct answer with no reasons is a suspect with no evidence: the marker must let it walk away with nothing.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: darken the radii, shade the arc, claim your free ninety at every diameter, and give every statement its reason.
