**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! The most asked one by far: how do I remember which letters go where in the cosine rule?

**Tutor:** Anchor it to one sentence: the lonely side faces the cosine. The cosine rule always has one side alone on the left, squared, and the angle inside the cosine on the right is the angle directly opposite that lonely side — they stare at each other across the triangle. The other two sides do all the remaining work: squared and added first, then multiplied together, doubled, and attached to the cosine. So to write the rule for any letter, ask only: which side am I hunting, and which angle faces it? Hunting side q? Then q squared equals p squared plus r squared, minus two p r cosine Q — the capital letter matching the lonely side. That is also your instant error check on any line you have written: the small letter on the left and the capital letter in the cosine must match. If you ever see b squared on the left with cosine C on the right, the line is wrong before any arithmetic happens. And for the angle-hunting version, the same anchor works in reverse: the side whose square is subtracted on top is the one facing the angle you want, and the two sides embracing that angle sit doubled underneath.

***

**Bianca:** Next, and it split the class in half: when the angle is 110 degrees, why does subtracting end up making the side longer?

**Tutor:** Because you are subtracting a negative number, and the geometry genuinely demands it. Follow the two layers separately. Layer one is trigonometry: cosine is positive for angles under 90 degrees and negative for angles between 90 and 180, so cosine of 110 degrees is minus 0,3420. Layer two is the formula: the correction term is minus two p r cosine Q, and when the cosine itself is negative, minus times minus gives plus — the term adds to the sum of the squares. Now see why the geometry insists on this. Picture the two given sides as arms on a hinge. At 90 degrees exactly, the far side is the Pythagoras distance. Open the hinge wider than 90 and the endpoints move apart — the far side must beat Pythagoras. The negative cosine is precisely the mechanism that delivers that longer answer. This gives you a free sanity check on every cosine-rule calculation: angle under 90, expect an answer shorter than the root of the sum of squares; angle over 90, expect longer. In our example the Pythagoras value would be the root of 117, about 10,8, and the answer 12,41 is indeed longer. If your numbers break that pattern, a minus sign got lost — recompute with the whole line typed in one go.

**Bianca:** Third one: with three sides known, both rules could find the next angle. Which one should we actually use in the exam?

**Tutor:** With three sides and no angle, only the cosine rule can even start, so the choice question really arises one step later — after you have found the first angle and a complete pair now exists. At that point the sine rule is genuinely lighter: one fraction, one multiplication, done. The danger is specific, so learn it as a rule with a condition. The sine rule is safe for an angle you can argue is acute, and every triangle offers at least one guaranteed way to know: only the angle facing the LONGEST side can possibly be obtuse, so any angle facing a shorter side is certainly acute and the sine rule may find it without worry. Strategy for a three-sides triangle, then: use the cosine rule FIRST on the angle opposite the longest side — that is the only possibly-obtuse one, and inverse cosine will report it honestly, sign and all. The remaining two angles are guaranteed acute, so find one of them with the quick sine rule, or even quicker, get the third by subtracting from 180. Order matters more than tool loyalty: biggest angle by cosine rule, second by sine rule, third by angle sum. That sequence is fast, unambiguous, and marks-proof.

**Bianca:** Last one: the long two-triangle questions — where do people actually lose their marks?

**Tutor:** Almost never on the trigonometry; the marks bleed at three joints of the process. Joint one is the sketch. Learners work on the printed diagram, which is cramped, and misread which angle sits in which triangle. Redraw each triangle separately, side by side, each with its own labels, and copy the given values onto them — two clean triangles beat one crowded picture. Joint two is the bridge. In a two-triangle story, the side the triangles share is found in the first triangle and used in the second, and the mark-killer is carrying it across as a rounded number. If the shared side is really 5,9652 kilometres and you carry 5,97, every subsequent answer drifts, and papers say clearly that premature rounding is penalised. Store the exact value in calculator memory, quote the rounded version in your sentence, and compute with the stored one. Joint three is silent method: a bare equation with numbers in it earns less than one preceded by its justification. One sentence per step — in triangle one, two sides and the included angle are known, so the cosine rule gives the shared side — signals the plan and collects the method marks even if a digit slips later. Sketch separately, bridge exactly, narrate briefly: that is where the last third of the marks lives.
