**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! First one: how do we decide, at speed, whether a question is a sine rule question or a cosine rule question?

**Tutor:** Take a stocktake of the triangle, and let the stocktake decide — never the question's wording, never habit. Look only at what you HOLD inside the one triangle you are working in. If a complete pair exists — a side together with the angle directly opposite it — the sine rule is available, and being the lighter machine, it should do the work. If no complete pair exists, count again: two sides with the angle pinched BETWEEN them is Side-Angle-Side, cosine rule for the third side; three sides and no angle is Side-Side-Side, cosine rule rearranged for an angle. Those are the only two configurations the sine rule cannot touch, and they are precisely the two the cosine rule was built for. My routine, and I want it to become yours: mark every known piece on the sketch with a tick, then say the inventory aloud in one sentence — two sides and the included angle, therefore cosine rule. That sentence at the top of the answer earns the method mark by itself. And note the one-way traffic: after a cosine rule step you always hold enough for the sine rule to finish the triangle cheaply, so the classic rhythm of a long question is cosine once, sine afterwards. Starting with sine when no pair exists is the most common wasted minute at this level.

***

**Bianca:** Next: the minus sign with obtuse angles. Several learners got q equal to about 7,5 instead of 13 in the 120-degree example. What exactly goes wrong?

**Tutor:** They subtracted 56 instead of adding it — the double-minus trap, and it is worth dissecting slowly. The rule says minus two p r cosine Q. With Q at 120 degrees, cosine Q is minus a half — negative, because cosine is negative throughout the second quadrant. So the correction term is minus 112 times minus a half: minus times minus, PLUS 56. The learners who landed on 7,5 squared computed 113 minus 56, which is 57 — they saw the minus in the formula, saw the negative cosine, and applied only one of them. The protection is mechanical and it never fails: type the ENTIRE right-hand side into the calculator in one line — 49 plus 64 minus 2 times 7 times 8 times cos of 120, with the cosine in brackets — and press equals exactly once. The machine handles both minuses faultlessly; it is the human splitting the calculation into stages who drops one. Then run the sense check that geometry donates: at 90 degrees the answer would be the square root of 113, about 10,6. An angle WIDER than 90 pushes the far side LONGER than that, so 13 passes the check and 7,5 fails it instantly. Wide corner, longer side — say it before you compute, and the wrong answer cannot survive the comparison.

***

**Bianca:** Third: when we have all three sides and want an angle, some of us find one angle with the cosine rule and then use the sine rule for the second angle. Is that safe?

**Tutor:** It is efficient, and it is safe on one condition: take the cosine rule to the LARGEST angle first. Here is the logic. The largest angle faces the longest side, and it is the only angle in the triangle that could possibly be obtuse — a triangle has at most one obtuse angle, since two would exceed 180 on their own. Inverse cosine handles that possible obtuseness perfectly, because a negative cosine simply delivers the obtuse answer directly. Once the largest angle is settled, every remaining angle is guaranteed acute, and the sine rule's famous ambiguity — the second suspect at 180 minus the answer — becomes harmless: you may take the inverse sine at face value because the obtuse option is no longer geometrically possible. Run that order on our 4, 7, 9 triangle: cosine rule on the angle facing 9 gives 106,6 degrees; then the sine rule cheaply finds the angle facing 7, knowing it must be acute; the third angle falls out of 180. Reverse the order — sine rule first on a middle angle, cosine rule later — and you walk voluntarily into the ambiguity you could have avoided. So the rule of thumb: biggest angle first, by cosine; the small change afterwards, by sine; and total the three angles to 180 as your closing audit.

**Bianca:** Last one: in the two-triangle bearing questions, what do the strongest answers do differently from the rest?

**Tutor:** Four habits separate them, all visible on the page before any arithmetic starts. First, they build the diagram from the words rather than trusting a mental picture: north lines drawn at every station, bearings measured clockwise from north, and the enclosed angle computed explicitly — our 65 degrees appeared as 105 minus 40, written down as a line of working, because that subtraction is itself a mark. Second, they name the campaign: a sentence like triangle H P Q gives P Q; triangle P Q L then uses it — showing the marker the route before travelling it. The shared side is circled on the sketch: it is the bridge, produced by the first triangle and spent by the second. Third, they respect the bridge's precision: the full calculator value of P Q crosses over, stored in memory, while only the reported answer is rounded to the asked-for decimals. Round at the bridge and every subsequent figure drifts; the drift is small, but accuracy marks are exactly the marks it eats. Fourth, they finish with sense: angles of each triangle totalling 180, the longest side facing the largest angle, distances plausible against the story — a boat that sailed 5 and 7 kilometres cannot be 30 kilometres from its partner. None of these habits involves new mathematics; they are bookkeeping done in the open. But two-triangle questions are long chains, and chains are exactly where bookkeeping in the open outperforms brilliance in the head.
