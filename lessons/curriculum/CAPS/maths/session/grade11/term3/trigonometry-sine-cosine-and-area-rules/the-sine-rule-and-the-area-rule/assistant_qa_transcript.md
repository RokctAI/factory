**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! First one, asked three different ways: how do I decide between the sine rule and the area rule when a question just says "calculate"?

**Tutor:** Read the shopping list, not the question word. Each rule has a fixed price of admission, and the information in the question tells you which one you can afford. The area rule needs two sides and the angle wedged between them, and it only ever produces one thing: an area. The sine rule needs one complete opposite pair — a side and the angle facing it — plus one more piece, and it produces missing sides or missing angles. So make a quick inventory: write down every side and angle you know, and mark the complete pairs. If the question wants an area and you already hold two sides with their included angle, go straight to the area rule. If the question wants an area but you only have one side, the sine rule must first manufacture the second side, and the area rule finishes the job. And remember the free item on every list: the three angles total 180 degrees, so two known angles always buy you the third at no cost. Nine times out of ten, the free angle is what completes the pair the sine rule was waiting for.

**Bianca:** Next, from more than one person: why does my calculator sometimes give the wrong angle even when my working is perfect?

**Tutor:** Because inverse sine is only telling you half the truth, and it is not the calculator's fault — it is the nature of sine. Sine is positive in the first quadrant and in the second, so an acute angle and its obtuse partner at 180 minus that angle share exactly the same sine value. Your calculator has to choose one answer, and it always chooses the acute one. Usually that is the angle the triangle actually contains — but not always. So the full method has three lines, not one. Line one: inverse sine gives the acute candidate, say 27,93 degrees. Line two: compute the partner, 180 minus 27,93, which is 152,07 degrees. Line three: test the partner against the angle sum. In our example, 152,07 plus the given 42 exceeds 180 on its own, so the partner is impossible and you reject it, writing the reason. But if the partner had survived the test, the question would genuinely have two valid triangles, and a complete answer presents both. Examiners set that ambiguous case deliberately, and the learners who only ever write the calculator's answer lose the marks silently.

**Bianca:** This one from the back: do we really have to learn the proofs, or can we just use the formulae?

**Tutor:** Learn the proofs — they are short, they are asked directly, and they make the formulae harder to forget. The examinable chain for this lesson has only two links. Link one, the area rule: drop a perpendicular height inside the triangle, notice that the little right-angled triangle gives the height as a sine C, and substitute that into half base times height. Link two, the sine rule: write the three versions of the area rule, which must all be equal because they measure the same triangle, and divide the whole line by half a b c. Everything cancels beautifully, leaving sine A over a equals sine B over b equals sine C over c. That is maybe six lines of writing in total, and versions of it appear in Paper Two regularly for four or five marks. There is also a practical payoff: once you have seen that the sine rule IS the area rule rearranged, you stop mixing up their conditions, because you know the area rule is about a wedged angle and the sine rule is about opposite pairs. Understanding the machinery is the cheapest memory aid there is.

**Bianca:** Last one: in the multi-step example, how do I know which angle is the included one when three sides are floating around?

**Tutor:** Trace it on the sketch with two fingers — genuinely, physically. Put one finger on each of the two sides you plan to use in the area rule and slide them toward each other; the vertex where your fingers collide holds the included angle, and its capital letter is the letter your formula needs. In the worked example we used sides a and c. Side a runs from B to C, side c runs from A to B; they share vertex B, so B is included and the area line reads half a c sine B. The commonest error is grabbing the angle whose letter matches one of the sides — writing half a c sine A, for instance — and that feels plausible precisely because the letters look related. The labelling convention is actually warning you: side a faces AWAY from angle A, so a side and its same-letter angle can never touch, and a same-letter combination in the area rule is always wrong. Check every area line against that rule: the angle letter must be the one letter that does NOT appear among the two sides. Half a b sine C, half b c sine A, half a c sine B — in each version, all three letters are different. If your line breaks that pattern, the sketch is telling you to look again.
