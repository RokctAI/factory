**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! First, the one that filled my inbox: in three-circle questions, how do I know when a given number includes the centre?

**Tutor:** By the little words, and only by them — the numbers themselves never announce their own meaning. There are two families of phrasing. The first family names a region exactly: soccer and athletics ONLY, or soccer and athletics BUT NOT netball. Those words fence off one specific room of the diagram, so the number walks straight in with no arithmetic. The second family names an overlap loosely: 15 play soccer and athletics, full stop. That phrase describes everyone in both codes — and the all-three players in the centre certainly play both soccer and athletics, so they are inside that 15. The region for exactly-two then receives 15 minus the centre's 5, which is 10. The safe procedure: before filling anything, read every clue and underline the words only, but not, and exactly wherever they appear; clues carrying those words are region counts, clues without them are running totals that need the centre subtracted. Then fill from the centre outwards, because the centre is the one number that is always a clean region count. And close with the audit: sum all eight regions and confirm the population total. In our survey, 115 inside plus 35 outside returns 150 — if it does not, revisit the clue you trusted without underlining it.

**Bianca:** Next: on tree diagrams, why do the second branches change, and is there a quick way to get them right under exam pressure?

**Tutor:** They change because the situation itself changes — the tree is drawing a story, and by stage two the story has history. With 3 red and 2 blue sweets, the first draw faces five sweets. But the second draw never faces five; it faces the four survivors, and WHICH four depends on what left the packet. After a red: two red, two blue remain, so the branches read 2 over 4 twice. After a blue: three red, one blue, so 3 over 4 and 1 over 4. The quick method under pressure is to write the state on the branch before the probability. At the end of each first-stage branch, jot the tiny inventory — 2R 2B on the red branch, 3R 1B on the blue branch — and then the second-stage probabilities copy themselves off the inventory with no thinking left to do. Two checks lock it in. Along every node, the branches leaving it must sum to 1 — 2 over 4 plus 2 over 4 does, and any node that does not is broken on the spot. And the full audit at the end: the path products, 6, 6, 6 and 2 twentieths, total 20 twentieths, exactly 1. The inventory habit plus the two checks turns without-replacement trees from a memory test into bookkeeping.

**Bianca:** Third one: for the weather and soccer question, some people multiplied and some added and some did both. What is the actual logic?

**Tutor:** The logic is that and multiplies along a path, and or adds between paths — and this question contains both an and and an or, so both operations appear. Unpack the story into its futures. Saturday has two weather branches: rain at 0,3, dry at 0,7. Winning is a second stage whose chances depend on the branch — 0,4 in the rain world, 0,65 in the dry world. Now ask precisely what event we want: the team wins. That happens in two distinct futures. Future one: it rains AND the team wins — an and along one path, so multiply, 0,3 times 0,4 is 0,12. Future two: it stays dry AND the team wins — multiply again, 0,7 times 0,65 is 0,455. The two futures cannot both happen — Saturday has one weather — so they are mutually exclusive, and the win can arrive through either one OR the other: add, 0,575. People who only multiplied computed a single future and ignored the other; people who only added forgot that each future itself needs its two stages combined. The tree diagram exists precisely to stop both mistakes, because drawing it makes the two paths visible and the rule mechanical: multiply along, add across. Any question with the shape if this happens then the chances are, otherwise they are, is this same tree wearing different weather.

**Bianca:** Last one: in a contingency table, is checking one cell with the product test really enough to declare the whole table independent?

**Tutor:** For the two-by-two tables of this course, yes — one honest cell test settles the table, and it is worth seeing why rather than memorising it. A two-by-two table is a tightly locked structure: the margins fix the row and column totals, so once one cell is chosen, the other three cells are forced by subtraction. Suppose the boy-walk cell passes the test, meaning boys walk at exactly the population's walking rate — 37,5 percent in our register. Then the boys who do not walk must ride at exactly the population's riding rate, because the two rates within the row must total 100 percent. And the girls inherit the same rates automatically: the walkers not accounted for by boys are exactly the population rate applied to girls. One passing cell forces every cell to pass; one failing cell forces failure somewhere else too. In practice, then: run the product test once on a convenient cell, state it fully — probability of boy times probability of walk equals 0,4 times 0,375 equals 0,15, matching the cell's 30 over 200 — and conclude in a sentence that gender and transport are independent. For intuition, or when a question asks you to explain in words, compare the row rates instead: 37,5 percent of boys walk and 37,5 percent of girls walk, so the rows tell the same story. Same verdict, two languages — the multiplication for the proof, the percentages for the meaning.
