Hey, it's Bianca — if probability feels like a subject where the words keep changing meaning under you, let me settle the ground. This whole lesson is four ideas, and each one is something you already understand from ordinary life.

Idea one: there are two honest ways to get a probability. If the outcomes are genuinely equally likely — dice, coins, cards — count favourable over total. If they are not, you tally real data and divide. That is the entire model question: symmetric, or counted?

Idea two: the Venn diagram is just two overlapping membership lists drawn as circles, and it has one golden rule — fill in the overlap first, then subtract outwards, then put the leftovers outside. The addition rule is nothing deeper than refusing to count anyone twice: add the two circles, subtract the overlap once.

Idea three: the special words are two separate checks. Mutually exclusive asks only: can they happen together? Complementary asks two things: can they happen together, AND do they fill the whole space between them? Never grant the second word without both checks.

Idea four, the one that matters most: independent means no influence — the coin has no memory. The test is a single multiplication: does the probability of both equal the product of the two separate probabilities? Equal means independent; any gap, however small, means dependent. And keep this in your pocket for the exam: mutually exclusive is not independence — it is the strongest influence there is, because one event happening kills the other completely.

Overlap first. Never count twice. Two checks for complementary. Multiply to test influence. Four moves, and every question in this lesson is one of them wearing different clothes. You've got this!
