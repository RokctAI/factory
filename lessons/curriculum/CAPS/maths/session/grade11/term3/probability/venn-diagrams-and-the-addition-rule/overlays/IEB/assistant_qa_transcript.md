**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! First up: in the addition rule, how do we know when the minus term is actually needed? A few people say they just leave it out when the question doesn't mention 'both'.

**Tutor:** Dangerous habit — the minus term is needed for every pair of events that CAN happen together, whether or not the question chats about it. The rule in full, probability of A or B equals probability of A plus probability of B minus probability of A and B, is always true; the short version without the minus is the special case where the and-probability is zero, which is exactly the definition of mutually exclusive. So the decision procedure is: ask whether the two events can co-occur. Can a learner watch cricket and rugby? Obviously — 25 do — so the minus lives. Can a single die roll be a six and also less than three? Impossible — the minus term is zero and vanishes on its own. Notice the logic: you never CHOOSE to drop the minus; you show the overlap is zero and the rule drops it for you. If a question is silent about the overlap, that silence is often the question: use the addition rule backwards. Given probability of A, probability of B and the or-probability, the rule rearranges to hand you the and-probability — and if that number comes out zero, you have PROVEN the events mutually exclusive, which is frequently the actual mark. Treat the rule as a four-slot machine: any three slots filled, the fourth is one rearrangement away.

**Bianca:** Next: complementary versus mutually exclusive — is there a quick way to keep them apart under pressure?

**Tutor:** Picture a taxi with two conditions for the word complementary, and only one for exclusive. Exclusive needs one fact: the two events never share an outcome — circles that do not touch. Complementary needs that fact PLUS a second: between them, the two events must cover the entire sample space, leaving nothing outside. So run the two-question check. Question one: can both happen at once? If yes, neither word applies. If no, you have earned exclusive. Question two: is every outcome in one event or the other? Only if yes do you upgrade to complementary. Our die pair — six, and less than three — passes question one and fails question two, because three, four and five are homeless: exclusive, not complementary. An easy complementary pair on the same die: less than three, and at least three — no overlap, nothing left out, probabilities two sixths and four sixths totalling exactly 1. And that total is your arithmetic alarm: complementary probabilities MUST sum to 1, so if your two answers sum to anything else, either the events are not complementary or a calculation slipped. The payoff for spotting a true complement is the shortcut of the topic: at least one is 1 minus none — one subtraction replacing a pile of additions.

**Bianca:** Third: for the independence test, our product came out at 0,2 and the real overlap at about 0,167. That is fairly close — why doesn't close count?

**Tutor:** Because independence is a definition, not a vibe — the product rule states an equality, and an equality either holds or it does not. In pure-mathematics land there is no tolerance zone: 25 over 150 is one sixth, 0,1666 recurring, and the product 0,2 is one fifth; one sixth and one fifth are different numbers, so the events are dependent, full stop. Your instinct about closeness is not worthless — it just belongs to statistics, where sampling wobble is studied properly with tools you may meet after school. At this level, the examinable skill is cleaner: compute both sides exactly, preferably as fractions to dodge rounding traps, compare, and write the sentence. Since one sixth does not equal one fifth, the events are not independent. My strong advice is to work in fractions throughout: decimals invite you to round 0,1667 to 0,17 and then to hesitate over whether 0,17 is close enough to 0,2 — a hesitation fractions never create. And read the direction of the gap for the bonus insight: an actual overlap BELOW the prediction means the events push each other away, like our two sports audiences; an overlap ABOVE it means they travel together. Either way, dependency has a story, and one extra sentence telling it shows a marker complete command.

**Bianca:** Last one: when a question asks for the probability of 'at least one' of something, is the complement always the fastest road?

**Tutor:** Almost always, and it is worth understanding exactly why. At least one is a crowd of cases — exactly one, exactly two, exactly three, and so on — and attacking the crowd directly means computing every case and adding. The complement of the crowd is a single, lonely case: none at all. One computation, one subtraction, finished. The chance of at least one late delivery in a week is 1 minus the chance that every delivery runs on time; the chance of at least one head in five flips is 1 minus the chance of five straight tails, which is 1 minus one thirty-second — thirty-one thirty-seconds. Two disciplines keep the method safe. First, form the complement precisely: the opposite of at least one is NONE — not exactly one, not all. Sloppy complements are the classic loss here, so write the complement in words before any numbers. Second, the multiplication inside — chance of none as a product of individual misses — is only legal when the trials are independent, so say so: flips of a fair coin are independent, hence the probabilities multiply. When a question mixes it all — at least one, with data, plus an independence claim to verify — you are seeing the whole lesson in one item: addition rule for the or, complement for the at-least, product rule as the test. Name each rule as you use it, and the working reads like a proof instead of a guess.
