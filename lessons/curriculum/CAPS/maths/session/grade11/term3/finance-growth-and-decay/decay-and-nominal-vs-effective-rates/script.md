# Part 1 — Expert

Grade ten priced growth: money climbing under simple and compound interest. Grade eleven completes the picture with the downhill versions — simple and compound decay, which price every depreciating bakkie and ageing machine in the country — and then sharpens the uphill story with the question banks use to compete for your deposit: what happens when interest is paid monthly instead of yearly, and what is an advertised rate really worth? By the end you will convert any nominal rate into its honest effective equivalent and defend the answer.

## Subtopic: From Growth to Decay — the Two Depreciation Formulae

Recall the two growth formulae. Simple growth: A equals P times the quantity one plus i n. Compound growth: A equals P times the quantity one plus i, raised to the power n. Decay keeps both structures and flips one sign. Simple decay, also called straight-line depreciation: A equals P times the quantity one minus i n. Compound decay, called reducing-balance depreciation: A equals P times the quantity one minus i, raised to the power n. Same cast: P the starting value, A the ending value, i the rate per period as a decimal, n the number of periods.

The two models tell different stories about the SAME loss rate. Straight-line removes the same fixed slice every year — 15 percent OF THE ORIGINAL value, year after year. Reducing balance removes 15 percent of whatever the item is CURRENTLY worth, so the rand amount lost shrinks each year as the value shrinks.

The worked case: a delivery bakkie costs 240 000 rand and depreciates at 15 percent per annum over 4 years. Straight-line: i n is 0,15 times 4, which is 0,6, so A equals 240 000 times 0,4, which is 96 000 rand. Reducing balance: A equals 240 000 times 0,85 to the power 4. Now 0,85 to the fourth is 0,52200625, so A equals 125 281,50 rand. Reducing balance leaves MORE value — nearly thirty thousand rand more — because each year's 15 percent bites a smaller number.

One structural difference is exam gold: straight-line marches to zero and hits it — at 15 percent, after one over 0,15, about six and two thirds years, the value is exactly nothing. Reducing balance never reaches zero: multiplying by 0,85 forever shrinks the value but cannot kill it.

Pause here — the questions for this section are with you now. Same rate, two stories: fixed slice of the original, or shrinking slice of the current value.

## Subtopic: Different Compounding Periods

Banks rarely compound yearly. Interest arrives half-yearly, quarterly, monthly — and the formula adjusts with one principle: match i and n to the COMPOUNDING PERIOD. If the quoted annual rate is compounded m times per year, then the rate per period is the annual rate divided by m, and the number of periods is the years times m.

Worked example. Invest 10 000 rand at 9 percent per annum compounded monthly, for 2 years. The monthly rate is 0,09 over 12, which is 0,0075. The clock ticks in months: n is 24. So A equals 10 000 times 1,0075 to the power 24. That power is 1,19641, so A equals 11 964,14 rand.

Compare the same money at 9 percent compounded annually: A equals 10 000 times 1,09 squared, which is 11 881 rand. Monthly compounding pays about 83 rand more over the two years. Why? Because interest starts earning interest sooner: the January interest joins the principal in February instead of waiting for December. More frequent compounding at the same quoted rate always pays more — monthly beats quarterly beats yearly — though each step of extra frequency adds less than the last.

The discipline to drill: never mix periods. A monthly rate with n in years, or an annual rate with n in months, is the most common finance error in the paper. Write the conversion line first — i per period, n periods — before touching the formula. And a timeline sketch, with deposits and rate changes marked on it, turns every long question into bookkeeping.

Stop for this section's questions now — divide the rate, multiply the periods, and keep both on the same clock.

## Subtopic: Nominal versus Effective Rates

The 9 percent in the last example has a proper name: the NOMINAL rate — the advertised headline, meaningless until you attach its compounding frequency. The EFFECTIVE rate answers the honest question: what single once-a-year rate would grow money by the same amount as this nominal rate with its frequency?

Compute it from the example. One rand at 9 percent compounded monthly becomes 1,0075 to the power 12 after one year, which is 1,09381. The money grew by 9,381 percent — not 9. So 9 percent nominal, compounded monthly, is EFFECTIVELY 9,38 percent per annum. The general relationship: one plus i effective equals one plus the nominal rate over m, all raised to the power m.

The effective rate exists to make comparison fair. Bank A offers 9,4 percent compounded annually; Bank B offers 9,3 percent compounded quarterly. The headlines say A wins. Convert B to effective: one plus 0,093 over 4 is 1,02325, raised to the power 4 gives 1,09629 — an effective 9,63 percent. Bank B actually pays more: 9,63 beats 9,4. The lower headline wins because compounding four times a year more than makes up the difference. Advertisers know most people compare headlines; the effective rate is the defence the syllabus gives you.

Two notes for full marks. Effective is always bigger than nominal whenever compounding happens more than once a year — if your effective comes out smaller, the powers are inverted. And exam questions run the relationship both directions: given nominal and frequency, find effective; or given a target effective rate, solve back for the nominal rate that delivers it. The equation is the same line, rearranged with roots instead of powers.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — grow one rand for one year, read off the honest percentage, and let effective rates settle every bank duel.

## Subtopic: Exchange Rates and Mixed Timeline Problems

Two closing skills round out the finance toolkit. First, foreign exchange. An exchange rate prices one currency in another: 18,50 rand per dollar means every dollar costs 18,50 rand. A laptop priced at 800 dollars therefore costs 800 times 18,50, which is 14 800 rand. If the rand WEAKENS to 19,75 per dollar — more rand needed per dollar — the same laptop jumps to 15 800 rand: a thousand rand more, with no change in the dollar price. That is the mechanism by which a weakening rand feeds inflation: imported goods, fuel and machinery all arrive priced in foreign currency, so a weaker rand lifts prices across the economy. The flip side: a weaker rand makes South African exports and holidays cheaper for foreigners, boosting exporters and tourism. Strengthening runs the story in reverse. Exam questions ask both the arithmetic and the implications — have one sentence ready for each direction.

Second, the mixed timeline problem, where the skills combine. Deposit 8 000 rand at 10 percent per annum compounded half-yearly, for 3 years. Conversion line first: the half-yearly rate is 0,05; the clock ticks 6 half-years. A equals 8 000 times 1,05 to the power 6, and 1,05 to the sixth is 1,34010, so A equals 10 720,77 rand. Longer stories chain segments: a rate change mid-investment splits the timeline into two segments, each with its own i and n, and the output of segment one becomes the P of segment two. Draw the timeline, mark every event, convert every rate to its period, and multiply the growth factors through.

The final questions of this part are with you now — price the currency move in rand, then run the timeline one clean segment at a time.

## Subtopic: The Photocopy and the Ruler

Two ways a bakkie can lose value, and you have seen both in real life. The first is the ruler: draw a straight line from 240 000 down toward zero, dropping the same fixed amount every year. Fifteen percent of the ORIGINAL price — 36 000 rand — vanishes each year, every year, like clockwork. That is straight-line depreciation: four years times 36 000 is 144 000 gone, leaving 96 000. And because the drop is fixed, the line really does hit the floor: six and two-thirds years and the bakkie is worth nothing, on paper at least.

The second way is the photocopy of a photocopy. Copy a photo at 85 percent quality, then copy the COPY at 85 percent, then copy that. Each generation keeps 85 percent of the one before — not of the original. That is reducing-balance depreciation: 240 000 becomes 204 000, becomes 173 400, becomes 147 390, becomes 125 281,50 after four copies. The first year loses 36 000 rand; the fourth loses only about 22 000, because 15 percent of a smaller number is a smaller bite. And a photocopy never becomes a blank page — fainter and fainter, but never nothing. Reducing balance never reaches zero.

Same advertised rate, 15 percent; two completely different machines. The exam tells you which machine with its keywords: straight-line or simple decay means the ruler; reducing balance or compound decay means the photocopier. Underline the keyword before you write the formula.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on this are with you right now. First decision every time: fixed slice of the original, or fading copy of the current?

## Subtopic: Interest Paydays

Two savings accounts both advertise 9 percent per year. Account one pays all the interest in one December payday. Account two pays interest twelve times a year — a small payday every month, at nine twelfths of a percent each. Same headline; different machinery. Which is better?

Account two — and the reason is paydays. The January interest, tiny as it is, joins your balance in February, and from February onward it earns interest itself. Every month's interest is put to work immediately instead of waiting for December. Interest earning interest is the whole magic of compounding, and more paydays means the magic starts sooner. Over two years on 10 000 rand, the monthly account delivers 11 964 rand against the annual account's 11 881 — about 83 rand of pure payday timing.

The bookkeeping rule is a clock conversion. If paydays come monthly, everything must speak monthly: rate per payday is the yearly rate divided by twelve, and time is counted in paydays — 24 of them in two years. Divide the rate, multiply the periods, and never let a yearly clock mix with a monthly one in the same formula. Half-yearly paydays, divide by two and count half-years; quarterly, divide by four and count quarters.

One honest caution: each extra step of frequency helps a little less. Monthly beats yearly noticeably; daily beats monthly by only a whisker. Payday timing is powerful, but it is not magic without limit.

Your questions for this part are up now — count the paydays, convert the clock, and remember why earlier interest is worth more.

## Subtopic: The Headline and the Till Slip

A shop window shouts a special; the till slip tells the truth. The nominal rate is the window sticker — 9 percent, in big letters, with the small print compounded monthly underneath. The effective rate is the till slip: what your money ACTUALLY grew by after one full year, small print included.

Getting the till slip is one calculation: send one rand through the machine for a year. At 9 percent monthly, that is 1,0075 multiplied by itself twelve times — 1,09381. The slip reads 9,38 percent: that is what the 9 percent sticker truly costs the bank, and truly pays you. The small print always works in the same direction: more frequent compounding pushes the true rate ABOVE the sticker, never below. An effective rate that comes out below nominal is a sign the calculation inverted somewhere.

Why does this matter beyond honesty? Because stickers cannot be compared, and till slips can. Bank A's window says 9,4 percent, paid yearly; Bank B's says 9,3 percent, compounded quarterly. Stickers say A. Till slips say: A grows money by exactly 9,4; B grows it by 9,63. B wins by a clear margin — the lower sticker with the busier machinery pays more. Every compare-the-accounts question in the paper is this move: convert every offer to its till slip, then compare like with like. Write the comparison sentence with both effective rates in it; that sentence is the answer.

And the rand-dollar story is the same honesty applied to shopping: the sticker on the laptop says 800 dollars all year, but the till slip in rand depends on the day's exchange rate — 14 800 rand at 18,50, and 15 800 when the rand slips to 19,75. Currencies move, and the rand price moves with them, which is exactly how a weak rand walks into every shop in the country.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now — trust the till slip, never the sticker, and convert every offer to effective before you compare.
