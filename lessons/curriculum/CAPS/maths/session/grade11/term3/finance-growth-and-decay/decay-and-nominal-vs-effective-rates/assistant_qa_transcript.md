**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! The first one comes up every single year: in the exam, how do I know which depreciation formula the question wants?

**Tutor:** The question always tells you, but in one of three voices, so learn all three. Voice one is the direct keyword: straight-line or simple depreciation means A equals P times one minus i n; reducing balance, diminishing balance or compound depreciation means A equals P times one minus i, to the power n. Underline the keyword the moment you see it. Voice two is the description without the name: loses 15 percent of its original value each year is straight-line, because the slice is cut from the original; loses 15 percent of its value each year, where value means current value, is reducing balance. The word original is the giveaway. Voice three is the table or graph: values falling by the same rand amount each year — a straight line when plotted — signal simple decay; values falling by a shrinking rand amount, curving as they drop, signal compound decay. If a question gives the first two or three year-end values, subtract them: constant differences mean straight-line, constant RATIO means reducing balance. And keep the sanity check for the end: at the same rate and time, reducing balance always leaves the higher book value — 125 281,50 against 96 000 for our bakkie. If your two answers come out the other way around, the formulae were swapped.

**Bianca:** Next: for compounded monthly, why do we divide the annual rate by twelve instead of finding some cleverer monthly rate?

**Tutor:** Because that division is precisely what the phrase means — it is a naming convention of the banking world, and the syllabus follows it. A nominal rate of 9 percent per annum compounded monthly is banker's shorthand for: the rate we apply each month is 9 divided by 12, which is 0,75 percent. The nominal annual rate is literally twelve copies of the monthly rate stapled together; nobody claims that 9 percent is what your money actually earns in a year. Your instinct that this is not quite honest arithmetic is exactly right, and the effective rate exists to repair it: apply 0,75 percent twelve times and the year's true growth is 9,38 percent, more than the headline, because each month's interest starts earning on its own account. If banks instead quoted the cleverer rate you are imagining — the monthly rate that compounds to exactly 9 percent in a year — that would be a different convention, and the twelfth root of 1,09 would be involved; some university courses do that, but CAPS and the banks do not. So the working rule is mechanical: nominal rate divided by the number of compounding periods per year gives the rate per period, always. The intelligence goes into the next step — converting to effective when comparison or honesty demands it.

**Bianca:** Third: in the bank duel, 9,4 beat 9,3 on the stickers but lost on the till slips. Could a question trick us the other way — where the higher sticker still wins?

**Tutor:** Absolutely, and good questions do exactly that, because the point being examined is that you CANNOT predict the winner from the stickers — only the effective rates decide. Whether a lower sticker with busier compounding overtakes a higher sticker depends on the size of the gap versus the power of the frequency. Our duel had a small gap, one tenth of a percent, and quarterly compounding added about 0,33 percent to the 9,3 — easily enough to overtake. But make the gap wider — say 9,8 annual against 9,3 quarterly — and the quarterly machinery still only lifts 9,3 to about 9,63, so the 9,8 sticker wins comfortably. Same structure, opposite verdict. This is why the method must be unconditional: convert EVERY offer to its effective annual rate, whatever the frequencies involved, and only then compare. Even an annually compounded offer is converted, trivially — its effective rate equals its nominal rate, and saying so is part of the answer. Then write the comparison sentence with both effective values in it: Bank B pays an effective 9,63 percent against Bank A's 9,4, so Bank B is the better investment. The sentence with the two numbers is what the memo rewards; a bare letter B, even when correct, leaves marks behind.

**Bianca:** Last one: the timeline questions with a rate change halfway — where do those go wrong?

**Tutor:** They go wrong at the joints, in three predictable ways, and a drawn timeline prevents all three. Picture the standard beast: 8 000 rand invested at 10 percent compounded half-yearly for the first two years, then the rate changes to 9 percent compounded monthly for one more year. Error one is using one formula for the whole five segments of story: each rate regime is its own segment, with its own i and its own n, and the segments CHAIN — the output of segment one is the principal of segment two. Compute 8 000 times 1,05 to the power 4 for the first two years, and that entire amount enters the second segment to be multiplied by 1,0075 to the power 12. Error two is the clock slip inside a segment: two years at half-yearly compounding is 4 periods, not 2, and one year monthly is 12, not 1 — convert the clock inside every segment separately, because the clocks differ between segments. Error three is rounding at the joint: carrying a rounded 9 724,05 into segment two instead of the full calculator value lets a few cents of error breed; keep the running value on the calculator and round only the final answer to two decimals with the rand symbol. The drawing that prevents everything: a horizontal line, a tick at each event — deposit, rate change, withdrawal, end — with the rate and period written between ticks. Fill the growth factor for each stretch, multiply them across, and the scariest finance question in the paper becomes three short multiplications performed in order.
