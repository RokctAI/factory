**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! First one, and I hear it every year: when a question just says an item loses 12 percent of its value each year, how do we know which decay formula it wants?

**Tutor:** Read for the word that tells you what the 12 percent is taken OF. If the question says 12 percent of its original value, or of its purchase price, or of its cost price, that slice is fixed — straight-line, A equals P times one minus i n. If it says 12 percent of its value, and value can only sensibly mean current value, the slice shrinks every year — reducing balance, A equals P times one minus i to the power n. Many questions are kinder and name the model outright: straight-line, simple depreciation, or on a straight-line basis all mean the ruler-straight version; reducing balance, diminishing balance, or compound depreciation all mean the power version. A third disguise is data: if you are handed the first few year-end values, subtract consecutive pairs. Equal DIFFERENCES mean straight-line; equal RATIOS mean reducing balance. And carry the sanity check with you: at the same rate over the same time, reducing balance always leaves the higher book value — 189 983,49 against 144 000 for our minibus. If your two answers land the other way around, you have swapped the formulae.

**Bianca:** Next question: why is the rate per quarter just the yearly rate divided by four? Shouldn't there be something cleverer, since compounding is involved?

**Tutor:** Your suspicion is mathematically sound, and the answer is that the division is a naming convention, not a claim about growth. When a bank says 8 percent per annum compounded quarterly, the phrase is defined to mean: we apply 8 divided by 4, which is 2 percent, each quarter. The nominal annual rate is simply four copies of the quarterly rate written as one number — nobody is claiming your money grows by exactly 8 percent in a year. In fact it grows by more, and that is the whole point of the effective rate: apply 2 percent four times and one rand becomes 1,08243, so the honest yearly growth is 8,24 percent. If the convention instead published the clever rate you are imagining — the quarterly rate whose four applications give exactly 8 percent — you would need the fourth root of 1,08, and some university finance courses do work that way. School mathematics and the banking industry do not. So the mechanical rule stands: nominal rate divided by the number of periods per year gives the rate per period, every time. Save your cleverness for the conversion to effective, which is where honesty is restored.

**Bianca:** Third one: in the bank contest, the smaller headline won. Is that always how these questions end — should we just pick the busier compounding?

**Tutor:** No — and that instinct is exactly the trap the question is built to punish. The busier compounding adds a boost, but the boost is limited, so the outcome depends on the size of the headline gap. In our contest the gap was small: 8,3 against 8,1 is two tenths of a percent, and monthly compounding lifted 8,1 to an effective 8,41 — enough to overtake. Now widen the gap: 8,8 percent annually against 8,1 percent monthly. The monthly machinery still only lifts 8,1 to 8,41, and 8,8 wins comfortably. Same setup, opposite verdict. That is why the method must run unconditionally: convert EVERY offer to its effective annual rate, including the annually compounded one — whose effective rate simply equals its nominal, and saying so is part of your answer — and only then compare. Never predict the winner from the headlines, in either direction. Finish with the sentence that quotes both numbers: Bank Y pays an effective 8,41 percent against Bank X's 8,3, so Bank Y is the better offer. The sentence with both effective rates in it is the conclusion your working has been building toward; a bare letter is not a comparison.

**Bianca:** Last one: those long timeline questions where the rate changes halfway through — what actually goes wrong when people lose marks there?

**Tutor:** Three things, all preventable with one drawing. Take a typical monster: 9 000 rand invested at 7 percent compounded quarterly for two years, then the rate switches to 6 percent compounded monthly for one more year. Mistake one is forcing a single formula over the whole story. Each rate regime is its own segment with its own i and its own n, and the segments chain: 9 000 times 1,0175 to the power 8 closes segment one, and that entire closing value walks into segment two as its principal, to be multiplied by 1,005 to the power 12. Mistake two is a clock slip inside a segment: two years of quarterly compounding is 8 periods, not 2, and one year of monthly compounding is 12 periods, not 1 — and because the clocks differ between segments, the conversion must be redone for each segment separately. Mistake three is rounding at the joint: carrying a rounded intermediate value into segment two plants a small error that the second segment then grows; keep the full calculator value alive across the joint and round only the final answer, two decimals, rand symbol in place. The drawing that blocks all three: one horizontal line, a tick for every event — deposit, rate change, end — with each segment's rate and period count written between its ticks. Fill in one growth factor per segment, multiply them across in order, and the longest finance question on the page collapses into a short chain of multiplications.
