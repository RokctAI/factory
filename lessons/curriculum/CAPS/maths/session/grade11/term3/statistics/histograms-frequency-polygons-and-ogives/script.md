# Part 1 — Expert

Grouped data is how the real world arrives: forty travel times, two hundred test marks, a thousand incomes, already sorted into class intervals. This session revises the grade ten toolkit for grouped data — intervals, histograms, the estimated mean — and then builds the two new pictures grade eleven adds: the frequency polygon and the ogive, the cumulative frequency curve that lets you read medians, quartiles and percentiles straight off a graph. One dataset runs through the whole session: the travel times of forty learners to school.

## Subtopic: Grouped Data Revision — Intervals, Histograms and the Estimated Mean

Here is the dataset. Forty learners recorded their travel time to school in minutes, grouped into five classes: from 0 up to but not including 10 minutes, 6 learners; from 10 up to 20, 10 learners; from 20 up to 30, 14 learners; from 30 up to 40, 7 learners; and from 40 up to 50, 3 learners. Check the total first, every time: 6 plus 10 plus 14 plus 7 plus 3 is 40. If the frequencies do not total the number of learners, something is mistyped, and every later answer inherits the error.

The histogram draws this as touching bars — touching, because the classes share boundaries with no gaps between them; a bar chart with gaps is for categories, not for continuous measurements like time. Height shows frequency, so the tallest bar, 20 to 30, is the modal class: more learners live there than in any other interval.

Grouping costs us the individual values, so the mean can only be estimated. The assumption: every learner in a class sits at its midpoint. Midpoints are 5, 15, 25, 35 and 45. Multiply each by its frequency: 6 times 5 is 30; 10 times 15 is 150; 14 times 25 is 350; 7 times 35 is 245; 3 times 45 is 135. The sum is 910, and 910 divided by 40 gives an estimated mean of 22,75 minutes. For the median's home: the middle of 40 values sits between the twentieth and twenty-first. Counting up, 6, then 16, then 30 — both the twentieth and twenty-first values fall inside 20 to 30, the median class.

Pause here — the questions for this section are with you now. They check boundaries, midpoints and the estimated mean: totals first, midpoints times frequencies, divide by n.

## Subtopic: Frequency Polygons

A histogram shows one dataset well; comparing two histograms on the same axes is a mess of overlapping bars. The frequency polygon fixes that. The recipe: mark the MIDPOINT of the top of each bar — midpoint of the class on the horizontal axis, frequency on the vertical — and join consecutive points with straight lines.

For our data, the points are 5 with height 6, then 15 with height 10, then 25 with height 14, then 35 with height 7, then 45 with height 3. To close the polygon properly, anchor it to the horizontal axis at both ends: plot a zero at the midpoint of the empty class before the first — minus 5 — and a zero at the midpoint of the empty class after the last — 55. The shape now begins and ends on the axis, forming a genuine polygon whose enclosed area matches the histogram's total.

Why bother with a second picture of the same numbers? Comparison. Two polygons on one grid — this year's travel times against last year's, boys against girls — stay readable where two histograms collide. The polygon also exposes shape at a glance: our polygon rises to a single peak at 25 and falls away with a longer slide on the right, showing a hump around the twenties with a tail of long-distance travellers.

Drawing errors to avoid: plotting at class boundaries instead of midpoints — the point for the first class sits at 5, not at 10; joining points with curves — polygon means straight edges; and forgetting the two anchoring zeros, which leaves the shape hanging in the air.

Stop for this section's questions now — midpoints, straight lines, and anchor both ends to the axis.

## Subtopic: Ogives — Building the Cumulative Frequency Curve

The ogive answers a different question. The histogram asks how many in EACH class; the ogive asks how many SO FAR — a running total. Build the cumulative frequency column first: 6 learners take under 10 minutes; 6 plus 10 is 16 under 20; 16 plus 14 is 30 under 30; 30 plus 7 is 37 under 40; and 37 plus 3 is 40 under 50. The final cumulative value must equal n, the total count — a built-in check.

Now the plotting rule that decides the marks: each cumulative frequency is plotted at the UPPER boundary of its class, because 16 is the count of learners below 20, not below 15 and not below 10. So the points are 10 with 6; 20 with 16; 30 with 30; 40 with 37; 50 with 40. One more point starts the curve: the lower boundary of the first class with a cumulative count of zero — the point 0 with 0 — since nobody travels for less than no time. Join the points with a smooth curve. The result has the classic lazy-S profile: climbing slowly through the sparse early classes, steeply through the crowded middle, then flattening as the data runs out.

Read the geometry of that S. The ogive is steepest exactly where the histogram is tallest — where data is dense, the running total grows fast. Flat stretches mean empty classes. An ogive never slopes downward, not ever, because a running total cannot shrink; if your curve dips, a subtraction slipped into a column that only ever adds.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — cumulative column first, plot at upper boundaries, start from zero, and end at n.

## Subtopic: Reading the Ogive — Median, Quartiles and Percentiles

The payoff. The ogive turns position questions into two ruler moves: go ACROSS from the position on the vertical axis to the curve, then DOWN to read the value.

The median sits at position n over 2 — here, 20. Across from 20 on the vertical axis to the curve, then down: the reading lands inside the 20-to-30 class, at about 23 minutes. Check that against the cumulative column: 16 learners sit below 20 minutes and 30 sit below 30 minutes, so the twentieth learner indeed lives in that class, a few minutes past its lower boundary. The lower quartile lives at position n over 4, which is 10: across from 10, down to about 14 minutes. The upper quartile lives at three n over 4, which is 30 — and the cumulative count reaches 30 exactly at the boundary 30, so the upper quartile is about 30 minutes. From these, the interquartile range is about 30 minus 14, which is 16 minutes: the middle half of the learners spans roughly sixteen minutes of travel.

Percentiles generalise the same move: the ninetieth percentile sits at position 90 percent of 40, which is 36 — across from 36, down to a reading of about 39 minutes. And the ogive answers threshold questions in reverse: how many learners travel MORE than 35 minutes? Go UP from 35 on the horizontal axis to the curve — the reading is about 33 or 34 learners below 35 — and subtract from 40: about 6 learners travel longer than 35 minutes.

Language matters at the finish: ogive readings are estimates, because the raw values are hidden inside classes. Write approximately 23 minutes, and keep the graph construction lines on your answer — examiners award marks for the visible across-and-down.

The final questions of this part are with you now — position first, across, down, and say the word estimate.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same three pictures from a taxi rank and a stokvel ledger — same data, same readings, with a story that makes each graph obvious.

## Subtopic: The Skyline and the Mountain Profile

Look at a city skyline: buildings of different heights standing shoulder to shoulder, no gaps. That is a histogram. Each building is a class interval, and its height is how many people live inside — how many of our forty learners fall into that band of travel times. The buildings must touch because time has no gaps: 19 minutes and 21 minutes live in neighbouring buildings with a shared wall at 20, not across a street. The tallest building is the modal class, the address where travel times crowd.

But the skyline hides the individuals. Once a learner enters the 20-to-30 building, we no longer know their floor — 21 or 29, the building swallows the detail. So when we estimate the mean, we make the fairest guess available: everyone stands in the middle of their building, at the midpoint. Six people at 5, ten at 15, fourteen at 25, seven at 35, three at 45 — total the guesses, divide by forty, and 22,75 minutes is the best mean the grouped data can give. That word estimate is doing honest work: it admits the guess.

Now put a dot on each rooftop's centre and connect the dots with straight lines, letting the line run down to the ground before the first building and after the last. The skyline becomes a mountain profile — the frequency polygon. Same information, but now two cities can be compared by laying their profiles on one grid, something two skylines refuse to allow.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on this are with you right now. Ask of every picture: buildings for one dataset, mountain profiles for a comparison.

## Subtopic: The Running Total Tells a Different Story

A stokvel treasurer keeps two columns: what came in THIS month, and the total SO FAR. Both describe the same money, but they answer different questions. March's deposit answers how good March was; the running total answers when the fund crossed ten thousand rand. The histogram is the this-month column. The ogive is the total-so-far column, drawn as a curve.

So build the ogive like a treasurer. After each class, write down the total so far: 6, then 16, then 30, then 37, then 40. And notice the treasurer's logic on where each number belongs: the total 16 is only complete at the END of the second class — at 20 minutes, not before. That is why ogive points sit at upper class boundaries, and why the curve starts at zero learners at zero minutes: before the first class, nothing has accumulated.

The curve's shape now reads like a bank statement. Steep stretch? Deposits pouring in — the crowded classes. Flat stretch? Quiet months — empty classes. And the curve can never come downhill, because a running total never shrinks; money already counted stays counted. If your ogive dips, you subtracted somewhere in a column that only ever adds.

Your questions for this part are up now — this-month numbers make bars, so-far numbers make the climbing curve, and the curve only ever climbs.

## Subtopic: Across and Down — Reading Answers off the Curve

Here is the move that makes the ogive worth building, and it is the same two-step every time: across, then down. Picture the forty learners lined up along the curve in travel-time order, from the sprinter who lives next door to the learner who crosses town.

Who is the middle of the line? Position 20 of 40. Put your ruler at 20 on the vertical axis, slide ACROSS until it touches the curve, then turn DOWN to the horizontal axis: about 23 minutes. That is the median — half the school arrives quicker, half slower. The quarter mark of the line, position 10, reads about 14 minutes: the lower quartile. The three-quarter mark, position 30, reads about 30 minutes: the upper quartile. The gap between the quartile readings, about 16 minutes, is the interquartile range — the span of the middle half of the line, the ordinary crowd with the extremes trimmed off.

The same ruler answers percentage questions of every flavour. Ninety percent of the line is position 36: across and down, about 39 minutes — the ninetieth percentile. And the reverse question — how many travel more than 35 minutes — walks the path backwards: UP from 35 to the curve, across to read about 33 or 34, and subtract from 40. About six learners.

Two small honesties finish the topic. Every reading is an estimate, so write the word — approximately 23 minutes — because the exact values are sealed inside the classes. And leave your dotted ruler lines on the graph: the examiner pays for the journey, not just the destination.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now — find the position in the line, go across, go down, and say approximately like you mean it.
