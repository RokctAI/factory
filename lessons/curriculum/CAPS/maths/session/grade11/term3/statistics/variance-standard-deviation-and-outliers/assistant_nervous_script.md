Hey, it's Bianca — if the words variance and standard deviation sound like they belong in a laboratory and not in your exam, let me shrink this whole topic down to its real size. Every measure in this lesson answers one plain question: how far does the data wander from home?

The standard deviation is nothing more than the average distance from the mean, computed in five small steps you already know how to do: find the mean, write each value's distance from it, square those distances so they stop cancelling, average the squares, then square root your way back to normal units. Mean, distances, squares, average, root. Say it twice and the formula sheet becomes unnecessary.

The five number summary and the box plot are even friendlier — no formulas at all, just sorting and pointing: the start, the quarter mark, the middle, the three-quarter mark, the end. The box is the ordinary neighbourhood where the middle half lives; the whiskers are the roads out to the extremes. A long whisker is the data telling you its story before you calculate anything.

Skewness questions want one sentence with evidence in it. The mean chases the tail — so mean above median means a right tail, mean below means a left tail. Quote the two numbers, name the direction, done.

And outliers are caught with fences, not feelings: one and a half interquartile ranges beyond each quartile. Compute the two fence numbers, compare the suspect, and write the verdict as an inequality. Then remember the topic's one big idea: outliers bully the mean and the standard deviation, but the median and interquartile range stand firm. Choose the firm pair when a bully is present, and say why. That sentence is a guaranteed mark — and this whole topic is arithmetic wearing a lab coat. You've got this!
