**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! Top of the pile: are we allowed to just use the calculator's statistics mode for standard deviation, or must we show the whole table?

**Tutor:** Both — and the order matters. The mark allocation on a standard deviation question is built around visible method: the mean, the column of deviations, the column of squared deviations, the sum, the division, the root. When the question says calculate, those steps are where the marks sit, and a bare final answer, however correct, collects only a fraction of them. So the table is not optional decoration; it is the answer. The calculator's statistics mode plays a different role: it is your checking instrument. Enter the data, read off the standard deviation, and compare it with your table's final line — a mismatch means a slip somewhere in the columns, found before the marker finds it. Two refinements. First, know which button to read: calculators offer two standard deviations, one dividing by n and one by n minus 1, and the CAPS syllabus uses the n version — sigma x, sometimes labelled sigma sub n. Reading the wrong one produces a slightly larger answer that looks plausible and is wrong. Second, when a question explicitly says use your calculator, the table may be skipped — but write down the mean and the standard deviation both, since a stray answer with no companion working cannot earn part marks if a digit is mistyped.

**Bianca:** Next one, asked with genuine outrage: why does dividing by five, not four, matter, and why do some textbooks divide by n minus one?

**Tutor:** The outrage is understandable because both versions genuinely exist, for different jobs. Dividing by n — five, in our netball data — treats your five matches as the complete story: this is the population standard deviation, the exact average of the squared distances for the data in front of you. Dividing by n minus 1 exists for a subtler situation: when your five values are only a SAMPLE drawn from something bigger, and you want to estimate the spread of the whole population you did not see. A sample tends to underestimate the full spread — extreme values are exactly the ones a small sample is likely to miss — so statisticians divide by the slightly smaller n minus 1 to stretch the estimate upward and correct the bias. That is a beautiful idea, and it belongs to university statistics. For CAPS grade eleven, the rule is simple and settled: divide by n, use the sigma-n button, and treat the given data as the whole population. In our example that means 58 over 5, giving a variance of 11,6 and a standard deviation of 3,41. If you divide by four you get a variance of 14,5 and a standard deviation of about 3,81 — recognisably different, and marked wrong. One habit protects you: after any calculator reading, ask whether your value matches the by-hand table; the mismatch between the two buttons is exactly how this error reveals itself.

**Bianca:** Third: for skewness, can I just look at the box plot, or must I calculate something?

**Tutor:** Either instrument is acceptable — what is compulsory is quoting evidence rather than intuition. Skewness questions are interpretation questions, and the memo looks for a claim plus a justification in the same sentence. Route one is numerical: compare the mean with the median. The median counts heads, the mean shares out the total, so a long tail drags the mean toward itself while leaving the median nearly alone. Mean visibly above median — as with our 56 against 54 — justifies positively skewed, skewed to the right. Mean below median justifies the left version. Route two is visual: describe the box plot. If the right whisker is far longer, or the median bar is squeezed toward the left wall of the box, the data stretches rightward — positive skew again. Choose whichever route the question makes convenient: if a box plot is printed, read it; if summary statistics are given, compare them. Two cautions. Do not name the hump — skewness follows the tail, and the phrase to burn in is the mean chases the tail. And when mean and median sit essentially together, say approximately symmetric — with grouped or messy real data, a difference of a fraction of a mark is togetherness, not skew. The full-credit sentence is short: the data is positively skewed, because the mean of 56 exceeds the median of 54, dragged by the extreme value 84.

**Bianca:** Last one: if the outlier rule flags a value, do we throw it out of the dataset?

**Tutor:** No — flagging is a finding, not a sentence. The fence rule is a detector: it says 84 is implausibly far from the pattern of the rest, and that always deserves investigation before deletion. An outlier can be an error — a mistyped 48, a broken measuring instrument — and if the source confirms an error, correcting or excluding it is legitimate, stated openly. But an outlier can equally be the most important value in the dataset: one brilliant learner, one genuinely fraudulent transaction, one record rainfall. Deleting real data because it is inconvenient is not statistics; it is storytelling. What grade eleven expects from you is threefold. First, detect by rule, in writing: compute the fences, show 84 lies beyond 70, conclude outlier. Second, describe the CONSEQUENCE: with 84 included the mean is 56 and the standard deviation about 10,4; without it they fall to about 52,9 and 4,7 — so the fragile measures were being substantially distorted by a single value. Third, choose reporting tools accordingly: keep the value in the dataset, but summarise the data with the robust pair, median and interquartile range, and say in one sentence that you chose them BECAUSE an outlier is present. That final sentence — the choice plus its reason — is precisely what the interpretation mark at the end of a statistics question is waiting for.
