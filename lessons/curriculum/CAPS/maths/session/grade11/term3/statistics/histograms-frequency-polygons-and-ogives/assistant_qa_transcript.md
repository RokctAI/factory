**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! The one asked most: how do I keep the polygon and the ogive apart? I keep plotting the wrong thing at the wrong place.

**Tutor:** Separate them by the question each graph answers, and the plotting rules follow on their own. The frequency polygon answers how many in EACH class — it is the histogram's own numbers, so each point carries an ordinary frequency, and it sits at the class MIDPOINT, the fairest single address for everyone inside that class. The ogive answers how many SO FAR — a running total — so each point carries a cumulative frequency, and it sits at the UPPER boundary, because the running total is only complete once the whole class has been counted in. Say the pairing as a chant: polygon, plain frequency, midpoint; ogive, cumulative frequency, upper boundary. The start and end points differ for the same reason. The polygon anchors to zero at the midpoints of the empty classes on either side, so the shape closes onto the axis. The ogive starts at zero at the LOWER boundary of the first class, because nothing has accumulated before the data begins, and it must finish at n — forty in our lesson — because by the end everything has been counted. If your last ogive point is not n, the cumulative column has an arithmetic slip, and that check takes two seconds.

***

**Bianca:** Next one, from three different people: why is the mean of grouped data only an estimate, and how far off can it actually be?

**Tutor:** It is an estimate because grouping deliberately throws information away, and no formula can conjure it back. Once fourteen learners are recorded as simply between 20 and 30 minutes, their individual values are gone — one might be 20,5, another 29. The midpoint assumption places all fourteen at 25, which is the fairest neutral guess: it assumes the values inside the class are spread evenly, so the ones below the midpoint balance the ones above. How far off can it drift? At worst, by less than half a class width per learner — if every learner in a class huddled at its very bottom, the guess of the midpoint overshoots each of them by nearly 5 minutes in our 10-wide classes. In practice the errors in different classes point in different directions and mostly cancel, so the estimated mean of 22,75 is typically within a minute or so of the truth. Two exam consequences. First, always call it an estimated mean in words — that vocabulary earns a mark. Second, if a question supplies both the raw data AND a grouped table, compute the real mean from the raw data when asked for accuracy, and use the table only for the grouped-data techniques it requests.

***

**Bianca:** Third: on the ogive, how do I know whether to start reading from the vertical axis or the horizontal axis? People get lost halfway through.

**Tutor:** Decide before touching the graph by naming what kind of thing the question hands you. If the question hands you a POSITION in the ordered line — the median at n over 2, a quartile at n over 4 or three n over 4, a percentile at some percentage of n — then positions live on the vertical axis, so you start there: across to the curve, then down, and the answer is a data value in minutes. If the question hands you a VALUE — how many learners take more than 35 minutes, how many finish under a quarter of an hour — then values live on the horizontal axis, so you start there: up to the curve, then across, and the answer is a count of learners. In short: given a position, answer a value; given a value, answer a count. One more turn of the handle for more-than questions: the ogive always reads how many are BELOW a value, so a more-than answer needs one subtraction from n — up from 35, read roughly 34, and 40 minus 34 leaves about 6. Forgetting that subtraction is the single commonest ogive error, and writing the subtraction line explicitly is what convinces the marker you did not just misread the graph.

***

**Bianca:** Last one: my ogive readings never exactly match the memo. How close must I be, and how do I protect the marks?

**Tutor:** Memos for graph-reading questions carry a tolerance band, precisely because two careful people with two sharp pencils will read slightly different values off the same curve — the median might be marked as anything from 22 to 24 minutes, for instance. So a small mismatch is expected and forgiven; what loses marks is unshown method and impossible answers. Protect yourself four ways. First, draw the construction lines and leave them there — the dotted line across from 20 and down to your reading IS the method mark, and an answer floating alone can earn nothing if it drifts outside the band. Second, plot precisely: use a sharp pencil, mark points as small crosses at exact coordinates, and draw the ogive as one smooth curve through every point rather than wobbly segments — a curve plotted 2 millimetres off manufactures a reading outside the tolerance. Third, sanity-check each reading against the cumulative table before writing it: the twentieth learner must land in the 20-to-30 class because the running total says 16 below 20 and 30 below 30; a median reading of 17 contradicts the table and must be a graph slip. Fourth, use estimate language — approximately 23 minutes — which is both honest and expected. Accurate plotting, visible lines, a table check and the word approximately: that combination makes ogive questions some of the safest marks in Paper Two.
