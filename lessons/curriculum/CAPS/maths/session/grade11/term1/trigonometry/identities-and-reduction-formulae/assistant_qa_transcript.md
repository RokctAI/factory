**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! The first one comes up every single year: why do we need the x, y, r definitions at all? The triangle definitions worked fine in grade ten.

**Tutor:** They worked fine because grade ten never asked about an angle past ninety degrees, and a right-angled triangle physically cannot hold one — the other two angles of a right-angled triangle must add to ninety, so each is acute. The moment a question says sine of one hundred and fifty degrees, the triangle has nothing to offer. The coordinate definitions rescue the whole subject: put the angle at the origin, pick any point on its arm, and define sine as y over r, cosine as x over r, tangent as y over x. For an acute angle this gives exactly the same numbers as the triangle — opposite over hypotenuse is y over r — so nothing you learned is thrown away. But the new definitions keep working at one hundred and fifty degrees, at three hundred degrees, at minus forty degrees, because coordinates happily go negative where triangle sides cannot. That is also where negative ratio values come from: cosine of an obtuse angle is negative not by decree but because the point's x-coordinate genuinely sits to the left of the origin. So think of it as an upgrade that contains the old version, the way a map of the whole province contains the map of your street.

**Bianca:** Next: is there a way to remember all the reduction formulae without memorising a dozen separate lines? People are drowning in them.

**Tutor:** There are exactly two facts to carry, and everything else is manufactured on the spot. Fact one: for one eighty minus, one eighty plus, three sixty minus, and minus theta, the function never changes — sine stays sine, cosine stays cosine — and only the sign can flip. Fact two: the sign is decided by the quadrant, through CAST. So when you meet sine of one eighty plus theta, you do not search your memory; you ask where one eighty plus theta lives — third quadrant — and whether sine is positive there — no, only tangent is. So the answer is minus sine theta, deduced in two seconds. The only extra rule is ninety degrees, where the function does change: sine and cosine swap, and then the quadrant still sets the sign. Ninety plus theta is second quadrant, so sine of ninety plus theta is plus cosine theta, but cosine of ninety plus theta is minus sine theta. Two facts and a diagram replace twelve memorised formulae, and unlike memory, deduction does not collapse under exam pressure. Practise producing each formula from the diagram until it takes under five seconds — that speed is the real preparation.

**Bianca:** This one caused an argument: in a proof, are we allowed to work on both sides of the identity at once? Different teachers seem to say different things.

**Tutor:** The safe rule, and the one CAPS marking follows, is that you work on one side at a time and never treat the identity as an equation. The danger of working on both sides is logical: if you assume the statement is true and manipulate it into something true, you have proved nothing, because false statements can also lead to true ones after squaring or multiplying. The accepted formats are these. Best: start from the more complicated side — usually the one with fractions — and simplify it step by step until it becomes the other side. Also acceptable: simplify the left side to some expression, separately simplify the right side to the same expression, and say both sides reduce to it. Not acceptable: cross-multiplying the identity and showing the results match. In practice the complicated side almost always wants one of three openings: put everything over a common denominator, replace tangent with sine over cosine, or replace a one minus a square using the square identity. In our worked proof, the common denominator was the opening, and the square identity finished it. And remember the follow-up question that hides in every proof: state the values for which the identity is undefined, by checking every denominator you used, including ones that appeared mid-proof.

**Bianca:** Last one: when a question says sine theta equals three fifths and theta is obtuse, why is it wrong to press inverse sine and work with the angle?

**Tutor:** Two separate reasons, one about marks and one about mathematics. The calculator's inverse sine of three fifths returns thirty-six comma eight seven degrees — an acute angle, in the first quadrant. But the question told you theta is obtuse, so the calculator has answered a different question: it always returns the reference solution, not your solution. If you then compute cosine of that acute angle you get plus four fifths, and the sign is wrong — the single mark the question was really testing. You could repair it by subtracting from one eighty, but now you are carrying a rounded decimal through the rest of the problem, and the final answers become approximations where exact fractions were demanded. The sketch method avoids both traps: place y equal to three and r equal to five in the second quadrant, get x equals minus four from Pythagoras, and read off cosine as minus four fifths and tangent as minus three quarters — exact, correctly signed, and worth full marks. The habit to build: when a question gives a ratio and a quadrant, reach for a sketch; the calculator is for the final decimal step of a calculation, never for reasoning about quadrants.
