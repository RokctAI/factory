# Part 1 — Expert

Grade ten trigonometry lived inside a right-angled triangle, so no angle ever passed ninety degrees. This session removes that ceiling. Four pieces of equipment do it: the two identities that connect the three ratios to each other, the sign rules that govern the four quadrants, the reduction formulae that turn any angle into an acute one, and the co-function rules that let sine and cosine trade places at ninety degrees. Together they are the engine room of every trigonometry question in Paper 2.

## Subtopic: The Two Identities Every Proof Leans On

Redefine the ratios first, because the triangle definitions cannot survive past ninety degrees. Place an angle theta at the origin, measured anticlockwise from the positive x-axis, and pick any point on its arm. Call the coordinates of that point x and y, and call its distance from the origin r, which is always positive. Then sine theta is y over r, cosine theta is x over r, and tangent theta is y over x. Nothing about this definition needs the angle to be acute — it works for any angle at all.

The first identity falls straight out. Divide sine by cosine: y over r, divided by x over r. The r cancels, leaving y over x — which is exactly tangent. So tangent theta equals sine theta over cosine theta, wherever cosine theta is not zero.

The second comes from Pythagoras. The point, the origin and the foot of the perpendicular form a right-angled triangle, so x squared plus y squared equals r squared. Divide every term by r squared: x squared over r squared, plus y squared over r squared, equals one. The first term is cosine squared theta and the second is sine squared theta. So sine squared theta plus cosine squared theta equals one — the square identity, true for every angle without exception.

Learn its two rearrangements as reflexes: sine squared theta equals one minus cosine squared theta, and cosine squared theta equals one minus sine squared theta. Whenever a one minus a square appears in a trigonometric expression, an identity is being offered.

Use them once now. Simplify one minus sine squared theta, all multiplied by tangent theta. The bracket is cosine squared theta. Multiply by sine theta over cosine theta: one cosine cancels, leaving sine theta times cosine theta.

One caution attaches to the quotient identity. Tangent is a fraction with cosine underneath, so tangent does not exist where cosine equals zero — at ninety degrees, two hundred and seventy degrees, and every angle differing from those by full turns. An identity involving tangent holds for all angles except the ones that break its denominators, and examiners ask for exactly those exceptions.

Pause here — the questions for this section reach you now. They ask you to rebuild both identities from the x, y, r definitions and to spot a disguised one minus a square.

## Subtopic: Signs in the Four Quadrants — the CAST Rule

The x, y, r definitions do something the triangle never could: they let ratios go negative, and the signs follow directly from coordinates.

Walk the four quadrants. In the first quadrant both x and y are positive, so all three ratios are positive. In the second quadrant x turns negative while y stays positive: sine, which is y over r, stays positive, but cosine and tangent go negative. In the third quadrant both x and y are negative: sine and cosine are negative, but tangent — a negative over a negative — is positive. In the fourth quadrant y is negative and x positive: cosine positive, sine and tangent negative.

The memory aid is the word CAST, written into the quadrants starting at the fourth: C in the fourth quadrant where Cosine is positive, A in the first where All are positive, S in the second where only Sine is positive, T in the third where only Tangent is positive.

Now the technique the whole topic runs on: working with a sketch instead of a calculator. Suppose sine theta equals three fifths, and theta is obtuse. Obtuse means second quadrant. Draw the arm in the second quadrant. Sine is y over r, so take y equal to three and r equal to five. Pythagoras gives x squared equals twenty-five minus nine, sixteen, so x is four in size — but in the second quadrant x is negative, so x equals minus four. Now read off everything: cosine theta is minus four fifths, and tangent theta is three over minus four, minus three quarters.

The sketch method earns full marks and never rounds. The single most common error is forgetting the sign on x or y — the quadrant decides it, so mark the quadrant before you write any number.

Stop for this section's questions now — place each ratio in its quadrant, and let the sketch carry the signs for you.

## Subtopic: Reduction Formulae — One Eighty, Three Sixty and the Minus

A reduction formula rewrites the ratio of a big angle as the ratio of an acute one. There are three families, and one rule powers all of them: the size of the answer comes from the acute angle, and the sign comes from the quadrant the big angle lives in.

Family one: one hundred and eighty degrees minus theta lands in the second quadrant. Only sine is positive there, so sine of one eighty minus theta equals sine theta, while cosine of one eighty minus theta equals minus cosine theta and tangent of one eighty minus theta equals minus tangent theta.

Family two: one hundred and eighty degrees plus theta lands in the third quadrant, where only tangent is positive. So sine goes to minus sine theta, cosine to minus cosine theta, and tangent stays tangent theta.

Family three: three hundred and sixty degrees minus theta lands in the fourth quadrant, where only cosine is positive. Sine goes to minus sine theta, cosine stays cosine theta, tangent goes to minus tangent theta. A negative angle, minus theta, points into the same fourth quadrant, so it obeys the same signs: sine of minus theta is minus sine theta, and cosine of minus theta is cosine theta. Adding or subtracting full turns of three hundred and sixty degrees changes nothing at all.

Apply the machine. Cosine of one hundred and twenty degrees: write one twenty as one eighty minus sixty. Second quadrant, cosine negative, so the answer is minus cosine sixty, which is minus a half. Sine of two hundred and ten degrees: two ten is one eighty plus thirty, third quadrant, sine negative — minus sine thirty, minus a half. Tangent of three hundred degrees: three hundred is three sixty minus sixty, fourth quadrant, tangent negative — minus tangent sixty, minus the square root of three.

One combined evaluation, no calculator: sine of one hundred and fifty degrees times tangent of two hundred and twenty-five degrees. Sine one fifty is sine of one eighty minus thirty, which is sine thirty, a half. Tangent two twenty-five is tangent of one eighty plus forty-five, third quadrant where tangent is positive, so tangent forty-five, one. The product is a half.

The discipline: always reduce to an acute angle, always decide the sign from the quadrant before touching the size, and write both steps down. A sign decided by guesswork is a coin flip worth two marks.

The questions for this section are with you now — name the quadrant first, fix the sign second, and only then swap in the acute angle.

## Subtopic: Co-functions, Simplifying Chains and Where an Identity Breaks

The last family involves ninety degrees, and it behaves differently: crossing ninety swaps the function. Sine of ninety minus theta equals cosine theta, and cosine of ninety minus theta equals sine theta — the complement rule you glimpsed in grade ten. For ninety plus theta, the swap still happens but the second quadrant sets the sign: sine of ninety plus theta equals cosine theta, positive because sine rules the second quadrant, while cosine of ninety plus theta equals minus sine theta.

The summary worth memorising: one eighty and three sixty keep the function and change only the sign; ninety swaps sine and cosine, then the quadrant sets the sign.

Now run a full simplification chain, the standard examination format. Simplify: sine of one eighty plus x, times cosine of three sixty minus x, times tangent of minus x, all divided by cosine of ninety minus x, times sine of ninety plus x. Take the numerator piece by piece. Sine of one eighty plus x is minus sine x. Cosine of three sixty minus x is cosine x. Tangent of minus x is minus tangent x. Two minuses multiply away, so the numerator is sine x times cosine x times tangent x; write tangent as sine over cosine and the cosines cancel, giving sine squared x. The denominator: cosine of ninety minus x is sine x, and sine of ninety plus x is cosine x, so the denominator is sine x cosine x. Divide: sine squared x over sine x cosine x is sine x over cosine x — tangent x. A five-line chain collapsing to a single ratio is exactly how these questions are designed.

Finally, proving an identity, with its attached question about validity. Prove that sine x over one plus cosine x, plus one plus cosine x over sine x, equals two over sine x. Work on the left. The common denominator is sine x times one plus cosine x. The first numerator becomes sine squared x; the second becomes one plus cosine x, all squared, which expands to one plus two cosine x plus cosine squared x. Add them: sine squared x plus cosine squared x is one, so the total is two plus two cosine x — factorise to two times one plus cosine x. The bracket cancels with the denominator, leaving two over sine x. Proved.

For which values of x does it hold? Exactly where no denominator dies. Sine x must not be zero, and one plus cosine x must not be zero. Cosine x equals minus one only at one eighty plus full turns, and sine is zero at every multiple of one eighty — so the second condition is swallowed by the first. The identity holds for all x except multiples of one hundred and eighty degrees. Writing that sentence is a mark on its own.

The final questions of this part arrive now — swap at ninety, keep at one eighty and three sixty, and interrogate every denominator before you declare an identity true.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same machinery again, built from a bicycle wheel and a folded piece of paper — same rules, same answers, no calculator anywhere.

## Subtopic: The Spoke and the Shadow

Picture a bicycle wheel lying flat, one spoke painted red, spinning anticlockwise from the three o'clock position. The angle is how far the spoke has turned. The tip of the spoke always sits some distance east or west, and some distance north or south. Sine is the north-south share of the spoke, cosine is the east-west share, and tangent is the one divided by the other. The spoke's length never changes — that is r, always positive.

Turn the spoke to one hundred and twenty degrees. It now leans into the north-west. Its north-south share is still upward, still positive — but its east-west share points west, negative. Nothing mystical happened: cosine went negative because the tip is west of centre. Every sign in this topic is just a direction — north or south for sine, east or west for cosine.

And here is the identity as a picture. The east-west share and the north-south share are two sides of a right-angled triangle whose longest side is the spoke itself. Pythagoras says the squares of the shares add up to the square of the spoke. Shrink the spoke to length one and the statement reads: sine squared plus cosine squared equals one. It is not a formula to memorise; it is Pythagoras riding a bicycle.

Tangent is the odd one out because it is a fraction of the other two — north-south over east-west. When the spoke points straight up, the east-west share is zero, and dividing by zero is forbidden. That is why tangent simply does not exist at ninety degrees.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on this section come to you now. For each one, ask only two things: which direction is the tip pointing, and which share is the question about?

## Subtopic: Four Neighbourhoods, One Rule

The wheel divides the world into four quarters, and each quarter has its own signature. First quarter, everything east and north: all three ratios positive. Second quarter, west but still north: only the north-south ratio — sine — stays positive. Third quarter, west and south: both shares negative, but their fraction, tangent, is a negative over a negative — positive. Fourth quarter, east but south: only cosine positive.

Read the letters around the wheel starting from the fourth quarter: C, A, S, T. Cosine, All, Sine, Tangent — whichever letter sits in a quarter names the only positive thing there.

Now the trick that replaces the calculator. Someone tells you sine of an angle is three fifths and the angle is obtuse. Obtuse means the spoke is in the second quarter. Sine is north-south over spoke, so the tip sits three units north on a spoke of five. How far west? Pythagoras: five squared minus three squared is sixteen, so four units — west, so minus four. Now every answer is sitting in the sketch: cosine is minus four fifths, tangent is three over minus four. No button was pressed, and no rounding happened. The sketch is the calculator.

Your questions for this part are up now. Draw the quarter first, place the two shares with their signs, and read the answers off the drawing.

## Subtopic: Folding the Angle Back Home

Every big angle is an acute angle wearing a costume. Take one hundred and fifty degrees: fold the wheel along the north-south line and the spoke lands on thirty degrees. The fold changed east into west and nothing else — so the size of every ratio at one fifty matches thirty, and only the signs that depend on east-west flip. That is the whole content of the reduction formulae: one eighty minus theta is a fold, one eighty plus theta is a half-turn, three sixty minus theta is a fold along the east-west line, and each move carries its own quadrant, whose letter from the CAST wheel tells you which ratios keep a plus.

The recipe never varies. Step one: find the acute home angle — the gap between the spoke and the east-west line. Step two: name the quarter and its sign for the ratio in question. Step three: write sign, then home angle. Cosine of one twenty: home angle sixty, second quarter, cosine negative there — minus cosine sixty, minus a half. Sine of two ten: home angle thirty, third quarter, sine negative — minus a half. Tangent of two twenty-five: home angle forty-five, third quarter, tangent positive — plus one.

Ninety degrees plays a different game: it swaps the players instead of the signs. Turn the spoke a quarter-turn and the north-south share becomes what the east-west share was. So sine of ninety minus theta is cosine theta, and cosine of ninety minus theta is sine theta. Keep the two behaviours separate in your head: one eighty and three sixty change signs only; ninety swaps sine with cosine, and then the quarter sets the sign as usual.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: find the home angle, let the CAST letter fix the sign, and whenever ninety degrees appears, swap before you sign.
