**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! First one, asked in three different ways: why does k have to be an integer, and can it be negative?

**Tutor:** The letter k counts complete revolutions of the wheel, and a revolution is an all-or-nothing thing — you cannot add on half a revolution and land in the same place, so k must be a whole number. It can absolutely be negative, and forgetting that costs real marks on interval questions. A negative k rewinds the wheel: if thirty degrees is a solution of sine x equals zero comma five, then k equals minus one gives thirty minus three sixty, minus three hundred and thirty degrees, and that angle points in exactly the same direction, so it satisfies the equation identically. On an interval like minus three sixty to three sixty, half the answers usually come from negative values of k, so a learner who only tries k equal to zero and one hands in half a solution. The professional habit is mechanical: for each family, run k through minus two, minus one, zero, one, two, test every result against the interval, and stop in each direction once you have clearly left the interval — the results move in steps of the period, so once you are out on one side, you stay out.

**Bianca:** Next: for cosine, some write plus or minus alpha plus k three sixty, others write two separate families like for sine. Are both right?

**Tutor:** Both are right, and it is worth seeing why they describe the same set. Cosine is positive in the first and fourth quadrants. Solving cosine x equals zero comma five the sine way, you would write sixty plus k three sixty for the first quadrant, and three hundred minus, that is, three sixty minus sixty, plus k three sixty for the fourth. Now notice that three hundred degrees and minus sixty degrees point in the same direction — three hundred is just minus sixty after one forward revolution. So the second family, three hundred plus k three sixty, is exactly the family minus sixty plus k three sixty with the counter shifted by one, and shifting an integer counter changes nothing about the set. That is why the two families compress into the tidy form plus or minus sixty plus k three sixty. Sine cannot compress this way, because its two doors — alpha and one eighty minus alpha — are mirror images across the vertical axis, not negatives of each other. Use whichever form your teacher prefers, but be able to expand the plus-minus back into two families, because interval harvesting is easier when each family is written out on its own line.

**Bianca:** This one caused a proper debate: solving sine x equals cosine x, one group divided by cosine and another said dividing is illegal and you must factorise. Who won?

**Tutor:** The dividers won this particular battle, but only because they could defend the division — and the defenders of factorising are right in general, so both groups deserve credit. The blanket rule from algebra stands: dividing an equation by an expression containing x risks destroying solutions, exactly like x squared equals three x loses x equals zero when you divide by x. The question is whether cosine x can be zero at a solution of this equation. Suppose it were: cosine x equals zero forces sine x to be plus one or minus one, by the square identity. Then the equation sine x equals cosine x would read one equals zero or minus one equals zero, both impossible. So no solution of this equation has cosine x equal to zero, the division throws nothing away, and tangent x equals one follows legally, giving forty-five plus k one eighty. The one-line defence — if cosine x were zero, sine x would be plus or minus one, contradiction — is what turns a risky move into full marks. When you cannot construct such a defence, do not divide: bring everything to one side and factorise, and let each factor produce its own family of solutions.

**Bianca:** Last one: how do we know how many answers an interval should contain before we start, so we can catch our own mistakes?

**Tutor:** Count crossings on a rough sketch — it takes fifteen seconds and it audits your algebra. Sine and cosine each cross a middle height, any value strictly between minus one and one, exactly twice per period of three hundred and sixty degrees. Tangent crosses every height exactly once per period of one eighty. So multiply crossings per period by the number of periods in the interval. Sine x equals zero comma five on minus three sixty to three sixty: two crossings per turn, two turns, four answers — and indeed we found minus three thirty, minus two ten, thirty and one fifty. The exceptions are the boundary heights. At plus one or minus one, sine and cosine touch rather than cross — the peak and the floor of the wave — so there is only one solution per period, not two. And at zero there are two crossings per period but they are evenly spaced every one eighty, which learners sometimes miscount as four. So the audit is: classify the height as middle, boundary or zero, count per period, multiply by the periods, and if your list disagrees with the count, go hunting for the missing k or the extra sign before you hand in.
