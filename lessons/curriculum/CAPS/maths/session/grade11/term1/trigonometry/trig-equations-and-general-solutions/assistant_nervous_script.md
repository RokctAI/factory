Hey, it's Bianca — I saw the moment the plus-k-three-sixty appeared and half the class quietly panicked. An equation with infinitely many answers sounds like an equation you can never finish. It is actually the opposite: one short line finishes all of them at once, and the line writes itself in four moves.

Move one: isolate. Get the equation down to sine x equals a number, cosine x equals a number, or tangent x equals a number. Nothing else happens before that.

Move two: size. Feed the POSITIVE version of the number into the inverse function and get the reference angle. The calculator's job ends there — it finds sizes, never places.

Move three: place. The sign of the number picks the two quadrants, exactly like the CAST wheel from the last lesson. Write the one or two anchor angles inside the first turn.

Move four: repeat. Add k times three sixty for sine and cosine, k times one eighty for tangent, and say to yourself what k really is — a counter of full loops, forwards or backwards. If the question gives an interval, run k through minus two, minus one, zero, one, two, and keep only what lands inside the fence.

The scary-looking quadratic one — two sine squared x minus sine x minus one — is a costume party. Give sine x a nickname, call it s, and it is last term's factorising. Solve for s, swap the nickname back, and run the four moves on each small equation. And if s comes out bigger than one or smaller than minus one, reject it in writing; sine and cosine never leave that range, and the examiner pays you for saying so.

Tonight: solve sine x equals a half, cosine x equals minus a half, and tangent x equals one, general solutions only, then harvest each on zero to three sixty. Three equations, fifteen minutes, and the infinite stops being frightening. You've got this!
