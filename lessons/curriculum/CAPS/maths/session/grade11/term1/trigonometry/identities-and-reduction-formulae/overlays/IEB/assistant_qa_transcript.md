**Bianca:** Let's take the class questions! First one: why did we have to redefine sine and cosine with x, y and r? The triangle definitions worked fine last year.

**Tutor:** They worked fine because last year every angle you met was squeezed between zero and ninety degrees — a right-angled triangle physically cannot hold an angle bigger than that, since its other two angles must share what remains of one eighty. The moment a question says the angle is obtuse, or two hundred and forty degrees, or negative, the triangle has nothing to say. The coordinate definitions rescue everything: put the angle at the origin, pick any point on its arm, and define sine as y over r, cosine as x over r, tangent as y over x. For acute angles this agrees exactly with opposite over hypotenuse — the triangle is still there, drawn between the arm and the x-axis. But now the definitions keep working when the arm swings into any quadrant, and they bring a gift the triangle never offered: signs. In the second quadrant x is negative, so cosine is negative; in the third, y is negative too. Negative ratios are not a new convention bolted on — they fall straight out of coordinates being negative. And both identities come from the same picture: divide the definitions to get tangent equals sine over cosine, and divide Pythagoras by r squared to get sine squared plus cosine squared equals one.

**Bianca:** Next: when we're told something like cosine theta is minus five thirteenths and given a quadrant, why must we draw the sketch? A few people just used inverse cos on the calculator.

**Tutor:** The calculator route fails twice on that question. First, precision: inverse cos of minus five over thirteen gives a rounded decimal angle, and every ratio you compute from a rounded angle inherits the rounding — while the marker wants exact fractions like minus twelve thirteenths, which no decimal detour will return. Second, and worse, the calculator only ever hands back ONE angle, usually in the wrong quadrant for the question. Inverse cos of a negative gives an angle in the second quadrant — but your question said theta lies between one eighty and two seventy, the third. Same cosine, different angle, different sine. The sketch cannot make either mistake. You mark the third quadrant, write x equals minus five and r equals thirteen — r is a distance, always positive, so the minus must live on x — then Pythagoras gives the missing side as twelve, and the quadrant forces y to be minus twelve, since the third quadrant lies south. Now sine and tangent read off as exact fractions with correct signs: minus twelve thirteenths, and twelve fifths. Three lines, full marks, no button. The sketch is not the slow method; it is the only method that answers the question actually asked.

**Bianca:** This one's about reductions: is there a way to stop mixing up what one eighty plus does versus three sixty minus? The sign chaos is real.

**Tutor:** Stop memorising the six formulae as separate spells and use the one engine underneath them: size from the home angle, sign from the neighbourhood. Every reduction question is the same three moves. Move one: find the home angle, the acute gap between the arm and the horizontal axis — for two forty it is sixty, for three fifteen it is forty-five, for one fifty it is thirty. Move two: name the quadrant the big angle lives in and recall its CAST letter — two forty is third quadrant, letter T, so only tangent is positive there. Move three: write the sign first, then the same function of the home angle. Sine of two forty: third quadrant, sine not the letter, so negative — minus sine sixty. Done. The formulae you were mixing up are just this engine pre-run for the three families: one eighty minus lands second quadrant, one eighty plus lands third, three sixty minus lands fourth. If you ever blank in a test, run the engine from scratch; it takes ten seconds. The only special case to keep separate is ninety degrees, because ninety does something none of the others do — it swaps sine and cosine before the quadrant applies the sign. My rule of thumb: horizontal references, one eighty and three sixty, never change the function; the vertical reference, ninety, always does.

***

**Bianca:** Last question: in identity proofs, why do we lose a mark for not saying which x values the identity holds for? The proof itself was perfect.

**Tutor:** Because an identity is a claim about ALL angles, and a fraction quietly breaks that claim wherever a denominator hits zero — so the honest statement of your result must name the broken places. Think about what you proved tonight: one over one minus cosine x, plus one over one plus cosine x, equals two over sine squared x. At x equals zero the first denominator is zero; at one eighty the second one is; and sine squared x dies at every multiple of one eighty. At those angles the left side is not false — it is meaningless, because division by zero has no value. So the finished answer says: the identity holds for all x except multiples of one hundred and eighty degrees. Finding that exception set is a small algebra job of its own: list every denominator in the ORIGINAL statement, set each equal to zero, solve, and take the union — then notice when one condition swallows the others, as sine x equals zero swallowed both cosine conditions here. The same duty applies to any identity carrying tangent, because tangent smuggles a hidden cosine denominator: tangent exists only where cosine is non-zero, so ninety and two seventy join the banned list automatically. The proof shows the equation is true; the validity sentence shows you know where it is even allowed to speak. Markers pay for both.
