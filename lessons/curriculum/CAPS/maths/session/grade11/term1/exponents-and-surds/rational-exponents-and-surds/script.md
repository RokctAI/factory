# Part 1 — Expert

Grade ten exponents dealt in whole numbers only. This session opens the exponent to fractions, and with that one move the root sign and the power sign become the same instruction written two ways. Four things follow: simplifying expressions whose bases hide behind composite numbers, solving equations where the unknown sits in the exponent, treating surds as exact numbers, and the checking discipline surd equations demand.

## Subtopic: Rational Exponents — a Root Written as a Power

The definition first, because everything leans on it. For x greater than zero and q a positive whole number, x to the power one over q means the q-th root of x. Extend it: x to the power p over q means the q-th root of x to the power p. Bottom number is the root, top number is the power. Read a fractional exponent that way every time and the topic loses most of its fear.

The restriction x greater than zero is not decoration. The square root of a negative number is non-real, and once negative bases are allowed the exponent laws start contradicting one another, so CAPS keeps the base positive whenever the exponent is rational.

Every law you already own survives: multiplying adds exponents, dividing subtracts, a power of a power multiplies, a negative exponent means the reciprocal, and any non-zero base to the power zero is one.

Work sixteen to the power three quarters. Write sixteen as two to the fourth — the most useful habit in this topic, turning every base into a prime. Two to the fourth, all to the power three quarters, multiplies the exponents: four times three quarters is three, so the answer is two cubed, eight. The root route agrees: the fourth root of sixteen is two, and two cubed is eight.

Work twenty-seven to the power minus two thirds. The minus flips it: one over twenty-seven to the power two thirds. Twenty-seven is three cubed, so three cubed to the power two thirds is three squared, nine. The answer is one ninth — inverted, never negative.

One more, with variables. Sixteen x to the eighth, all to the power three quarters, is eight x to the sixth.

The trap here is the sum. The square root of nine plus sixteen is the root of twenty-five, five — not three plus four. Roots and powers distribute over multiplication and division, never over addition.

Pause here — the questions for this section are with you now. They hand you fractional exponents to convert into roots and back, and one will test whether you split a root across a plus sign.

## Subtopic: Simplifying — Prime Bases and Common Factors

Simplification questions come in two families, each with one opening move.

Family one: everything can be written to a common prime base. Simplify nine to the power x plus one, times three to the power two x minus one, all over twenty-seven to the power x. Nine is three squared, so the first factor is three to the power two x plus two. Twenty-seven is three cubed, so the denominator is three to the power three x. Add the numerator exponents: two x plus two, plus two x minus one, gives three to the power four x plus one. Subtract three x, and the answer is three to the power x plus one.

Family two: nothing shares a base neatly, but a term factorises out. Simplify two to the power x plus three, minus two to the power x plus one, all over three times two to the power x. The numerator is a difference of two powers, and the smaller power is the common factor. Two to the power x plus three equals two to the x times eight; two to the power x plus one equals two to the x times two. So the numerator is two to the x, times the bracket eight minus two — six times two to the x. Divide by three times two to the x and the powers cancel, leaving two. A whole expression in x collapsing to a plain number is normal here.

The sign between the terms chooses your method. Multiplication and division mean prime bases and laws; a plus or minus means factorise, because there is no law for adding powers.

The error that costs most marks is reading two to the power x plus three as two to the x plus eight. It is two to the x MULTIPLIED by eight. Addition in the exponent is multiplication in the base.

Stop for this section's questions now — decide first whether the expression wants a common prime base or a common factor, and never let an exponent addition become a base addition.

## Subtopic: Exponential Equations, Including the Hidden Quadratic

When the unknown sits in the exponent, force both sides onto the same base and equate exponents — legal because an exponential function takes each value exactly once.

Solve three to the power two x minus one equals eighty-one. Eighty-one is three to the fourth, so two x minus one equals four, giving x equals five over two.

When the unknown is in the base and the exponent is rational, raise both sides to the reciprocal power. Solve x to the power three over two equals twenty-seven. Raise both sides to the power two over three: x equals twenty-seven to the power two over three, which is three squared, nine. Check: nine to the power three over two is the square root of nine, cubed — twenty-seven. Correct.

Watch the even-numerator case. Solve x to the power two over three equals four. Cube both sides: x squared equals sixty-four, so x is eight or minus eight. Both survive the check, because the cube root accepts a negative and the squaring then removes the sign. A learner who writes only eight loses half the marks.

Now the type that dominates examinations: the exponential equation that is secretly a quadratic. Solve three to the power two x, minus ten times three to the power x, plus nine equals zero. Three to the power two x is three to the power x, all squared. Let k stand for three to the power x. The equation becomes k squared minus ten k plus nine equals zero, factorising to k minus one, times k minus nine. So k is one or nine. Substitute back: three to the power x equals one gives x equals zero; three to the power x equals nine gives x equals two.

One discipline attaches. If a substitution produces a negative value for k, reject it in writing — an exponential with a positive base is always positive, so no real x satisfies it. The rejection earns a mark.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — bring both sides to one base, watch for the squared exponential that invites a substitution, and reject any negative value that substitution throws out.

## Subtopic: Surds — Simplify, Rationalise, and Check

A surd is a root that will not resolve into a rational number, and it is left exact rather than decimalised, because a decimal is an approximation.

Simplifying means removing every perfect-square factor. The square root of fifty is the root of twenty-five times two, which is five root two. The root of seventy-two is six root two. Once every surd is in that form, like surds add and subtract exactly like like terms. Take three root eight, minus root eighteen, plus root fifty. Root eight is two root two, so three root eight is six root two; root eighteen is three root two; root fifty is five root two. Six root two minus three root two plus five root two is eight root two.

Multiplication uses ordinary distribution, remembering that root a times root a is a. Expand two root three minus one, times root three plus four: six, plus eight root three, minus root three, minus four. Collect: two plus seven root three.

Rationalising removes surds from the denominator. For a single surd, multiply top and bottom by that surd: three over root seven becomes three root seven over seven. For a two-term denominator, multiply by the conjugate — the same expression with the middle sign reversed — because that produces a difference of two squares and kills both surds. Simplify six over root five minus root two: multiply top and bottom by root five plus root two, the denominator becomes five minus two, three, and dividing gives two root five plus two root two.

Finally, surd equations, which carry a compulsory check. Solve root of x plus seven equals x minus five. Square both sides: x plus seven equals x squared minus ten x plus twenty-five. Rearrange: x squared minus eleven x plus eighteen equals zero, factorising to x minus two, times x minus nine, so x is two or nine. Check both in the ORIGINAL. At x equals two the left side is root nine, three, while the right side is minus three — a square root sign never returns a negative value, so two is rejected. At x equals nine the left side is four and the right side is four. The only solution is nine. Squaring manufactures false solutions, so the check is part of the method.

The final questions of this part are with you now — simplify surds fully before combining them, use the conjugate on a two-term denominator, and never leave a surd equation unchecked.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same exponents and surds from a savings book and a spaza till — same rules, same answers, built from things you can picture.

## Subtopic: Half a Doubling

Imagine money that doubles every year. After one year it is times two. After two years, two squared. After three, two cubed. The exponent is simply how many doublings happened.

Now the question that invents fractional exponents: what happens in half a year? Whatever that factor is, applying it twice must give one full doubling. So it is the number whose square is two — the square root of two. We write it two to the power one half. That is all a fractional exponent is: the bottom says how many equal steps make one whole multiplication, and the top says how many of those steps you take.

So sixteen to the power three quarters means chop sixteen into four equal multiplying steps — the fourth root of sixteen is two — then take three of them: two times two times two, eight. Bottom is the root, top is the power. Say it aloud until it is automatic.

A negative exponent is the same story running backwards. Twenty-seven to the power minus two thirds means step two thirds backwards, so flip it: one over twenty-seven to the two thirds. The cube root of twenty-seven is three, squared is nine, so the answer is one ninth. The minus never makes the answer negative — it turns it upside down.

One warning from the till. If a shop takes nine rand and then sixteen rand, the square root of the total is not root nine plus root sixteen. Nine plus sixteen is twenty-five, whose root is five, not seven. Roots split across times and divide, never across plus and minus.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on fractional exponents are coming to you right now. For each one, just ask: how many equal steps, and how many of them do I take?

## Subtopic: Everything in the Same Currency

You cannot compare two hundred rand with two hundred dollars until both are in one currency. Exponential equations are identical: you cannot compare exponents until both sides sit on the same base.

Solve three to the power two x minus one equals eighty-one. Convert eighty-one: three, nine, twenty-seven, eighty-one — that is four threes, so eighty-one is three to the fourth. Now both sides are in threes, and the exponents must match: two x minus one is four, so x is two and a half.

Same idea when the bases hide. Nine is three squared and twenty-seven is three cubed, so an expression full of nines, threes and twenty-sevens is one currency wearing three different notes. Change everything into threes, then add exponents when multiplying and subtract when dividing.

Here is a trick that saves a whole class of hard-looking questions. Sometimes the top reads two to the power x plus three, minus two to the power x plus one. Adding in the exponent means multiplying in the base, so that is two to the x times eight, minus two to the x times two — six lots of two to the x. Put it over three lots of two to the x and the two to the x cancels like a common item on both sides of a till slip, leaving two.

Last one, the exam favourite. When you see three to the power two x, minus ten times three to the power x, plus nine, give the awkward thing a nickname. Call three to the x by the letter k. The line becomes k squared minus ten k plus nine, which factorises into k minus one, times k minus nine. So k is one or nine — then swap the nickname back: three to the x equals one means x is zero, and three to the x equals nine means x is two. A nickname makes a monster look like homework you already did.

Your questions for this part are up now. Convert to one base first, and if you see a squared exponential, reach straight for the nickname.

## Subtopic: Tidying the Awkward Bottom

Surds are exact numbers that refuse to become neat decimals, and there are only three things you do with them: tidy them, combine them, and get them off the bottom of a fraction.

Tidying works like packing a crate. Root fifty means fifty inside the root; break fifty into twenty-five times two, and twenty-five is a perfect square, so its root walks out as a five. Root fifty is five root two. Root eight is two root two, root eighteen is three root two. Once everything is tidied, root two behaves like a unit you can count: six of them minus three of them plus five of them is eight of them. You would never add three apples to two oranges, and you never add root two to root three either.

Getting a surd off the bottom is like being handed change in a currency the next shop will not accept. The value is fine; the form is unusable. So convert it. For three over root seven, multiply top and bottom by root seven — that is multiplying by one, so nothing changes in value — and the bottom becomes seven: three root seven over seven.

When the bottom has two terms, like root five minus root two, use the partner with the opposite sign — root five PLUS root two. Multiply top and bottom by that and the bottom becomes five minus two, three, because the cross terms cancel. Six over root five minus root two turns into two root five plus two root two. That partner is called the conjugate, and it is the only tool that clears a two-term surd denominator.

Now the habit that saves marks. If an equation has a root sign over the unknown, you square both sides to remove it — but squaring forgets minus signs. Ask which number squared gives nine and both three and minus three put up a hand. So squaring can hand you an answer that never belonged to the original question. Solving root of x plus seven equals x minus five gives x is two or nine — but put two back and the left side is three while the right side is minus three. A root sign never gives a negative answer, so two is a gate-crasher and gets shown out. Only nine is real.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: tidy every surd first, use the opposite-sign partner to clear a two-term bottom, and put every answer from a squared equation back into the original before you commit to it.
