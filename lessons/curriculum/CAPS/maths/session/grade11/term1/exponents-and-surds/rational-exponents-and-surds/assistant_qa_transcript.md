**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! First one, and it came up three times: why does the rule say x must be greater than zero? We happily take the cube root of minus eight and get minus two.

**Tutor:** You do, and that answer is perfectly good arithmetic — the trouble starts when a negative base meets the exponent LAWS, which is what the restriction protects. Consider minus one to the power one half. Written as a root, that asks for the square root of minus one, which is non-real, so already the definition fails for even root indices. Worse, if negative bases were permitted the laws would contradict each other. Take minus one to the power two sixths. Simplify the exponent first and you get minus one to the power one third, the cube root of minus one, which is minus one. Now use the power-of-a-power law in the other order: minus one squared is one, and the sixth root of one is one. Same expression, two different answers — the law has broken. Mathematicians close that door by insisting the base be positive whenever the exponent is rational, and CAPS follows. So in practice: when you meet a cube root of a negative number written with a root sign, evaluate it normally; when you meet a rational exponent in an algebraic manipulation, assume the base is positive, and if a question states a restriction such as x greater than zero, that is why it is there.

***

**Bianca:** Next: in the simplifying questions, how do I decide between changing everything to a prime base and factorising something out? I keep choosing the wrong one and wasting time.

**Tutor:** There is a reliable test, and it takes seconds. Look at the operations joining the powers. If the powers are joined only by multiplication and division, change every base into a prime and use the laws — that route always finishes. If a plus or a minus sign joins two powers, the laws cannot touch them, because there is no law for adding powers; the only way forward is to factorise out the smallest power. So the sign between the terms chooses your method for you. Take two to the power x plus three, minus two to the power x plus one, over three times two to the power x. The minus sign upstairs tells you immediately: factorise. The smallest power present is two to the x, so write the top as two to the x times the bracket eight minus two, which is six times two to the x. Then the division is trivial and the answer is two. Had the top been two to the power x plus three, MULTIPLIED by two to the power x plus one, you would simply add exponents instead. One more piece of advice: when you factorise, always take out the SMALLEST power, because that leaves whole numbers rather than fractions inside the bracket, and whole numbers are easier to keep correct under pressure.

**Bianca:** This one caused an argument: solving x to the power two thirds equals four, half of us wrote x equals eight and half wrote x equals plus or minus eight. Who is right?

**Tutor:** Both eight and minus eight satisfy that equation, so the complete answer carries the plus or minus — but the reasoning matters more than the answer, because the same pattern will appear with a different fraction and you need to know when a second solution exists. Read the exponent two thirds as: take the cube root, then square. The cube root accepts any number, positive or negative, and hands back a number with the same sign. The squaring that follows then destroys the sign, so both a positive and a negative input can produce the same positive output. Test it: the cube root of eight is two and two squared is four; the cube root of minus eight is minus two and minus two squared is also four. Both work. Now compare it with x to the power three halves equals twenty-seven. Here you take the SQUARE root first, and a square root refuses negative inputs, so only positive values of x are admissible and the answer x equals nine stands alone. The general rule: an even number on top with an odd number on the bottom gives two solutions; an even number on the bottom permits only non-negative bases. Solve it, then test each candidate in the original — the test settles every case without you memorising anything.

**Bianca:** Last one: why bother rationalising the denominator at all? My calculator gives the same decimal either way, so it feels like a rule invented to catch us out.

**Tutor:** Your calculator does give the same decimal, and that is exactly the point — a decimal is an approximation, and the reason for the convention lies in what happens before calculators are allowed near the work. Three reasons, in order of usefulness to you. First, comparison: two learners can hand in six over root five minus root two, and two root five plus two root two, and a marker cannot see at a glance that they match; a single agreed form makes every correct answer look correct. Second, further algebra: a surd in the denominator blocks the next step. If you must add such a fraction to another, or substitute it into a formula, or simplify a longer expression containing it, the surd underneath makes a common denominator almost impossible to build, whereas a rational denominator lets ordinary fraction arithmetic proceed. Third, and historically the origin of the rule: dividing by an irrational number by hand is genuinely difficult, while multiplying by one and dividing by a whole number is easy — so the convention converts a hard calculation into an easy one. The method itself is just the difference of two squares wearing a different hat. Multiplying root five minus root two by root five plus root two gives five minus two, three, because the two cross terms cancel exactly. Recognise that partner — the conjugate — and no two-term denominator can hold you up.
