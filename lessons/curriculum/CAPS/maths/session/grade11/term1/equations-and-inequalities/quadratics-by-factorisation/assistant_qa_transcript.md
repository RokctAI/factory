**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! First: how do I know whether a quadratic will actually factorise, so I do not waste half the exam trying?

**Tutor:** Use a two-minute rule and a one-line test. The rule of thumb: try factorising first, because when it works it is the fastest and most accurate method available, and CAPS questions are usually built to factorise. But do not grind. The test is the discriminant, b squared minus four a c, which you can compute in one line. If that value is a perfect square — nought, one, four, nine, sixteen, twenty-five and so on — the expression factorises over the rational numbers and it is worth persisting. If it is not a perfect square, no arrangement of whole numbers will ever work, and you must switch to the quadratic formula or completing the square. Take three x squared minus five x minus two: the discriminant is twenty-five plus twenty-four, which is forty-nine, a perfect square, so factors exist, and indeed the answer is three x plus one, times x minus two. Now take two x squared minus eleven x plus six: the discriminant is one hundred and twenty-one minus forty-eight, which is seventy-three, not a perfect square — stop hunting and use the formula. One more practical point: always take out a common factor before applying any test, because a common factor makes every subsequent number smaller and the numbers you have to search through fewer.

**Bianca:** Next one, and it caused real confusion: when we clear fractions, why does a value sometimes have to be thrown away at the end? It came from correct factorising, so surely it is a real answer.

**Tutor:** Your factorising was correct — the point is that the equation you factorised is not quite the equation you were asked about. Multiplying both sides by an expression containing x is only a legal move while that expression is not zero. At the banned value the multiplication is a multiplication by zero, which turns any statement into the true statement nought equals nought, and that is how a value which fails the original can sneak into the cleared version. Picture it concretely: solve one over x minus three, equals four over x squared minus nine. Multiply through by x squared minus nine and you eventually reach x equals three. But three makes both original denominators zero, so the original equation does not exist at three; it is undefined, not satisfied. The answer is that the equation has no solution, and stating that is worth marks. This is why the restriction list belongs at the very top of your working, before you touch the algebra: at that moment every dangerous value is visible, and at the end you simply compare your solutions against the list and cross out anything that appears there — with a short reason written next to it.

**Bianca:** This one came from the back: with two equations, one linear and one quadratic, why must we substitute from the linear one? Someone rearranged the quadratic and says their answer was still right.

**Tutor:** Their answer may well have been right — the method is not wrong, only far more dangerous, and in an exam that difference matters more than elegance. Rearranging a linear equation gives you a clean expression: from x plus y equals five you get y equals five minus x, with no roots and no fractions and no ambiguity. Rearranging the quadratic instead means making y the subject of something like x squared plus y squared equals seventeen, which gives y equal to plus or minus the square root of seventeen minus x squared. Now you are carrying a square root and a plus-or-minus sign into the substitution, which means squaring later, which means possible false solutions and a compulsory check. It is three times the work with three times the risk. There is a second reason for the linear route: after you have found the x values, you go back to the linear equation to find each partner, and that step is one subtraction, so it cannot go wrong. Finally, a caution about presenting the answer. The solutions come in pairs, and you must show which x belongs with which y — one and four, or four and one. A list of x values beside a list of y values claims every combination, and half of those combinations do not satisfy the quadratic.

**Bianca:** Last one: I keep getting the substitution question wrong. I find k, and then I lose myself. What exactly should the working look like?

**Tutor:** The cure is structural: treat it as two separate solving jobs stacked on top of each other, and physically draw a line between them. Job one. Write "let k equal" and then the repeated chunk, exactly as it appears in the question — for instance let k equal x squared minus three x. Rewrite the whole equation in k, so it becomes k squared minus two k minus eight equals zero. Solve it completely and write the answers clearly: k equals four or k equals minus two. Now draw your line, because job one is finished and k has served its purpose. Job two. Take the first value and write out the equation it produces: x squared minus three x equals four, which becomes x squared minus three x minus four equals zero, factorising to x minus four times x plus one, so x equals four or x equals minus one. Then take the second value and do exactly the same: x squared minus three x equals minus two, giving x squared minus three x plus two equals zero, factorising to x minus one times x minus two, so x equals one or two. Collect all four at the bottom. Two checks protect you. First, expect twice as many answers as you found for k, unless a quadratic fails to have real roots. Second, k must never appear in your final line — if it does, you have handed in the middle of the question instead of the end of it.
