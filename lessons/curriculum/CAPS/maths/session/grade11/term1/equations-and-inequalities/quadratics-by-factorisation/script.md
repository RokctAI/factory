# Part 1 — Expert

A quadratic equation is any equation that can be arranged into the form a x squared plus b x plus c equals zero, with a not zero. Factorisation is the first of the three solving methods, and the one examiners expect whenever the expression will factorise, because it is exact and it is fast. This session fixes the principle the method rests on, drills the factorising shapes, then extends the same principle to equations carrying fractions, to equations that hide a quadratic inside a bracket, and to a linear equation paired with a quadratic one.

## Subtopic: Standard Form and the Zero-Product Principle

Every factorisation solution begins with the same instruction: get everything to one side so the other side reads zero. Not five, not eight — zero. The reason is a property that only zero possesses.

The zero-product principle states that if a product A times B equals zero, then A equals zero or B equals zero. Nothing else can happen: two non-zero numbers multiplied never give zero. Now compare that with a product equal to eight. From A times B equals eight you learn nothing about A, because eight can be one times eight, two times four, sixteen times a half, or minus two times minus four. Only zero forces a conclusion, and that is why the equation must be arranged to equal zero before any bracket is set equal to anything.

Two errors follow from ignoring it. Solve x minus two, times x plus five, equals eight. A learner who writes x minus two equals eight has applied the zero principle where there is no zero, and both answers will be wrong. Expand instead: x squared plus three x minus ten equals eight, so x squared plus three x minus eighteen equals zero, factorising to x plus six, times x minus three. The solutions are minus six and three, and both check: one times eight is eight, and minus eight times minus one is eight.

The second error destroys a solution silently. Solve x squared equals three x. Dividing both sides by x gives x equals three — and x equals zero, which also satisfies the original, has vanished. Dividing by a variable is dividing by something that might be zero. Do it correctly: x squared minus three x equals zero, take out the common factor, x times x minus three equals zero, so x equals zero or three.

Write the rule into your habits: never divide an equation by a variable expression. Factorise it out instead, and let it produce its own solution.

Pause here — the questions for this section are with you now. They will present products set equal to numbers other than zero, and at least one will tempt you to divide by x.

## Subtopic: Factorising Every Shape a Quadratic Takes

With the right-hand side at zero, the work is factorising, and grade eleven quadratics arrive in four shapes.

A common factor only. Two x squared minus eight x equals zero gives two x, times x minus four, equals zero, so x equals zero or x equals four. Take out the common factor first in every question, always — it makes whatever remains easier.

A trinomial with leading coefficient one. Solve x squared minus seven x plus twelve equals zero. Find two numbers multiplying to twelve and adding to minus seven: minus three and minus four. The factors are x minus three, times x minus four, and the solutions are three and four.

A trinomial with a leading coefficient other than one. Solve three x squared minus five x minus two equals zero. Multiply a by c: three times minus two is minus six. Find two numbers multiplying to minus six and adding to minus five: minus six and one. Split the middle term: three x squared minus six x, plus x minus two. Group: three x, times x minus two, plus one, times x minus two. Factor out the common bracket: three x plus one, times x minus two. So x equals minus one third or x equals two.

A difference of two squares. Solve four x squared minus twenty-five equals zero. It factorises to two x minus five, times two x plus five, giving x equals five halves or minus five halves. The same equation is often written nine x squared equals sixteen, and the danger there is answering x equals four thirds only. A squared term equal to a positive number always yields two values — plus four thirds and minus four thirds. Losing the negative one is among the most common single-mark losses in the paper.

One discipline to close. After factorising, set EACH bracket equal to zero on its own line and solve each separately. Markers award those lines. And a quadratic has at most two solutions; if your work produces three, one of them came from an illegal step.

Stop for this section's questions now — take out common factors first, split the middle term when the leading coefficient is not one, and never drop the negative square root.

## Subtopic: Equations with Fractions — and the Restriction Rule

When the unknown appears in a denominator, the equation is not yet quadratic; it becomes quadratic once the fractions are cleared. The order of operations is fixed, and the first step is not algebra at all.

Step one: state the restrictions. Look at each denominator and write down the values of x that would make it zero, because those values can never be answers. Doing this first, not last, is what protects you.

Step two: multiply every term on both sides by the lowest common denominator. Step three: simplify to standard form and factorise. Step four: reject any solution that violates a restriction.

Worked example one. Solve x plus six over x equals five. The restriction is x not equal to zero. Multiply every term by x: x squared plus six equals five x. Standard form: x squared minus five x plus six equals zero. Factorise: x minus two, times x minus three. So x equals two or x equals three, and neither is zero, so both stand. Check the first: two plus six over two is two plus three, which is five. Correct.

Worked example two, with two denominators. Solve four over x, plus four over x plus two, equals three. Restrictions: x not equal to zero and x not equal to minus two. The lowest common denominator is x times x plus two. Multiplying every term: four times x plus two, plus four x, equals three x, times x plus two. Expand: four x plus eight, plus four x, equals three x squared plus six x, so eight x plus eight equals three x squared plus six x. Standard form: three x squared minus two x minus eight equals zero. Using a times c, which is minus twenty-four, the two numbers are minus six and four, so the factors are three x plus four, times x minus two. Then x equals minus four thirds or x equals two, and neither is banned.

Verify x equals two in the original: four over two is two, four over four is one, and two plus one is three. Correct.

The trap in this section is arithmetic, not concept: when you multiply through by the lowest common denominator you must multiply EVERY term, including the constant on the right-hand side. A learner who leaves the three untouched produces a wrong quadratic and loses everything after that line.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — restrictions first, multiply every single term by the lowest common denominator, and check each answer against the banned list before you write it down.

## Subtopic: Substitution, and One Linear with One Quadratic

Two harder question types reduce to the work you have just done.

The first is the equation with a repeated expression. Solve the quantity x squared minus three x, all squared, minus two times the quantity x squared minus three x, minus eight equals zero. Expanding would produce a fourth-degree equation. Instead, notice that x squared minus three x appears twice, once squared and once plain. Let k stand for x squared minus three x. The equation becomes k squared minus two k minus eight equals zero, which factorises to k minus four, times k plus two. So k equals four or k equals minus two.

Now restore the substitution — and this is where marks are lost, because each value of k generates its own quadratic. From k equals four: x squared minus three x equals four, so x squared minus three x minus four equals zero, which factorises to x minus four, times x plus one, giving x equals four or x equals minus one. From k equals minus two: x squared minus three x equals minus two, so x squared minus three x plus two equals zero, factorising to x minus one, times x minus two, giving x equals one or x equals two. Four solutions altogether: minus one, one, two and four.

The second type is a linear equation paired with a quadratic one. Solve x plus y equals five together with x squared plus y squared equals seventeen. The method is substitution, and the choice of which equation to rearrange is not free: rearrange the LINEAR one, because it produces a clean expression with no squares. From the first, y equals five minus x. Substitute into the second: x squared plus the quantity five minus x, all squared, equals seventeen. Expand the bracket carefully: twenty-five minus ten x plus x squared. So two x squared minus ten x plus twenty-five equals seventeen, giving two x squared minus ten x plus eight equals zero. Divide through by two — legal, since two is a constant, not a variable: x squared minus five x plus four equals zero, which factorises to x minus one, times x minus four. So x equals one or x equals four.

Now find the partners, and substitute back into the LINEAR equation, which is far less work and cannot introduce errors. When x equals one, y equals four. When x equals four, y equals one. Present the answers as ordered pairs — one and four, or four and one — because an examiner marks the pairing, not two loose lists. A learner who writes "x equals one or four and y equals one or four" has claimed four combinations, two of which are false.

The final questions of this part are with you now — name the repeated expression before you expand anything, rearrange the linear equation rather than the quadratic one, and keep every solution paired with its partner.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same equations from a spaza counter and a shared airtime bill — same steps, same answers, but with a reason behind each move.

## Subtopic: Zero Is the Only Number That Talks

Two friends run a stall for a day. At closing time you are told their earnings, multiplied together, come to zero. What do you know? You know at once that at least one of them sold nothing all day. There is no other way to get zero out of a multiplication.

Now suppose instead that their earnings multiplied together come to eight hundred. What do you know about the first friend? Nothing at all. She might have taken eight hundred rand while he took one, or forty while he took twenty, or four while he took two hundred. Every split is possible.

That is the whole reason your teacher insists on getting a quadratic equal to zero before factorising. Zero is the only number that forces a confession out of a product. So when you see x minus two, times x plus five, equals eight, you may not say x minus two is eight. Multiply the brackets out, subtract the eight, and get x squared plus three x minus eighteen equals zero. Now factorise into x plus six, times x minus three — and now, with zero on the right, one of those brackets must be empty. The answers are minus six and three.

There is a second habit that quietly saves you a whole mark. Never divide an equation by x. Look at x squared equals three x. Dividing by x gives three, and that is one right answer — but zero was also an answer, and dividing it away means it never gets counted. Think of it as removing a suspect from the line-up before anyone has been questioned; if that person did it, you will never find out. Instead, move everything to one side: x squared minus three x equals zero, take out the x, and let it stand as x times x minus three equals zero. Now x itself is one of your brackets and it hands you the answer zero honestly.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on this are coming to you right now. For each one, first ask whether the right-hand side is really zero, and second, check that you have not divided anything away.

## Subtopic: Clear the Fractions Before You Fight

Six friends share a bundle of data and each contribution is written as a fraction. Nobody wants to do the arithmetic in sixths — the first thing anyone does is convert everything into a whole number of rands. Equations feel exactly the same way: as long as there is an x sitting underneath a line, the equation resists you. So get rid of the fractions first.

The tool is the lowest common denominator, and the rule is that you multiply EVERY term by it, including the plain number on the other side of the equals sign. That is the step people skip when they are rushing, and skipping it makes every line afterwards wrong.

Take x plus six over x equals five. There is one denominator, x, so multiply everything by x: x times x is x squared, six over x times x leaves six, and five times x is five x. So x squared plus six equals five x. Move it all one side: x squared minus five x plus six equals zero, which opens into x minus two, times x minus three. The answers are two and three, and putting two back in gives two plus three, which is five. The till balances.

Now the banned numbers. Before you touch the algebra, look at each bottom and ask what would empty it. In x plus six over x, the bottom is x, so x may not be zero — you cannot share anything among zero people; the question stops making sense. In the harder example, with bottoms of x and x plus two, two values are banned: zero and minus two. Write them down at the top of your working, and when your answers arrive, check them against that list. If a solution is on the banned list, cross it out and say why. That crossing-out is worth a mark on its own.

Your questions for this part are up now. Write the banned values first, then multiply every term by the lowest common denominator — every single one, including the number standing alone.

## Subtopic: Nicknames, and Keeping the Pairs Together

Two last ideas, and both are about making a big problem look like one you already solved.

The first is the nickname. Suppose you are handed the quantity x squared minus three x, all squared, minus two lots of x squared minus three x, minus eight. It looks enormous. But look again: the same chunk, x squared minus three x, is sitting there twice. Give that chunk a nickname — call it k. Now the line reads k squared minus two k minus eight, which is an ordinary trinomial you can factorise in your sleep: k minus four, times k plus two. So k is four or k is minus two.

Here is the part to be careful with. You are not finished, because k was never the question. Swap the real chunk back in, one value at a time, and solve each. From four: x squared minus three x minus four is zero, giving four and minus one. From minus two: x squared minus three x plus two is zero, giving one and two. Four answers in total. Handing in the k values is like giving someone a shopping list instead of the groceries.

The second idea is two facts about the same pair of numbers. Suppose two amounts add up to five, and their squares add up to seventeen. The first fact is simple and the second is not, so use the simple one to rewrite the whole story with only one unknown in it: if they add to five, then the second amount is five minus the first. Feed that into the complicated fact and you get a single quadratic, which solves to one and four.

Then keep the pairs together. Answers here come in couples, like a left shoe and a right shoe. If the first amount is one, its partner is four; if the first is four, the partner is one. Go back to the SIMPLE fact to find each partner, because it is quicker and there is nothing in it to get wrong. And write your answers as pairs, not as two separate lists, so that nobody can accuse you of claiming combinations you never checked.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: hunt for the repeated chunk before you expand anything, and when two unknowns are in play, keep every answer holding hands with its partner.
