**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! First: if the quadratic formula always works, why does anyone still learn completing the square? It feels like extra work for no reward.

**Tutor:** Three rewards, and each of them appears in the examination. The first is that completing the square is where the formula comes from, and CAPS asks you to derive it — divide by a, shift the constant, add b squared over four a squared to both sides, square-root with a plus or minus, and subtract b over two a. A learner who has only memorised the formula cannot produce those lines, and cannot rebuild the formula if memory fails on the day. The second reward is the turning point. Rewriting two x squared minus eight x plus five as two, times x minus two all squared, minus three, tells you instantly that the graph's minimum sits at the point two, minus three, and the axis of symmetry is x equals two. No other method hands you that. The third reward is that some questions simply instruct you to solve by completing the square, and using the formula there earns nothing regardless of the answer. So think of it as three tools with three jobs: factorise for speed when factors exist, use the formula when they do not, and complete the square when you need the graph's turning point or when the question names the method.

***

**Bianca:** Next: why do we divide by a when solving but factor a out when rewriting an expression? That inconsistency confused a lot of us.

**Tutor:** It is not inconsistency — it follows from what you are allowed to do to each object. An EQUATION has two sides joined by an equals sign, so dividing both sides by the same non-zero number leaves a true statement; two x squared minus eight x plus five equals zero divided by two becomes x squared minus four x plus five halves equals zero, and the solutions are untouched, because zero divided by two is still zero. An EXPRESSION has no second side to divide. If you divide two x squared minus eight x plus five by two, you have not rewritten it, you have changed it into a different expression worth half as much, and the graph you describe will be the wrong graph. So for an expression you must keep the total value intact, which means factoring the two out of the first two terms only and carrying it along: two, times the bracket x squared minus four x, plus five. Then complete the square inside the bracket and distribute the two across everything inside — including the constant the completion produced. That last distribution is where marks disappear. Two, times x minus two all squared minus four, plus five, becomes two times x minus two all squared, minus eight, plus five, which is minus three, not minus four plus five.

***

**Bianca:** This one is from someone who keeps getting the formula slightly wrong: how do I stop losing signs when b is negative?

**Tutor:** Build a fixed ritual and never deviate from it, because sign errors come from doing the substitution in your head. The ritual has three lines. Line one: write the equation in standard form with zero on the right. Line two: write a equals, b equals, c equals, each on its own, with the sign attached — for three x squared minus seven x plus one, write a equals three, b equals minus seven, c equals one. Line three: write the formula out fully with brackets around every substituted value, so it reads minus, open bracket, minus seven, close bracket, plus or minus the root of, open bracket, minus seven, close bracket squared, minus four, times three, times one, all over two times three. Those brackets do the thinking for you: minus a negative seven is plus seven, and a negative seven inside a square becomes positive forty-nine, because a negative times a negative is positive. Then simplify. Two sanity checks catch almost everything. First, the number under the root should be positive whenever the graph crosses the axis twice; a negative there means either no real roots or a sign slip, and you should recheck four a c. Second, if c is negative, then minus four a c is positive and the discriminant must be larger than b squared — if yours came out smaller, you dropped a sign.

**Bianca:** Last one: how do I know when to leave the root sign in and when to give a decimal? I keep doing whichever feels safer and losing marks anyway.

**Tutor:** The question tells you, in a fixed vocabulary, and it is worth learning those phrases as precisely as you learn the method. If the instruction says "leave your answer in simplest surd form", or "give the exact value", or "leave the answer in terms of surds", then the root sign must remain — seven plus or minus root thirty-seven, all over six, is the finished answer, and converting it to a decimal loses accuracy marks even though the decimal is close. Simplest surd form also implies tidying the surd itself: root six over two rather than the square root of three halves, since a surd is not left in a denominator. If the instruction says "correct to two decimal places" or "correct to one decimal place", then a decimal is required, and rounding to the wrong number of places is marked wrong even when the arithmetic was right. When the question says nothing at all, the exact form is always acceptable and is the safer choice. One habit protects both cases: do the entire calculation exactly, keeping surds and fractions intact, and only at the very last line decide whether to round. Rounding partway through — turning root thirty-seven into six before dividing — shifts the second decimal and quietly costs the accuracy mark.
