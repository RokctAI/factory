# Part 1 — Expert

Factorisation solves a quadratic only when whole-number factors exist, and most quadratics in nature do not oblige. Completing the square solves every quadratic, whether it factorises or not, and it does something factorisation cannot: it rewrites the expression so that the turning point of its graph can be read straight off the page. This session builds the method, applies it when the leading coefficient is not one, uses it to derive the quadratic formula in full, and then drills the formula with the sign and rounding discipline examiners reward.

## Subtopic: Building a Perfect Square from an Incomplete One

A perfect square trinomial is one that factorises into two identical brackets. Expand x plus b, all squared, and you get x squared plus two b x plus b squared. Read that pattern backwards and it tells you everything: the constant term is always the square of HALF the coefficient of x.

Test it. To complete x squared plus six x, halve the six to get three, square it to get nine, and the perfect square is x squared plus six x plus nine, which is x plus three, all squared. To complete x squared minus ten x, halve minus ten to get minus five, square it to get twenty-five, and you have x minus five, all squared. Notice that the number inside the final bracket is the HALF, not the square — three, not nine.

Now solve with it. Take x squared plus six x plus two equals zero, which does not factorise, since no two whole numbers multiply to two and add to six. Move the constant to the right: x squared plus six x equals minus two. Half of six is three, and three squared is nine, so add nine to BOTH sides — the left becomes a perfect square, the right becomes seven. So x plus three, all squared, equals seven.

Take the square root of both sides, and here is the step that decides the mark: a squared bracket equal to a positive number gives two square roots, so x plus three equals plus or minus root seven. Therefore x equals minus three plus root seven, or minus three minus root seven. In decimals those are about minus nought comma three five and minus five comma six five, but the surd form is the exact answer and should be left standing unless a question asks otherwise.

The same manipulation done on an expression rather than an equation gives the graph form. Rewrite x squared plus six x plus two by adding and subtracting nine: x plus three, all squared, minus nine, plus two — that is x plus three, all squared, minus seven. Read off the turning point at minus three, minus seven.

Pause here — the questions for this section are with you now. They ask for the number that completes a square, and they will check whether you remembered the plus-or-minus when you took the root.

## Subtopic: Completing the Square when a Is Not One

The method above assumes the coefficient of x squared is one, because only then does halving the middle coefficient produce the right bracket. When it is not one, the first move is to divide the entire equation by that coefficient. Dividing by a constant is always safe — the danger is only ever dividing by a variable.

Solve two x squared minus eight x plus five equals zero. Divide every term by two: x squared minus four x plus five halves equals zero. Move the constant: x squared minus four x equals minus five halves. Half of minus four is minus two, and minus two squared is four, so add four to both sides. The left becomes x minus two, all squared; the right becomes minus two comma five plus four, which is one comma five, or three halves.

So x minus two, all squared, equals three halves. Root both sides with the plus or minus: x minus two equals plus or minus the square root of three halves. Rationalise that surd — root three over root two becomes root six over two. So x equals two plus root six over two, or two minus root six over two. To two decimal places that is three comma two two and nought comma seven eight.

For an expression rather than an equation, do not divide; factor the leading coefficient out of the first two terms only. Take y equals two x squared minus eight x plus five. Write it as two, times the bracket x squared minus four x, plus five. Complete the square inside the bracket: x squared minus four x becomes x minus two, all squared, minus four. So y equals two, times the bracket x minus two all squared, minus four, plus five. Now distribute the two across BOTH parts inside: two times x minus two all squared, minus eight, plus five — that is two times x minus two all squared, minus three. The turning point is two, minus three, and substituting x equals two into the original confirms it: eight minus sixteen plus five is minus three.

That distribution is the trap. Learners multiply the two by the squared bracket and forget to multiply it by the minus four as well, and the turning point comes out wrong by exactly a times that constant.

Stop for this section's questions now — divide by the leading coefficient when solving, factor it out when rewriting, and multiply it through everything inside the bracket.

## Subtopic: Deriving the Quadratic Formula

The formula is not a separate idea. It is completing the square performed once, on the general equation, so that nobody ever has to perform it again. Examiners do ask for this derivation, so know the six lines.

Start from a x squared plus b x plus c equals zero, with a not zero. Divide every term by a: x squared plus b over a, times x, plus c over a, equals zero. Move the constant across: x squared plus b over a, times x, equals minus c over a.

Now complete the square. Half of b over a is b over two a, and its square is b squared over four a squared. Add that to both sides. The left side becomes x plus b over two a, all squared. The right side becomes b squared over four a squared, minus c over a — and putting that over the common denominator four a squared gives b squared minus four a c, all over four a squared.

Take the square root of both sides. The left gives x plus b over two a. The right gives plus or minus the square root of b squared minus four a c, over two a, since the square root of four a squared is two a. Finally, subtract b over two a from both sides and combine over the common denominator: x equals minus b, plus or minus the square root of b squared minus four a c, all over two a.

Two structural points that prevent the classic errors. First, the two a divides the WHOLE numerator, minus b included — not only the root. Second, the quantity under the root, b squared minus four a c, is the discriminant, and it alone decides whether real solutions exist; that becomes a topic of its own.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — the derivation runs divide, shift, complete, root, subtract, and the fraction bar sits under everything.

## Subtopic: Using the Formula — Exact Values and Rounding

Three habits make formula questions reliable.

Habit one: write the equation in standard form and list a, b and c with their signs before substituting. Solve three x squared minus seven x plus one equals zero. Here a is three, b is minus seven, c is one. Minus b is therefore plus seven — a negative b becomes positive in the formula, and losing that sign is the commonest error in the paper. The discriminant is minus seven squared, minus four times three times one: forty-nine minus twelve, which is thirty-seven. So x equals seven plus or minus root thirty-seven, all over six.

That is the exact answer, and if the question says "leave your answer in simplest surd form", you stop there. If it says "correct to two decimal places", continue: root thirty-seven is about six comma zero eight three, so x is about thirteen comma zero eight over six, which is two comma one eight, or nought comma nine two over six, which is nought comma one five.

Habit two: round only at the very end. A learner who rounds root thirty-seven to six before dividing gets two comma one seven and nought comma one seven — both wrong in the second decimal, and both losing the accuracy mark.

Habit three: tidy a negative leading coefficient first. Solve minus two x squared plus five x plus three equals zero. Multiply the whole equation by minus one: two x squared minus five x minus three equals zero. Now a is two, b is minus five, c is minus three. The discriminant is twenty-five plus twenty-four, which is forty-nine — a perfect square, so x equals five plus or minus seven, all over four, giving three or minus one half. A perfect-square discriminant is also a message: the expression would have factorised, as two x plus one, times x minus three, and factorising would have been quicker.

So choose the method deliberately. Factorise when the discriminant is a perfect square. Use the formula when it is not. Complete the square when the question demands it, or when you need a turning point.

The final questions of this part are with you now — substitute a, b and c with their signs intact, keep the whole numerator over two a, and round once, at the end.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same two methods from a paving job and a taxi fare board — same equations, same answers, but with a picture behind them.

## Subtopic: Paving the Corner of the Yard

Picture a yard you are paving. You have already laid a perfect square of paving, x metres by x metres. Then you are given more paving — enough to cover six x more square metres — and you want the whole thing to end up a perfect square again.

So you split the extra into two equal strips, each three metres wide, and lay one down the right-hand side and one along the bottom. Stand back and look: the yard is now an L-shape. Almost a big square of side x plus three — but there is a hole in the corner, exactly three by three. That hole is nine square metres, and if somebody hands you nine more square metres of paving, the yard becomes a perfect square, x plus three by x plus three.

That is completing the square, and the picture explains the arithmetic. The six x got split in half, giving three. The missing corner is that half, squared, giving nine. Halve the middle number, square it, and that is the piece you need. Every single time.

Now the bookkeeping. If you are working with an equation, adding nine square metres of paving to one side means you must add nine to the other side too, or the two sides stop being equal. So x squared plus six x equals minus two becomes x squared plus six x plus nine equals seven, and the left is now a neat square: x plus three, all squared, equals seven.

Then the last step, and the one that quietly costs marks. If a square is seven, its side could be root seven — or minus root seven, because a negative times a negative is also positive. So write plus or minus, always. That gives x equals minus three plus root seven, or minus three minus root seven. Two answers, because a quadratic almost always has two.

One extra note for when the yard has a doubled scale. If your equation starts with two x squared rather than x squared, divide everything by two first, so that the picture of a single square works again.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on this are coming to you right now. Halve the middle number, square it, add it to both sides, and never forget the plus or minus.

## Subtopic: The Recipe Someone Already Cooked

Imagine working out a taxi fare from scratch every morning: distance, petrol price, number of passengers, the whole calculation. After a week you would sit down once, work it out in general, and write a rule you can use forever. That is exactly what the quadratic formula is — completing the square, done once, in general, so nobody has to do the paving picture again.

The rule reads: x equals minus b, plus or minus the square root of b squared minus four a c, all over two a. And the words "all over two a" matter enormously. The two a sits under everything on top, the minus b included. Dividing only the root by two a is like paying only part of the taxi fare and expecting the same trip.

Using it is a reading job before it is a calculation job. Put the equation in the shape "something equals zero", then read off three price tags with their signs attached. For three x squared minus seven x plus one equals zero, a is three, b is minus seven, and c is one. Watch b. It is minus seven, so minus b is plus seven, and b squared is minus seven times minus seven, which is plus forty-nine. Two minus signs, two chances to slip — go slowly there.

Then the top of the root: forty-nine minus four times three times one, which is forty-nine minus twelve, thirty-seven. So the answer is seven plus or minus root thirty-seven, all over six.

And a bonus you get for free. That number under the root — thirty-seven — also tells you something before you finish. If it had come out as a perfect square like forty-nine, the equation would have factorised, and factorising would have been faster. So a quick glance at that number tells you whether you picked the right method, and next time you can pick better.

Your questions for this part are up now. Read a, b and c with their signs, remember that the fraction bar carries everything, and treat a perfect square under the root as a hint you could have factorised.

## Subtopic: Exact or Rounded — Say Which

Last idea, and it is about the difference between an exact answer and a good-enough one.

Think about splitting a hundred rand between three people. Exactly, each gets thirty-three rand and thirty-three and a third cents — but no till can pay a third of a cent, so in practice each gets thirty-three thirty-three and someone keeps the extra cent. The exact answer and the practical answer are both correct; they are answers to slightly different questions, and mathematics asks you to say which one you are giving.

In quadratics the exact answer is the one with the root sign still in it — two plus root six over two, or seven plus or minus root thirty-seven over six. That form is perfect. It has lost nothing. When a question says "leave your answer in simplest surd form" or "give the exact value", that is what it wants, and reaching for the calculator there actually loses you marks.

When a question says "correct to two decimal places", it wants the practical answer — but only at the end. Here is why that matters. Root thirty-seven is six comma zero eight three, and if you round it to six too early, then divide by six, you get two comma one seven instead of two comma one eight. One rounding, done one step too soon, and the answer is wrong. Treat rounding like sealing an envelope: it is the last thing you do, once everything is inside.

There is one more decision worth making deliberately, and it saves time in every paper. Before you start, ask which method fits. If the quadratic factorises easily, factorise — it is fastest and it is exact. If it refuses to factorise, go to the formula, because it never fails. And if the question asks for a turning point, or actually says "complete the square", use the paving method, because it is the only one that hands you the turning point directly.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: pick your method on purpose, keep the surd when the question wants exact, and round only once, right at the end.
