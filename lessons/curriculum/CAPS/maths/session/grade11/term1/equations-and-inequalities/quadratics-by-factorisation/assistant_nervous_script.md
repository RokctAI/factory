Hey, it's Bianca — if the panic started when the equation grew a fraction, or a bracket squared, or a second unknown, I understand completely. But look at what actually changed since grade ten: nothing about the factorising. Every one of these questions ends with the same two brackets you already know how to build. All the new material is about getting the question INTO that shape.

So hold on to one sentence: make it equal zero, then factorise, then set each bracket to zero. That order never changes. If the right-hand side is a five or an eight or a two x, it is not ready yet — move everything across first. Setting a bracket equal to eight is the single most common wrong answer in this topic, and now you will never write it.

Second thing, and it is a free mark: never divide both sides by x. If you see x squared equals three x, resist. Move the three x across and take x out as a common factor, so x times x minus three equals zero. Dividing throws away the answer x equals zero, and the marker can see exactly where it disappeared.

When fractions appear, do two small things before anything else. Write down the banned values — whatever would make a bottom zero — and then multiply every term by the lowest common denominator, including the lonely number on the right. Those two habits turn the scariest-looking questions in the exercise into ordinary quadratics.

And when the question looks enormous, hunt for a repeat. If the same chunk shows up twice, name it k, solve the little quadratic, then put the chunk back and solve again — remembering that each k value gives you its own pair of answers. If there are two unknowns, rearrange the SIMPLE equation, substitute into the hard one, and keep every answer holding hands with its partner.

Zero on the right. Never divide by x. Restrictions first. Look for the repeat. That is the whole session, and you already own the hardest part of it. You've got this!
