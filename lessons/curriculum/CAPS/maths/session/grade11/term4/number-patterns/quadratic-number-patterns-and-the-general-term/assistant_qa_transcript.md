**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! First, the perennial: I mix up the shortcut facts — 2a, 3a plus b, a plus b plus c. Is there a way to actually remember which is which?

**Tutor:** Remember them by where each one LIVES in the difference table, because each fact is simply the table's own structure written as algebra. Draw the table in your head: top row, the terms; middle row, the first differences; bottom row, the second differences. The bottom row is the deepest layer, and the deepest layer holds the purest information: only the n squared part of the formula survives two rounds of differencing, so the constant down there is 2 a — the second difference belongs to a alone. The middle row's FIRST entry — the gap between term one and term two — is 3 a plus b: derive it once in your life by computing T 2 minus T 1 from the formula, and after that trust it. Notice it contains a but not c: differencing killed the constant, and b has entered the story. The top row's first entry is the formula evaluated at n equals 1: a plus b plus c, all three letters present, nothing yet differenced away. So the pattern of the pattern is: bottom row, one letter; middle row's start, two letters; top row's start, three letters. Solve in that order — a, then b, then c — because each equation hands its answer to the next. If memory fails entirely in the exam, substitute n equals 1, 2, 3 into a n squared plus b n plus c and solve the three simultaneous equations; it is slower but bulletproof, and it always agrees.

***

**Bianca:** Next: my formula tested fine on term one but failed on term five. Someone else's did the opposite. What is actually going wrong in each case?

**Tutor:** Those two failures point at different loose screws, and the direction of failure is diagnostic. Testing fine early but failing late is the signature of a wrong a or b — the letters that multiply n. An error in c shifts EVERY term by the same amount, so it fails immediately, at term one; but an error in a or b grows with n, sometimes agreeing at small positions by coincidence and then drifting. Failing at term five with term one intact usually means the second difference was misread — perhaps computed from unsorted or miscopied terms — or the first gap was taken from the wrong pair. Conversely, a formula that is off by the same fixed amount everywhere has a wrong c, and only c: go back to a plus b plus c equals the first term and redo one line. The repair procedure, in order: recompute both difference rows carefully from the printed sequence, checking each subtraction; halve the second difference and confirm a; substitute a into 3 a plus b equals the first gap and confirm b; then c from term one. And adopt the habit that catches all of this before the marker does: test the finished formula on TWO terms, one early and one late — term one and the final given term. Early catches c; late catches a and b. Ten seconds of substitution, and every loose screw announces itself.

**Bianca:** Third one: for the question is 200 a term, why does the discriminant decide? I thought the discriminant was about the nature of roots.

**Tutor:** It IS about the nature of roots — and that is exactly what a membership question asks, once you see what you are solving. Is 200 a term means: does there exist a POSITION, a natural number n, with n squared plus 2 n equal to 200? Rearranged, that is the quadratic equation n squared plus 2 n minus 200 equals zero, and membership of the pattern has become existence of a suitable root. Now the discriminant does its usual work, with one extra filter on top. If b squared minus 4 a c is negative, there are no real roots at all — certainly no position. If it is positive but NOT a perfect square, the roots are irrational — and no irrational number is a position in a sequence, so the answer is no; that is our case, with 804. If it IS a perfect square, the roots are rational, and you must still check the last two conditions by actually solving: the candidate must be a whole number, and it must be positive. The equation n squared plus 2 n minus 120 equals zero passes every stage: discriminant 484, which is 22 squared, roots 10 and minus 12, and 10 survives the final filter while minus 12 is rejected in writing. So the full chain is: real, then rational, then whole, then positive. Last year's nature-of-roots theory is not a separate topic making a guest appearance — it is the engine room of this year's membership questions.

**Bianca:** Last one: when a picture pattern is given, should we count and difference, or hunt for the structure? People swear by both.

**Tutor:** Do both, in a fixed order, because the two routes protect each other. Start by counting and differencing — it is the guaranteed route. Count the tiles in each printed figure carefully, write the sequence, take both difference rows, and build T n with the three facts. This works on every quadratic picture ever set, whether or not you see anything clever in it. THEN spend thirty seconds hunting structure: ask how the figure is built — rows times columns, a square plus a border, two triangles back to back. Our decks reveal themselves as n rows of n plus 2 tiles, so T n equals n times n plus 2 — and expanding gives n squared plus 2 n, exactly what the difference table built. That agreement is the payoff: two independent routes landing on one formula is the strongest verification available in the exam, far better than re-checking your own arithmetic along one route. If the two routes disagree, trust neither and recount the figures — miscounting a printed picture is the commonest error in context questions, more common than any algebra slip. Structure alone earns full marks when correct, and examiners enjoy it, but it carries a risk: a plausible-looking structure that misreads the picture produces a confident wrong formula. The professional habit is count first, structure second, agreement as proof — and then answer the story's questions in the story's own words: tiles, figures, rows.
