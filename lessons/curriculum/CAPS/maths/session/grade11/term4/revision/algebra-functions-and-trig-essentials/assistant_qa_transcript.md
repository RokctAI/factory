**Bianca:** Revision season, same questions every year — let's settle them. First up: with factorising, the formula and completing the square all available, how do you choose fast without second-guessing halfway through?

**Tutor:** Choose by what the question ASKS, then by what the numbers OFFER. If the instruction says solve and the trinomial is friendly — leading coefficient one, small numbers — spend a brief moment hunting the factor pair; two numbers multiplying to six and adding to minus five present themselves or they do not. The moment the hunt stalls, switch to the formula without regret: it never jams, and the discriminant it computes along the way is information the factors would have hidden. If the instruction says determine the nature of the roots, do not solve at all — delta alone answers it: positive means two real roots, zero means one repeated, negative means none, and a perfect square adds the word rational. If the instruction says write in the form a, x plus p squared, plus q, or asks for a maximum or minimum, complete the square — the turning point is the actual product. The halfway wobble you describe comes from choosing by habit instead of by instruction. Read the ask, commit, and remember all three roads meet at the same roots: two plus or minus root seven by formula IS x minus two squared equals seven completed. The marks are in the fit between tool and task, not in loyalty to a favourite tool.

**Bianca:** Next: the ghost solution in surd equations. Learners do the check because they're told to — but what actually creates the false root?

**Tutor:** The squaring step is a one-way door, and knowing why makes the check feel necessary instead of ritual. An equation is a claim about equality, and squaring both sides preserves every true claim — but it also CREATES new ones, because squaring destroys sign information: three squared and minus three squared are both nine. Root of x plus seven equals x minus five claims a square root — never negative — equals x minus five. For x equals two, the right side is minus three: the original claim is false on its face. But square it, and minus three becomes nine, the same nine that root nine squared gives — the squared equation cannot tell the two claims apart, so it happily serves up x equals two alongside the genuine x equals nine. The quadratic you solved is a faithful record of the SQUARED claim, not the original one. That is why the check substitutes into the ORIGINAL equation, and why rejecting the ghost must be written down: the marker needs written proof that you distinguish the equation you were given from the one squaring manufactured. One line — x equals two invalid, since root nine is three, not minus three — closes the question completely and banks the mark reserved exactly for it.

**Bianca:** Trig general solutions: the k business feels like decoration, and plenty of answers stop at thirty degrees. What is k actually doing, and how do the restricted-interval questions fit in?

**Tutor:** The k is not decoration — it is most of the answer. Sine of x equals a half is a question posed to a function that repeats every three hundred and sixty degrees, forever, in both directions. Within one turn of the circle there are already TWO places at the right height: thirty degrees, and its mirror across the vertical, one hundred and eighty minus thirty — one hundred and fifty. Stopping at thirty misses half the circle; stopping at both misses every other turn of it. So the full answer is two families: thirty plus k times three sixty, and one fifty plus k times three sixty, with k running through all integers — each family an infinite ladder of solutions spaced one full turn apart. Cosine mirrors across the horizontal instead, so its two base angles are plus and minus the reference, which is why cosine answers compress into plus or minus the angle, plus k turns. Tangent repeats every half turn, so one family with k times one eighty covers everything. The restricted-interval question is the same answer with a fence: write the general families FIRST, then let k take values minus one, nought, one, and keep whatever lands inside the fence. Doing it in that order guarantees no solution escapes — the families are the net, the interval is just the sorting.

**Bianca:** Last one: how should the final weeks of revision be split across algebra, functions and trig — what genuinely moves marks?

**Tutor:** Split by question type, not by topic, and weight by mark density. First, build a one-page decision sheet per machine: for quadratics — solve, nature, inequality, surd, each with its two-line method; for functions — sketch, read-the-equation, domain and range, each anchored on turning point or asymptotes; for trig — reduce, prove, general solution, triangle rules, each with its trigger words. Recite the sheet, then work mixed past-paper sets against it, because the exam's difficulty is recognition speed, and blocked practice never trains recognition. Second, drill the four ghosts of this material by name: the unchecked surd root, the unflipped p in a bracket, the missing second solution family, the non-included angle fed into the cosine or area rule. One example of each, self-marked against a memo, twice in the final week — those four are where this paper bleeds marks. Third, harvest the free checks as a habit: substitute roots back, confirm the turning point satisfies the equation, verify the longer side faces the bigger angle, and test one k value inside the interval. Each check is seconds, each converts hope into certainty, and certainty is cumulative — a candidate who has verified question three walks into question four calmer, and calm is worth marks nothing else buys.
