# Part 1 — Expert

An equation asks which single value balances two sides. An inequality asks a larger question: which values satisfy a condition — and the answer is almost always a whole stretch of the number line rather than one point. Solving one uses the same algebra you already own, with exactly one rule of its own, and then demands two further skills the examination marks separately: the number-line sketch and interval notation. Four worked inequalities carry this session.

## Subtopic: Solving Like an Equation — With One Exception

The five signs, named precisely: less than, greater than, less than or equal to, greater than or equal to. The two with the bar under them include the boundary value; the two without exclude it, and that distinction survives to the very last line of your answer.

Almost every operation you use on equations is legal here. Adding the same quantity to both sides, subtracting the same quantity, multiplying or dividing both sides by a POSITIVE number — all leave the inequality pointing the same way.

Solve three x minus five is less than or equal to seven. Add five to both sides: three x is less than or equal to twelve. Divide both sides by three, a positive number: x is less than or equal to four. The sign never moved.

Now the exception, which is the whole reason this topic exists as a separate skill. Multiplying or dividing both sides by a NEGATIVE number reverses the inequality sign.

See why with plain numbers. Five is greater than three — beyond argument. Multiply both sides by minus one: minus five and minus three. Is minus five greater than minus three? No. Minus five is colder, poorer, further left on the number line. The truth survives only if the sign flips: minus five is LESS than minus three. Multiplying by a negative reflects both numbers through zero, and reflection reverses their order.

So solve minus two x is greater than six. Divide both sides by minus two — a negative — and flip: x is less than minus three. Test one value to be certain: try x equal to minus four. Minus two times minus four is eight, and eight is indeed greater than six. Correct. Had we not flipped, we would have claimed x is greater than minus three, and x equal to zero would have to work: minus two times zero is zero, which is not greater than six. The unflipped answer fails its own test.

One safe alternative worth knowing: avoid negative coefficients entirely by moving the variable to the side where it is positive. Minus two x is greater than six becomes minus six is greater than two x, then minus three is greater than x — read right to left, x is less than minus three. Same answer, no flip needed.

Pause here — the questions for this section are with you now. They will hand you divisions by negatives, and each one is asking whether you remembered to reverse the sign.

## Subtopic: The Number Line and Interval Notation

An inequality's answer is a set of numbers, and CAPS requires you to present it three ways: in words or symbols, as a number-line sketch, and in interval notation.

The sketch. Draw a horizontal line with an arrow at each end and mark the boundary value. If the boundary is INCLUDED — the sign has the bar, less than or equal to, or greater than or equal to — draw a solid, filled dot. If it is EXCLUDED — strictly less than or strictly greater than — draw an open, hollow dot. Then shade the side that satisfies the inequality and put an arrowhead on the shaded end to show it continues forever.

For x is less than or equal to four: a solid dot at four, shading running left, arrow pointing left. For x is less than minus three: an open dot at minus three, shading running left.

Interval notation. A round bracket excludes its endpoint; a square bracket includes it. Infinity is never a number you can reach, so it always takes a round bracket. South African convention separates the two endpoints with a semicolon.

So x is less than or equal to four is written as the interval from negative infinity to four, with a round bracket at infinity and a SQUARE bracket at four. And x is less than minus three is the interval from negative infinity to minus three, round brackets at both ends.

Two disciplines. First, always write the smaller value on the left; an interval written the wrong way round describes nothing. Second, state the domain: x is an element of the real numbers, unless the question restricts it. If the question says x is an integer, the answer is a list of dots on the number line, not a continuous shading — and for x less than or equal to four, the largest integer satisfying it is four itself.

Stop for this section's questions now — solid dot or open dot, square bracket or round, and the smaller value always on the left.

## Subtopic: Compound Inequalities — Trapped Between Two Fences

A compound inequality states two conditions at once, written as a single sentence with the variable in the middle.

Solve minus three is less than two x plus one, which is less than or equal to seven. Read it correctly first: two x plus one is greater than minus three AND at the same time is less than or equal to seven.

The method: perform every operation on all THREE parts simultaneously. Subtract one from each part: minus four is less than two x, which is less than or equal to six. Divide each part by two — positive, so no flips: minus two is less than x, which is less than or equal to three.

The answer, read in the natural direction: x is greater than minus two and less than or equal to three. On the number line, an open dot at minus two, a solid dot at three, and shading only between them — a finite segment, not an arrow. In interval notation: from minus two to three, round bracket at minus two, square bracket at three.

Where learners lose marks: operating on only two of the three parts, so that the middle drifts out of step with the ends. And if you ever divide a compound inequality by a negative, BOTH signs reverse and the whole statement must be rewritten with the smaller value on the left.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — three parts, every operation applied to all of them, and one dot solid while the other stays open.

## Subtopic: Word Problems, Integers, and the Trap Museum

Inequalities earn their place through context, and the marks live in the translation.

A stallholder at the Mthatha market pays R120 to rent her table for the day and sells vetkoek at R12 each. How many must she sell to make a profit of at least R300? Translate phrase by phrase. Let n be the number sold. Profit is income minus costs: twelve n minus one hundred and twenty. "At least R300" means greater than or equal to three hundred. So twelve n minus one hundred and twenty is greater than or equal to three hundred.

Solve: add one hundred and twenty to both sides — twelve n is greater than or equal to four hundred and twenty. Divide by twelve, positive, no flip — n is greater than or equal to thirty-five.

Now interpret in context, which is a separate mark. She cannot sell part of a vetkoek, so n is a whole number, and the answer is that she must sell at least thirty-five vetkoek. Check the boundary: thirty-five times twelve is four hundred and twenty; less the R120 rental, exactly R300 profit — the "at least" is satisfied by thirty-five itself, which is why the dot at thirty-five is solid.

Learn the translation table, because examiners rotate the phrasing. "At least" and "no less than" mean greater than or equal to. "At most", "no more than" and "maximum" mean less than or equal to. "More than" and "exceeds" mean strictly greater than. "Fewer than" and "under" mean strictly less than. And read carefully: "she wants to sell more than 35" excludes thirty-five, while "at least 35" includes it — one word, one dot, one mark.

The trap museum, four exhibits. One: forgetting to reverse the sign when dividing by a negative — the topic's signature error, and always catchable by testing a single value in the original. Two: reversing the sign when merely adding or subtracting a negative number, where no flip is warranted. Three: a solid dot where the sign has no bar. Four: an answer left as pure algebra in a context question, when the examiner wants a sentence — thirty-five vetkoek, not n is greater than or equal to thirty-five.

The final questions of this part are with you now — translate the phrase precisely, solve, then answer in the language of the problem.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same four inequalities from a wallet and a taxi queue — same answers, but this time the flipping rule will make obvious, everyday sense.

## Subtopic: Debt Turns Everything Upside Down

An equation is a scale that balances. An inequality is a scale that tips — one side is genuinely heavier, and your job is to say which numbers keep it tipping that way.

Almost everything you do to an equation is fine here. Add R50 to both sides of a comparison and the heavier side stays heavier. Take R20 off both, same. Double both, same. Halve both, same. Nothing dramatic happens.

Then comes the one move that changes everything, and money explains it perfectly. R500 in your pocket is more than R300 in your pocket — obviously. Now flip both into debt: you OWE R500, versus you OWE R300. Which position is better? Owing R300, clearly. The bigger number has become the worse position. In symbols: five hundred is greater than three hundred, but minus five hundred is LESS than minus three hundred.

That is the whole rule. Multiplying or dividing by a negative turns amounts into debts — it flips everything through zero — and the comparison must flip with it.

So when you meet minus two x is greater than six, dividing by minus two gives x is less than minus three. Not greater. Less.

And you never have to trust your memory on this, because one test settles it. Take any number that your answer says should work — say minus four — and put it into the ORIGINAL: minus two times minus four is eight, and eight is bigger than six. It works, so your answer is sound. Try that test with the unflipped version by putting in zero: minus two times zero is zero, and zero is not bigger than six. Fails. Ten seconds of testing beats a memorised rule every time.

Quick check before we carry on — a few questions on this are coming to you right now. Whenever a negative gets divided out, flip the sign, then test one number to prove you were right.

## Subtopic: Dots, Shading, and the Word "Or"

Your answer is not one number — it is a whole neighbourhood of numbers, so you have to show which ones.

Think about age rules at a venue. "Eighteen and over" includes the eighteen-year-old at the door — she gets in. "Over eighteen" leaves her outside. Same boundary, completely different result for the person standing on it.

That is exactly what the two kinds of dot mean. Solid, filled-in dot: this boundary number counts, and it comes from a sign with a bar under it. Open, hollow dot: this number does NOT count, and it comes from a plain less-than or greater-than. Then you shade the side where the answer lives, and draw an arrow to say it carries on forever.

For x is less than or equal to four: filled dot on four, shade everything to the left, arrow off the left end. For x is less than minus three: hollow dot on minus three, shade left.

Interval notation is the same picture written on one line, and the brackets do the same job as the dots. A square bracket is a filled dot — the number is invited. A round bracket is a hollow dot — it is not. Infinity always gets a round bracket, because you can never arrive at infinity. So x less than or equal to four reads: from negative infinity, round bracket, up to four, square bracket.

One rule of order: always write the smaller number first, left to right, exactly as it sits on the number line. And if a question says x is a whole number, do not shade a solid stretch — mark separate dots, because only the whole numbers count.

Your questions for this part are up now. For each one, decide first whether the boundary is invited or turned away, then draw the dot to match.

## Subtopic: Between Two Fences, and the Vetkoek Test

Some answers are fenced on both sides. A job advert says the salary is more than R8 000 but at most R12 000. That is a stretch with a gate at each end: the R8 000 gate is closed — you must earn more than that — while the R12 000 gate is open to you, because "at most" includes it.

Algebra writes that as one sentence with the unknown in the middle. Minus three is less than two x plus one, which is less than or equal to seven. Deal with it by doing the same thing to all three parts at once, like sharing something fairly. Take one off each part: minus four, then two x, then six. Halve each part — halving is friendly, no flip: minus two, then x, then three. So x sits between minus two and three, not touching minus two, but allowed to be exactly three. Hollow dot at minus two, filled dot at three, shade only the piece between them.

Now the money question that shows why any of this matters. A stallholder at the Mthatha market pays R120 for her table for the day and sells vetkoek at R12 each. She wants to make at least R300 profit. Her profit is twelve n minus one hundred and twenty, where n is how many she sells. "At least R300" means it must reach three hundred or go past it. So twelve n minus one hundred and twenty must be at least three hundred. Add the R120 back: twelve n must be at least four hundred and twenty. Divide by twelve: n must be at least thirty-five.

Then say it like a human being, because that is the mark: she must sell at least thirty-five vetkoek. And check the boundary yourself — thirty-five at R12 is R420, minus the R120 table, exactly R300. She hits her target on the thirty-fifth vetkoek, which is precisely why thirty-five counts and the dot is filled in.

Two words to keep straight for life. "At least" and "at most" INCLUDE the number — filled dot. "More than" and "fewer than" leave it out — hollow dot. One word decides whether the thirty-fifth vetkoek pays her, and you now read that word correctly every time.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: translate the words first, solve second, then answer in the language of the story — vetkoek, rands, people. That is a full-marks answer, and it is yours.
