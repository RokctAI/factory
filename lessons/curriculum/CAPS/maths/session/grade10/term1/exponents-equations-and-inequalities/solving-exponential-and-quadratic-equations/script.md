# Part 1 — Expert

Two families of equation dominate the algebra section of Paper 1, and both are solved by turning them into something you can already handle. A quadratic equation is forced into standard form and factorised, so that a product equal to zero can be read apart. An exponential equation is forced onto a single base, so that the exponents can be equated. Six worked equations carry this session, along with the two errors that cost more marks in this topic than any others: losing a solution, and equating exponents across different bases.

## Subtopic: Standard Form and the Zero-Product Law

A quadratic equation is one in which the highest power of the unknown is two. Solving it rests on a single fact of arithmetic, and the fact must be quoted correctly: if a product of two quantities equals ZERO, then at least one of those quantities is zero. That is the zero-product law, and it works for zero alone. There is no corresponding law for six — a product equal to six tells you nothing about either bracket.

That single restriction dictates the method. Before factorising anything, drive every term to one side so that the other side reads zero. Only then does factorising pay.

Solve x squared minus five x plus six equals zero. The right side is already zero, so factorise the left: two numbers multiplying to six and adding to minus five — minus two and minus three. So x minus two, times x minus three, equals zero. By the zero-product law, either x minus two is zero or x minus three is zero, giving x equals two or x equals three.

Write both. A quadratic has at most two solutions, and the word "or" between them is part of the answer — the equation is satisfied by either value, not by both together. Check by substitution, because checking is fast here: at x equal to two, four minus ten plus six is zero; at x equal to three, nine minus fifteen plus six is zero.

One more, to fix the standard-form habit. Solve two x squared equals eight. Bring everything across: two x squared minus eight equals zero. Common factor two: two, times x squared minus four, equals zero. The bracket is a difference of squares: two, times x minus two, times x plus two, equals zero. The constant two can never be zero, so the solutions come from the brackets: x equals two or x equals minus two. Learners who square-root both sides at the start and write x equals two have quietly discarded the negative root — the plus-minus is not optional.

Pause here — the questions for this section are with you now. They test standard form first, the zero-product law second, and whether you kept both solutions.

## Subtopic: Quadratics in Disguise — Brackets, Fractions, and the Lost Solution

Examiners rarely hand you standard form. They hand you something that becomes a quadratic after honest rearrangement.

First disguise: a product equal to something other than zero. Solve x plus three, times x minus two, equals six. The temptation is to set each bracket equal to six, which is meaningless. Expand instead: x squared plus x minus six equals six. Now drive everything to one side: x squared plus x minus twelve equals zero. Factorise: two numbers multiplying to minus twelve and adding to one — four and minus three — so x plus four, times x minus three, equals zero, giving x equals minus four or x equals three.

Second disguise: a fraction. Solve six over x equals x minus five. First state the restriction: x may not be zero. Multiply both sides by x: six equals x squared minus five x. Rearrange: x squared minus five x minus six equals zero. Factorise: minus six and plus one — so x minus six, times x plus one, equals zero, giving x equals six or x equals minus one. Neither is the forbidden zero, so both stand.

Third disguise, and the one that eats marks: an equation with no constant term. Solve x squared equals five x. The instinct is to divide both sides by x, leaving x equals five — half right, which in mathematics is wrong. Dividing by x assumes x is not zero, and here zero is a genuine solution: substitute it into the original and zero equals zero. Do it correctly — x squared minus five x equals zero; common factor x, times x minus five, equals zero; so x equals zero or x equals five. Fix the rule permanently: never divide an equation by an expression containing the unknown. Division destroys solutions; factorising preserves them.

Stop for this section's questions now — expand or clear fractions first, drive everything to one side, and never divide the variable away.

## Subtopic: Exponential Equations — the Same-Base Method

An exponential equation carries the unknown in the exponent. The method rests on a second single fact: if two powers with the SAME base are equal, their exponents are equal. Different bases, no such conclusion — that is why the whole method is an exercise in rewriting until the bases agree.

Solve two to the power x equals thirty-two. Rewrite thirty-two as a power of two: two to the fifth. Now two to the x equals two to the five, same base, so x equals five.

Solve three to the power x plus one equals eighty-one. Eighty-one is three to the fourth, so equate exponents: x plus one equals four, giving x equals three. Note the whole exponent is equated, brackets and all.

Solve nine to the power x equals twenty-seven. The bases look unrelated until you notice both are powers of three. Nine is three squared, so the left side is three squared, all to the power x — by the power law, three to the two x. Twenty-seven is three cubed. So two x equals three, giving x equals three over two. A fractional answer is perfectly acceptable; leave it exact.

Solve five to the power two x minus one equals one over one hundred and twenty-five. One hundred and twenty-five is five cubed, and the reciprocal makes the exponent negative, so the right side is five to the minus three. Equate: two x minus one equals minus three, so x equals minus one.

The habit that makes all of this fast: know your small powers on sight — powers of two up to sixty-four, of three up to eighty-one, of five up to one hundred and twenty-five.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — rewrite both sides onto one base, then equate the exponents in full.

## Subtopic: Common Factors, Rational Exponents, and the Trap Museum

Two harder types complete the Grade 10 picture.

Type one: an exponential equation with two terms. Solve three to the power x, plus three to the power x plus one, equals thirty-six. You cannot equate anything yet, because the left side is a sum. Split the second term using the multiplication law: three to the power x plus one is three to the x, times three to the one. So the left side is three to the x, times the quantity one plus three — that is four, times three to the x. The equation becomes four times three to the x equals thirty-six, so three to the x equals nine, which is three squared, giving x equals two. The move to remember: take out the common power as a factor, exactly as you would take out a common algebraic factor.

Type two: a rational exponent on the base, with the unknown in the base rather than the exponent. Solve x to the power two thirds equals nine. Raise both sides to the reciprocal power, three over two: x equals nine to the power three over two — the square root of nine, cubed, which is twenty-seven. Check: twenty-seven to the power two thirds is the cube root of twenty-seven, squared, which is nine. Correct.

Now a word problem, because the examination dresses quadratics in context. A rectangular vegetable garden in Polokwane is three metres longer than it is wide, and its area is forty square metres. Let the width be x metres, so the length is x plus three: x times x plus three equals forty. Rearranged, x squared plus three x minus forty equals zero, which factorises to x plus eight, times x minus five, so x equals minus eight or x equals five. Now the context speaks: a width of minus eight metres does not exist, so that root is rejected with a stated reason. The width is five metres and the length is eight metres. Always reject in writing; a silently discarded root reads as a missed root.

The trap museum, four exhibits. One: setting brackets equal to a non-zero number — the zero-product law applies to zero only. Two: dividing by the unknown and losing a root, as in x squared equals five x. Three: equating exponents across different bases — two to the x equals eight to the two is not x equals two; convert first. Four: expecting an exponential to reach zero or a negative value — two to the power x equals zero has no solution, because a positive base raised to any real power stays positive.

The final questions of this part are with you now — the common-factor move, the reciprocal power, and a context root rejected with its reason stated.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same equations from a taxi rank and a spaza till — same six answers, but this time built from ideas you can hold in your hand.

## Subtopic: Two Things That Multiply to Nothing

Start with a fact about money that you already believe. A stall's takings equal the price of one item times the number sold. If the takings for the day are R0, what happened? Either the price was zero — she was giving things away — or she sold nothing at all. There is no third story. Two numbers multiply to zero only when at least one of them IS zero.

Now notice how special zero is. If the takings were R60, that tells you nothing useful: maybe R6 each times ten, maybe R60 once, maybe R2 times thirty. A product equal to sixty has endless stories. A product equal to zero has one. That is why every quadratic must be pushed until one side reads zero before you factorise — zero is the only number that talks.

So, x squared minus five x plus six equals zero. Factorise the left side into two brackets — two numbers that multiply to six and add to minus five, which are minus two and minus three — giving x minus two, times x minus three. Two things multiplying to zero. So x minus two is zero, meaning x is two; or x minus three is zero, meaning x is three. Two answers, joined by "or", both correct.

And check them like a shopkeeper checks change. Put two in: four minus ten plus six, nothing left over. Put three in: nine minus fifteen plus six, also nothing. Both work — and you proved it yourself, without the memo.

Quick check before we carry on — a few questions on this are coming to you right now. For each one, first ask: is one side zero yet? If not, move everything across before you do anything else.

## Subtopic: Get Everything Into the Same Currency

Here is a shopping problem. Which is more: five hundred grams of maize meal, or two kilograms? You cannot compare them until they are in the same unit. Convert — two kilograms is two thousand grams — and the answer is instant.

Exponential equations are exactly that conversion job. When you see two to the power x equals thirty-two, the two sides are quoted in different "currencies": the left is in powers of two, the right is a plain number. Convert the right one: thirty-two is two, doubled five times — two to the fifth. Now both sides are in the same currency, and you can compare like with like. Two to the x equals two to the fifth, so x is five. Nothing clever happened; you just changed the units.

Try a harder pair. Nine to the power x equals twenty-seven. Neither side is a power of the other, so hunt for a currency they share — and both are built from threes. Nine is three times three, so nine to the power x is three to the power two x. Twenty-seven is three cubed. Same currency now, so two x equals three, and x is three over two. Leave it as a fraction; that is the exact answer.

One more, with a reciprocal. Five to the power two x minus one equals one over one hundred and twenty-five. One hundred and twenty-five is five cubed, and a one-over makes the exponent negative — so the right side is five to the minus three. Equate: two x minus one equals minus three, and x is minus one.

The single skill here is recognising the family a number belongs to. Learn the small ones like phone numbers: powers of two up to sixty-four, powers of three up to eighty-one, powers of five up to one hundred and twenty-five. Once you spot the family, the equation solves itself.

Your questions for this part are up now. For each equation, name the shared base first, then rewrite both sides in it before you compare anything.

## Subtopic: The Answer You Threw in the Bin

Last piece, and it is the difference between full marks and half marks.

Look at x squared equals five x. Almost everyone divides both sides by x and writes x equals five. It is not wrong — five really is a solution — but it is not the whole truth, because zero is a solution too. Put zero into the original: zero equals zero. Perfectly true.

Why did dividing hide it? Because dividing by x quietly assumes x is not zero. You may divide by four, or by minus seven, or by any number you know is not zero — but x is not a known number. It might be zero, and the moment you divide by it you have thrown that possibility in the bin.

Do it the safe way instead. Move everything to one side: x squared minus five x equals zero. Take out the common factor: x, times x minus five, equals zero. Now the takings rule applies: either x is zero, or x minus five is zero. Two answers, both kept. Make that a permanent rule: never divide an equation by anything containing the unknown. Dividing destroys answers; factorising keeps them.

And the opposite habit matters too — sometimes a correct answer must be sent away, with a reason. A vegetable garden in Polokwane is three metres longer than it is wide and covers forty square metres. Call the width x: x times x plus three equals forty, which becomes x squared plus three x minus forty equals zero, factorising to x plus eight, times x minus five. The maths gives minus eight or five. But a garden cannot be minus eight metres wide, so you write that the negative root is rejected because a width cannot be negative, and you answer: five metres by eight metres. Keep every root the algebra gives you, and reject only the ones reality refuses — out loud, in writing.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: hunt for the hidden root in each one, and where a context appears, say plainly which root you are rejecting and why. You have earned this one.
