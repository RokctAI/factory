**Student:** I wrote $(2x^3y)^2 = 2x^6y^2$ and lost a mark. The x and y got squared — why not the 2?

**Thandi:** The 2 is a factor inside the bracket, and the squaring outside applies to EVERY factor — no exceptions, no favourites! $(2x^3y)^2$ means $(2x^3y) \times (2x^3y)$: the twos multiply to give 4, the $x^3$'s give $x^6$, the $y$'s give $y^2$. So it's $4x^6y^2$. Here's a habit that fixes it forever: when you see a bracket raised to a power, physically tick each factor inside as you apply the power — tick the 2, tick the $x^3$, tick the $y$. Three factors, three ticks, nothing escapes.

**Student:** Why does $x^{-2}$ mean $\frac{1}{x^2}$? Who decided that, and why should a minus mean a fraction?

**Thandi:** It's not a decision — it's forced by the subtraction law! Watch the pattern: $\frac{x^5}{x^2} = x^3$, subtract the exponents. Now do $\frac{x^2}{x^5}$: subtracting gives $x^{-3}$. But you can also see it directly — two x's cancel and three are left *underneath*: $\frac{1}{x^3}$. Same question, two answers, so they must be equal: $x^{-3} = \frac{1}{x^3}$. The negative exponent was never a choice; it's what "keep cancelling past zero" looks like. The minus doesn't make anything negative — it points to the basement of the fraction.

**Student:** For $3^{x+1} = 27$, why can't I just divide both sides by 3 to get $x + 1 = 9$?

**Thandi:** Because dividing by 3 doesn't peel the exponent off — it changes it by one! By the subtraction law, $3^{x+1} \div 3 = 3^{x}$, and the right side becomes 9. So you'd get $3^x = 9$... which is a *correct* step, just not the shortcut you hoped. The clean road: rewrite 27 as $3^3$, so $3^{x+1} = 3^3$, and now the bases match — the exponents must be equal: $x + 1 = 3$, $x = 2$. Golden rule for exponential equations: never fight the exponent — match the bases and let the exponents talk to each other directly.

**Student:** How do I check my simplification without redoing the whole thing? Redoing takes forever and I make the same mistake twice anyway.

**Thandi:** You've discovered exactly why redoing is a weak check — same brain, same slip! Use the substitution test instead: pick easy values like x = 1, y = 1 (or x = 2 if the x's matter a lot), and evaluate BOTH the original expression and your answer as plain numbers. Our lesson's monster at x = 1, y = 1: original works out to 2; our answer $2x^3$ gives 2. Match — confidence earned in twenty seconds. One caution: x = 1 can hide exponent errors (1 to any power is 1), so if you have time, test x = 2 as well. Two matches and your answer is basically bulletproof.
