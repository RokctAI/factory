**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! First: someone wrote two x cubed y, all squared, as two x to the six y squared, and lost the mark. The x and the y got squared — so why not the two as well?

**Tutor:** Because the two is a factor sitting inside the bracket, and a power placed on a bracket applies to every factor inside it without exception and without favourites. Unfold what the squaring actually means: two x cubed y, all squared, is two x cubed y multiplied by two x cubed y. Now collect. The twos multiply to give four. The x cubed multiplied by x cubed gives x to the six. The y multiplied by y gives y squared. So the bracket becomes four x to the six y squared, and the two has changed to four exactly as the letters changed. Learners lose this one so often because the letters look like the mathematics and the number looks like decoration, when in truth the number is a factor with the same rights and duties as everything else. The fix is a counting habit: when a bracket takes a power, count the factors inside — here there are three — and confirm you have handed the power to all three before writing the next line.

**Thandi:** Next one: why does x to the negative two mean one over x squared? The class wants to know who decided that, and why a minus should mean a fraction rather than a negative number.

**Tutor:** Nobody decided it — it is forced by the dividing law, and you can watch it being forced. Start with something ordinary: x to the five divided by x squared. Subtracting the counters gives x cubed, and cancelling agrees, because two of the x's underneath knock out two above and three survive on top. Now turn the same division upside down: x squared divided by x to the five. Subtracting gives x to the negative three. But cancelling still works and gives a plain answer: the two x's on top knock out two of the five below, leaving three x's underneath and nothing special above, which is one over x cubed. One division, two correct routes, so the two answers must be the same thing: x to the negative three is one over x cubed. The minus never made anything negative. It records that the counting ran past zero and the surviving x's ended up below the line rather than above it. That is why we call it a position instruction: four x to the negative two is four over x squared, with the four staying upstairs where it always was.

**Thandi:** This one caused an argument: for three to the power x plus one equals twenty-seven, why can we not simply divide both sides by three and get x plus one equals nine?

**Tutor:** Because dividing by three does not lift the exponent off — it changes the exponent by one and leaves you still inside a power. By the dividing law, three to the power x plus one, divided by three, is three to the power x. Meanwhile twenty-seven divided by three is nine. So the honest result of that move is three to the power x equals nine, which is perfectly true and perfectly solvable, but it is not what was written down; writing x plus one equals nine treats the power as though it were a product, and that step is simply false. The reliable road is base-matching. Twenty-seven is three cubed, so the equation becomes three to the power x plus one equals three cubed. Both sides are now powers of the same base, and equal powers of the same positive base force their exponents to agree, so x plus one equals three and x is two. Substituting back gives three cubed, which is twenty-seven. The golden rule for exponential equations is never to fight the exponent, but to make the bases agree and let the exponents speak to each other.

**Thandi:** Last one: how do we check a simplification without doing the whole thing again? Redoing takes ages and people say they just make the same mistake twice.

**Tutor:** You have identified exactly why redoing is a weak check — the same brain repeats the same slip, and a wrong answer arrives twice looking confident. Use a substitution test instead, which checks the result rather than the route. Choose easy values, put them into the original expression, put the same values into your answer, and compare the two plain numbers. For today's expression with x equal to one and y equal to one: the bracket is two, squared is four; multiplied by four gives sixteen; divided by eight gives two. The answer, two x cubed, gives two times one, which is two. They agree, so the simplification survives its first real test. One caution worth carrying: the value one hides exponent errors, because one raised to any power is still one, so if x mistakenly became x to the four instead of x cubed the test would not notice. Where the x's matter, run it again with x equal to two. Two agreeing tests make an answer very hard to doubt.
