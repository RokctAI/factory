Hey, it's Thandi — if this topic feels like two completely different subjects sharing one page, that is because it nearly is. Quadratics and exponentials look nothing alike. The good news: each one has exactly one idea holding it up, and once you name the idea, the working is short.

Quadratics stand on zero. Push every term to one side until the other side reads zero, factorise, then read the brackets apart — because a product equal to zero means one of the parts is zero. That is the only number that gives you that information, which is why standard form comes first, always, before you factorise anything. If a question hands you brackets equal to six, expand and move the six across. If it hands you a fraction, note the restriction, multiply it away, and carry on.

Exponentials stand on matching bases. You may only equate exponents when both sides sit on the SAME base, so the whole job is rewriting until they do. Learn the small powers the way you know a phone number — two, four, eight, sixteen, thirty-two, sixty-four; three, nine, twenty-seven, eighty-one; five, twenty-five, one hundred and twenty-five. Once you spot which family a number belongs to, the equation is basically finished.

Now the habit that protects your marks more than any other: never divide an equation by anything with an x in it. Not once. If there is no constant term, take the x out as a common factor instead. Dividing by x throws away the root x equals zero, and markers see that missing zero instantly.

And its twin habit: when a question has a real-life setting, check each root against reality and reject the impossible one in writing. A width of minus eight metres, a negative number of learners, a negative price — say "rejected, because a length cannot be negative". A silently discarded root looks like a root you never found.

So: quadratics go to zero, exponentials go to a shared base, never divide by x, and reject out loud. Four sentences, and the whole topic is inside them. You've got this!
