# Part 1 — Expert

This session builds full command of the laws of exponents — the grammar of algebra. Two tasks organise everything: first, simplify the expression: open bracket, two x cubed y, close bracket, all squared, multiplied by four x to the power negative two, all divided by eight x y squared. Second, solve the exponential equation: three to the power x plus one equals twenty-seven. One expression, one equation — and between them, every law you need for Paper 1.

## Subtopic: The Laws — and Why They Are True

You met these laws in Grade 9; today we make them permanent by understanding why each one works. An exponent counts repeated multiplication: x to the power three means x times x times x. Every law flows from that single fact.

Law one: when multiplying powers of the same base, ADD the exponents. x cubed times x squared is five x's multiplied together — x to the five. Same base, add.

Law two: when dividing powers of the same base, SUBTRACT the exponents. x to the five over x squared: two x's cancel, three survive. Same base, subtract.

Law three: a power raised to a power — MULTIPLY the exponents. x cubed, squared, means x cubed times x cubed: six x's. Multiply.

Law four: a bracket containing a product, raised to a power, gives that power to EVERY factor inside. Two x, all squared, is two squared times x squared — four x squared. The most-broken law in Grade 10: learners square the x and forget the two. Every factor takes the power.

Two special definitions complete the kit. Anything nonzero to the power ZERO equals one — because x cubed divided by x cubed is both one and, by law two, x to the zero. And a NEGATIVE exponent means reciprocal: x to the negative two is one over x squared — because subtracting exponents can drive the count below zero, and the x's keep cancelling into the denominator. A negative exponent is a position instruction — move to the other side of the fraction line — not a negative number.

The questions for this section are with you now: each law matched to its operation, the zero exponent justified rather than asserted, and the negative exponent read as a position instruction.

## Subtopic: Simplifying the Expression — Brackets First

Now the expression: two x cubed y, all squared, times four x to the negative two, over eight x y squared. Strategy: clear the bracket power first, then gather like bases, then apply the laws base by base.

Step one — the bracket. Two x cubed y, all squared: law four gives the square to every factor. Two squared is four. X cubed squared: law three, multiply exponents — x to the six. Y squared stays y squared. The bracket becomes four x to the six y squared.

Step two — the numerator. Four x to the six y squared, times four x to the negative two. Multiply the coefficients: four times four is sixteen. Same-base x's: law one, add exponents — six plus negative two is four. So x to the four. The y squared rides along. Numerator: sixteen x to the four y squared.

Step three — the division. Sixteen x to the four y squared, over eight x y squared. Take it base by base, deliberately. Coefficients: sixteen over eight is two. The x's: law two, subtract — four minus one is three; x cubed. The y's: two minus two is zero — y to the zero, which is one; the y's vanish entirely.

The answer: two x cubed.

Verify with a number test — the professional's habit. Let x equal one and y equal one, purely for checking. Original: bracket is two, squared is four; times four times one to the negative two, which is four — sixteen; over eight — two. Our answer at x equals one: two times one — two. They match. One substitution never proves an identity, but it catches nearly every slip, and it costs twenty seconds.

The discipline to copy: one law per line, coefficients handled as ordinary numbers, each base processed on its own. Exponent errors come from rushing three laws into one line — slow is smooth, and smooth earns full marks.

Take this section's questions before moving to the equation: the bracket cleared with every factor given its power, the bases collected separately, and the y terms disappearing through the zero exponent.

## Subtopic: Solving Three to the x Plus One Equals Twenty-Seven

Now the equation: three to the power x plus one equals twenty-seven. An exponential equation — the unknown lives in the exponent. The strategy has one central move: make the bases match.

Why? Because if two powers are equal AND their bases are equal — same positive base, not equal to one — then their exponents must be equal. Three to the something equals three to the something-else forces the somethings to agree. That is the bridge from an exponent equation to an ordinary linear one.

Twenty-seven — is it a power of three? Three, nine, twenty-seven: yes — three cubed. Rewrite the equation: three to the x plus one equals three to the three.

Bases match; equate the exponents: x plus one equals three. Solve: x equals two.

Verify — substitute back, always: three to the power two plus one is three cubed, twenty-seven. Confirmed.

That worked because twenty-seven cooperated. The exam's favourite variations, so you recognise the same move in costume. If the right side were one: rewrite one as three to the zero, so x plus one equals zero — x equals negative one. If it were one over twenty-seven: three to the negative three, so x equals negative four. If it were, say, five to the x plus one equals one hundred and twenty-five: five cubed, same machine, different base. The move never changes: express both sides as powers of one shared base, then equate exponents.

And a warning about a tempting wrong turn: do not divide both sides by three hoping to peel the exponent — three to the x plus one divided by three is three to the x, by law two, which can be useful, but learners who "divide by three" and write x plus one equals nine have broken the mathematics. The safe road is the base-matching road.

This section's questions are in front of you now — the base-matching move executed, the variations with one and with one over twenty-seven handled, and the reason equal bases permit equal exponents stated properly.

## Subtopic: The Error Museum — and the Full Method

Walk through the error museum: four exhibits, each a mark-loser you will now never repeat.

Exhibit one: the bracket power that misses the coefficient. Two x cubed y, all squared, written as two x to the six y squared — the two escaped unsquared. Law four applies to EVERY factor: the two becomes four. Whenever a bracket is raised to a power, tick off each factor inside as you give it the power.

Exhibit two: adding exponents across DIFFERENT bases. X squared times y cubed is not x y to the five — the laws only compress powers sharing a base. Gather x's with x's, y's with y's, numbers with numbers.

Exhibit three: the negative exponent treated as a negative number. Four x to the negative two is not negative four x squared, and it is not minus four over x squared. The negative exponent relocates the x squared to the denominator; the four stays where it is: four over x squared. Position, not sign.

Exhibit four: multiplying the base by the exponent — three to the x plus one becoming three times x plus one. An exponent counts multiplications; it never multiplies the base directly. If this were legal, three cubed would be nine instead of twenty-seven.

Now the full method, gathered. For simplifying: clear bracket powers first, giving the power to every factor; collect like bases; add exponents when multiplying, subtract when dividing; convert negative exponents to reciprocals for the final answer; check with a quick substitution. For exponential equations: express both sides as powers of one base; equate the exponents; solve the ordinary equation that remains; substitute back to confirm.

These laws never leave you. Grade 11 hands you surds and rational exponents — the same laws with fraction powers. Functions, finance formulas, and eventually logarithms in matric — all of them are these five laws, wearing new contexts. The twenty minutes of practice you do tonight compounds for three years — fittingly, just like the exponents themselves.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the four museum errors identified by name, the simplifying sequence recited in order, and the exponential-equation method separated cleanly from it.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same laws from a taxi rank and a crate of cooldrink — counting, sorting, and one rule about who pays the fare.

## Subtopic: Exponents Just Count How Many Times

Strip the fear off first. An exponent is a counter, nothing more. X cubed — x³ — means "three x's multiplied together". X to the five means five of them. The little number counts how many times the big letter shows up in the multiplication, and every law in this topic is just bookkeeping on that count.

Multiplying? Push the piles together. Three x's times two x's is one pile of five x's, so x cubed times x squared is x to the five. You added three and two because you were counting bodies, not adding values. Same base multiplied: add the counters.

Dividing? Cancel one against one. Five x's on top, two underneath: each of the two underneath knocks out one on top, and three survive. So x to the five over x squared is x cubed. Same base divided: subtract the counters.

A power on a power? Repeat the pile. X cubed, squared, means the pile of three taken twice: six x's altogether, so x to the six. Multiply the counters.

Now the two that everyone finds strange, and both fall straight out of the counting. Anything nonzero to the power zero is one. Why? X cubed divided by x cubed is obviously one, because a thing divided by itself is one — and by the subtracting rule it is also x to the zero. Two names for the same answer, so x to the zero is one. And a negative counter? Take two x's and divide them by five: subtracting gives x to the negative three, but by cancelling you can also see two knocked out on top and three left sitting underneath, which is one over x cubed. So x to the negative three and one over x cubed are the same thing. The minus does not make anything negative. It is a location — it tells the power it belongs on the other side of the fraction line, downstairs instead of upstairs.

Your questions for this section are with you now — answer them by counting rather than by remembering, and the laws will start to feel like common sense.

## Subtopic: Everyone in the Taxi Pays the Fare

Now the messy-looking expression: two x cubed y, all squared, times four x to the negative two, all over eight x y squared. It looks like one enormous problem. It is four small ones standing close together.

Start with the bracket, and here is the rule that saves the most marks in Grade 10: when a bracket gets a power, every single passenger inside pays the fare. Nobody rides free. Inside the bracket sit three passengers — the two, the x cubed and the y — so all three get squared. Two squared is four. X cubed squared is x to the six, because you repeat the pile. Y squared is y squared. The bracket becomes four x to the six y squared. The classic mistake is leaving the two unsquared: that is one passenger skipping the fare, and the marker notices immediately.

Next, sort the washing into piles: numbers with numbers, x's with x's, y's with y's. Across the top we have four x to the six y squared times four x to the negative two. Numbers: four times four is sixteen. X's: six plus negative two is four, so x to the four. Y's: just y squared, riding along. The top is sixteen x to the four y squared.

Now the division by eight x y squared, again pile by pile. Numbers: sixteen over eight is two. X's: four minus one is three, so x cubed. Y's: two minus two is zero, and anything to the power zero is one — so the y's cancel out completely and simply leave. The answer is two x cubed.

Then check it, because you can. Put x equal to one and y equal to one into the original: the bracket is two, squared is four; times four times one, which is sixteen; over eight, which is two. Now the same values in your answer: two times one cubed is two. They match. That is a proper check, and it costs almost nothing.

Take the questions for this section now — go pile by pile, one line per move, and let the monster fall apart politely.

## Subtopic: Speak Both Sides in the Same Language

Last piece: the equation. Three to the power x plus one equals twenty-seven. The unknown is up in the counter, which feels wrong the first time you see it, so here is the way in.

Two people can only agree if they are speaking the same language. On the left the language is "powers of three". On the right, twenty-seven is sitting there in plain numbers. So translate it: three, nine, twenty-seven — twenty-seven is three cubed. Now both sides speak powers of three: three to the x plus one equals three to the three.

And once both sides are powers of the same base, the counters have to match, because there is no other way for the two sides to be equal. So x plus one equals three, which gives x equals two. Check by putting it back: three to the power two plus one is three cubed, which is twenty-seven. Correct.

The same move handles every variation the exam dresses up. If the right side were one, translate one as three to the zero, so x plus one equals zero and x is negative one. If it were one over twenty-seven, that is three to the negative three, so x plus one equals negative three and x is negative four. If the question used five to the power x plus one equals one hundred and twenty-five, translate one hundred and twenty-five as five cubed, and x is two again. Different costume, same move: get both sides speaking one language, then let the counters talk.

One tempting wrong turn to refuse. Do not divide both sides by three hoping the exponent will fall off. Dividing by three does not remove the power — it lowers the counter by one, giving three to the x on the left and nine on the right, which is a true statement but not the shortcut anyone wanted. And never turn three to the power x plus one into three times x plus one: the counter counts multiplications of three by itself, it does not multiply the three. If that were allowed, three cubed would be nine instead of twenty-seven.

Here come the last questions of the lesson: counting, sorting, and translating. Answer them and you own the grammar that every formula from here to matric is written in.
