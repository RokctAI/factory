**Thandi:** Class questions before we finish! First: someone asks how to recognise, at speed, whether an equation is the quadratic kind or the exponential kind — they say the two methods blur together under pressure.

**Tutor:** Ask one diagnostic question: WHERE does the unknown live? If x sits down in the base — squared, multiplied, added — you are holding a quadratic or something that becomes one: the road is standard form, factorise, zero-product law. If x lives upstairs in the exponent — two to the power x, five to the power x minus two — you are holding an exponential: the road is one shared base, then equate exponents. The methods never mix, because the two facts powering them never mix: zero-product for quadratics, equal-bases-equal-exponents for exponentials. Where learners blur is the in-between costume — something like x to the power three over two, where the unknown is in the base but the exponent is a fraction. That one belongs to the base family: free the x by raising both sides to the reciprocal power. So before writing anything, say the location out loud: unknown downstairs, factorise; unknown upstairs, translate bases; unknown downstairs with a fraction power, reciprocal power. Three sentences, and the blur is gone.

***

**Thandi:** Next: the restriction on the fraction equation. A learner solved eight over x equals x minus two, got four and minus two, and wants to know why they had to write x not equal to zero when zero was never one of the answers anyway.

**Tutor:** Because the restriction is not a prediction about the answers — it is a condition on the QUESTION, and it must stand whether or not it ends up biting. The original equation contains eight over x, and at x equal to zero that expression is undefined: the equation does not even exist there. When you multiplied both sides by x, you created a new equation — x squared minus two x minus eight equals zero — that is genuinely different: it is defined at zero, and nothing in it remembers that zero was forbidden. The restriction is the thread connecting the new equation back to the old one. In this question the thread stayed slack: four and minus two are both legal, both roots survive. But shift the numbers slightly and the thread pulls tight — equations exist where the algebra hands back the very value the original banned, and without the written restriction you would present an illegal root as an answer. So the habit is: the moment you see the unknown in a denominator, write its ban BEFORE multiplying through, then audit your final roots against it. One line of insurance, written in advance, priced at zero marks lost.

**Thandi:** Third one, and half the class got caught: two to the x, plus two to the x plus two, equals forty. Several people added the left side into two to the power two x plus two. Why is that wrong, and how should they see the right move?

**Tutor:** The law they reached for — add the exponents — belongs to MULTIPLICATION, and the left side is an ADDITION. Two to the x times two to the x plus two would indeed add counters; two to the x plus two to the x plus two does not. The right move is the one you already trust from algebra: take out a common factor. Both terms contain two to the x — the second term is two to the x times two squared, by the very multiplication law being misused. So the left side is two to the x, times the quantity one plus four: five times two to the x. Now the equation reads five times something equals forty, so the something is eight, and two to the x equals eight means x is three. If the abstraction wobbles, hold the parcels picture: five identical parcels together weigh forty, so one parcel weighs eight. The deeper lesson: exponent laws compress products, never sums. When plus signs appear between powers, reach for factorising — the same reflex you use on x squared plus x. Same base visible in both terms, out it comes.

**Thandi:** Last question: on the vegetable bed problem, someone wants to know why the negative root even shows up if it was never a possible width. Is the algebra making a mistake?

**Tutor:** The algebra makes no mistake — it answers a bigger question than the one you asked. When you write x times x plus two equals thirty-five, the equation knows nothing about gardens: it asks, with complete generality, which numbers multiplied by two-more-than-themselves give thirty-five. There are exactly two: five, and minus seven — check the second: minus seven times minus five is thirty-five. Both answers are TRUE for the equation. The garden entered only when you chose what x means, and it is that modelling choice, not the algebra, that rules minus seven out: widths are not negative. This is why the rejection must be written and reasoned — you are completing the translation from mathematics back to the world, and the marker needs to see that step, because a missing root and a rejected root look identical on a page that says only "x equals five". Write: x equals minus seven rejected, since width cannot be negative; therefore width five metres, length seven metres. And expect the pattern for years: quadratic models routinely produce one physical root and one ghost. The ghost is not an error. It is the algebra being thorough, and your written rejection is you being thorough back.
