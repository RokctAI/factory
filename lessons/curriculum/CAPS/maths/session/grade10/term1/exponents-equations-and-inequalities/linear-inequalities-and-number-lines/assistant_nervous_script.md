Hey, it's Thandi — inequalities feel scary for a strange reason: they look like equations you already know, so every little difference feels like a trap waiting to spring. Let me shrink the whole topic down, because there is genuinely only one new rule in it.

Solve it exactly like an equation. Add, subtract, multiply by a positive, divide by a positive — nothing changes, the sign stays as it is. The one and only exception: if you multiply or divide both sides by a NEGATIVE number, the sign flips over. Less than becomes greater than, and the other way round. That is the whole difference between this chapter and the equations chapter.

If the rule slips your mind under pressure, do not panic and do not guess — test it. Take one number your answer allows and put it into the original inequality. If it makes a true statement, you flipped correctly. If it does not, flip and move on. That fifteen-second check turns the scariest rule in the chapter into something you can verify on the spot.

The dots are just as easy once you tie them to real life. Think of a venue: "eighteen and over" lets the eighteen-year-old in — that is a filled-in dot, and it comes from a sign with a bar underneath. "Over eighteen" turns her away — hollow dot, from a plain less-than or greater-than. Same brackets in interval notation: square bracket for invited, round bracket for turned away, and infinity always gets a round bracket because you never actually arrive there.

Two habits that quietly protect marks. First, always write your interval with the smaller number on the left, the way it sits on the number line. Second, in a word problem, finish with a sentence in the story's own language — "she must sell at least thirty-five vetkoek" — not just the algebra. Examiners give a separate mark for that interpretation, and it is one of the easiest marks in the paper to collect.

One new rule, one test to check it, two kinds of dot. That is the whole chapter, and you already know the rest. You've got this!
