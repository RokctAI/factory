**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! First: we get that dividing by a negative flips the sign. But what if there is a negative number in the question and we only add or subtract it — do we flip then too?

**Tutor:** No, and this is one of the most common over-corrections in the topic. The rule is specific: the reversal is caused by multiplying or dividing both sides by a negative quantity, not by the mere presence of a negative number somewhere in the working. Adding or subtracting never changes the direction, whatever the sign of the number. Picture a scale where the left pan is genuinely heavier. Put the same mass on both pans, or take the same mass off both pans, and the left pan is still heavier — the difference between them has not changed at all. So in five x minus seven is greater than eight, adding seven to both sides gives five x greater than fifteen, sign untouched, then dividing by five, a positive, gives x greater than three, still untouched. Contrast that with minus five x greater than fifteen: dividing by minus five reverses it to x less than minus three. Ask yourself one question at each line: am I multiplying or dividing the whole inequality by a negative? If the answer is no, leave the sign alone. And if you would rather never face the decision, rearrange so the variable ends up with a positive coefficient — move the term to the other side instead of dividing by a negative — and no flip ever arises.

**Thandi:** Next: why is the bracket next to infinity always round? Infinity is not really a number we could land on anyway, so does the bracket even matter?

**Tutor:** It matters because notation is a promise about what is in your set, and a square bracket promises the endpoint itself belongs to it. Infinity is not a real number; it is a direction, a statement that the set carries on without end. Since there is no number called infinity for the set to contain, you can never claim to include it, so it always takes a round bracket. Think of it as the difference between a fence post and a road that never ends. A square bracket says "the fence post is inside my property"; a round bracket says "my land runs up to here but that point is not mine". At the infinity end there simply is no post to own. So x less than or equal to four is written from negative infinity to four with a round bracket on the left and a square bracket on the right, because four is a genuine number that satisfies the inequality and is therefore included. The same logic decides your dots: filled where a real endpoint qualifies, hollow where it does not, and an arrowhead rather than any dot at the infinite end. Keep the two ends independent — one interval can perfectly well have a round bracket on one side and a square bracket on the other.

**Thandi:** Someone asked: in a compound inequality, why can't we just split it into two separate inequalities, solve each, and put the answers together?

**Tutor:** You can, and it gives the right answer — the compound form is simply a compact way of writing the same two conditions. Minus three less than two x plus one, less than or equal to seven, genuinely means two x plus one is greater than minus three AND two x plus one is less than or equal to seven. Split them and you get x greater than minus two, and x less than or equal to three, which combine to minus two less than x, less than or equal to three. Same answer. Two cautions, though. First, the joining word is AND, not or: only values satisfying both conditions qualify, so you take the overlap of the two answers, not everything either one allows. Second, working all three parts at once is faster and less error-prone in an examination, provided you apply every operation to all three parts — subtract one from each part, divide each part by two. Learners lose marks by operating on the two outer parts and forgetting the middle, which quietly destroys the statement. And one special case worth naming: if you ever divide a compound inequality by a negative, both signs reverse, and you must then rewrite the whole statement with the smaller value on the left so it still reads correctly from left to right.

**Thandi:** Last one: in word problems I get the algebra right and still lose a mark. What exactly is the marker looking for at the end?

**Tutor:** Two things beyond the algebra: a correct translation at the start, and an interpretation in context at the end. The translation is where phrasing decides your sign. "At least" and "no less than" mean greater than or equal to; "at most", "no more than" and "maximum" mean less than or equal to; "more than" and "exceeds" are strictly greater; "fewer than" and "under" are strictly less. One word moves a filled dot to a hollow one and changes the answer by one whole unit. The interpretation is the closing sentence. If the stallholder's inequality gives n greater than or equal to thirty-five, the mathematics is finished but the question is not — she cannot sell part of a vetkoek, so n must be a whole number, and the answer to write is that she must sell at least thirty-five vetkoek. Notice the boundary is worth checking out loud: thirty-five at twelve rand is four hundred and twenty rand, less her hundred and twenty rand table, is exactly three hundred rand profit. That confirms thirty-five itself qualifies, which is exactly why the wording "at least" was chosen. Get into the habit of ending every context question with a sentence a non-mathematician could understand — that sentence is often a mark on its own.
