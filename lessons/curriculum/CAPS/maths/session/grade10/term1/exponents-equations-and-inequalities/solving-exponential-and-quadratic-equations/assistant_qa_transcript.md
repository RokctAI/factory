**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! This one came up three times: if a quadratic equals six instead of zero, why can't we just set one bracket equal to six and the other equal to one?

**Tutor:** Because that guesses at one of infinitely many possibilities and then treats the guess as proof. The zero-product law is a genuine theorem: if a product of two real numbers is zero, at least one of them must be zero, since no two non-zero numbers can multiply to nothing. There is no matching theorem for six. A product of six could be six times one, or two times three, or twelve times a half, or minus four times minus one and a half, or any pair from an endless list — so knowing the product is six tells you nothing about either factor on its own. Setting one bracket to six and the other to one picks one pair out of that endless list, and there is no reason the true values should be that pair. The reliable route is mechanical: expand the brackets, move the six across so the right side reads zero, tidy into standard form, factorise the new expression, and only then apply the law. On the lesson's example, x plus three times x minus two equals six becomes x squared plus x minus twelve equals zero, which factorises to x plus four times x minus three, giving x equals minus four or x equals three. Notice neither root is six or one — the guess would have been wrong on both counts.

**Thandi:** Next: when we solve six over x equals x minus five, why do we bother writing x is not equal to zero if zero was never going to be one of the answers anyway?

**Tutor:** Two reasons, one mathematical and one practical. Mathematically, the restriction is part of the truth of the equation. The expression six over x does not exist when x is zero, so the equation itself is only asking a question about values other than zero. Writing that down states the domain you are working in, and a marker awards it. Practically, the restriction is your safety net for a different kind of error. When you multiply both sides by x to clear the fraction, you are performing an operation that is only valid if x is not zero — and multiplying an equation by an expression can sometimes create a solution that satisfies the new equation but not the original. Those are called extraneous solutions, and the restriction is what lets you catch and discard them. So the procedure is: state the restriction the moment you see the fraction, clear the fraction, solve the quadratic, and then check each root against the restriction. Here six equals x squared minus five x becomes x squared minus five x minus six equals zero, factorising to x minus six times x plus one, so x equals six or x equals minus one. Neither is the forbidden zero, so both survive — and you can say so with confidence rather than hope.

**Thandi:** Someone asked about bases: what if the two sides genuinely cannot be written on the same base? Like two to the power x equals ten. Have we just failed?

**Tutor:** No — you have found the boundary of the Grade 10 method, and knowing where a method ends is part of understanding it. The same-base technique only applies when both sides can be expressed as powers of one common number, and in an examination at this level they always can, because the paper is set that way. Ten is not a power of two, so there is no whole-number or simple fractional exponent that works; the true solution sits between three and four, since two cubed is eight and two to the fourth is sixteen. Reaching it exactly needs logarithms, which arrive later in the curriculum. So in a test, if you cannot find a shared base, that is a strong signal you have misread the question or missed a rewrite — go back and hunt harder. Common hiding places: a root, which is a fractional exponent, so the square root of two is two to the power a half; a reciprocal, which is a negative exponent, so one over eight is two to the minus three; and a base that is itself a power, like four, eight, sixteen and thirty-two all being powers of two, or nine, twenty-seven and eighty-one all being powers of three. Nine times out of ten the shared base was there and simply had not been spotted yet.

**Thandi:** Last one: how do I know which roots to reject in a word problem? I once rejected a perfectly good answer and lost the mark.

**Tutor:** Reject only for a reason you can state in one sentence, drawn from the situation rather than from a feeling that the number looks odd. The genuine grounds are few. A length, width, height, distance, area, volume, price, mass or time cannot be negative, so a negative root is rejected there. A number of people, items, buses or days must be a whole number, so a fractional root is rejected in a counting problem. And sometimes the problem states its own limits — a plank three metres long cannot yield a piece of four metres. Outside those grounds, keep the root. Negative answers are perfectly legitimate for temperatures, bank balances, coordinates, gradients and heights below a reference level, so rejecting a negative simply because it is negative is exactly the error that cost you the mark. Then write the rejection explicitly: "x equals minus eight is rejected, since the width of a garden cannot be negative." That sentence does two jobs — it shows the marker you found both roots, and it shows you understood the context. In the garden problem the algebra gives minus eight or five; the rejection is stated, and the answer is that the garden is five metres by eight metres.
