# Part 1 — Expert

Every factorising method of the past three weeks now goes to work. An algebraic fraction is a fraction whose numerator and denominator are polynomials, and every operation on one — simplifying, multiplying, dividing, adding, subtracting — opens with the same instruction: factorise first, cancel second. Four worked expressions carry this session, and running beside them is the discipline examiners reward and learners forget — stating the restrictions on the variable.

## Subtopic: Factors, Not Terms — the Cancelling Law

State the law before touching an example, because the law itself is examined. You may cancel a FACTOR that the whole numerator and the whole denominator share. You may never cancel a TERM.

A factor is something the whole expression is multiplied by. A term is something added or subtracted. The moment a plus or a minus sign stands between parts of your numerator, nothing inside it may be cancelled with anything below, because those parts are terms, not factors.

Take the expression: x squared minus nine, all over x squared plus seven x plus twelve. Many learners see x squared upstairs, x squared downstairs, and strike both out. Test that with a number and watch it collapse. Let x equal one. The true value is one minus nine, over one plus seven plus twelve — minus eight over twenty, which is minus two fifths. The illegal cancellation leaves minus nine over nineteen. Two different numbers, so the cancellation destroyed the expression.

Now do it correctly. The numerator, x squared minus nine, is a difference of two squares: it factorises to x minus three, times x plus three. The denominator, x squared plus seven x plus twelve, is a trinomial: two numbers multiplying to twelve and adding to seven — three and four — so it factorises to x plus three, times x plus four.

Written as products, the fraction reads: x minus three, times x plus three, over x plus three, times x plus four. Now the bracket x plus three is a genuine factor of the whole top and the whole bottom, so it may be divided out. The simplified answer is x minus three, over x plus four — and at x equal to one it gives minus two fifths, exactly the original value. Factorising did not change the expression; it only revealed what was already shared.

Pause here — the questions for this section are with you now. They test the difference between a factor and a term, and they will hand you cancellations to convict or acquit.

## Subtopic: Multiplying, Dividing, and Denominators Hiding Cubes

Multiplication of algebraic fractions has one honest advantage: no common denominator is needed. Factorise every numerator and every denominator, cancel any factor appearing above and below anywhere in the product, then write what survives.

Division brings one extra move, and it must happen before any cancelling: invert the second fraction and multiply. Take x squared minus four, over x plus three, divided by x plus two, over x squared minus nine. First flip the divisor: x squared minus four, over x plus three, multiplied by x squared minus nine, over x plus two. Now factorise every part: x minus two, times x plus two, over x plus three, multiplied by x minus three, times x plus three, over x plus two. The bracket x plus two cancels top and bottom; so does x plus three. What remains is x minus two, times x minus three — and that is a complete answer; there is no obligation to expand it.

Denominators up to the sum and difference of two cubes are in the syllabus, so recognise them on sight. Simplify x cubed minus eight, over x squared minus four. The numerator is a difference of two cubes — x cubed minus two cubed — which factorises to x minus two, times the trinomial x squared plus two x plus four. The denominator is a difference of squares: x minus two, times x plus two. The factor x minus two divides out, leaving x squared plus two x plus four, over x plus two.

Then stop. The trinomial x squared plus two x plus four does not factorise over the rational numbers — no two whole numbers multiply to four and add to two. Learners who try to cancel the two x with the x below are back to cancelling terms. When a factor is irreducible, leave it standing.

Stop for this section's questions now — invert before you cancel, factorise before you invert, and recognise a difference of cubes the moment it appears.

## Subtopic: Adding and Subtracting — the Lowest Common Denominator

Addition and subtraction refuse to proceed until the denominators are identical, exactly as with numerical fractions. The route is fixed: factorise every denominator, build the lowest common denominator from all distinct factors, rewrite each fraction over it, then combine the numerators only.

Simplify three over x minus three, minus two over x squared minus nine. Factorise the second denominator: x squared minus nine is x minus three, times x plus three. Now the lowest common denominator is visible — it needs an x minus three and an x plus three, so the LCD is x minus three, times x plus three. Note what it is not: it is not the product of the two original denominators, which would carry a needless second x minus three.

The first fraction is short of an x plus three, so multiply its numerator and denominator by x plus three: three times x plus three is three x plus nine. The second fraction already sits over the LCD, so its numerator stays two. Combine: three x plus nine, minus two, over x minus three times x plus three — which is three x plus seven, over x minus three times x plus three. The numerator does not factorise, so the answer is final. Leave the denominator in factor form; examiners prefer it, and it displays the restrictions.

Now the subtraction trap, in its purest form. Simplify two x plus one, over x minus four, minus x minus three, over x minus four. The denominators already match, so the numerator becomes two x plus one, minus the whole quantity x minus three. Bracket it: two x plus one, minus, open bracket, x minus three, close bracket. Distribute the minus across both terms: two x plus one, minus x, plus three — giving x plus four. The answer is x plus four, over x minus four. Learners who omit the bracket write two x plus one minus x minus three and get x minus two — one sign error, every subsequent mark gone. Whenever you subtract a numerator with more than one term, bracket it before you distribute.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — build the lowest common denominator from factors, and bracket every numerator you subtract.

## Subtopic: Restrictions, and the Error Museum

A fraction with zero in the denominator is undefined — not zero, not infinity, undefined — so every algebraic fraction carries a silent condition: the denominator may not be zero. Writing that condition is the restriction, and in the examination it is a mark.

Read restrictions from the ORIGINAL denominators, before any cancelling. Return to the first expression: x squared minus nine, over x squared plus seven x plus twelve. The denominator factorises to x plus three, times x plus four, so x may not be minus three and may not be minus four. The simplified answer, x minus three over x plus four, appears to forbid only minus four — but minus three was forbidden the moment the original was written, and cancelling a factor cannot retrospectively make a value legal. Both restrictions stand. For the division example, restrict on every denominator that ever existed, and on the numerator of the divisor as well, because dividing by a fraction equal to zero is still dividing by zero: x may not be minus three, three, or minus two.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: cancelling terms across a plus sign — the commonest error in the topic, and always disprovable by substituting a number. Two: cancelling before factorising, the same crime wearing a hat. Three: the missing bracket in subtraction, leaving a minus three that never got its sign changed. Four: adding denominators — three over x plus two over x plus one is not five over two x plus one; denominators are never added.

The method, compressed: factorise every numerator and denominator; for multiplication cancel across, for division invert first; for addition and subtraction build the LCD from distinct factors, rewrite, then combine numerators with brackets; factorise the new numerator to check for a final cancellation; state restrictions from the original denominators.

The final questions of this part are with you now — expect a restriction to be worth its own mark, and read it from the original expression every time.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same fractions from a kitchen and a spaza shop counter — same expressions, same answers, but this time you will see why the rules are the way they are.

## Subtopic: You Can Only Cancel a Whole Crate

Picture a spaza shop stockroom. On the counter: six crates of cooldrink. Under the counter: nine crates of the same cooldrink. Six over nine — and because both piles are built out of nothing but crates, you can shrink the comparison honestly: divide both by three crates and you get two over three. Fine.

Now change one thing. On the counter there are six crates PLUS one loose bottle. Can you still divide out a crate? No — because the loose bottle is not part of any crate, and the moment you start chopping crates you have to explain what happens to it. That loose bottle is a plus sign. In algebra, the instant a plus or minus appears in your top line, you may not cancel anything out of it — the parts are separate items, not one packed block.

That is the whole cancelling law in one picture: cancel only what the WHOLE top and the WHOLE bottom are built out of. And factorising is exactly the repacking job — taking a messy pile with loose bottles and rewriting it as neat crates multiplied together.

Watch it happen. The top, x squared minus nine, repacks into x minus three, times x plus three. The bottom, x squared plus seven x plus twelve, repacks into x plus three, times x plus four — because three and four multiply to twelve and add to seven. Now both piles have an x plus three crate, so out it comes, and you are left with x minus three, over x plus four.

Test it like a shopkeeper checks a till. Put x equal to one in the original: minus eight over twenty, or minus two fifths. Put x equal to one in your answer: minus two over five. Same number. The till balances, and you can trust the work.

Quick check before we carry on — a few questions on crates and loose bottles are coming to you right now. For each one, just ask whether the whole top and the whole bottom are made of the same thing.

## Subtopic: Cut the Slices the Same Size Before You Add

Try to add a third of a loaf of bread to a quarter of a loaf. Nobody says "one plus one over three plus four". You cut both into the same size slice — twelfths — and then you can add: four twelfths plus three twelfths is seven twelfths.

Algebraic fractions are identical. The denominator is the slice size. You cannot add until both fractions are cut into the same size, and the lowest common denominator is simply the smallest slice size that both can be cut into cleanly.

Take three over x minus three, minus two over x squared minus nine. Repack the second bottom: x squared minus nine is x minus three, times x plus three. So one fraction is sliced by an x minus three, and the other by an x minus three AND an x plus three. The smallest slice size that suits both is x minus three, times x plus three — you take each different crate once.

The first fraction is missing an x plus three, so multiply its top and bottom by it — the fraction's value does not change, only how it is cut. Three times x plus three gives three x plus nine. Now both are the same size and you may add the tops: three x plus nine, take away two, is three x plus seven. The answer sits over x minus three times x plus three, and it is done.

One warning that costs more marks than anything else in the topic. When you subtract a top with two pieces in it, you are taking away the WHOLE parcel — both pieces. If a customer returns a packet holding one loaf and owing you three rand, you must reverse both items, not just the loaf. So take away x, and take away minus three — which puts a plus three back. That is why two x plus one, minus the parcel x minus three, becomes two x plus one minus x plus three — giving x plus four. Put a bracket around the parcel before you unpack it and the sign can never escape you.

Your questions for this part are up now. Cut the slices to the same size first, then add or subtract the tops — and bracket every parcel you take away.

## Subtopic: The Numbers the Fraction Will Not Allow

Last idea, and it is the one that quietly earns marks. Suppose you have R240 to share equally among the learners going on a tour. Among four learners, R60 each. Among six, R40 each. Among zero learners — the question stops making sense. There is nothing to share among, and no answer exists. Not R0. Not a huge number. Simply no answer.

The bottom of a fraction is exactly that "how many to share among". So the bottom may never be zero, and any value of x that would make it zero is banned from the start. Those bans are the restrictions, and writing them down is part of the answer. Find them by asking of each original bottom: what would make this zero? In our first expression the bottom was x plus three, times x plus four — so x is not minus three, and x is not minus four.

Here is the part learners lose. After cancelling, the x plus three disappeared from the answer — but minus three is still banned, because it was banned in the original question, and cancelling cannot un-ban it. Once a number is refused at the door, tidying up inside the shop does not let it in. So always hunt restrictions in the ORIGINAL bottoms, before you cancel anything.

Put the whole session together and it is four moves you already know. Repack everything into crates — factorise. Cancel only whole crates. To add or subtract, cut to the same slice size first and bracket any parcel you take away. And before you finish, walk back to the original bottoms and ban the values that would empty them.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: check the bottoms first, name the banned numbers, then simplify — you have every move you need.
