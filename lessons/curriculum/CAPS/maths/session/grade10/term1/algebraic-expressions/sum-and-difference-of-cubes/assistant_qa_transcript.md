**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! First one: how are we actually supposed to SPOT that eight is a cube in the middle of a test? Someone said they would just see x cubed plus eight and start hunting for a common factor.

**Tutor:** Spotting is a vocabulary skill, and the vocabulary is small enough to own completely: one, eight, twenty-seven, sixty-four, one hundred and twenty-five, two hundred and sixteen. Six numbers, drilled the way times tables were drilled, until eight announces itself as two cubed without you deciding to think about it. Then add the trigger that tells you to look in the first place: exactly two terms, joined by a plus or a minus, with the ordinary tools failing — nothing common to take out, and not a difference of squares, because either the sign is wrong or the terms are not squares. Two stubborn terms, one of them from that list of six or carrying an exponent that divides by three, and the cubes identity is almost certainly the intended route. Examiners draw their numbers from that same short list, because the arithmetic at this level has to stay clean. Know the list and you cannot be ambushed; miss it and a straightforward question looks impossible.

***

**Thandi:** Next: the class keeps mixing up which bracket takes which sign. Is SOAP genuinely reliable for both the plus version and the minus version, or does it only really work for one of them?

**Tutor:** It is reliable for both, and handling both is the entire reason the mnemonic exists. Take it letter by letter. Same: the short bracket copies the sign standing in the question. The sum of cubes, x cubed plus eight, gives x plus two; the difference of cubes, x cubed minus twenty-seven, gives x minus three. Opposite: the middle term of the long bracket takes the other sign — minus two x for the plus version, plus three x for the minus version. Always Positive: the final term is plus, in every case, in both versions, with no exception at this level or any later one. Three letters, three signs, and the two identities differ nowhere else, because the terms themselves are identical — square, multiply, square — in both. If doubt strikes mid-paper, you can settle it yourself by multiplying your two brackets back together in your head: correct signs make the four middle products cancel in pairs and leave exactly the two terms you started with, while wrong signs leave debris behind. You carry your own marker into the examination.

***

**Thandi:** This one had several hands up: why does the middle of the long bracket come out as two x and not four x? People keep landing on x squared minus four x plus four.

**Tutor:** Because the recipe says multiply in that position, and they are squaring. The long bracket is built in three moves, in a fixed order: square the first name, multiply the two names together, square the second name. For the names x and two, that gives x squared, then x times two which is two x, then two squared which is four. The middle position holds a product, never a square. There is a second reason x squared minus four x plus four feels right, and it is worth naming out loud: it is a familiar face from earlier work — it is exactly x minus two, all squared, from the perfect-square pattern. Familiarity is doing the damage, because the hand completes the shape it has met most often. The cure is to say the cubes recipe as three separate words — square, times, square — and to test the middle term against it before moving on. The multiplication check catches this error every time too: with the wrong middle term the brackets simply refuse to collapse back to two terms.

***

**Thandi:** Last one, and it cost someone a whole question: they went straight at two x cubed minus fifty-four with the identity, got stuck because two and fifty-four are not cubes, and want to know whether the question itself was broken.

**Tutor:** The question was not broken; it was doing its job. That arrangement is the examiners' favourite in this topic, and it tests discipline rather than the identity. Rule zero of all factorising comes before every pattern you know: take out the common factor first. Both terms here share a factor of two, so two x cubed minus fifty-four becomes two, times the quantity x cubed minus twenty-seven. Look again — twenty-seven is three cubed, the bracket is now a difference of cubes, and SOAP finishes it: two, times x minus three, times x squared plus three x plus nine. The trap is deliberately built, because the raw terms are chosen so that a learner who skips the common factor concludes the identity does not apply and abandons a question worth full marks. Notice too that the two stays in front of the answer permanently — it is part of the factorised form, not something to drop once the interesting work is done. Common factor first, then the pattern, then the full stop.
