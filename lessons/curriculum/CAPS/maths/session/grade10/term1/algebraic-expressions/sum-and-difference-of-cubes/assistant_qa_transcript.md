**Student:** How do I even spot that 8 is a cube? In the test I'd just see "x³ + 8" and try to take out a common factor or something.

**Thandi:** Spotting is a vocabulary skill, and the vocabulary is tiny: 1, 8, 27, 64, 125, 216 — the first six cubes. Drill those like times tables and "8" starts glowing "I'm 2 cubed!" at you. The trigger to even LOOK for cubes: exactly two terms, joined by plus or minus, and the familiar tools failing — no common factor, not a difference of squares. Two stubborn terms with an $x^3$ or a number from the magic six? Cubes identity, almost guaranteed. Exam setters pick from that list; know it and you can't be ambushed.

**Student:** I always mix up which bracket gets which sign. Is SOAP really reliable for both versions?

**Thandi:** Completely reliable — that's its whole job! Walk it: **S**ame — first bracket copies the original's sign. $x^3 + 8$? First bracket $(x + 2)$. $x^3 - 27$? First bracket $(x - 3)$. **O**pposite — the middle of the long bracket flips the original's sign: $-2x$ for the plus version, $+3x$ for the minus version. **A**lways **P**ositive — the last term: $+4$, $+9$, no exceptions ever. Three signs, three letters, both identities covered. And if doubt creeps in mid-test, expand quickly — wrong signs refuse to cancel, right signs collapse beautifully back to two terms.

**Student:** Why does the middle term come out as 2x and not 4x? I keep writing $x^2 - 4x + 4$.

**Thandi:** You're squaring when the pattern says multiply! The long bracket's recipe is: SQUARE the first ($x^2$), **MULTIPLY the two roots** ($x \times 2 = 2x$), square the last ($2^2 = 4$). The middle is a product of the roots, not a square of anything. I suspect $x^2 - 4x + 4$ feels right because it's a familiar face — it's $(x-2)^2$ from your squares work — but that's a different pattern from a different identity. Chant the cubes recipe as "square, times, square" and your hand will stop autocompleting the wrong bracket.

**Student:** For $2x^3 - 54$ I went straight to cubes and got stuck because 2 and 54 aren't cubes. Was the question broken?

**Thandi:** The question was hiding its cube behind a curtain — and the curtain is a common factor! Rule zero of ALL factorising: common factor first, every time, before any pattern-hunting. Take out the 2: $2(x^3 - 27)$ — and hello, 27 is 3 cubed, the identity applies perfectly: $2(x - 3)(x^2 + 3x + 9)$. Examiners love this two-step: the raw terms look like non-cubes precisely so that students who skip rule zero get stuck, exactly like you did. Nothing broken — just a test of the discipline. Common factor first, then the cube steps out from behind the curtain.
