# Part 1 — Expert

This session builds the most-used factorisation technique in all of algebra: factorising trinomials. The anchor problem is the kind with a twist — factorise fully six x squared minus seven x minus three, a trinomial whose leading coefficient is not one. What gets constructed here is the full procedure: hunting the two governing numbers, splitting the middle term, finishing by grouping, and verifying the result — enough to handle any trinomial the CAPS syllabus can offer.

## Subtopic: From Expanding to Factorising

You already know how to expand. Multiply the binomials x plus two and x plus four: first terms give x squared; outer and inner give four x plus two x — six x; last terms give eight. Result: x squared plus six x plus eight. A trinomial — three terms.

Factorising is that film played backwards: starting from x squared plus six x plus eight, we recover the brackets x plus two and x plus four. Why bother? Because factors reveal what a trinomial hides: the values that make it zero, the ways an expression simplifies, the structure underneath. Next year, factorised trinomials solve quadratic equations; in matric they anchor calculus. This technique never retires.

Watch the reverse carefully, because it teaches the method. When we expanded, the eight came from two TIMES four — the product of the two numbers. The six came from two PLUS four — their sum. So to reverse the process for x squared plus b x plus c, we hunt two numbers whose product is c and whose sum is b. For x squared plus six x plus eight: product eight, sum six — the numbers two and four. Brackets: x plus two, x plus four. Done.

One discipline before the main event, and it applies to every factorisation you ever do: check for a common factor FIRST. Three x squared plus eighteen x plus twenty-four hands you a common factor of three before any bracket hunting: three times the quantity x squared plus six x plus eight — and then the trinomial inside still factorises further. Skip the common factor and "factorise fully" marks are gone.

Pause here — the questions for this section are with you now: expansion read backwards, the product-and-sum rule when the leading coefficient is one, and the common factor that must be taken out before anything else.

## Subtopic: The Product-and-Sum Hunt — When a Is Not One

Now our real problem: six x squared minus seven x minus three. The leading coefficient is six, not one — and that changes the hunt in exactly one way.

Identify the coefficients against the standard shape a x squared plus b x plus c: a is six, b is negative seven, c is negative three. Signs attached, always — b is NEGATIVE seven, c is NEGATIVE three; drop a sign here and everything downstream breaks.

The rule for a bigger than one: we hunt two numbers whose product is a TIMES c — not c alone — and whose sum is still b. Compute the target product: six times negative three is negative eighteen. Target sum: negative seven.

Before listing factors, read the signs like a detective. The product is negative — so the two numbers have OPPOSITE signs. The sum is negative — so the larger of the two, in size, carries the minus. Two conclusions before touching a single factor pair; this sign-reading halves the search every time.

Now the factor pairs of eighteen: one and eighteen; two and nine; three and six. Test them against a sum of negative seven with opposite signs — meaning the DIFFERENCE of each pair must be seven. One and eighteen: difference seventeen. No. Two and nine: difference seven. Yes! Three and six: difference three. No.

So the pair is two and nine, opposite signs, the bigger one negative: NEGATIVE nine and POSITIVE two. Verify both conditions like a professional: negative nine times two is negative eighteen — product correct. Negative nine plus two is negative seven — sum correct. Both keys turn; the lock opens.

The questions on this section stand in front of you now: the coefficients named with their signs, the target product a times c set against the target sum b, and the sign-reading that predicts the pair before a single factor is listed.

## Subtopic: Splitting the Middle Term and Grouping

What are the two numbers FOR? They let us rewrite — split — the middle term. Negative seven x becomes negative nine x plus two x. The trinomial becomes: six x squared minus nine x plus two x minus three.

Pause on what just happened: nothing. Negative nine x plus two x IS negative seven x — we changed the outfit, not the value. But the new four-term outfit can be factorised by grouping.

Group the four terms into two pairs. First pair: six x squared minus nine x. The common factor: both share three and an x — three x. Factor it out: dividing six x squared by three x leaves two x; dividing negative nine x by three x leaves negative three. First pair becomes: three x times the quantity two x minus three.

Second pair: plus two x minus three. The only common factor is one — so write it as one times the quantity two x minus three. Writing the one explicitly is not pedantry; it keeps the structure visible for the next move.

Now look: three x times two x minus three, plus one times two x minus three. Both groups hold the IDENTICAL bracket — two x minus three. That match is the heartbeat of this method. If your brackets do not match exactly, the technique is telling you something went wrong: the split numbers, or a sign inside a group. Do not push forward with mismatched brackets — go back and fix.

Factor out the common bracket: the quantity two x minus three, times what remains from each group — three x plus one.

The factorisation: six x squared minus seven x minus three equals the quantity two x minus three, times the quantity three x plus one.

Does the split order matter? Try minus seven x as plus two x minus nine x instead: six x squared plus two x minus nine x minus three groups as two x times three x plus one, minus three times three x plus one — giving three x plus one times two x minus three. The same answer through a different door. Either order works; consistency of signs is all that matters.

Work through this section's questions now: the split written out in full, each pair reduced to its common factor, and the matching bracket recognised for what it is — the method's own checkpoint.

## Subtopic: Verification, Sign Patterns and the Full Method

Never leave a factorisation unverified — expansion is your proof. Expand two x minus three times three x plus one: two x times three x is six x squared; two x times one is two x; negative three times three x is negative nine x; negative three times one is negative three. Collect: six x squared plus two x minus nine x minus three — six x squared minus seven x minus three. The original returns exactly. Marks secured.

Now compress the sign-reading into the pattern you will use forever. Product a c positive and sum positive: both numbers positive. Product positive, sum negative: both negative. Product negative: opposite signs, and the sum's sign belongs to the bigger number. Three lines that turn the hunt from guesswork into deduction.

The complete method, seven steps. One: common factor first, always. Two: identify a, b and c with their signs. Three: compute the target product a times c and target sum b. Four: read the signs to predict the pair's pattern. Five: hunt the factor pair — testing sums or differences as the signs dictate. Six: split the middle term and factorise by grouping, expecting matching brackets. Seven: verify by expanding, and check every bracket for further factorisation.

The error museum, three exhibits. Exhibit one: hunting product c instead of product a times c when a exceeds one — the six x squared work collapses instantly. Exhibit two: sign amnesia — using seven instead of negative seven and finding a pair that sums the wrong way; the mismatched brackets in the grouping step will catch it if you let them. Exhibit three: stopping at three x times two x minus three plus one times two x minus three — half-factorised is not factorised; the common bracket must come out.

One boundary worth knowing: not every trinomial factorises over the integers. If no factor pair passes both tests — product AND sum — the trinomial is irreducible for now, and saying so IS the answer. Next year the quadratic formula handles those; this year, recognising them saves you from inventing numbers that do not exist.

Where this lesson sits in the bigger toolkit: trinomials are the three-term tool. Two-term expressions call the difference of squares or the cubes identities; four-term expressions call grouping in pairs — each has its own lesson. Together they answer the standing question of this topic: which tool does the term-count summon? Three terms — the answer is now complete.

The closing questions of this part are with you now: verification by expansion, the three sign patterns held apart, the seven-step method in its proper order, and the trinomial that honestly does not factorise.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same trinomial from behind a spaza shop counter — two numbers, one till, and every move said in ordinary words.

## Subtopic: Two Numbers That Multiply and Add

Picture buying airtime for two cousins — R2 for one, R4 for the other. Together that is R6, and the two amounts multiplied give 8. Six and eight, born from two and four. Factorising a trinomial is that little game played backwards.

Forwards in algebra: multiply the bracket x plus two by the bracket x plus four, letting every term in the first bracket meet every term in the second. x times x is x squared; x times four is four x; two times x is two x; two times four is eight. Add the middle bits — four x plus two x is six x — and you have x squared plus six x plus eight. Three terms: a trinomial.

Backwards: somebody hands you x squared plus six x plus eight and wants the brackets back. The eight came from two numbers multiplied; the six came from those same two numbers added. So the whole job is to find two numbers that multiply to eight and add to six. One and eight multiply to eight but add to nine — no. Two and four multiply to eight and add to six — yes. Brackets: x plus two, and x plus four.

One habit before any of that, and it costs nothing: check whether the same thing divides into all three terms. In three x squared plus eighteen x plus twenty-four, three divides into every term — pull it out in front, leaving three times the bracket x squared plus six x plus eight, which is the friendly one you just did. Skip that step and the word "fully" quietly eats your marks.

That is already a real win: find two numbers that multiply and add to order, and half the trinomials in the paper are yours. The questions for this section are with you now — the multiplying, the adding, and the shared factor that comes out first.

## Subtopic: When the Number in Front Is Not a One

Sometimes a number sits out front: six x squared minus seven x minus three. The game does not change; one detail does.

Say the three parts out loud with their signs attached. The number in front of x squared is six. The number in front of x is negative seven. The number standing on its own is negative three. That minus belongs to the seven the way a minus belongs to money you owe — let it slip off and everything after it is wrong.

Here is the single change: your multiply-target is no longer the last number by itself, but the front number times the last number. Six times negative three is negative eighteen. The add-target stays negative seven.

Now read the signs before hunting anything. The multiply-target is negative, and a negative product only happens when a plus meets a minus — so one number is positive and the other negative. The add-target is also negative — so the bigger of the two carries the minus. You know the shape of the pair before writing down a single factor.

The ways of making eighteen: one and eighteen; two and nine; three and six. Because one of each pair goes negative, adding them really means taking their difference. One and eighteen differ by seventeen — no. Two and nine differ by seven — that is the target. Three and six differ by three — no.

So the numbers are negative nine and positive two. Check both promises the way you check change at the till: negative nine times two is negative eighteen, and negative nine plus two is negative seven. Both agree, so the pair is right.

That sign-reading turns a long wrestle into a quick answer, and it is yours now. Your questions on this section are ready — front number times last number, and what the two signs tell you before you list a thing.

## Subtopic: Splitting the Bill and Checking Your Change

Here is what those two numbers were for. Take the middle term, negative seven x, and split it into the pieces they name: negative nine x plus two x. Nothing is gained or lost — R100 split into R70 and R30 is still R100. The expression becomes six x squared minus nine x plus two x minus three. Four terms, which sounds like going backwards and is the way forward.

Take them two at a time. The first two, six x squared minus nine x: three goes into six and into nine, and there is an x in both, so three x comes out in front, leaving the bracket two x minus three. The second two, plus two x minus three: nothing divides into those except one, so write the one in front on purpose, times the bracket two x minus three.

Now look — the identical bracket, twice: three x times two x minus three, plus one times two x minus three. That match is the sign that everything before it was right, like two people arriving at the same address from different streets. If your brackets come out different, do not shove them together; go back, re-test the two numbers, and check that every minus survived the trip.

Since the bracket two x minus three sits in both parts, take it out in front, and what is left behind — the three x and the one — becomes the second bracket. The answer: two x minus three, times three x plus one.

Prove it by multiplying back out. Two x times three x is six x squared; two x times one is two x; negative three times three x is negative nine x; negative three times one is negative three. Two x minus nine x is negative seven x, so you are standing on six x squared minus seven x minus three — the very expression you started with. That is knowing, not hoping.

And if no pair ever works, that is allowed: some trinomials simply refuse to break into whole-number brackets, and writing "it does not factorise" is the correct answer, not a surrender. The last questions of the lesson are with you now — the split, the two pairs, the shared bracket and the check.
