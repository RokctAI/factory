Welcome, student. Today we master the four-term factorisation technique: grouping in pairs. Our exam problem: factorise fully two a x plus two a y minus b x minus b y. Four terms, two letters too many, and — by the end of this lesson — one clean two-bracket answer. Grouping is also the engine hiding inside the trinomial method, so mastering it here strengthens everything else you factorise.

## Subtopic: Why Four Terms Demand a Different Tool

Count the terms in our expression: two a x, plus two a y, minus b x, minus b y. Four terms. That count matters, because each factorisation tool answers to a term count. Two terms call the difference of squares or the cubes identities. Three terms call the trinomial method. Four terms call grouping in pairs.

Why can none of the other tools touch this? Look for an overall common factor first — as always. The four terms share... nothing: two a x has no b, minus b y has no a, and no number divides all four beyond one. No identity fits either — this is no difference of squares, no cubes pattern. The old doors are locked.

But look closer, with a detective's eye. The FIRST two terms, two a x and two a y, share plenty: both hold two a. And the LAST two, minus b x and minus b y, share b. The expression has no factor common to ALL four terms — but it has factors common to PAIRS. That local structure is exactly what grouping exploits: divide the four terms into two pairs, factor each pair separately, and hope — no, engineer — that the two pairs then confess to sharing a bracket.

The plan in one sentence: pair up, factor each pair, factor out the common bracket. Three moves. Let us execute them slowly, because move two hides the sign trap that catches half of every Grade 10 class.

## Subtopic: Factoring Each Pair — and the Negative Factor Trap

Move one: pair the terms as they stand. First pair: two a x plus two a y. Second pair: minus b x minus b y. Keep each term's sign glued to it as you group — the minus signs BELONG to b x and b y and travel with them.

Move two, first pair: the common factor of two a x and two a y. Both contain two; both contain a. Take out two a: dividing two a x by two a leaves x; dividing two a y by two a leaves y. First pair becomes: two a, times the quantity x plus y.

Now the second pair — and here stands the trap. Minus b x minus b y. The common content is b — but WHICH b: positive or negative? Try positive b: dividing minus b x by b leaves minus x; dividing minus b y by b leaves minus y. Result: b times the quantity minus x minus y. Legal, but look at the bracket: minus x minus y. Our first pair produced x plus y. The brackets do NOT match — and without a common bracket, move three is impossible. Dead end.

Now try NEGATIVE b: dividing minus b x by minus b leaves POSITIVE x; dividing minus b y by minus b leaves POSITIVE y. Result: minus b, times the quantity x plus y. The signs inside flipped — because dividing by a negative flips signs — and the bracket now reads x plus y. A perfect match with the first pair.

There is the rule, worth a box around it: when a pair BEGINS with a minus sign, factor out a NEGATIVE common factor, so the bracket's signs flip into agreement. This single habit dissolves the most common grouping failure in the country.

The expression now reads: two a times the quantity x plus y, MINUS b times the quantity x plus y.

## Subtopic: The Common Bracket — Finishing the Factorisation

Move three. Stare at what we built: two a times x plus y, minus b times x plus y. Two big terms now — and they share a factor. Not a letter this time, but a whole BRACKET: the quantity x plus y appears in both.

A bracket is a factor like any other, and it obeys the same rule: factor it out. Think of x plus y as a single object — call it K if it helps. Then we have two a times K, minus b times K — and factoring K out of both terms leaves two a minus b. So: K times the quantity two a minus b — which is the quantity x plus y, times the quantity two a minus b.

The factorisation: two a x plus two a y minus b x minus b y equals the quantity x plus y, times the quantity two a minus b.

Verify — expansion, the thirty-second proof. Multiply out: x times two a is two a x; x times minus b is minus b x; y times two a is two a y; y times minus b is minus b y. Collect them: two a x plus two a y minus b x minus b y. All four original terms return, each with its correct sign. Proven.

Understand what the matching bracket really is: a checkpoint built into the method. If the brackets match, the pairing worked and the answer is one step away. If they do not match — like our x plus y versus minus x minus y moment — the method is flagging an error or a wrong turn: usually a positive factor where a negative belonged, sometimes a pairing that needs rearranging. Never force mismatched brackets together; read the flag, find the cause, fix it.

One more skill: sometimes the given ORDER of terms refuses to group. Consider two a x minus b y minus b x plus two a y. Pair as given: the first pair two a x minus b y shares nothing useful. The cure is rearrangement — addition lets terms travel: rewrite as two a x plus two a y minus b x minus b y, reuniting the a-family and the b-family. Then the grouping runs exactly as before. When pairs refuse to factor, shuffle the terms so that kin sit with kin — the a terms together, the b terms together — and try again.

## Subtopic: The Full Method, the Checks, and Where Grouping Hides

The complete method, five steps. One: overall common factor first — always the opening move of any factorisation. Two: count four terms and pair them, keeping signs attached; rearrange first if the given order separates the families. Three: factor each pair — and when a pair leads with a minus, take out a negative factor. Four: confirm the brackets MATCH, then factor the common bracket out, collecting the leftovers into the second bracket. Five: verify by expanding, and check both final brackets for any further factorisation — "fully" is a contract.

The error museum, four exhibits. Exhibit one: the positive-instead-of-negative factor — b instead of minus b — leaving mirror-image brackets that cannot merge; the flag waves, the learner forces them anyway, the marks vanish. Exhibit two: sign amnesia while grouping — writing the second pair as b x plus b y because the minuses got lost in transit; keep every sign glued to its term. Exhibit three: stopping at two a times x plus y minus b times x plus y — that is a rest stop, not the destination; the common bracket must still come out. Exhibit four: pairing blindly when rearrangement is needed, then declaring the expression unfactorisable; shuffle kin together before surrendering.

A worked bonus to bind the habits: factorise three p q plus six p minus q minus two. Common factor overall? No. Pair: three p q plus six p — common factor three p — gives three p times the quantity q plus two. Second pair: minus q minus two — leads with minus, so factor out MINUS ONE: minus one times the quantity q plus two. Matching brackets. Result: the quantity q plus two, times the quantity three p minus one. Even when the only factor available is minus one, the negative-factor rule earns its keep.

Where does grouping hide in your future? Everywhere. Inside the trinomial method: after splitting the middle term, the four resulting terms are finished by grouping — today's technique is that method's second half. In matric, the factor theorem reduces cubic polynomials to exactly this kind of finishing work. And in Grade 11 algebraic fractions, grouped factorisations cancel entire brackets at a stroke. Four terms, two pairs, one shared bracket — the pattern repeats for years. Train your eye to see the pairs, and train your hand to respect the minus.
