# Part 1 — Expert

This session builds the four-term factorisation technique: grouping in pairs. The anchor problem is to factorise fully two a x plus two a y minus b x minus b y — four terms, two letters too many, and one clean two-bracket answer waiting at the end of the method. Grouping is also the engine hiding inside the trinomial method, so what is constructed here strengthens every other factorisation in the topic.

## Subtopic: Why Four Terms Demand a Different Tool

Count the terms in our expression: two a x, plus two a y, minus b x, minus b y. Four terms. That count matters, because each factorisation tool answers to a term count. Two terms call the difference of squares or the cubes identities. Three terms call the trinomial method. Four terms call grouping in pairs.

Why can none of the other tools touch this? Look for an overall common factor first — as always. The four terms share... nothing: two a x has no b, minus b y has no a, and no number divides all four beyond one. No identity fits either — this is no difference of squares, no cubes pattern. The old doors are locked.

But look closer, with a detective's eye. The FIRST two terms, two a x and two a y, share plenty: both hold two a. And the LAST two, minus b x and minus b y, share b. The expression has no factor common to ALL four terms — but it has factors common to PAIRS. That local structure is exactly what grouping exploits: divide the four terms into two pairs, factor each pair separately, and hope — no, engineer — that the two pairs then confess to sharing a bracket.

The plan in one sentence: pair up, factor each pair, factor out the common bracket. Three moves. Let us execute them slowly, because move two hides the sign trap that catches half of every Grade 10 class.

Take the questions for this section now: the term count that selects the tool, the failed search for a factor common to all four terms, and the pairwise structure that grouping is built to exploit.

## Subtopic: Factoring Each Pair — and the Negative Factor Trap

Move one: pair the terms as they stand. First pair: two a x plus two a y. Second pair: minus b x minus b y. Keep each term's sign glued to it as you group — the minus signs BELONG to b x and b y and travel with them.

Move two, first pair: the common factor of two a x and two a y. Both contain two; both contain a. Take out two a: dividing two a x by two a leaves x; dividing two a y by two a leaves y. First pair becomes: two a, times the quantity x plus y.

Now the second pair — and here stands the trap. Minus b x minus b y. The common content is b — but WHICH b: positive or negative? Try positive b: dividing minus b x by b leaves minus x; dividing minus b y by b leaves minus y. Result: b times the quantity minus x minus y. Legal, but look at the bracket: minus x minus y. Our first pair produced x plus y. The brackets do NOT match — and without a common bracket, move three is impossible. Dead end.

Now try NEGATIVE b: dividing minus b x by minus b leaves POSITIVE x; dividing minus b y by minus b leaves POSITIVE y. Result: minus b, times the quantity x plus y. The signs inside flipped — because dividing by a negative flips signs — and the bracket now reads x plus y. A perfect match with the first pair.

There is the rule, worth a box around it: when a pair BEGINS with a minus sign, factor out a NEGATIVE common factor, so the bracket's signs flip into agreement. This single habit dissolves the most common grouping failure in the country.

The expression now reads: two a times the quantity x plus y, MINUS b times the quantity x plus y.

This section's questions are with you now: each pair reduced to its own common factor, the dead end that a positive factor creates, and the negative factor that a leading minus sign demands.

## Subtopic: The Common Bracket — Finishing the Factorisation

Move three. Stare at what we built: two a times x plus y, minus b times x plus y. Two big terms now — and they share a factor. Not a letter this time, but a whole BRACKET: the quantity x plus y appears in both.

A bracket is a factor like any other, and it obeys the same rule: factor it out. Think of x plus y as a single object — call it K if it helps. Then we have two a times K, minus b times K — and factoring K out of both terms leaves two a minus b. So: K times the quantity two a minus b — which is the quantity x plus y, times the quantity two a minus b.

The factorisation: two a x plus two a y minus b x minus b y equals the quantity x plus y, times the quantity two a minus b.

Verify — expansion, the short proof. Multiply out: x times two a is two a x; x times minus b is minus b x; y times two a is two a y; y times minus b is minus b y. Collect them: two a x plus two a y minus b x minus b y. All four original terms return, each with its correct sign. Proven.

Understand what the matching bracket really is: a checkpoint built into the method. If the brackets match, the pairing worked and the answer is one step away. If they do not match — like our x plus y versus minus x minus y moment — the method is flagging an error or a wrong turn: usually a positive factor where a negative belonged, sometimes a pairing that needs rearranging. Never force mismatched brackets together; read the flag, find the cause, fix it.

One more skill: sometimes the given ORDER of terms refuses to group. Consider two a x minus b y minus b x plus two a y. Pair as given: the first pair two a x minus b y shares nothing useful. The cure is rearrangement — addition lets terms travel: rewrite as two a x plus two a y minus b x minus b y, reuniting the a-family and the b-family. Then the grouping runs exactly as before. When pairs refuse to factor, shuffle the terms so that kin sit with kin — the a terms together, the b terms together — and try again.

Turn to the questions for this section: the bracket treated as a factor in its own right, the expansion that proves the answer, and the rearrangement that rescues an uncooperative order.

## Subtopic: The Full Method, the Checks, and Where Grouping Hides

The complete method, five steps. One: overall common factor first — always the opening move of any factorisation. Two: count four terms and pair them, keeping signs attached; rearrange first if the given order separates the families. Three: factor each pair — and when a pair leads with a minus, take out a negative factor. Four: confirm the brackets MATCH, then factor the common bracket out, collecting the leftovers into the second bracket. Five: verify by expanding, and check both final brackets for any further factorisation — "fully" is a contract.

The error museum, four exhibits. Exhibit one: the positive-instead-of-negative factor — b instead of minus b — leaving mirror-image brackets that cannot merge; the flag waves, the learner forces them anyway, the marks vanish. Exhibit two: sign amnesia while grouping — writing the second pair as b x plus b y because the minuses got lost in transit; keep every sign glued to its term. Exhibit three: stopping at two a times x plus y minus b times x plus y — that is a rest stop, not the destination; the common bracket must still come out. Exhibit four: pairing blindly when rearrangement is needed, then declaring the expression unfactorisable; shuffle kin together before surrendering.

A worked bonus to bind the habits: factorise three p q plus six p minus q minus two. Common factor overall? No. Pair: three p q plus six p — common factor three p — gives three p times the quantity q plus two. Second pair: minus q minus two — leads with minus, so factor out MINUS ONE: minus one times the quantity q plus two. Matching brackets. Result: the quantity q plus two, times the quantity three p minus one. Even when the only factor available is minus one, the negative-factor rule earns its keep.

Where does grouping hide in your future? Everywhere. Inside the trinomial method: after splitting the middle term, the four resulting terms are finished by grouping — today's technique is that method's second half. In matric, the factor theorem reduces cubic polynomials to exactly this kind of finishing work. And in Grade 11 algebraic fractions, grouped factorisations cancel entire brackets at a stroke. Four terms, two pairs, one shared bracket — the pattern repeats for years. Train your eye to see the pairs, and train your pen to respect the minus.

The closing questions of this part are in front of you now: the five-step method held in order, the four exhibits of the error museum, the minus-one bonus example, and the later topics that grouping quietly supports.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same four terms from a shop counter and two shopping bags — the whole method in ordinary words, with the sign trap defused.

## Subtopic: Sorting the Shopping Into Two Bags

Four terms: two a x, plus two a y, minus b x, minus b y. Read them as four items lying on a counter.

Before anything clever, ask the question that opens every factorising problem: is there one thing that divides into all four? Look properly. The first two carry an a and no b. The last two carry a b and no a. And no number divides all four except one. Nothing fits everybody, so the usual doors are shut.

Now look at the items in twos. The first two, two a x and two a y, both carry a two and an a. The last two, minus b x and minus b y, both carry a b. That is packing groceries: no single bag holds everything, but the cold things go together and the dry things go together. Grouping in pairs does exactly that — sort into two bags, take out what each bag shares, and then see what the two bags have in common.

Open the first bag: two a x plus two a y. What is inside both? A two and an a. So pull out two a. Two a x divided by two a leaves x; two a y divided by two a leaves y. Bag one becomes two a, times the bracket x plus y.

Notice what ended up inside the bracket: x and y — the parts the two terms did NOT share. A bracket always holds the leftovers.

And it was the count of terms that sent us here in the first place. Two terms means the squares or cubes identities; three terms means the product-and-sum hunt; four terms means grouping in pairs. Count first, choose second, and you will never open a question with the wrong tool in your hand.

Your questions for this section are with you now — counting the terms, testing for something common to all four, and sorting four terms into two sensible pairs.

## Subtopic: The Minus That Must Come Out

Second bag: minus b x minus b y. This is where most of the country loses the mark, so go slowly.

Both terms carry a b. Pull out a plain positive b and watch. Minus b x divided by b leaves minus x. Minus b y divided by b leaves minus y. So you get b, times the bracket minus x minus y. Nothing illegal has happened — but compare it with bag one, which gave x plus y. Minus x minus y and x plus y are not the same bracket, and the next step needs them to be twins. Dead end.

Now pull out negative b instead. Minus b x divided by negative b leaves positive x — a minus divided by a minus gives a plus, the same way cancelling a debt against a debt leaves you up. Minus b y divided by negative b leaves positive y. Bag two becomes negative b, times the bracket x plus y. The signs inside flipped, and the two brackets are twins at last.

There is the rule, and it deserves saying out loud until your hand believes it: when a pair starts with a minus, take out a negative. The signs inside flip, and the brackets match.

One warning, because there is a tempting shortcut here that is simply cheating. You may not rub out the minuses inside the bracket to make things match. Turning b times minus x minus y into b times x plus y changes the value of the whole expression, and multiplying out would expose it at once. A bracket may only change if the thing outside it changes to pay for it. Want the signs inside flipped? Then make the factor outside negative. Same result, honestly earned.

The expression now reads: two a times the bracket x plus y, minus b times the bracket x plus y.

That single habit clears up the most common grouping failure there is — well done. Your questions on this section are ready: factoring each pair, and what has to come out when a pair leads with a minus.

## Subtopic: The Same Bracket in Both Hands

Look at what you are holding: two a times the bracket x plus y, minus b times the same bracket x plus y. Two big pieces, and both of them contain x plus y.

Here is the idea that unlocks the last step: a bracket is a factor like any other. If that feels strange, give it a nickname. Call the bracket x plus y by the single letter K. Then you are holding two a K minus b K — and anybody can factorise that: K sits in both, so it comes out in front, leaving K times the bracket two a minus b. Now put the real name back: the bracket x plus y, times the bracket two a minus b. That is the answer.

Check it by multiplying out, which takes seconds. x times two a is two a x. x times minus b is minus b x. y times two a is two a y. y times minus b is minus b y. Gather them: two a x plus two a y minus b x minus b y — every original term back, every sign in place.

Two more things worth having. First, the matching bracket is a free error-detector. Twins mean the sorting and the signs were right; different brackets mean something upstream is wrong, and the honest move is to go back, never to force them together.

Second, sometimes the four terms are just standing in the wrong queue. Take two a x minus b y minus b x plus two a y: the first two share nothing useful. Addition lets terms move around, so reshuffle until family sits with family — two a x plus two a y minus b x minus b y — and the grouping runs exactly as before. A grouping question that looks broken is usually a seating problem, not a maths problem.

And this keeps coming back. Split the middle term of a trinomial and you are left with four terms, finished by grouping. In matric, cubic factorisations end the same way. Four terms, two bags, one shared bracket.

The final questions of the lesson are with you now: pulling out the shared bracket, checking by multiplying back, and reshuffling the terms when the given order refuses to pair.
