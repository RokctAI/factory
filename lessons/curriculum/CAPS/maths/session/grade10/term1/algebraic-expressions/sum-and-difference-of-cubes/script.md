Welcome, student. Today we master a factorisation identity that looks intimidating and becomes, with one mnemonic, completely mechanical: the sum and difference of two cubes. Our exam problem: factorise fully x cubed plus eight. Two innocent terms — and a technique that CAPS examiners test every single year, precisely because so few learners own it properly.

## Subtopic: Recognising a Cube When You See One

The technique begins with recognition, because a cubes question never announces itself. X cubed plus eight does not say "I am a sum of cubes" — you must see it.

A perfect cube is a number or expression that is something raised to the power three. Build the recognition list you will carry into every exam: one is one cubed. Eight is two cubed. Twenty-seven is three cubed. Sixty-four is four cubed. One hundred and twenty-five is five cubed. Two hundred and sixteen is six cubed. Learn these six cold — they are the entire vocabulary of Grade 10 cubes questions.

Variables cube too: x cubed is obviously a cube; but so is y to the six — it is y squared, cubed — and eight a cubed b cubed, which is two a b, cubed. The test for any expression: can I write it as something to the power three? If yes, name that something — it becomes your building block.

So x cubed plus eight passes the test twice: x cubed is x, cubed; eight is two, cubed. A sum of two cubes: a cubed plus b cubed, with a equal to x and b equal to two. Identifying a and b EXPLICITLY, in writing, is the professional's first move — every later step just reads off those two letters.

Why does this need its own identity at all? Try the tools you already have. A common factor? X cubed and eight share nothing. Difference of squares? This is a SUM, and neither term is a square anyway. The trinomial method? Only two terms. Every familiar door is locked — the cubes identity is the key cut precisely for this lock.

## Subtopic: The Identity and the SOAP Mnemonic

Here is the identity for the sum of cubes: a cubed plus b cubed equals the quantity a plus b, times the quantity a squared minus a b plus b squared.

And its twin for the difference: a cubed minus b cubed equals the quantity a minus b, times the quantity a squared plus a b plus b squared.

Look at the structure before memorising anything. The first bracket is short — just a and b with a sign. The second bracket has three terms built only from a and b: a squared, then a times b, then b squared. Square the first, multiply the pair, square the last. The TERMS are identical in both identities; only the SIGNS dance.

And the signs obey one mnemonic: SOAP. Same — Opposite — Always Positive. The first bracket's sign is the SAME as the original expression. The second bracket's middle sign is the OPPOSITE of the original. The second bracket's last sign is ALWAYS POSITIVE.

Run SOAP on the sum: a cubed PLUS b cubed. Same: a plus b. Opposite: minus a b. Always positive: plus b squared. Exactly the identity. Run it on the difference: a cubed MINUS b cubed. Same: a minus b. Opposite: PLUS a b. Always positive: plus b squared. Both identities, one mnemonic, zero memory strain.

Where do these identities come from? Do not take them on faith — multiply one out and own it. Expand a plus b times a squared minus a b plus b squared. Distribute the a: a cubed minus a squared b plus a b squared. Distribute the b: plus a squared b minus a b squared plus b cubed. Now watch the middle collapse: minus a squared b cancels plus a squared b; plus a b squared cancels minus a b squared. Survivors: a cubed plus b cubed. The identity is not magic — it is engineered so the middle terms annihilate each other.

## Subtopic: Factorising x Cubed Plus Eight

Now the exam problem, executed with the discipline of a checklist.

Step one: recognise and name. X cubed plus eight is a cubed plus b cubed with a equal to x, b equal to two. Written down, visibly — this line earns a mark on many memos.

Step two: first bracket by SOAP. Same sign as the original plus: x plus two.

Step three: second bracket, terms by the pattern, signs by SOAP. Square the first: x squared. Multiply the pair, opposite sign: minus two x. Square the last, always positive: plus four — two squared, not two times two carelessly done but exactly four.

Assembled: x cubed plus eight equals the quantity x plus two, times the quantity x squared minus two x plus four.

Step four: verify by expanding — the same collapse we engineered in general must happen in numbers. X times x squared is x cubed; x times minus two x is minus two x squared; x times four is four x. Two times x squared is plus two x squared; two times minus two x is minus four x; two times four is plus eight. Collect: minus two x squared plus two x squared — gone. Four x minus four x — gone. Left: x cubed plus eight. Verified, provably.

Step five — and this is where marks quietly die — ask whether the second bracket factorises further. Learners see x squared minus two x plus four, recognise a trinomial, and reflexively hunt two numbers: product four, sum minus two. Try the pairs: minus one and minus four sum to minus five; minus two and minus two sum to minus four. No pair works — and no pair EVER works on the quadratic factor of a cubes identity. It does not factorise over the real numbers. "Fully factorised" here means knowing when to stop: the answer stands as x plus two, times x squared minus two x plus four.

One more common wreck to avoid: the middle term of the second bracket is a times b — two x — not two a b, and not b squared. Learners who write minus four x have squared the two instead of multiplying two by x. The pattern is square, MULTIPLY, square — the middle is a product, not a square.

## Subtopic: The Difference Twin and the Full Method

The difference of cubes runs on the same rails. Factorise x cubed minus twenty-seven. Recognise: twenty-seven is three cubed — a is x, b is three. SOAP: same sign — x minus three. Opposite — plus three x. Always positive — plus nine. Result: the quantity x minus three, times the quantity x squared plus three x plus nine.

Now a fuller example of the exam's favourite combination: factorise fully two x cubed minus fifty-four. No panic — run the standing discipline: common factor FIRST. Both terms share two: two times the quantity x cubed minus twenty-seven. And the bracket is our difference of cubes: two times x minus three, times x squared plus three x plus nine. Two techniques chained — common factor, then cubes — and "fully" satisfied. Cubes questions in exams very often hide behind a common factor; take it out and the cube reveals itself.

Contrast with the difference of squares, because confusing the two costs dearly. X squared minus nine: SQUARES, two brackets, x minus three times x plus three — both linear, done. X cubed minus twenty-seven: CUBES, two brackets but one linear and one quadratic, and the quadratic stays. Squares split symmetrically; cubes split into a short bracket and a long bracket. Check the exponent before choosing the identity: squared calls squares; cubed calls cubes.

The complete method, six steps. One: common factor first. Two: confirm both terms are perfect cubes and name a and b explicitly. Three: write the first bracket — cube roots with the SAME sign. Four: build the second bracket — square, multiply, square — with signs Opposite then Always Positive. Five: verify by expansion, watching the middle terms cancel. Six: declare the quadratic factor final — it never factorises further at this level.

Where this identity travels: Grade 11 uses it inside algebraic fractions, where cancelling a cubes factorisation simplifies monsters. Matric's factor theorem hands you one factor of a cubic polynomial and expects you to finish the factorisation — the quadratic factor pattern you practised today is exactly what emerges. Six perfect cubes memorised, one mnemonic, one collapsing expansion: that is the entire technique. Own it tonight; it earns for three years.
