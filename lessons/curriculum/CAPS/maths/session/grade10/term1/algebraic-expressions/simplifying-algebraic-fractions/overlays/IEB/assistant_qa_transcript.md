**Thandi:** Let's take the class questions! First one: someone says they understand "factors not terms" in theory, but under exam pressure they still cannot tell at a glance whether a cancellation is legal. Is there a fast test?

**Tutor:** There are two, and together they take ten seconds. The structural test: before cancelling anything, ask whether the ENTIRE top and the ENTIRE bottom are written as products — brackets multiplied together, nothing added or subtracted at the top level. If either side still contains a loose plus or minus between major pieces, you have terms, and cancelling is forbidden until you factorise. That is why the working order is fixed: factorise first, cancel second — the factorised form is the only form in which legality is even visible. And then the numerical test, for when doubt lingers: substitute one small value of x into the original and into your simplified version. Equal values mean the move preserved the expression; different values convict it on the spot. At x equal to one, our opening fraction gives minus one both before and after cancelling x plus five — and gives nonsense if you strike the x squared terms instead. Ten seconds, certainty either way, and both tests travel with you into every exam.

***

**Thandi:** Next: the LCD. A learner built the common denominator by multiplying the two original denominators together, got an enormous expression, and wants to know if that is actually wrong or just ugly.

**Tutor:** It is legal, and it is a tax on everything that follows — so treat it as wrong in practice. The product of the denominators is always A common denominator; it is rarely the LOWEST one. Take four over x minus two, minus three over x squared minus four. Multiplying denominators gives x minus two, times x squared minus four — which secretly contains x minus two TWICE, because x squared minus four is x minus two times x plus two. Every numerator you now build is one degree bigger than necessary, the combined top may refuse to factorise cleanly, and the final simplification demands an extra cancellation you could have avoided. The professional route: factorise every denominator first, then take each DISTINCT factor once — here, one x minus two and one x plus two. The LCD is the shopping list of different factors, not the pile of all of them. Smaller numerators, cleaner combining, and the restrictions sit visibly in the factored bottom. Same destination, half the walking.

***

**Thandi:** The next one caused an actual argument at the break board: three x plus two, minus x minus four, over the same denominator. Half the class got two x minus two, half got two x plus six. Who won?

**Tutor:** The two x plus six half won, and the losing half all made the identical error, which is why this trap has its own name in every marking room. When you subtract a numerator, you subtract the WHOLE numerator — every term of it. The safe mechanism is a bracket: write three x plus two, minus, bracket, x minus four, close bracket, and only then distribute the minus across both terms inside. Minus x, and minus negative four — which is PLUS four. So: three x plus two minus x plus four, giving two x plus six, which factorises to two times x plus three. The losing half skipped the bracket, so their minus reached only the x, and the four kept its old sign: two x minus two, one sign wrong, every subsequent mark gone. Make the bracket a reflex, not a decision: any subtracted numerator with more than one term gets bracketed before anything is distributed. That reflex costs one second per question and repays it in full marks for the rest of your mathematical life.

***

**Thandi:** Last question, and it is the sneaky one: if a factor cancels out of the fraction, why on earth does its restriction survive? Cancelled means gone, surely?

**Tutor:** Cancelled means invisible, not innocent. Walk the logic through slowly, because it settles the matter forever. The restriction is issued by the ORIGINAL expression: at x equal to minus five, the original bottom, x plus three times x plus five, equals zero, so the original fraction is undefined there — no value exists, full stop. Simplifying is a promise to preserve the expression's values wherever they exist; it is not a machine for creating new ones. The simplified form, x minus five over x plus three, happily accepts minus five and outputs a number — but that number answers a question the original never allowed to be asked. The two expressions agree everywhere except at the banned point, and the ban is part of the answer precisely because it marks where the agreement breaks. So the full, mark-earning answer reads: x minus five over x plus three, with x not equal to minus three AND x not equal to minus five. The habit to build: read the restrictions off the original denominators BEFORE you cancel anything, write them beside your first line of working, and carry them untouched to the final line. Restrictions are born early and never die.
