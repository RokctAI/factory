**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! First one, and half the group had it: why is cancelling the x squared on the top with the x squared on the bottom actually wrong? They look identical.

**Tutor:** They are identical, and that is precisely the trap — being identical is not enough. Cancelling means dividing the whole numerator and the whole denominator by the same quantity, and division distributes over multiplication, never over addition. So x squared may be divided out only if the ENTIRE top is x squared multiplied by something, and the ENTIRE bottom is x squared multiplied by something. In x squared minus nine, the x squared is added to a minus nine; it is one term of a sum, not a factor of the whole. Settle the argument with arithmetic. Put x equal to one. The genuine value is one minus nine over one plus seven plus twelve — minus eight over twenty, which is minus two fifths. Now cancel the x squared terms illegally and you get minus nine over nineteen. Those are different numbers, so the cancellation changed the expression, and any move that changes the expression is not simplification. The cure is mechanical: factorise both lines completely, so that everything you see is a product of brackets. Once the top reads x minus three, times x plus three, and the bottom reads x plus three, times x plus four, the only thing you can cancel is a whole bracket — and the shared bracket is x plus three.

***

**Thandi:** Next: after we cancel a bracket, it is gone from the answer. So why do we still have to ban the value that made that bracket zero?

**Tutor:** Because the restriction belongs to the question, not to your final line. Think about what the original expression actually instructs you to do when x equals minus three: it instructs you to divide by x plus three times x plus four, and at minus three that is zero times one — zero. Division by zero has no answer at all, so the original expression simply does not exist at minus three. Your simplified version, x minus three over x plus four, does exist there — it would give minus six over one, which is minus six. That means the two expressions are not quite the same object: they agree everywhere except at the one value the original refuses. Mathematicians handle that honestly by carrying the restriction along, which is why you write x is not equal to minus three and x is not equal to minus four. A useful way to remember it: once a value is refused at the door, tidying up inside the shop does not let it in. And practically, hunt restrictions the moment you finish factorising the denominators, before you cancel anything — at that point every forbidden value is visible in front of you, and you cannot lose one.

***

**Thandi:** Someone asked about division: why do we flip the second fraction, and can we cancel before we flip if we spot something?

**Tutor:** Flip first, always, and here is why the flip is legal. Dividing by a quantity and multiplying by its reciprocal are the same operation — dividing by two is multiplying by a half, and dividing by three quarters is multiplying by four thirds. Fractions built from polynomials obey the same arithmetic, so dividing by x plus two over x squared minus nine is multiplying by x squared minus nine over x plus two. Now to your real question: you may not cancel across a division sign, because until you flip, the parts are not in the positions the cancelling law needs — what looks like a top is about to become a bottom. Cancel too early and you will cancel a bracket with itself in the wrong direction and lose the reciprocal entirely. So the order is fixed: flip, then factorise everything, then cancel anywhere in the resulting product. On our example that gives x minus two times x plus two, over x plus three, multiplied by x minus three times x plus three, over x plus two. The x plus two and the x plus three both go, leaving x minus two times x minus three. One more thing that is often forgotten: in a division question the numerator of the divisor also generates a restriction, because dividing by a fraction worth zero is still dividing by zero — so x may not be minus two either.

***

**Thandi:** Last one: how do I know when to stop? Sometimes my answer still looks like it could be simplified further, and I waste time trying.

**Tutor:** Use a definite stopping test rather than a feeling. You are finished when both the numerator and the denominator are fully factorised and they share no common bracket. So the routine is: factorise the top completely, factorise the bottom completely, then scan bracket by bracket for a match. If nothing matches, you are done, no matter how long the expression looks. Two specific reassurances. First, a trinomial that will not factorise is a finished factor — x squared plus two x plus four is irreducible over the rationals, because no two whole numbers multiply to four and add to two, so an answer of x squared plus two x plus four over x plus two is complete and correct. Second, after adding or subtracting, do factorise the new numerator once, because sometimes it does share a bracket with the denominator and a final cancellation is waiting; but if it does not factorise, leave it as it stands. And resist expanding your brackets at the end — an answer written as x minus two times x minus three is fully acceptable and is easier for a marker to award than the expanded version. Neat factor form is the finish line, not a stepping stone.
