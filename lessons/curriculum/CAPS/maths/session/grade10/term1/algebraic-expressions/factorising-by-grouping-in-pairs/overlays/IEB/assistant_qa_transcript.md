**Thandi:** Class questions before we close! First up: how do you know grouping is even the right method, before you waste time on it? Someone says they tried grouping on a trinomial last week and got nowhere.

**Tutor:** The term count is the dispatcher, and it never lies. Count before you choose. Two terms send you to the identities — difference of squares, or the cubes pair. Three terms send you to the product-and-sum hunt. Four terms send you to grouping. A trinomial handed to grouping directly will always refuse, because grouping needs two pairs, and three terms cannot form them — which is exactly what your classmate discovered the slow way. There is one beautiful crossover, though: the trinomial method itself ends in grouping. After you split the middle term, three terms become four, and grouping finishes the job. So the honest description is this: grouping is the four-term tool, and the second half of the three-term tool. Count the terms, let the count choose, and check for an overall common factor before anything else — that discipline opens every factorisation, whatever the count says afterwards.

***

**Thandi:** Next: the negative factor rule. A lot of the class can recite it — leading minus, take out a negative — but somebody asks, what happens if you just ignore it and push on with the positive factor?

**Tutor:** Then the method itself stops you, which is the elegant part. Take our second pair, minus n p minus n q, and factor out positive n: you get n times the bracket minus p minus q. Nothing illegal has occurred — that line is true. But now stand it next to the first pair's bracket, p plus q. The two brackets are mirror images, not twins, and move three requires twins: you cannot factor out a shared bracket that is not shared. So the method halts, and the halt IS the message. Two repairs exist. The principled one: go back and take out negative n, which flips the signs inside to p plus q, because dividing by a negative flips signs. The desperate one — rubbing out the minus signs to force a match — is fraud against the expression: it changes the value, and expanding would expose it instantly. A bracket's contents may only change when the factor outside changes to pay for it. So no, ignoring the rule does not produce a wrong answer; it produces no answer, plus a flag telling you which repair to make.

***

**Thandi:** This one comes from three different people, so it matters: their pairs factored beautifully, brackets matched, and then they wrote the two factored pairs next to each other and moved on. Why did that lose the final mark?

**Tutor:** Because three m times p plus q, minus n times p plus q, is not a factorised expression — it is still a SUM of two terms, just better-dressed ones. Factorised means written as a product, and a product has factors multiplied together, not pieces added and subtracted. The last move is the whole destination: the bracket p plus q sits in both terms, so it comes out the front like any other common factor, leaving three m minus n gathered in the second bracket. If treating a bracket as a factor feels strange, rename it — call p plus q the single letter B, and suddenly three m B minus n B factorises the way any Grade 8 expression would: B times three m minus n. The halfway line is a rest stop with a lovely view of the answer; the marks are only paid at the destination. Train the habit of asking one closing question of every factorisation: is my final line a pure product? If there is still a plus or minus between major pieces, you are not done.

***

**Thandi:** Last one: somebody met a four-term expression where the first pair shared absolutely nothing, decided it was unfactorisable, and moved on. Was that the right call?

**Tutor:** It was an early surrender, and the exam counts on exactly that. When the given order refuses to pair, the expression is usually fine — the seating is wrong. Take three m p minus n q minus n p plus three m q. Paired as written, the first two terms share nothing useful, and a learner who stops there walks away from full marks. But addition lets terms move: reshuffle so that family sits with family — three m p plus three m q, then minus n p minus n q — and the grouping runs perfectly, landing on p plus q times three m minus n. So make the reshuffle a standard checkpoint: before declaring any four-term expression unfactorisable, regroup it at least one other way, gathering the terms that share letters. And keep the humble cousin of this trick in mind too: sometimes a pair's only shared content is one, and the pair leads with a minus — then minus one comes out, as in minus d minus three becoming minus one times d plus three. Between the reshuffle and the minus-one move, very few four-term expressions genuinely refuse to factorise at this level.
