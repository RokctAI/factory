**Thandi:** Questions from the class! First one: the factor lists. When the target product is something like sixty, people say they freeze — how do you list factor pairs quickly without missing one?

**Tutor:** Work the ladder from the bottom, and the ladder never lets you skip a rung. Start at one and climb: does one divide sixty? Yes — one and sixty. Does two? Yes — two and thirty. Three? Yes — three and twenty. Four? Yes — four and fifteen. Five? Yes — five and twelve. Six? Yes — six and ten. Seven? No. And once your climbing number passes the square root of the target — for sixty, that is between seven and eight — the ladder is complete, because any new pair would just repeat an old one in reverse. Six pairs, found in under a minute, none missed. Then let the sign-reading shrink the work further: if the signs told you the pair differs by four, you are scanning differences down the ladder — fifty-nine, twenty-eight, seventeen, eleven, seven, four — and the last rung hands you the answer. The freeze usually comes from hunting randomly; the ladder replaces randomness with a route.

**Thandi:** Next: a debate broke out about the split. One learner wrote minus ten x plus six x, her friend wrote plus six x minus ten x, and they were convinced one of them had to be wrong. Who was?

**Tutor:** Neither — and the fact that both roads exist is worth understanding rather than just trusting. The split rewrites minus four x as a sum of the two governing numbers times x, and addition does not care about order: minus ten x plus six x and plus six x minus ten x are the same quantity in different seating. What changes is the scenery on the way. The first order groups as two x times two x minus five, plus three times two x minus five, and the shared bracket is two x minus five. The second order groups as two x times two x plus three, minus five times two x plus three, and the shared bracket is two x plus three. Different intermediate brackets — but the final products, two x minus five times two x plus three, are identical, because multiplication does not care about order either. The one thing BOTH roads demand is that every sign travels attached to its term through the grouping. Sign discipline, not split order, is where answers live or die.

***

**Thandi:** This one is the top question by a distance: why hunt with a times c at all? A few people factorised with just c, like last week's easy trinomials, and everything fell apart.

**Tutor:** Because last week's shortcut was never the real rule — it was the real rule wearing a disguise. The genuine hunt is ALWAYS product a times c, sum b. When a equals one, a times c equals c, so the shortcut and the truth coincide, and nobody notices the difference. The moment a becomes four, the two part ways. Here is the reason the true rule works: when you expand any pair of brackets p x plus q times r x plus s, the two middle contributions are p s x and q r x, and the product of those two coefficients, p s times q r, always equals p r times q s — which is exactly a times c. The middle term's two pieces must multiply to a times c; that is a structural fact of expansion, not a memory trick. So the split numbers live inside a times c, and hunting inside c alone searches the wrong territory entirely. If you take one sentence from today, take this: name a, b and c with their signs, multiply a by c before anything else, and the rest of the method is bookkeeping.

***

**Thandi:** Last one: someone spent five minutes hunting for a pair with product twelve and sum five for the trinomial x squared plus five x plus twelve, found nothing, and assumed they had made an error. Had they?

**Tutor:** No — they had found the correct answer and not recognised its face. Run the hunt honestly: pairs of twelve are one and twelve, two and six, three and four; their sums are thirteen, eight and seven. Five appears nowhere, and since the product is positive and the sum positive, only positive pairs qualify — the list is complete. When the list is exhausted, the conclusion is not failure; it is information: x squared plus five x plus twelve does not factorise over the integers. Write exactly that, and the marks are yours. The skill being tested is knowing that irreducible trinomials exist, and next year the quadratic formula will measure them precisely — some will surrender solutions in surd form, others will have none at all. What must never happen is inventing a pair that almost works, or forcing brackets that expand to the wrong middle term. An expansion check exposes forced brackets in seconds, which is one more reason the check closes every question: it defends right answers and convicts wrong ones with the same thirty seconds of work.
