**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we wrap up! First: a few people say the spotting is the scary part. In the middle of an exam, how do you actually SEE that sixty-four is a cube instead of just a big number?

**Tutor:** Spotting is vocabulary, not talent, and the vocabulary has six words: one, eight, twenty-seven, sixty-four, one hundred and twenty-five, two hundred and sixteen. Say them in the shower, write them on the inside cover of your exercise book, test yourself at the taxi stop — whatever it takes for each number to shout its own cube root at you. Then attach the trigger that tells you to check in the first place: exactly two terms, a plus or minus between them, and every ordinary tool refusing — no common factor, and not a difference of squares because the sign or the terms disqualify it. Two stubborn terms where one is on the six-number list, or carries an exponent divisible by three, and you are almost certainly looking at a cubes question. Question setters pick from that same short list, because the arithmetic must stay clean at this level. Own six numbers and no cubes question can sneak past you.

***

**Thandi:** Next one: someone in the class swears SOAP only works on the plus version, because their friend got the minus version wrong using it. Can you settle that?

**Tutor:** SOAP is built precisely to handle both versions, and the friend's error will have been in applying it, not in the mnemonic. Walk the letters. Same: the short bracket copies the question's sign — x plus two for x cubed plus eight, x minus four for x cubed minus sixty-four. Opposite: the long bracket's middle term flips that sign — minus two x in the sum, plus four x in the difference. Always Positive: the final term is plus four and plus sixteen respectively, positive in both, forever. Notice the terms never changed between the versions — square, multiply, square in both — so the only moving parts are three signs, and SOAP dictates each one. And when doubt creeps in mid-exam, you carry your own judge: multiply the brackets back. Correct signs make the four middle products cancel in pairs and return exactly the original two terms; a wrong sign leaves rubble behind. Thirty seconds of expansion settles any argument with certainty.

***

**Thandi:** This one had the most hands up: why is the middle of the long bracket two x and not four x? Half the class wrote x squared minus four x plus four.

**Tutor:** Because the middle position holds a product, and they manufactured a square. The long bracket is assembled in three fixed moves: square the first name, multiply the two names together, square the second name. With names x and two, that is x squared, then x times two — two x — then two squared, four. Whoever writes minus four x has squared the two in the middle position instead of multiplying it by x. And there is a reason the wrong version feels so comfortable: x squared minus four x plus four is an old friend — it is x minus two, all squared, from the perfect-square pattern you drilled earlier this term. The hand reaches for the familiar shape. The cure is to slow down at the middle position and say the recipe aloud — square, TIMES, square — before writing. The expansion check exposes this error every time as well: with minus four x in place, the brackets refuse to collapse back to x cubed plus eight, and the leftover terms point straight at the culprit.

***

**Thandi:** Last question, and it is a good one: someone attacked five x cubed minus forty with the identity straight away, found that five and forty are not cubes, and decided the question was impossible. What actually happened there?

**Tutor:** The question was working exactly as designed — it tests the discipline that comes BEFORE the identity. Rule zero of all factorising: hunt the common factor first, every single time. Five x cubed minus forty shares a factor of five, and removing it gives five, times the quantity x cubed minus eight. Now look at the bracket: eight is two cubed, and a difference of cubes stands revealed. SOAP completes it: five, times x minus two, times x squared plus two x plus four. The numbers five and forty were chosen deliberately so that a learner who skips the common factor sees no cubes anywhere and abandons the question — that is the whole trap. Two further details protect your marks. The five stays in front of the final answer permanently; it is part of the factorised form, not scaffolding to discard. And after the identity is applied, the long bracket still does not factorise — product four, sum two, no pair — so the answer ends there. Common factor, pattern, full stop: three moves, in that order, always.
