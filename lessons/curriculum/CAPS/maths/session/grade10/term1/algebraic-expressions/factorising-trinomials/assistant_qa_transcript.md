**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! First one: for six x squared minus seven x minus three, why must we multiply the six by the negative three? With the friendly ones like x squared plus six x plus eight we only ever used the eight.

**Tutor:** The front number was in the calculation all along — it was simply hiding, because it was a one. In x squared plus six x plus eight the leading coefficient is one, and one times eight is eight, so "use the last number" and "use the front number times the last number" produced the identical target. The moment the front number becomes six, those two instructions separate, and only six times negative three — negative eighteen — survives. The reason sits in the step that follows. The two numbers exist so that the middle term can be split into two pieces, giving four terms that are then taken in pairs; and the two pairs only share a common bracket when the numbers multiply to the leading coefficient times the constant. Any other pair produces two different brackets and the method stalls with nowhere to go. So carry one rule for every trinomial you will ever meet: multiply to a times c, add to b. The easy ones were never a separate method — they were this method with the front number wearing a disguise.

***

**Thandi:** Next: someone found negative nine and positive two, then wrote the split as six x squared plus two x minus nine x minus three, while their friend wrote six x squared minus nine x plus two x minus three. Surely one of them is wrong?

**Tutor:** Neither is wrong, and it is worth understanding why, because the fear of picking the "wrong order" wastes a great deal of exam time. Negative nine x plus two x and plus two x minus nine x are the same quantity written in a different order — addition does not care which piece comes first. Both orders group successfully. Take the first version: six x squared plus two x gives two x times the bracket three x plus one, and minus nine x minus three gives minus three times the bracket three x plus one. Matching brackets, so the answer is three x plus one, times two x minus three. Take the second version: six x squared minus nine x gives three x times the bracket two x minus three, and plus two x minus three gives one times the bracket two x minus three. The answer there is two x minus three, times three x plus one. Same two brackets, written in the opposite order — and multiplication does not care about order either. What genuinely matters is that each number keeps its own sign when it moves into the split. Two doors, one room.

***

**Thandi:** Here is a confession from the back row: when the two brackets came out different, somebody just made them the same and carried on. What should have happened instead?

**Tutor:** That confession is more useful than a correct answer, because it names the one move that is never allowed. Mismatched brackets are not a nuisance; they are the method's built-in alarm, and they almost always point at one of two faults. Fault one: the two numbers are not actually the right pair. Re-test both promises out loud — do they multiply to the leading coefficient times the constant, and do they add to the middle coefficient? Most mismatches die right there, because a pair that satisfies only one of the two conditions will split the middle term correctly and still refuse to group. Fault two: the sign taken out of the second pair. When a pair begins with a minus — say minus six x minus nine — factoring out a positive three leaves minus two x minus three, which will never match a bracket of two x minus three; factoring out negative three leaves two x plus three instead, and the signs inside flip into agreement. Fix the cause, and the brackets snap together on their own. The alarm is irritating, but it is protecting marks — never silence it by forcing.

***

**Thandi:** Last one: what if you test every single pair and nothing adds to the middle number? Somebody sat on one question for ages and never got there.

**Tutor:** Then run a three-point audit before drawing any conclusion. Point one: was a common factor taken out at the start? A hidden two in every term changes both targets, and hunting the wrong targets can look exactly like an impossible question. Point two: was the target product the leading coefficient times the constant, rather than the constant alone? That single slip makes perfectly ordinary trinomials look unfactorisable. Point three: did every sign survive? A constant of negative three treated as positive three changes the pair completely. If all three points check out and no factor pair passes both conditions, then the honest conclusion is that the trinomial does not factorise over the integers — and writing that down is the answer, worth full marks. Examiners plant these deliberately, precisely to catch learners who invent a nearly-right pair and hope nobody checks. Next year the quadratic formula opens those stubborn ones. For this year: hunt honestly, audit carefully, and when the pair truly does not exist, say so with confidence.
