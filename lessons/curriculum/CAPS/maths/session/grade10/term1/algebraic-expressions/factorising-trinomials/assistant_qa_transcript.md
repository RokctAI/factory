**Student:** For $6x^2 - 7x - 3$, why do I multiply 6 by −3 to get the product target? In the easy ones like $x^2 + 6x + 8$ I just used the 8.

**Thandi:** You were secretly using a-times-c all along! In $x^2 + 6x + 8$, the leading coefficient a is 1, and 1 × 8 = 8 — so "just use c" and "use a × c" gave the same number, and the shortcut hid the real rule. When a grows to 6, the two rules split apart, and only a × c = −18 still works. Here's why: the middle term's split has to survive the grouping step, and only pairs multiplying to a × c produce matching brackets. So one rule for all trinomials: product a × c, sum b. The easy ones were never a different method — just a disguised version.

**Student:** I found the pair −9 and +2, but then I wrote the split as $6x^2 + 2x - 9x - 3$ and my friend wrote $6x^2 - 9x + 2x - 3$. Who's right?

**Thandi:** Both of you! The split order doesn't matter — minus nine x plus two x and plus two x minus nine x are the same value, and BOTH orders group successfully. Your version groups as $2x(3x + 1) - 3(3x + 1)$, giving $(3x + 1)(2x - 3)$. Your friend's gives $3x(2x - 3) + 1(2x - 3)$, so $(2x - 3)(3x + 1)$. Same two brackets, opposite order — and multiplication doesn't care about order. The only thing that matters is carrying each number's own sign faithfully into the split. Two doors, one room.

***

**Student:** When I grouped, I got brackets $(2x - 3)$ and $(2x + 3)$ and just... forced them together anyway. I know that's wrong, but what should I have done?

**Thandi:** Respect to you for confessing — and yes, forcing is the one illegal move! Mismatched brackets are the method's built-in alarm: they mean something earlier is broken, usually one of two things. Check one: do your split numbers REALLY multiply to a × c and add to b? Re-test both conditions — most mismatches die right here. Check two: signs when factoring the second group — if the pair starts with a minus, you usually need to pull out a negative common factor to flip the signs inside into a match. Fix the cause and the brackets snap into agreement. The alarm is annoying, but it's saving your marks — never silence it by forcing.

***

**Student:** What if I try every factor pair and nothing adds up to b? I sat for five minutes on one in homework.

**Thandi:** Then you've likely discovered a trinomial that genuinely doesn't factorise over the integers — and recognising that IS the skill! First, run a quick audit: did you take out a common factor at the start? (A hidden 2 changes all the targets.) Did you use a × c, not just c? Signs right? If all three check out and no pair passes both tests, write "does not factorise" with confidence — some exam questions plant exactly this to catch students who invent nearly-right numbers. Next year the quadratic formula will crack those stubborn ones. For now: hunt honestly, and when the pair truly doesn't exist, saying so is full marks.
