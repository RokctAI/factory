It is that moment when the expression fills half the page, there are brackets inside brackets, and you cannot tell what is allowed to disappear. Breathe. This whole topic is four moves, and you already own three of them from the factorising weeks.

Move one: factorise everything, top and bottom, before you touch anything else. Difference of squares, trinomial, common factor, grouping, cubes — that is all it will ever be. Move two: cancel only whole brackets that appear above AND below. If a plus or minus sign is sitting loose in your top line, nothing in there may be cancelled — that is the crates-and-loose-bottles rule, and it is the single thing examiners test hardest.

Move three, only when you are adding or subtracting: factorise the bottoms, build the lowest common denominator from each different bracket taken once, rewrite both fractions over it, then combine the tops. And when you subtract, put a bracket around the numerator you are taking away before you distribute the minus. That one bracket has saved more marks than any other habit in Grade 10 algebra.

Move four is the free mark almost everyone forgets: restrictions. Look at the ORIGINAL bottoms, ask what would make each one zero, and write "x is not equal to" those values. Not the tidy answer's bottoms — the original ones. Even a bracket that cancelled away still bans its value.

Here is the habit that protects you when you are unsure: pick a friendly number, like x equals one, and put it into the original and into your answer. If the two numbers match, your work is sound. If they do not, you cancelled something illegal, and you have just caught it yourself before the marker did. That check takes about fifteen seconds and it turns guessing into knowing.

Factorise, cancel whole brackets, common denominator with a bracket on the subtraction, restrictions from the original. Four moves, in that order, every single time. You've got this!
