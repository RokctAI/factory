**Thandi:** Questions from the class! First: someone asks, with complete honesty — why does mathematics even need three ratios? Wouldn't sine alone be enough?

**Tutor:** In principle, yes — any one ratio, plus Pythagoras and the angle sum, could eventually recover everything, the way a builder could theoretically manage with only a hammer. In practice, three ratios exist so that ANY pair of sides gives you a direct route, without detours. Watch what the alternatives cost. Suppose you know the adjacent and the hypotenuse, but insist on sine: first Pythagoras to manufacture the opposite, then sine — two steps, two rounding hazards, where cosine does it in one. Know the opposite and adjacent but refuse tangent? Pythagoras again to build the hypotenuse first. Every extra step in an exam is a place to bleed accuracy and time. The three ratios are the three possible pairings of the sides — opposite-hypotenuse, adjacent-hypotenuse, opposite-adjacent — so whichever two sides the question names, exactly one ratio connects them immediately. That is the entire logic of the choosing routine: circle your two sides, and the pairing has already chosen your ratio for you. Three tools, three pairings, zero detours.

**Thandi:** Next: the special triangles. A learner wants to know why they should bother rebuilding triangles when the six values could just be memorised from a table — isn't the rebuild slower?

**Tutor:** Slower the first week, faster forever after — and crucially, crash-proof. A memorised table is a single point of failure: under exam pressure, was sine sixty root three over two, or one over root three? The table in your head has no way to answer; it can only be right or wrong. The triangles ARE the answer sheet. Ten seconds rebuilds the equilateral triangle of side two, the perpendicular drops, Pythagoras hands you root three, and every value for thirty and sixty degrees is sitting there to be READ, not recalled — sine sixty is the cut over the slant, root three over two, visibly. The serviette fold does the same for forty-five. And the rebuild pays a second dividend: it rehearses the exact skills the rest of the paper tests — Pythagoras, halving, surds — so the practice is never wasted. My honest advice is both: rebuild the triangles daily for two weeks, and the table will memorise itself as a side effect. Then the drawing becomes your verification tool, the thing that settles any mid-exam doubt in ten seconds flat. Memory that can be reconstructed is not really memory at all — it is understanding, and understanding does not crash.

**Thandi:** Third one: surds. Why is one over root two an acceptable final answer when it looks so unfinished? A few learners keep converting everything to decimals to be safe.

**Tutor:** They have the safety backwards — the decimal is the dangerous version. One over root two is EXACT: it names the number perfectly, completely, forever. Zero comma seven zero seven is an approximation that has already thrown information away, and every calculation built on it inherits the damage. Watch the difference in action: one over root two times one over root two is exactly one over two — clean, provable, final. Zero comma seven zero seven times zero comma seven zero seven is zero comma four nine nine eight four nine — a number pretending to be a half, and now you must decide how to round the pretence. Multiply enough approximations together and the error compounds until the final answer visibly drifts. That is why no-calculator questions exist at all: they test whether you can hold exactness all the way through — root three times root three IS three, root three over root three IS one — and an exact fraction like three halves is a complete, polished, full-marks answer. The convention some teachers add, rationalising one over root two into root two over two, is a formatting preference, not a correction; both are exact. The rule of thumb: while working, keep surds as surds; convert to decimals only if the question explicitly asks for a rounded value, and only at the very last line.

**Thandi:** Last question: the inverse. A learner keeps pressing sine when they mean inverse sine and the other way around, and asks how to know which one a question wants — before the calculator tells them something strange.

**Tutor:** Ask one question of every problem: what am I HUNTING — a length or an angle? That single word chooses the gear. Hunting a side, with the angle known: forward gear, the plain function — the angle goes in, the ratio comes out, and multiplying or dividing by a known side produces your length. Hunting an angle, with two sides known: reverse gear, the inverse — the ratio of your two sides goes in, and the angle comes out. The notation confuses because sine to the minus one LOOKS like a power, but it is not one over sine; it is sine run backwards, the same relationship read in the opposite direction — like a taxi that drives the identical route in reverse. And you have a built-in sanity monitor either way. Forward gear outputs for acute angles are ratios: sine and cosine must land between zero and one — a sine of three means something went wrong. Reverse gear outputs are angles: inverse sine of seven over twenty-five landing at sixteen comma three degrees is plausible; landing at zero comma two eight — the ratio echoed back — means you never engaged the inverse at all. Say the hunt out loud, choose the gear, then check the output is the right SPECIES: ratio or angle. Three seconds, and the shift key stops being a gamble.
