Welcome, student. Today we open the gateway to the largest section of Paper 2: trigonometry. Two exam tasks will carry the whole lesson. First, without a calculator, evaluate sine sixty degrees times cosine thirty degrees, minus tangent forty-five degrees. Second, solve for the angle x in a right-angled triangle whose hypotenuse is thirteen and whose side opposite x is five. Ratios, special angles, and the inverse move — the complete starter kit.

## Subtopic: The Three Ratios and SOH CAH TOA

Take any right-angled triangle and choose one of its acute angles — call it theta. The three sides now acquire names relative to theta. The hypotenuse: always the longest side, always opposite the right angle — it never changes. The opposite side: the one facing theta, across the triangle from it. The adjacent side: the one lying next to theta — the arm of the angle that is not the hypotenuse.

Now the definitions, the heart of all trigonometry. Sine of theta equals opposite over hypotenuse. Cosine of theta equals adjacent over hypotenuse. Tangent of theta equals opposite over adjacent. The memory aid the whole world uses: SOH CAH TOA.

But why do these ratios deserve names at all? Here is the deep fact: in any right-angled triangle with the same angle theta, these ratios come out IDENTICAL, no matter how large or small the triangle is. Double every side and the ratio five over thirteen is ten over twenty-six — unchanged. Similar triangles guarantee it. So sine of thirty degrees is a fixed number of the universe, not a property of one drawing. That constancy is what lets your calculator store the values, and what lets engineers, surveyors and astronomers compute unreachable distances from a known angle.

One discipline before we move: the labels opposite and adjacent belong to the ANGLE, not to the triangle. Switch your attention to the other acute angle and the opposite and adjacent sides swap places. Always re-label after choosing your angle — mislabeling is the root of most trig errors in Grade 10.

## Subtopic: The Special Angles — Values Without a Calculator

The exam says "without a calculator" — which means the values of thirty, forty-five and sixty degrees must live in your head. But do not memorise blindly: build them from two triangles you can redraw in ten seconds any time you forget.

Triangle one: take an equilateral triangle with sides of two, every angle sixty degrees. Drop a perpendicular from the top vertex: it splits the base into one and one, and the apex angle into thirty and thirty. Pythagoras on either half: the height squared is two squared minus one squared — three — so the height is root three. Read off the half-triangle, angle sixty at the base: opposite is root three, adjacent is one, hypotenuse is two. So sine sixty is root three over two; cosine sixty is one half; tangent sixty is root three. And from the thirty-degree angle at the top, the sides swap roles: sine thirty is one half; cosine thirty is root three over two; tangent thirty is one over root three.

Triangle two: a right-angled isosceles triangle with legs of one — both acute angles forty-five degrees. The hypotenuse: root two, by Pythagoras. So sine forty-five and cosine forty-five are both one over root two, and tangent forty-five is one over one — exactly one.

Now the evaluation, term by term. Sine sixty: root three over two. Cosine thirty: root three over two. Multiply them: root three times root three is three; two times two is four — three quarters. Tangent forty-five: one.

The expression: three quarters minus one, which is minus one quarter.

Two habits shown there worth naming. Surds multiplied cleanly — root three times root three is exactly three, no decimals, no rounding. And a negative final answer left proudly as a fraction: minus one quarter. Exam answers without calculators are exact answers; decimals are the enemy of exactness.

## Subtopic: Finding the Angle — the Inverse Move

Now the triangle problem: hypotenuse thirteen, the side opposite our unknown angle x is five. Find x.

Step one: choose the ratio. Interrogate what you have, relative to the angle x. The side facing x: opposite, five. The longest side: hypotenuse, thirteen. Opposite and hypotenuse — SOH — sine. So: sine of x equals five over thirteen.

Step two: understand what that sentence says. The sine OF the angle equals five thirteenths — but we want the angle itself. We must run sine backwards: from the ratio, recover the angle. That reverse gear is the inverse sine, written sine to the negative one, or arcsine. On the calculator: shift, then sine.

Step three: x equals inverse sine of five over thirteen. Calculator — in DEGREE mode, always check the little D on the screen — gives twenty-two comma six degrees, rounded to one decimal place.

Step four: verify with sense checks, two of them. First: five over thirteen is a smallish ratio — less than half — and sine thirty is exactly a half, so our angle should be smaller than thirty degrees. Twenty-two comma six: consistent. Second: this is the famous five, twelve, thirteen triangle — twelve squared plus five squared is one hundred and sixty-nine, thirteen squared. The third side is twelve, so the other acute angle should be inverse sine of twelve over thirteen, about sixty-seven comma four degrees — and indeed twenty-two comma six plus sixty-seven comma four is ninety. The triangle closes perfectly.

Understand the inverse as a question-flip. Sine answers: "given the angle, what is the ratio?" Inverse sine answers: "given the ratio, what was the angle?" Same relationship, opposite direction — like the reverse substitution you have met elsewhere in mathematics: same machine, driven backwards.

## Subtopic: Choosing the Right Ratio — and the Complete Method

The skill that decides marks is not computing — it is CHOOSING. Every right-triangle problem gives you two named sides, or one side and a mission. The choosing routine: one, mark the angle you care about. Two, label the three sides relative to that angle — hypotenuse first, it never moves; then opposite, facing the angle; then adjacent, the remaining arm. Three, circle the two sides that are known or wanted. Four, pick the ratio whose letters match your circle: opposite and hypotenuse — sine; adjacent and hypotenuse — cosine; opposite and adjacent — tangent.

Run the routine on quick variations of our triangle. Given the angle twenty-two comma six degrees and hypotenuse thirteen, find the opposite side: SOH again, but forwards — opposite equals thirteen times sine of the angle, giving five. Given opposite five and adjacent twelve, find the angle: TOA — tangent of x is five twelfths, inverse tangent gives the same twenty-two comma six degrees. Different givens, same triangle, same routine.

Now the error museum for trigonometry, three exhibits. Exhibit one: labels assigned to the wrong angle — the opposite of one acute angle is the adjacent of the other; always label AFTER choosing your angle. Exhibit two: calculator in radian mode — sine of thirty giving minus zero comma nine eight eight instead of zero comma five; check the D before you trust a single digit. Exhibit three: using the inverse when the angle is known, or forgetting it when the angle is wanted — the direction of the question decides; ratio wanted, plain function; angle wanted, inverse.

The complete method: label relative to your angle; choose by SOH CAH TOA; write the ratio equation; solve — forwards for a side, inverse for an angle; verify with a sense check on sizes, and Pythagoras when three sides are in play.

Where this gateway leads: Grade 11 pushes these ratios beyond ninety degrees, onto the Cartesian plane, into reduction formulae and general solutions; matric brings the sine, cosine and area rules for any triangle, plus three-dimensional problems — fifty marks of Paper 2 in Grade 11, forty in matric. Every one of those questions begins with what you did today: three ratios, two special triangles, one routine. Practise until SOH CAH TOA stops being a phrase and becomes a reflex.
