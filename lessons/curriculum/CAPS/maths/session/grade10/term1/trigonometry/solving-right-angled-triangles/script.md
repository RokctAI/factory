# Part 1 — Expert

The three ratios are defined; now they go to work. Solving a right-angled triangle means finding every unknown side and angle in it, and then using that skill on the two-dimensional problems the examination actually sets — masts, ladders, cliffs and shadows, reached through angles of elevation and depression. Four worked problems carry this session, and each one turns on the same decision: which ratio, and where does the unknown sit.

## Subtopic: Choosing the Ratio, and Finding a Side on Top

Every solution begins with labelling, and labelling begins with a choice of angle. Fix the acute angle you are working with. The hypotenuse is the side opposite the right angle — it never moves. The opposite side faces your chosen angle across the triangle. The adjacent side is the remaining arm of your angle. Change your angle, and opposite and adjacent trade places; the hypotenuse does not.

Then the choosing routine, four steps. One: mark the angle. Two: label the three sides relative to it. Three: identify the two sides that matter — the one you know and the one you want. Four: select the ratio whose initials match that pair. Opposite with hypotenuse is sine. Adjacent with hypotenuse is cosine. Opposite with adjacent is tangent.

Problem one. A ladder twenty metres long leans against a wall in Gqeberha, making an angle of thirty-five degrees with the ground. How far up the wall does it reach?

Label from the thirty-five degree angle at the ground. The ladder is the hypotenuse, twenty. The wall height is opposite the angle. Opposite and hypotenuse — that is sine. So sine of thirty-five degrees equals the height over twenty.

The unknown is in the numerator, so isolate it by multiplying: height equals twenty times sine thirty-five degrees. On the calculator, in DEGREE mode, sine thirty-five is zero comma five seven three six. Multiply: eleven comma four seven metres, to two decimal places.

Sense-check before moving on, always. At forty-five degrees a twenty-metre ladder would reach about fourteen comma one metres; thirty-five degrees is a shallower lean, so the height must come out below that. Eleven comma four seven sits comfortably there. And notice the structural fact: because sine of any acute angle is less than one, the opposite side is always shorter than the hypotenuse. An answer larger than the hypotenuse is impossible and signals a misplaced unknown.

Pause here — the questions for this section are with you now. Label relative to your chosen angle first, then let the pair of sides name the ratio for you.

## Subtopic: When the Unknown Sits Underneath

The second structural case is the one that separates careful workers from careless ones: the unknown in the denominator.

Problem two. A support cable is anchored to the ground and runs to a point on a mast, making an angle of forty degrees with the ground. The anchor is fifteen metres from the foot of the mast. How long is the cable?

Label from the forty-degree angle. The fifteen metres along the ground is adjacent. The cable is the hypotenuse, unknown. Adjacent with hypotenuse — cosine. So cosine of forty degrees equals fifteen over the cable length.

Now the unknown is underneath, and there is exactly one correct move: multiply both sides by the unknown, then divide by the cosine. That gives cable length equals fifteen divided by cosine forty degrees. Cosine forty is zero comma seven six six zero. Fifteen divided by that is nineteen comma five eight metres.

The error this case invites is multiplying instead of dividing — writing fifteen times cosine forty, which gives eleven comma four nine. Catch it with the sense check: the hypotenuse is the longest side of any right-angled triangle, so an answer of eleven comma four nine, shorter than the fifteen it should exceed, is impossible. State the rule as a habit: unknown on top, multiply; unknown underneath, divide the known side by the ratio.

Stop for this section's questions now — decide where the unknown sits before you touch the calculator, and check every hypotenuse against the sides it must exceed.

## Subtopic: Finding Angles, and Solving the Whole Triangle

To find a side you use the ratio forwards. To find an angle you run it backwards, using the inverse function — sine to the minus one, cosine to the minus one, tangent to the minus one — reached with the shift key.

Problem three. A wheelchair ramp at a Bloemfontein clinic rises eight hundred millimetres over a horizontal run of five metres. Find the angle of the ramp.

First, one unit system: eight hundred millimetres is zero comma eight metres. Relative to the ramp's angle at the bottom, the rise is opposite and the run is adjacent — opposite with adjacent, so tangent. Tangent of theta equals zero comma eight over five, which is zero comma one six. Then theta equals inverse tangent of zero comma one six, which is nine comma zero nine degrees.

Now "solve the triangle" in full, which means every side and every angle. The third angle: the angles of a triangle sum to one hundred and eighty, and one of them is ninety, so the two acute angles sum to ninety. Ninety minus nine comma zero nine is eighty comma nine one degrees. The third side, the ramp surface itself, comes from Pythagoras: zero comma eight squared is zero comma six four; five squared is twenty-five; the sum is twenty-five comma six four; the square root is five comma zero six metres.

Two disciplines here. Use Pythagoras rather than a rounded trigonometric answer whenever three sides are in play — it is exact and it does not inherit rounding error. And keep full calculator accuracy in your working, rounding only the final answer; rounding an intermediate angle to nine degrees and continuing produces a visibly different third side.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — inverse functions when an angle is wanted, the ninety-degree complement for the third angle, and Pythagoras for the third side.

## Subtopic: Angles of Elevation and Depression

Both angles are measured from the HORIZONTAL, never from the vertical, and getting that definition exactly right is worth marks on its own.

The angle of elevation is the angle between the horizontal and your line of sight when you look UP at an object. The angle of depression is the angle between the horizontal and your line of sight when you look DOWN at an object. Because the two horizontals — the one at your eye and the one at the object — are parallel, the angle of depression from the top equals the angle of elevation from the bottom: they are alternate angles between parallel lines. That equality is the single most useful fact in the whole application.

Problem four, elevation. A learner stands twenty-four metres from the foot of a cellphone mast in Mbombela. Her eyes are one comma six metres above the ground, and the angle of elevation from her eyes to the top of the mast is sixty-two degrees. How tall is the mast?

The right-angled triangle here has its horizontal side at eye level, twenty-four metres long, and its vertical side running from eye level to the top of the mast. That vertical part is opposite the sixty-two degrees; twenty-four is adjacent. Opposite with adjacent — tangent. So the vertical part equals twenty-four times tangent sixty-two degrees. Tangent sixty-two is one comma eight eight zero seven, so the vertical part is forty-five comma one four metres.

But that is not the mast's height. The triangle sat on the learner's eye line, so add the one comma six metres beneath it: forty-six comma seven four metres. Forgetting to add the observer's height is the classic elevation error, and it is always worth a mark.

Problem four, depression. From the top of a forty-metre cliff at Hermanus, the angle of depression to a boat at sea is twenty-five degrees. How far is the boat from the foot of the cliff?

Draw the horizontal at the top of the cliff; the twenty-five degrees sits between that horizontal and the line of sight down to the boat. Inside the triangle, the angle at the boat — between the sea surface and the line of sight — also equals twenty-five degrees, by alternate angles. Work at the boat: the cliff, forty metres, is opposite; the distance we want is adjacent. Opposite with adjacent — tangent. So tangent of twenty-five degrees equals forty over the distance. The unknown is underneath, so divide: distance equals forty over tangent twenty-five degrees. Tangent twenty-five is zero comma four six six three, so the distance is eighty-five comma seven eight metres.

The trap museum, four exhibits. One: the calculator in radian mode — check for the D on the display before trusting a single digit. Two: placing the angle of depression inside the triangle at the top vertex, where the true interior angle is its complement, sixty-five degrees. Three: omitting the observer's eye height. Four: rounding mid-calculation. The full method: sketch and label, mark the angle, choose the ratio, note whether the unknown is above or below the line, solve, then adjust for anything the triangle did not include.

The final questions of this part are with you now — elevation and depression defined from the horizontal, the alternate-angle equality used, and every observer height accounted for.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same four problems from a street corner and a ladder against a wall — same answers, but this time you will never be unsure which ratio to pick.

## Subtopic: Naming the Sides from Where You Stand

Stand in the corner of a room and look diagonally across it. Three things are around you: the wall straight in front of you, the wall running along beside you, and the long diagonal to the far corner. Three sides, and their names depend entirely on where you are standing.

A right-angled triangle works the same way. First pick your angle — that is where you stand. The longest side, the one facing the square corner, is the hypotenuse: it never changes, no matter where you stand. The side directly facing you across the triangle is the opposite. The side running beside you, from your corner along to the square corner, is the adjacent.

Move to the other corner and the two swap: what faced you now runs beside you. That is why you must always choose your angle first and label afterwards. Nearly every wrong trig answer starts with a label written before an angle was chosen.

Now the choosing part, and it is simpler than it looks. Look at only two sides: the one you know, and the one you want. Everything else in the triangle is noise. Opposite and hypotenuse together mean sine. Adjacent and hypotenuse mean cosine. Opposite and adjacent mean tangent. That is the whole decision.

Try it on a ladder in Gqeberha. Twenty metres long, leaning at thirty-five degrees to the ground, and you want to know how high up the wall it touches. Stand at the bottom angle. The ladder is the long diagonal — hypotenuse, twenty. The wall height faces you — opposite. Opposite and hypotenuse: sine. So the height is twenty times sine thirty-five, which is eleven comma four seven metres. And it makes sense: a twenty-metre ladder at forty-five degrees would reach about fourteen metres, and thirty-five degrees is a lazier lean, so a bit under twelve is exactly right.

Quick check before we carry on — a few questions on this are coming to you right now. For each one, stand at the angle first, name only the two sides that matter, and let them choose the ratio.

## Subtopic: Upstairs You Multiply, Downstairs You Divide

Here is the one thing that trips people who understand everything else: where the unknown is sitting.

Think about the price of bread. Twelve loaves cost R216. To get the price of one loaf, you divide: R18. But if you know one loaf costs R18 and you want twelve, you multiply. Same relationship, opposite directions — and which one you use depends entirely on which number is missing.

Trig ratios behave the same way. When you write sine of thirty-five equals the height over twenty, the unknown is upstairs, sitting on top of the fraction. To free it, multiply: height equals twenty times sine thirty-five.

But now the cable problem. A cable runs from a ground anchor up to a mast at forty degrees, and the anchor is fifteen metres from the mast's foot. The fifteen is beside the angle — adjacent. The cable is the long diagonal — hypotenuse. Adjacent and hypotenuse: cosine. So cosine of forty equals fifteen over the cable. This time the unknown is downstairs, underneath. To free it, you divide the known side by the ratio: cable equals fifteen divided by cosine forty, which is nineteen comma five eight metres.

And here is your safety net, free of charge. The hypotenuse is always the longest side in the triangle. So a cable that is meant to be longer than fifteen metres must come out bigger than fifteen. If you had multiplied instead of divided you would have got eleven comma four nine — shorter than a side it must beat — and you would know instantly, without any memo, that you divided the wrong way round.

Unknown upstairs, multiply. Unknown downstairs, divide. Then check the hypotenuse is the biggest thing in the triangle. Three seconds, and that whole family of errors disappears.

Your questions for this part are up now. Before pressing a single button, say out loud whether the unknown is upstairs or downstairs — that one sentence decides the move.

## Subtopic: Looking Up and Looking Down

Last piece, and it is the reason this maths exists at all: measuring things you cannot reach.

Stand still and look straight ahead. That flat line from your eyes is the horizontal — it is the line everything gets measured from. Tilt your head up to the top of a cellphone mast and the angle your gaze has swung through is the angle of elevation. Stand on a cliff and tilt down to a boat, and the angle your gaze swings down through is the angle of depression. Both measured from that flat eye-level line, never from a vertical pole.

And one lovely fact that saves you every time: the angle you look DOWN at the boat is exactly the angle the person in the boat looks UP at you. Two flat lines, yours and theirs, are parallel, so the two angles must match. Whenever a depression angle confuses you, move yourself to the bottom of the picture and use the same number as an elevation.

Now the mast in Mbombela. A learner stands twenty-four metres away, her eyes one comma six metres above the ground, and she looks up at sixty-two degrees to the top. Her triangle starts at her eyes, not her feet: twenty-four metres flat along, and the unknown rising up. Beside the angle is twenty-four, facing it is the height — that pairing is tangent. So the rise above her eye level is twenty-four times tangent sixty-two, which is forty-five comma one four metres.

Nearly done — but her eyes are not on the ground. Add the one comma six metres of learner beneath the triangle, and the mast is forty-six comma seven four metres tall. That little addition is the mark everyone forgets. Ask yourself every time: did my triangle start at ground level, or somewhere above it?

And the cliff at Hermanus. Forty metres up, looking down at twenty-five degrees to a boat. Move to the boat and use twenty-five degrees looking up. From there, the cliff faces you — forty, opposite — and the sea distance runs beside you — adjacent. Tangent again. But careful: the unknown is downstairs this time, so divide. Forty divided by tangent twenty-five gives eighty-five comma seven eight metres. The boat is nearly eighty-six metres out, measured by a person who never left the cliff top — and that is the whole point of trigonometry.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: find the horizontal line first, move to the bottom of the picture if a depression angle troubles you, and remember to add the height you are standing on. You have earned this one.
