**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! First: how do we actually decide which ratio to use? In the moment it feels like guessing between three options.

**Tutor:** It is never a guess, because the two sides involved make the decision for you — you only have to read them. Work in a fixed order. Choose the acute angle you are working from, and hold it firmly, because every label depends on it. Name the hypotenuse first: it is the side facing the right angle, the longest side, and it never changes whichever acute angle you pick. Name the opposite next: the side facing your angle across the triangle, not touching it at all. Whatever remains, touching your angle and not being the hypotenuse, is the adjacent. Now ignore the whole triangle except two sides: the length you were given, and the length you are asked for. If those two are the opposite and the hypotenuse, the ratio is sine. If they are the adjacent and the hypotenuse, it is cosine. If they are the opposite and the adjacent, it is tangent. There is exactly one match every time, so once you have read the pair, the choice is made. The most common cause of a wrong ratio is labelling the sides before choosing the angle — because opposite and adjacent swap when you move to the other acute angle, while the hypotenuse stays put. Angle first, labels second, ratio third.

**Thandi:** Next: sometimes we multiply by sine and sometimes we divide by it, and people get that backwards constantly. Is there a reliable way to tell?

**Tutor:** Yes — look at where the unknown is sitting in the fraction, and treat it exactly like any other equation. When you write sine of thirty-five degrees equals the height over twenty, the unknown height is in the numerator, being divided by twenty. To undo a division you multiply, so the height equals twenty times sine thirty-five, which is eleven comma four seven metres. But when you write cosine of forty degrees equals fifteen over the cable length, the unknown is in the denominator. Multiply both sides by the cable length and you get cable times cosine forty equals fifteen; divide both sides by cosine forty and the cable equals fifteen divided by cosine forty, which is nineteen comma five eight metres. Short version: unknown upstairs, multiply; unknown downstairs, divide the known side by the ratio. And carry a free safety net. Sine and cosine of any acute angle are always less than one, so multiplying makes a length smaller and dividing makes it bigger. Since the hypotenuse is the longest side of the triangle, any answer for a hypotenuse must come out larger than the other sides you were given. If your cable comes out as eleven comma four nine metres when it has to beat fifteen, you divided the wrong way — and you caught it yourself.

**Thandi:** Someone asked about the observer's height. Why do we add the one comma six metres at the end instead of just building it into the triangle from the start?

**Tutor:** Because the triangle you can solve is not the whole picture, and keeping those two things separate is what makes the method reliable. The angle of elevation is measured from the observer's eye, so the horizontal side of the right-angled triangle runs at eye level — one comma six metres above the ground — straight to the mast. The vertical side of that triangle therefore runs from eye level up to the top of the mast, and nothing below eye level is inside it at all. Tangent sixty-two degrees equals that vertical part over twenty-four, so the vertical part is twenty-four times tangent sixty-two, which is forty-five comma one four metres. That is a genuine, correct answer — to a question nobody asked. The mast is measured from the ground, so the strip of mast hidden below eye level, exactly one comma six metres of it, must be added back: forty-six comma seven four metres. Build the eye height into the triangle instead and you break the right angle, because the horizontal from the eye and the ground are different lines. So the discipline is: solve the triangle honestly, then ask what the triangle left out. The same question saves you when a person stands on a building, when a cliff is measured from sea level, or when an instrument sits on a tripod.

**Thandi:** Last one: how much accuracy should we keep in the working? Someone rounded halfway and got a different answer from the memo.

**Tutor:** Keep everything until the last line, and round once. Rounding partway through is not a small tidying-up; it changes the number you then multiply or divide by, and the error grows with every operation that follows. In the ramp problem, the angle is nine comma zero nine degrees when rounded, but the calculator holds many more digits; if you round to nine degrees and use that to find the third side, your answer drifts away from the true five comma zero six metres. The safest practice is to leave each result sitting in the calculator's memory or on the answer key and feed it directly into the next step, writing the rounded version on your page only as a report of progress, not as the value you continue with. Better still, when three sides come into play, use Pythagoras rather than a trigonometric ratio for the last side, because Pythagoras uses the two exact given lengths and inherits no rounding at all — zero comma eight squared plus five squared is twenty-five comma six four, and its square root is five comma zero six metres. Then follow the question's instruction on the final rounding: two decimal places for lengths and one for angles is the usual convention unless the paper says otherwise, and the unit must be written down with it.
