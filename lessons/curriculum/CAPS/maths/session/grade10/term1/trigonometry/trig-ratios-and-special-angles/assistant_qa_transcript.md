**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! First one: someone labelled the opposite and adjacent sides and the memo had them the other way round — and they insist their triangle looked exactly like the memo's. What went wrong?

**Tutor:** Almost certainly nothing about the triangle, and everything about which angle was in use. Opposite and adjacent are not fixed to the shape; they are fixed to the angle you have chosen to work with. The side facing your angle across the triangle is that angle's opposite, and the arm touching your angle which is not the hypotenuse is that angle's adjacent. Switch to the other acute angle and those two labels trade places immediately, because what faces one angle lies alongside the other. Only the hypotenuse is immune, since it is defined by the right angle and takes no notice of your choice. So a memo working with the angle at one end of a triangle will name the sides exactly opposite to a learner working with the angle at the other end, and both are correct for their own angle. The ritual that ends this permanently is angle first, labels second: fix the angle you are using, say out loud which side faces it, and only then write anything about opposite and adjacent.

***

**Thandi:** Next: why must we know the special-angle values by heart when a calculator knows them perfectly, and knows them faster?

**Tutor:** Two reasons, one immediate and one strategic. The immediate one is that these questions carry the instruction "without a calculator", and they award marks for the exact value. Sine sixty is root three over two; nought comma eight six six is a rounded decimal, not that value, and it earns nothing where exactness is being tested. The strategic reason arrives next year: special angles turn up buried inside longer problems, and recognising that sine sixty times cosine thirty is precisely three quarters keeps an entire page of algebra clean, whereas decimals contaminate everything downstream. But there is real relief in this too, because the requirement is not memory — it is reconstruction. An equilateral triangle of side two, cut down the middle, hands you every value for thirty and sixty degrees through Pythagoras. A square of side one, cut corner to corner, hands you forty-five. Memory fades under pressure; a shape you can rebuild from two facts does not.

**Thandi:** This one comes up in every test: when do we press shift-sine and when do we press plain sine? People keep choosing the wrong one and only noticing at the end.

**Tutor:** Decide it with a single question, asked before you touch the calculator: am I hunting a side or an angle? If the angle is given and a side is wanted, use the plain function — you are feeding an angle in and getting a ratio out, then multiplying, as in thirteen times sine of twenty-two comma six degrees to recover the side of length five. If two sides are known and the angle is wanted, use the inverse — you are feeding the ratio five over thirteen in and asking which angle produced it. Think of it as one machine with two gears: forward for sides, reverse for angles. There is also a self-catching signal. An inverse always returns something measured in degrees, and a plain function always returns a plain number with no unit, so if your answer has degrees where the question wanted a length, or a bare number where the question wanted an angle, you used the wrong gear and you can see it immediately in the shape of your own answer.

**Thandi:** Last one, and it cost real marks: a calculator gave sine thirty as minus nought comma nine eight eight, and the learner used that value through an entire question before noticing anything was wrong.

**Tutor:** That is the radian trap, and it is the single most expensive calculator error in this subject. The calculator was not faulty and it was not guessing: it was set to radian mode, so it read the instruction as sine of thirty radians. A radian is a different unit for measuring angles — you meet it properly much later — and thirty radians is an enormous rotation, which is why the answer came out negative and nowhere near what was expected. In degree mode, sine thirty is exactly nought comma five. Two habits protect you completely. First, check the mode indicator before starting any trigonometry: you want the letter D, or the word DEG, showing, and if you see R or RAD you change it in the setup menu. Second, build a tripwire out of something you already know for certain — ask the calculator for sine thirty and confirm it answers nought comma five. That single test, run whenever you pick the machine up, protects every trigonometry mark on the paper.
