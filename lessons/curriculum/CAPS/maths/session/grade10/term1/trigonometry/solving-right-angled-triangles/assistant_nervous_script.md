Hey, it's Thandi — word problems with masts and cliffs and ladders feel like a completely different subject from the neat triangles in your book, don't they? They are not. Every single one of them collapses into one right-angled triangle and one decision, and I want to hand you that decision cleanly.

Start by sketching, even a rough sketch. Mark the right angle, mark the angle you are given, and write the known lengths on the sides they belong to. Then stand at your chosen angle and name only two sides: the one you know, and the one you want. Opposite and hypotenuse means sine. Adjacent and hypotenuse means cosine. Opposite and adjacent means tangent. Everything else in the picture is noise you can ignore.

Then the move that decides half the marks: look at where the unknown is sitting. If it is on top of the fraction, multiply. If it is underneath, divide the known side by the ratio. Say it out loud before you touch the calculator — "the unknown is downstairs, so I divide" — because that sentence stops the single most common error in this section.

For elevation and depression, remember that both angles are measured from the flat, horizontal line, never from a vertical pole. And if a depression angle confuses you, just move yourself to the bottom of the picture: the angle you look down at the boat is exactly the angle the boat looks up at you, because the two horizontals are parallel. Same number, easier triangle.

Two small habits that quietly save marks. Check for the D on your calculator screen before you trust any answer — radian mode gives beautiful, confident nonsense. And ask, every single time, whether your triangle started at ground level or somewhere above it, because the observer's eye height has to be added back at the end.

Sketch, stand at the angle, pick two sides, notice upstairs or downstairs, and add back anything the triangle left out. That is the whole method, and it works on every problem they can set. You've got this!
