Welcome, student. Today we assemble the complete Grade 10 factorisation toolkit. Factorisation is the reverse gear of multiplication: expanding turns factors into a long expression; factorising turns the long expression back into its multiplicative parts. We will factorise fully three expressions, each demanding a different tool: six x squared minus seven x minus three; x cubed plus eight; and two a x plus two a y minus b x minus b y. Master these three and you hold the keys to the Algebra section of every Paper 1 — and, in matric, to calculus itself.

## Subtopic: The Toolkit and the First Question to Ask

Before any specific technique, learn the discipline that orders them all. Whenever you meet an expression to factorise, ask the questions in this sequence.

Question one — always first: is there a common factor? Every term sharing a number or a letter? Take it out before anything else. Nothing here today has an overall common factor, but skipping this check is the most common way to lose "factorise fully" marks: if you factorise six x squared plus nine x as three x times the quantity two x plus three, but only after first writing other steps, you waste time; if you never take out the three x at all, you lose the mark for "fully".

Question two: how many terms remain? The count points to the tool. Two terms: look for a difference of two squares, or a sum or difference of two cubes. Three terms: a trinomial — try the product-and-sum method. Four terms: grouping in pairs.

Question three, after factorising: is every bracket itself fully factorised? "Fully" means no bracket can be broken down further. A difference of squares hiding inside a bracket must still be split.

That decision tree — common factor, count the terms, check the brackets — is the whole strategy. The rest of the lesson is the tools themselves, one per expression.

## Subtopic: The Trinomial — Six x Squared Minus Seven x Minus Three

Our first expression: six x squared minus seven x minus three. Three terms — a trinomial with a leading coefficient bigger than one. The tool: split the middle term using product and sum.

Identify the coefficients: a is six, b is negative seven, c is negative three. We hunt two numbers whose product equals a times c — six times negative three, which is negative eighteen — and whose sum equals b, negative seven.

Since the product is negative, the two numbers carry opposite signs; since the sum is negative, the larger one is negative. Walk the factor pairs of eighteen: one and eighteen — difference seventeen, no. Two and nine — difference seven. Yes! Signs: negative nine and positive two. Check: negative nine times two is negative eighteen; negative nine plus two is negative seven. Both conditions met.

Split the middle term using these numbers: six x squared minus nine x plus two x minus three. Nothing has changed in value — negative seven x has merely been rewritten as minus nine x plus two x.

Now group in pairs. First pair: six x squared minus nine x — common factor three x, leaving two x minus three. Second pair: plus two x minus three — the only common factor is one, so write one times the quantity two x minus three. And there is the hallmark of success: both groups contain the identical bracket, two x minus three. If your brackets do not match, your split numbers or a sign are wrong — go back.

Factor out the common bracket: the quantity two x minus three, times the quantity three x plus one.

Verify by expanding — thirty seconds that guarantees the marks: two x times three x is six x squared; two x times one is two x; negative three times three x is negative nine x; negative three times one is negative three. Combine: six x squared minus seven x minus three. Perfect match.

## Subtopic: The Sum of Cubes — x Cubed Plus Eight

Second expression: x cubed plus eight. Two terms, joined by a plus. Not a difference of squares — but recognise eight as two cubed. This is a sum of two cubes, and CAPS Grade 10 expects you to know its factorisation.

The identity: a cubed plus b cubed equals the quantity a plus b, times the quantity a squared minus a b plus b squared. Memorise it with its partner: a cubed MINUS b cubed equals a minus b, times a squared PLUS a b plus b squared. The mnemonic for the signs: SOAP — Same, Opposite, Always Positive. The first bracket takes the SAME sign as the original; the middle term of the second bracket takes the OPPOSITE sign; the last term is ALWAYS POSITIVE.

Apply it. Here a is x and b is two, because two cubed is eight. First bracket, same sign: x plus two. Second bracket: a squared is x squared; opposite sign on the middle term gives minus two x; always positive at the end: plus four — two squared.

So x cubed plus eight equals the quantity x plus two, times the quantity x squared minus two x plus four.

Two warnings born from a thousand marking sessions. First: the second bracket does NOT factorise further — resist the urge to hunt for two numbers in x squared minus two x plus four; none exist in the real numbers, and forcing them is a classic error. Answering "fully factorised" here means stopping correctly. Second: do not confuse the middle term's coefficient — it is a times b, two x, not four x; learners who square instead of multiplying write four x and lose everything after.

Verify by expanding: x times x squared is x cubed; x times minus two x is minus two x squared; x times four is four x; two times x squared is two x squared; two times minus two x is minus four x; two times four is eight. The x squared terms cancel, the x terms cancel, leaving x cubed plus eight. The identity holds — and now you have proven it rather than trusted it.

## Subtopic: Grouping in Pairs — and the Full-Factorisation Discipline

Third expression: two a x plus two a y minus b x minus b y. Four terms — the signature of grouping in pairs.

Pair them as they stand. First pair: two a x plus two a y — common factor two a, leaving x plus y. So: two a times the quantity x plus y. Second pair: minus b x minus b y. Here is the sign trap of the whole technique: factor out NEGATIVE b, not positive b. Taking out negative b from minus b x leaves x; from minus b y leaves plus y. So: minus b times the quantity x plus y.

The expression reads: two a times x plus y, minus b times x plus y. Matching brackets — the green light. Factor the bracket out: the quantity x plus y, times the quantity two a minus b.

Had we pulled out positive b instead, the second bracket would have been negative x minus y — not matching x plus y — and the technique stalls. When a grouping refuses to match, check your signs first: nine times out of ten, a negative common factor was taken out as a positive.

Verify: x times two a is two a x; x times minus b is minus b x; y times two a is two a y; y times minus b is minus b y. All four original terms recovered.

Now step back and see the whole toolkit in action order. Common factor first, always. Two terms: difference of squares, or sum or difference of cubes — SOAP for the signs. Three terms: product and sum, split the middle, group. Four terms: group in pairs, watch the negative factor. And after every factorisation: expand mentally to verify, and inspect each bracket asking "can this break down further?" — because "factorise fully" is a contract, and the examiner checks it.

Where is this going? Next year, these factorisations solve quadratic and cubic equations. In matric, the factor theorem hands you one factor of a cubic and grouping finishes the job inside your calculus questions — thirty-five marks of differential calculus stand on the moves you practised today. Factorise until your hands do it without your eyes. That fluency is the investment; the returns arrive every exam for three years.
