**Student:** For $6x^2 - 7x - 3$, how did we know to use $-9$ and $+2$ and not, like, $-6$ and $-1$? Both multiply to give negative numbers!

**Thandi:** Both conditions have to hold at once — that's the whole game! The numbers must multiply to $a \times c = 6 \times (-3) = -18$ AND add to $b = -7$. Test your candidates: $-6 \times -1 = +6$ — wrong product already (two negatives make a positive!). Now $-9$ and $+2$: product $-18$ ✓, sum $-7$ ✓. Double tick, so they're the ones. My tip: list factor pairs of 18 first (1×18, 2×9, 3×6), then play with signs until BOTH the product and sum work. It's a lock with two keys — one key turning isn't enough.

**Student:** I always forget the cubes formula. Is there a way to rebuild it if I blank in the test?

**Thandi:** Yes — and it's better than memory! First bracket: easy, it's just the cube roots with the same sign as the question: for $x^3 + 8$, that's $(x + 2)$. Second bracket, use SOAP for signs — Same, Opposite, Always Positive — and this pattern for the terms: *square the first, multiply the two, square the last*. So: $x^2$, then $2x$ (with the opposite sign: $-2x$), then $4$. And if you're still unsure? Expand $(x+2)(x^2-2x+4)$ right there in the margin — thirty seconds, and you'll watch the middle terms cancel and $x^3 + 8$ appear. That expansion IS the proof you never have to memorise.

**Student:** When I grouped $2ax + 2ay - bx - by$ I got brackets $(x+y)$ and $(-x-y)$ and got stuck. What happened?

**Thandi:** Classic sign trap — you factored $+b$ out of the second pair instead of $-b$! Watch: $-bx - by$ with $+b$ removed leaves $-x - y$. But take out $-b$ and the signs flip inside: $-bx \div -b = +x$, $-by \div -b = +y$, leaving $(x + y)$ — a perfect match with the first bracket. Rule of thumb: if a pair *starts* with a minus sign, factor out a *negative*. And notice how kind this technique is: mismatched brackets aren't failure, they're the method tapping your shoulder saying "sign error, one step back." Fix the sign, brackets match, done.

**Student:** How do I know when I'm actually FINISHED? I factored something in a test and still lost a mark for "not fully factorised".

**Thandi:** Ah, the sneaky "fully"! After you factorise, interrogate every bracket like a suspect: "Can YOU be broken down?" The usual escapee is a difference of squares hiding inside: say you reach $2(x^2 - 9)$ — looks done, but $x^2 - 9 = (x-3)(x+3)$, so the full answer is $2(x-3)(x+3)$. Checklist for "finished": no common factors anywhere, no difference of squares lurking in any bracket, no factorable trinomial left inside. One caution the other way: don't over-factorise the cubes quadratic $x^2 - 2x + 4$ — it genuinely won't break. Check each bracket, know the unbreakable ones, then you're truly done.
