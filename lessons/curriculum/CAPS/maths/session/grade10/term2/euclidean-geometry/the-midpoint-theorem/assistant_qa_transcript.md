**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! The first one is honest: how do we actually know when to reach for the midpoint theorem? In the exam every triangle looks the same to us.

**Tutor:** Then train your eye on one specific signal rather than on triangles in general. The theorem announces itself in exactly two costumes, and you should learn to name them out loud. Costume one: the question hands you two midpoints, or marks two sides with matching pairs of ticks, and those two sides are different sides of the same triangle. The moment you see that, you own two facts about the segment joining them — it runs parallel to the third side, and its length is half of that third side. Costume two: the question hands you only one midpoint, but it also tells you a line drawn from that midpoint runs parallel to another side. That is the converse, and it hands you a second midpoint on the remaining side. Now the discipline that turns the signal into marks: when you spot costume one, write both conclusions down straight away, even if the question appears to want only one of them. Riders almost always need the second conclusion a line or two later, and having it already on the page costs you nothing but saves you the panic of hunting for it. If neither costume is present — one midpoint and nothing parallel — the theorem simply does not apply, and forcing it there wastes minutes you cannot spare.

**Thandi:** Here's one that came up twice during the break. Why does the proof start by extending the line and inventing a new point? It feels like cheating to add something that wasn't there.

**Tutor:** It feels that way, but a construction is the most legitimate move in Euclidean geometry — you are allowed to draw anything, because drawing does not assume anything. What you may never do is assume a property; what you may always do is add a line and then prove what it does. And the construction in this proof is chosen for a very specific reason. The claim we want is about a segment inside a triangle, and there is no theorem in the Grade 10 toolkit that says anything useful about segments floating inside triangles. There are, however, powerful facts about parallelograms. So the construction manufactures a parallelogram. Producing the joining segment beyond the second midpoint, by exactly its own length, creates a second triangle that must be congruent to the first — side, angle, side, using the midpoint, the constructed equal length, and vertically opposite angles. That congruence delivers one pair of segments that are both equal and parallel, and one such pair is precisely the condition that makes a four-sided figure a parallelogram. Once you have the parallelogram, both halves of the theorem drop out together: opposite sides of a parallelogram are equal and parallel, and the original segment is half of that side by construction. So the invented point is not a trick — it is the bridge from a problem with no tools to a problem with plenty.

**Thandi:** This one is a confession. Someone wrote that the joining line was twice the third side instead of half. Is there a way to never get that backwards again?

**Tutor:** There is, and it is worth building deliberately, because that single slip probably costs more marks in this topic than every other error combined. The reliable fix is a picture rather than a rule. Hold the roof in your head: two rafters leaning together, a long beam along the bottom, and a short brace nailed between the halfway marks on the two rafters. Now ask yourself which of those two pieces of timber a builder would need more wood for. Obviously the beam along the bottom — the brace sits higher up, where the triangle has already narrowed. The joining segment is always the shorter piece, always the half. Say the words "the join is the small one" before you commit any number to paper. Then run the sense check in both directions so neither surprises you: if the joining segment is twelve, the far side is twenty-four; if the far side is twelve, the joining segment is six. And in a rider with a diagram, glance at the sketch — if your answer makes the short segment longer than the long one, you have inverted it, and you now know before the marker does. That two-second check is the difference between losing three marks and losing none.

**Thandi:** Last one, and it's a bit of a challenge. If joining the midpoints gives a triangle with sides half as long, why is its area a quarter and not a half? Half the sides should mean half of everything, surely?

**Tutor:** That instinct is extremely common and it is worth dismantling properly, because it comes back in the measurement topic later this year. Length and area are different kinds of quantity, and a scale factor does not treat them the same way. Think about a floor being tiled. Halve the length of the room and you halve the number of tiles, yes — but halve the length AND the breadth, and the number of tiles falls to a quarter, because you have halved twice, once in each direction. Area lives in two dimensions, so a scale factor applies to it twice. In the triangle, joining the three midpoints produces an inner triangle whose every side is half the corresponding side of the original, so the shape is identical and only the scale has changed — and the area therefore shrinks by a half times a half, which is a quarter. You can see it without any formula, too: the three midpoint segments cut the original triangle into exactly four small triangles, all identical to one another. Four equal pieces, one of them the inner triangle — a quarter, plainly counted. Keep that pairing in mind: sides scaled by a factor, area scaled by that factor multiplied by itself. It is the same idea that will later explain why doubling every dimension of a box multiplies its volume by eight rather than by two.
