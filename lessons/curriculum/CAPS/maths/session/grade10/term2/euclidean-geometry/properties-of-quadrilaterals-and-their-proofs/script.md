# Part 1 — Expert

Euclidean geometry is the only place in Paper 2 where marks are awarded for reasoning rather than for arithmetic, and the special quadrilaterals are where that reasoning is first tested seriously. This session builds three things: the family of quadrilaterals and what each one owns; the proofs behind the parallelogram's properties; and the conditions that let you prove a shape IS a parallelogram, rectangle or rhombus. One worked rider closes the session.

## Subtopic: The Family of Quadrilaterals

A quadrilateral is any closed four-sided figure, and its interior angles always sum to three hundred and sixty degrees. The special quadrilaterals are defined by what is added to that.

The TRAPEZIUM: one pair of opposite sides parallel. That is the whole definition — nothing else is guaranteed.

The PARALLELOGRAM: both pairs of opposite sides parallel. Everything else it owns follows from that one condition.

The RECTANGLE: a parallelogram with an interior angle of ninety degrees. Since consecutive angles of a parallelogram are supplementary, one right angle forces all four.

The RHOMBUS: a parallelogram with two adjacent sides equal. Combined with opposite sides being equal, that makes all four sides equal.

The SQUARE: a parallelogram that is both a rectangle and a rhombus — four equal sides and four right angles.

The KITE: two pairs of ADJACENT sides equal. A kite is not a parallelogram; its parallel-free definition gives it a different set of properties.

Now the hierarchy, because examiners test it directly. Every rectangle is a parallelogram. Every rhombus is a parallelogram. Every square is both a rectangle and a rhombus, and therefore also a parallelogram. The relationship runs one way only: a rectangle need not be a rhombus, and a parallelogram need not be either. So a property proved for parallelograms is automatically true of rectangles, rhombuses and squares — and that inheritance saves you enormous time in a proof.

Pause here — the questions for this section are with you now. They test the definitions word for word, and which shapes inherit from which.

## Subtopic: The Parallelogram's Properties, and Where They Come From

A parallelogram owns four properties, and each is a legitimate reason to quote in a proof.

One: both pairs of opposite sides are equal. Two: both pairs of opposite angles are equal. Three: the diagonals bisect each other. Four: consecutive angles are supplementary — they add to one hundred and eighty degrees, because they are co-interior angles between parallel lines.

None of these is assumed; each is proved from the definition, and the first proof is the model for all geometric reasoning in this grade.

Prove that the opposite sides of parallelogram ABCD are equal. Draw the diagonal from A to C, splitting the figure into triangle ABC and triangle CDA. Now the chain, statement by statement, each with its reason.

Angle BAC equals angle DCA — alternate angles, because AB is parallel to DC. Angle BCA equals angle DAC — alternate angles, because AD is parallel to BC. Side AC equals side CA — it is common to both triangles. That is two angles and a corresponding side, so triangle ABC is congruent to triangle CDA, by angle-angle-side. Congruent triangles have all corresponding parts equal, so AB equals CD and BC equals DA. The opposite sides are equal, and angle B equals angle D falls out of the same congruence for free.

Notice what the diagonal did: it converted a statement about a quadrilateral into a statement about two triangles, where congruence is available. That move — draw the diagonal — is the standard opening for quadrilateral proofs.

A numerical application, because these properties are also examined with algebra. In parallelogram PQRS, angle P is three x plus ten degrees and angle Q is two x plus twenty degrees. Find all four angles. P and Q are consecutive angles, so they are supplementary: three x plus ten, plus two x plus twenty, equals one hundred and eighty. That gives five x plus thirty equals one hundred and eighty, so five x is one hundred and fifty and x is thirty. Then angle P is one hundred degrees and angle Q is eighty degrees. Opposite angles are equal, so angle R is one hundred and angle S is eighty. Check: one hundred plus eighty plus one hundred plus eighty is three hundred and sixty. Correct.

Stop for this section's questions now — the four properties named precisely, and the congruence chain that produces the first of them.

## Subtopic: Conditions — Proving a Figure IS a Parallelogram

A property tells you what a parallelogram HAS. A condition tells you what is ENOUGH to conclude that a figure is one. Confusing the two directions is the topic's deepest error, so keep them apart: you may not use a property of a parallelogram until you have established that the figure is a parallelogram.

There are five conditions, any one of which suffices. One: both pairs of opposite sides are parallel — the definition itself. Two: both pairs of opposite sides are equal. Three: both pairs of opposite angles are equal. Four: the diagonals bisect each other. Five: ONE pair of opposite sides is both equal AND parallel. That fifth condition is the most useful in examinations, because it needs only two facts about a single pair of sides — but both facts are required; equal alone is not enough, and parallel alone is not enough.

For the rectangle, two conditions. A parallelogram with one interior angle of ninety degrees is a rectangle. And a parallelogram with EQUAL diagonals is a rectangle — a fact worth memorising, since it is how coordinate and circle questions usually establish one.

For the rhombus, three conditions. A parallelogram with two adjacent sides equal is a rhombus. A parallelogram whose diagonals are PERPENDICULAR is a rhombus. And a parallelogram whose diagonals bisect its interior angles is a rhombus.

For the square, both sets together: a parallelogram with equal diagonals that intersect at right angles is a square.

The kite stands apart, so learn it separately: two pairs of adjacent sides equal; one pair of opposite angles equal — the pair between the unequal sides; and the long diagonal, which is the axis of symmetry, bisects the short diagonal perpendicularly and bisects the two angles it passes through.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — conditions, not properties, and the difference between what a shape has and what proves it is that shape.

## Subtopic: The Reasoning Chain — Writing a Proof That Scores

A geometry proof is marked line by line: a statement earns nothing without an acceptable reason beside it. Set the work out in two columns, statement on the left, reason on the right, and use the standard abbreviations examiners expect — "opp sides of parm", "alt angles, AB parallel to DC", "diags of parm bisect each other", "angles opp equal sides", "sum of angles of a triangle".

Now the rider. ABCD is a parallelogram with diagonals meeting at O. Points E and F lie on diagonal AC such that AE equals CF. Prove that BEDF is a parallelogram.

Read the target first: BEDF is a quadrilateral whose vertices are B, E, D and F, so its diagonals are BD and EF, and they cross at O. Since we have two diagonals available, aim at the condition "diagonals bisect each other".

The chain. Statement: AO equals OC — reason: the diagonals of parallelogram ABCD bisect each other. Statement: BO equals OD — same reason. Statement: AE equals CF — reason: given. Statement: EO equals AO minus AE, and OF equals OC minus CF — reason: the points lie in that order along the diagonal. Therefore EO equals OF, since equal quantities were subtracted from equal quantities. Now the diagonals of BEDF, which are EF and BD, bisect each other at O — because EO equals OF and BO equals OD. Conclusion: BEDF is a parallelogram, since its diagonals bisect each other.

Notice the shape of the argument: identify the target, choose the condition you will aim at, then assemble only the facts that condition needs. Working forwards blindly from everything you know is how learners fill a page and earn three marks.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: assuming what must be proved — quoting "opposite sides of a parallelogram are equal" about the very figure whose parallelogram status is the question. Two: reading a fact off the sketch because it looks true; a diagram is a guide, never evidence. Three: a statement with no reason, which scores nothing however true it is. Four: naming a congruence without checking that the equal parts correspond — angle-side-side is not a congruence condition.

The final questions of this part are with you now — target first, condition chosen, and every statement carrying its reason.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same shapes and the same proof from a family photograph and a shop receipt — same reasoning, but built out of things you already trust.

## Subtopic: The Family Where Every Child Inherits

Think of the quadrilaterals as one family, where each generation keeps everything it was given and adds one thing of its own.

The grandparent is just "four sides" — nothing special promised. The first child is the parallelogram, and its one new rule is that both pairs of opposite sides run parallel, like the two rails of a railway line and the two sleepers between them. Everything else the parallelogram owns comes free from that one rule.

Then two children of the parallelogram, each adding one thing. The rectangle adds square corners. The rhombus adds equal side lengths — all four the same. And then the square, the youngest, which inherits from BOTH: square corners AND four equal sides.

The inheritance is the useful part. A rectangle is still a parallelogram, so everything true of a parallelogram is automatically true of it — opposite sides equal, diagonals cutting each other in half, all of it. Same for the rhombus, same for the square. It works like a school uniform: everyone in the school wears the blazer; the prefects wear the blazer AND a badge. Seeing a badge tells you the person is in the school; seeing the blazer does not tell you they are a prefect.

That last sentence is the exam trap in one line. Inheritance runs downward only. Every square is a rectangle, but plenty of rectangles are not squares.

And the kite is the cousin who married in from elsewhere — no parallel sides at all, just two pairs of neighbouring sides that match, like a real kite in the wind. It has its own rules, so learn it on its own.

Quick check before we carry on — a few questions on the family are coming to you right now. For each shape, name the one thing it adds to its parent.

## Subtopic: Cut It Corner to Corner

Why should opposite sides of a parallelogram be equal? Not because it looks that way — because of something you can almost feel with your hands.

Take a parallelogram and cut it from one corner straight across to the opposite corner. You now hold two triangles. Turn one of them around and lay it on the other — they match exactly, corner for corner and edge for edge. Same triangle, twice. And if the two triangles are truly identical, then the side that was the top of the first one is the very same length as the side that was the bottom of the second. Opposite sides equal. That is not a coincidence; it is what the cut reveals.

Why do the two halves match? Because of parallel lines. Picture two straight streets running parallel, with one road cutting across both at a slant. The angle that road makes at the first street is the same angle it makes at the second — you can see it in the way the crossings look identical from above. In the parallelogram, the diagonal is that slanting road and the parallel sides are the two streets, so the angles at the two ends match up in pairs. Add the fact that both triangles share the diagonal itself — the same road, the same length for both — and the two triangles are locked into being identical.

That gives you the parallelogram's whole kit for free: opposite sides equal, opposite angles equal, and the diagonals cutting each other exactly in half. And one more, which is just the two streets again: any two corners next to each other add up to one hundred and eighty degrees.

Use that last one on real numbers. In a parallelogram, one corner is three x plus ten degrees and the corner next to it is two x plus twenty degrees. Neighbours add to one hundred and eighty, so five x plus thirty is one hundred and eighty, five x is one hundred and fifty, and x is thirty. The corners are one hundred degrees and eighty degrees — and their opposite partners copy them, so the four corners are one hundred, eighty, one hundred, eighty. Add them: three hundred and sixty. The shape closes, and you did it without measuring anything.

Your questions for this part are up now. Cut the shape corner to corner in your head first, and let the two matching triangles hand you the answer.

## Subtopic: A Proof Is a Chain of Receipts

Last piece, and it is about writing, not about shapes.

Imagine proving a phone is yours. Saying "it is mine" convinces nobody. Showing the till slip does. In geometry, every single line of your answer is a claim, and every claim needs its receipt — the reason, written next to it. A true statement with no reason earns zero, and a marker will not fill the reason in for you.

There is also an order to the receipts. You cannot use the receipt for a phone you have not yet proved you own. That is why you may never use a property of a parallelogram on a shape until you have first shown that shape IS a parallelogram. Properties come after, never before.

Watch that in action on a real question. In parallelogram ABCD the two diagonals cross at a point O. Two points, E and F, sit on the diagonal AC, with E as far from A as F is from C. Show that BEDF is a parallelogram.

First, decide what you are aiming at. BEDF has its own two diagonals — the line BD, and the line from E to F — and they cross at O. So aim at the rule that says: if a quadrilateral's diagonals cut each other in half, it is a parallelogram.

Now collect the receipts. In the original parallelogram, the diagonals cut each other in half, so A to O is the same as O to C, and B to O is the same as O to D. We were told E is as far from A as F is from C. Take those equal bits off the two equal halves, and what is left over must also be equal: E to O is the same length as O to F. So both diagonals of BEDF are cut in half at O — and by the rule we aimed at, BEDF is a parallelogram. Done, and every step had its receipt.

Three habits carry you through any rider like that one. Say what you are aiming at before you start. Never trust the picture — a sketch that looks like a right angle is not a right angle until something proves it. And write a reason beside every line, even the obvious ones. Do those three things and geometry stops being the section you fear and becomes the section where you can see the marks coming.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: name your target, gather only the receipts that target needs, and write the reason beside every claim. You have earned this one.
