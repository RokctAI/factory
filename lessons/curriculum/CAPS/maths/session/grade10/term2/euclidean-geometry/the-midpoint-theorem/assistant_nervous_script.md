Hey, it's Thandi — if the word "rider" makes your stomach drop, you are in very good company. Geometry feels like it wants you to be clever on demand. It doesn't. It wants you to notice one thing and then write four honest lines.

Here is the whole topic, shrunk. Two midpoints on two different sides of a triangle? Then the line joining them is parallel to the third side, and it is half as long. That is it. One sentence, two gifts. And the backwards version: one midpoint, plus a line from it running parallel to another side, lands you exactly on the middle of the last side. Two situations, two sentences. Nothing else to memorise.

The mistake I'd bet money on is the halving one. Under pressure, people double when they should halve. So take the roof with you: the brace between the two halfway marks is the SHORT piece. If the brace is three, the beam is six. Whisper "the join is the small one" before you write any number down.

Now the habit that actually protects marks: every time you use the theorem, write BOTH conclusions immediately — parallel, and half — even when the question looks like it only wants one. Nine times out of ten the other one is what you need two lines later, and it costs you nothing to have it already sitting on the page. Then put the reason beside every line. "Midpoint theorem" when you were handed two midpoints. "Line through midpoint parallel to second side" when you were handed one midpoint and a parallel. Six words, one mark, every time.

And do not trust the picture. If a point looks like the middle but the question never said so, it isn't. Diagrams are there to help you see, not to prove anything.

Two midpoints, third side, parallel and half. Say it in the taxi tomorrow morning. Then go do three riders — not ten, three — and watch how fast the pattern starts jumping out at you. You've got this!
