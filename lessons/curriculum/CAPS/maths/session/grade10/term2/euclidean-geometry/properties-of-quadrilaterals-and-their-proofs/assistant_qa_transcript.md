**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! First: everyone got stuck on the same thing. What actually is the difference between a property and a condition? They sound like the same facts said twice.

**Tutor:** They use the same facts, but they point in opposite directions, and geometry marks depend on knowing which direction you are travelling. A property answers: given that this figure IS a parallelogram, what do I now know for free? Opposite sides equal, opposite angles equal, diagonals bisecting each other, neighbouring angles adding to one hundred and eighty. A condition answers the reverse question: I do not yet know what this figure is — what would be enough to conclude it is a parallelogram? Both pairs of opposite sides parallel, or both pairs equal, or both pairs of opposite angles equal, or the diagonals bisecting each other, or one pair of opposite sides both equal and parallel. Notice that "diagonals bisect each other" appears on both lists, which is exactly why learners blur them. The discipline is about order of use. If a question says "ABCD is a parallelogram, prove that…", you may quote properties freely from the first line. If a question says "prove that ABCD is a parallelogram", you may not touch a single property of it, because that would assume the very thing you were asked to establish; you must build up to one of the conditions from the given information. Ask yourself before every line: have I earned this figure's name yet?

***

**Thandi:** Next: how do we know which condition to aim for? There are five, and picking the wrong one wastes the whole question.

**Tutor:** Let the given information choose for you, and decide before you write anything. Read what the question hands you and match it to the condition that uses that kind of fact. If you are given lengths of sides, aim at "both pairs of opposite sides equal", or better, at "one pair of opposite sides equal and parallel" if a parallel line is also mentioned. If you are given angles, aim at "both pairs of opposite angles equal". And if the figure's diagonals are drawn and the question talks about points on them or a common intersection point, aim at "the diagonals bisect each other" — that is almost always the intended route. The rider we did is a clean example: BEDF has B and D as opposite vertices and E and F as the other pair, and all four connect through the single point O, so the diagonals are staring at you, and the diagonal condition is clearly the target. Two practical tips. First, write your target down before the proof — something like "aim: diagonals of BEDF bisect each other" — because it keeps you from drifting. Second, if you get halfway and the facts refuse to fit, do not push on stubbornly; step back and ask which of the other four conditions your given information actually feeds.

***

**Thandi:** Someone asked about the sketch. If a figure clearly looks like a rhombus, why can't we just say so and carry on?

**Tutor:** Because a proof is an argument that must convince someone who has never seen your sketch, and because sketches are frequently drawn not to scale on purpose. Two shapes can look nearly identical while being genuinely different: a rhombus whose angles are eighty-eight and ninety-two degrees is almost indistinguishable from a square by eye, yet it is not a square, and every property that depends on right angles fails for it. Markers know this, so a statement whose only support is appearance earns nothing. What may you use? Three things: the information explicitly given in the question, the markings on the figure such as equal-length ticks and right-angle squares, and theorems and definitions you can name. Everything else must be derived. The habit that keeps you honest is to write the reason first in your head — if the only reason you can think of is "it looks like it", the statement is not yet available to you. And the reverse is worth knowing too: when a figure looks wrong but the given facts say otherwise, believe the facts. Some examination diagrams are deliberately distorted precisely to test whether you are reasoning or eyeballing.

***

**Thandi:** Last one: our proofs come out as a page of true statements but only earn a few marks. What are we doing wrong?

**Tutor:** Almost certainly two things: writing forwards instead of backwards, and leaving reasons out. Working forwards means listing everything you can deduce and hoping the conclusion appears — it produces long, true, unfocused answers, because a marker awards the steps that build the required argument, not every true sentence on the page. Working backwards means naming the finishing rule first, seeing what that rule needs, and then producing only those facts. In the BEDF rider, the finishing rule needs exactly two things: that EO equals OF, and that BO equals OD. Everything you write should serve one of those two, and the whole proof is then about five lines long. The second fault is the reason column. Every statement needs a named justification beside it — "diagonals of parm bisect each other", "given", "alternate angles, AB parallel to DC", "common side". Those phrases are worth marks on their own and they are completely memorisable, so they are the cheapest marks in Paper 2. Set your work in two columns, statement on the left and reason on the right, and never let a left-hand entry sit without a right-hand partner. Short, targeted, fully reasoned — that is what full marks looks like in geometry.
