# Part 1 — Expert

The quadrilateral work established what each special shape owns and which conditions prove a figure is one. This session spends that machinery on the most productive theorem in Grade 10 geometry — the midpoint theorem — then turns it around into its converse, and closes on the riders where both appear. Two conclusions, one hypothesis, and a proof that runs straight through the parallelogram.

## Subtopic: What the Midpoint Theorem Claims

The statement, precisely. In triangle ABC, let D be the midpoint of side AB and let E be the midpoint of side AC. Then the segment DE is parallel to BC, and DE is equal to half of BC.

Read the structure of that claim before anything else, because it decides how you use it. One hypothesis — two midpoints on two different sides of the same triangle — buys you two separate conclusions. A statement about DIRECTION: DE is parallel to the third side. And a statement about LENGTH: DE equals half of BC, which is the same as saying BC equals twice DE. In a rider you may quote either conclusion, or both, from the single reason "midpoint theorem".

Now the conditions, because this is where marks are lost. The two points must be midpoints, and they must lie on two DIFFERENT sides of the triangle. D halfway along AB and E halfway along AC gives you the theorem; D halfway along AB and E anywhere else on AC gives you nothing. The side you conclude about is always the third side — the one your midpoints do not sit on.

Put numbers on it immediately. In triangle PQR, PQ is sixteen centimetres, QR is twenty-four centimetres, and PR is twenty centimetres. S is the midpoint of PQ and T is the midpoint of PR. Then ST is parallel to QR, and ST is half of twenty-four — twelve centimetres. PS is half of sixteen, so eight centimetres; PT is half of twenty, so ten centimetres. The perimeter of triangle PST is eight plus twelve plus ten — thirty centimetres — against the big triangle's sixty. Exactly half, because every side of the inner triangle is half of a side of the outer one.

Extend that once and you have the medial triangle: join all three midpoints of triangle ABC and you cut it into four congruent smaller triangles, each with sides half the original's and therefore a quarter of its area. Examiners like to build riders on that picture.

Pause here — the questions for this section are with you now. They test the statement word for word, both conclusions, and the two conditions that must hold before you may quote it.

## Subtopic: Proving It — Two Copies Make a Parallelogram

The proof is examinable, and it is a model of the standard technique: convert a claim about a segment into a claim about a parallelogram.

Set up. In triangle ABC, D is the midpoint of AB and E is the midpoint of AC. Construction: produce DE to a point F so that DE equals EF, and join F to C. You have deliberately built a second copy of the segment on the far side of E.

Now the chain, statement and reason. In triangles ADE and CFE: AE equals EC, because E is the midpoint of AC. DE equals EF, by construction. Angle AED equals angle CEF, vertically opposite angles. Two sides and the included angle, so triangle ADE is congruent to triangle CFE, side-angle-side.

Harvest the congruence. Corresponding parts of congruent triangles are equal, so AD equals CF, and angle ADE equals angle CFE. That second equality is the hinge: angles ADE and CFE are alternate angles on the line DF, so AD is parallel to CF — which means DB is parallel to FC, since B lies on the line AD produced.

Now bring in the other midpoint. AD equals DB, because D is the midpoint of AB. Since AD equals CF as well, DB equals FC. So in quadrilateral DBCF the pair of opposite sides DB and FC are both equal AND parallel — and that is precisely the condition that makes a figure a parallelogram.

Read off the reward. DBCF is a parallelogram, so DF is parallel to BC and DF equals BC. But DE is half of DF, by construction. Therefore DE is parallel to BC and DE equals half of BC. Proved.

Look back at what did the work. The construction manufactured a congruent triangle; the congruence manufactured one pair of sides both equal and parallel; that pair manufactured a parallelogram; and the parallelogram handed over both conclusions at once. Nothing was measured, nothing assumed from the sketch.

Stop for this section's questions now — the construction step, the congruence condition used, and the exact reason that turns DBCF into a parallelogram.

## Subtopic: The Converse — One Midpoint and a Parallel Line

Every theorem has a converse, and the midpoint theorem's converse is examined at least as often as the theorem itself. It swaps a hypothesis and a conclusion.

The converse: if a line is drawn through the midpoint of one side of a triangle, parallel to a second side, then that line bisects the third side.

In symbols: in triangle ABC, if D is the midpoint of AB and DE is drawn parallel to BC with E on AC, then E is the midpoint of AC — that is, AE equals EC.

Compare the two carefully, because quoting the wrong one costs the reason mark. The theorem is given two midpoints and concludes parallel-and-half. The converse is given one midpoint plus a parallel line and concludes a second midpoint. The reason you write for the converse is "line through midpoint parallel to second side", and most memoranda expect exactly that phrasing.

Work it. In triangle ABC, AB is fourteen centimetres and BC is eighteen centimetres. D lies on AB with AD equal to seven centimetres, and DE is drawn parallel to BC with E on AC. Since AD is seven and AB is fourteen, D is the midpoint of AB. DE is parallel to BC. By the converse, E is the midpoint of AC — and now the original theorem applies as well, so DE is half of BC, nine centimetres. Two theorems chained, the second only available because the first delivered the missing midpoint.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — the converse stated in full, the reason phrased the way a memorandum wants it, and the difference between what each version is given and what each version concludes.

## Subtopic: Riders, Numbers and the Traps

A rider is marked line by line, so decide your target before writing anything, then gather only the facts that target needs.

The rider. In triangle ABC, D and E are the midpoints of AB and AC, and F is the midpoint of BC. Prove that DEFB is a parallelogram. Target first: a parallelogram, and the cheapest condition available is one pair of opposite sides both equal and parallel. Which pair? DE and BF — because DE is exactly what the midpoint theorem talks about, and BF is half of BC.

The chain. D and E are midpoints of AB and AC, so DE is parallel to BC and DE equals half of BC — midpoint theorem. F is the midpoint of BC, so BF equals half of BC — given. Therefore DE equals BF, since both equal half of the same segment. And DE is parallel to BF, because BF lies along BC and DE is parallel to BC. So DEFB has one pair of opposite sides equal and parallel, and DEFB is a parallelogram.

Four lines, one theorem, one condition — and both conclusions of the theorem were used, which is exactly why you write both down the moment you quote it.

Now the error museum, five exhibits. One: quoting the theorem when only ONE midpoint is given and nothing is parallel — the hypothesis fails, and every line after it is worth nothing. Two: doubling instead of halving. The joining segment is the SHORT one; if DE is twelve then BC is twenty-four, and if BC is twelve then DE is six. Three: reading midpoints off the sketch because two segments look equal — a tick mark or a statement is evidence, an appearance is not. Four: assuming E is a midpoint in order to prove DE is parallel to BC, which is the converse used circularly. Five: a correct statement with no reason beside it, which earns nothing.

One habit covers all five: before you use the theorem, say "two midpoints, two different sides, third side" to yourself, and check the question actually gives you all three.

The final questions of this part are with you now — the rider's target chosen first, both conclusions of the theorem used, and each of the five exhibits recognised on sight.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same theorem from a roof under construction — two rafters, one brace, and a length you can predict without ever climbing up to measure it.

## Subtopic: Halfway Up Two Rafters

Picture a roof being built on a house in Mamelodi. Two rafters lean up from the walls and meet at a point at the top. Along the bottom, between the two walls, runs one long beam — call it the base beam, six metres of it.

Now the builder wants a brace: a short piece of timber nailed straight across from one rafter to the other, to stop them spreading. He measures halfway up the left rafter and makes a mark. He measures halfway up the right rafter and makes a mark. He nails the brace between those two marks.

Two things happen, and they happen every single time. First, the brace comes out perfectly level — dead parallel with the base beam below it. Not roughly. Exactly. Second, and this is the part that saves him a trip up the ladder: the brace is exactly three metres, half the base beam. He could have cut it on the ground before climbing.

That is the whole theorem, plainly: mark the middle of two sides of a triangle, join those two marks, and the piece you have drawn runs parallel to the third side and measures half of it.

Try other numbers so the pattern sticks. Base beam ten metres, brace five. Base beam seven, brace three and a half. Base beam twenty-four centimetres on a page instead of a roof: the join is twelve. Always half, always parallel, no measuring required. And read it backwards, because that is just as useful: if the brace measures three metres, the beam below it is six. The join is always the short one.

Quick check before we carry on — a few questions on the roof are coming to you right now. For each one, decide which piece is the long one and which is the half.

## Subtopic: Why Exactly Half

Half is a suspiciously neat answer, so here is where it comes from, using nothing but a pair of scissors in your imagination.

Cut the triangle along the brace. You now hold two pieces: a small triangle from the top, and a wonky four-sided piece from the bottom. Take that small top triangle and spin it around — turn it a half-turn about the mark on the right rafter, so it swings down and sits next to the bottom piece.

Watch what it does. The mark on the right rafter was the exact middle of that rafter, so when the small triangle spins about it, the top half of the rafter lands precisely on the bottom half. Nothing overlaps, nothing sticks out. The small triangle slots into place beside the bottom piece, and the two together make one clean four-sided shape with both pairs of opposite sides running parallel — a parallelogram.

Now count. The brace and its spun-around copy sit end to end along the top of that parallelogram — two brace-lengths. The bottom of the parallelogram is the base beam, one beam-length. But the top and bottom of a parallelogram are always the same length. So two braces equal one beam, which is another way of saying one brace is half a beam. That is where the half lives, and it was never a guess. The parallel part comes free from the same picture, because the top and bottom of a parallelogram always run parallel.

There is a bonus if you join all three middle marks instead of two. The triangle falls apart into four little triangles, every one the same size and shape as the other three — like a milk tart cut into four identical wedges. Each is a quarter of the whole, with sides half as long as the big one's.

Your questions for this part are up now. Picture the spin and the parallelogram before you answer — the shape does the explaining for you.

## Subtopic: Spotting It Under Exam Pressure

The theorem is easy. Noticing that a question wants it is the actual skill.

Look for the word "midpoint", or two little tick marks showing a side has been halved, appearing on TWO different sides of one triangle. That is the calling card. When you see it, write down both things it gives you — the parallel and the half — even if the question seems to want only one. The other is usually needed two lines later, and writing it costs nothing.

Then the other version, which trips people because it works in the opposite direction. Sometimes a question gives you only ONE midpoint, and tells you the line drawn from it runs parallel to another side. That is enough: that line lands exactly on the middle of the remaining side. One midpoint plus a parallel hands you a second midpoint, free.

Now four things to stop doing, because they cost real marks. Do not double when you should halve — the joining piece is always the short one, so say "the join is the small one" before you write a number. Do not use the theorem when only one midpoint is given and nothing is parallel; that is not enough, and the whole answer collapses. Do not decide something is a midpoint because it looks like the middle — diagrams are drawn to be helpful, not accurate. And never write a line without its reason next to it: "midpoint theorem" when you were given two midpoints, "line through midpoint parallel to second side" when you were given one midpoint and a parallel. Two phrases, a mark each, handed over for six words.

Put it all together on a real question. Triangle ABC, with D the middle of AB, E the middle of AC, and F the middle of BC. Show that DEFB is a parallelogram. Aim at the easiest test: one pair of opposite sides equal and parallel. DE is half of BC and parallel to it — two midpoints, so the theorem applies. BF is half of BC too, because F is its middle. Half of the same thing equals half of the same thing, so DE and BF are equal, and they are parallel because BF lies right along BC. One pair, equal and parallel — it is a parallelogram.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: two midpoints or one plus a parallel, the join always the short one, and a reason beside every claim. Get these and the riders in the paper stop looking like riddles.
