Hey, it's Thandi — geometry has a special way of making people feel stupid, and it is not your fault. In algebra you always know what to do next; in a proof you stare at a figure and nothing tells you where to begin. So let me give you the starting point, because there is one, and it is the same every time.

Begin at the END. Read what you are asked to prove, and name the rule that would finish the job. Proving something is a parallelogram? Then one of five things will do it — opposite sides parallel, opposite sides equal, opposite angles equal, diagonals bisecting each other, or one pair of sides both equal and parallel. Pick the one the question has given you the most ingredients for, and now you are not wandering, you are collecting.

Keep properties and conditions in separate boxes in your head. A property is what a parallelogram HAS. A condition is what is ENOUGH to call something a parallelogram. You may only use the properties after you have earned the name — using them first is assuming what you were asked to prove, and markers spot it instantly.

The habit that protects your marks: a reason beside every single line, even the ones that feel too obvious to say. Alternate angles, AB parallel to DC. Diagonals of a parallelogram bisect each other. Common side. Given. Statements without reasons score nothing, no matter how true they are — and the reasons are the easiest marks in the paper because you can learn the standard wording once and reuse it forever.

And one warning that catches nearly everyone: never believe the picture. Sketches are drawn roughly and often deliberately misleadingly. If a side looks equal, it is not equal until something proves it. If a corner looks square, it is not square until something proves it. Only the given information and the theorems count.

Aim at a target, keep properties and conditions apart, write a reason on every line, and trust nothing you merely see. That is geometry — and it is the most learnable section in the whole paper. You've got this!
