# Part 1 — Expert

The parabola is the first curved graph the syllabus asks you to sketch from its equation alone, and in Grade 10 it arrives in one controlled form: y equals a x squared plus q. Two parameters, two effects, and a small set of features that a sketch must show. This session builds the mother function point by point, isolates what a does and what q does, then sketches a full example and runs the process backwards to find an equation from a graph.

## Subtopic: The Mother Function y Equals x Squared

Start with the simplest case, a equal to one and q equal to zero: y equals x squared, read aloud as "y equals x squared".

Build the table. Negative three gives nine. Negative two gives four. Negative one gives one. Zero gives zero. One gives one. Two gives four. Three gives nine. Notice the pairing: negative three and positive three both produce nine, because squaring destroys the sign.

Now describe the curve precisely enough for someone to draw it unseen. It sits entirely on or above the x-axis, touching its lowest point at the origin, zero comma zero. From there it rises on both sides, gently at first and then more and more steeply — one unit up at x equals one, four units up at x equals two, nine units up at x equals three. The left half is the mirror image of the right half, reflected in the y-axis. It is a smooth curve, never a V: at the bottom it flattens out and turns, it does not come to a point.

Name the features in the vocabulary the memorandum uses. The TURNING POINT is zero comma zero, where the curve stops falling and begins rising; here it is a MINIMUM. The AXIS OF SYMMETRY is the vertical line x equals zero, the y-axis. Both the x-intercept and the y-intercept sit at the origin in this case.

Then domain and range, which carry their own marks and are constantly confused. The DOMAIN is the set of x-values you are allowed to use: any real number squares happily, so the domain is all real x. The RANGE is the set of y-values that come out: a square is never negative, so y is greater than or equal to zero. Write the range in terms of y, never in terms of x — a range written as "x greater than or equal to zero" is simply wrong, and it is the most frequent error on this topic.

Pause here — the questions for this section are with you now. They test the table, the shape in words, and the difference between domain and range.

## Subtopic: The Effect of a — Stretch, Squash and Flip

Now let a vary while q stays zero, and watch what changes and what does not.

The SIGN of a decides which way the curve opens. When a is positive, the parabola opens upward — the shape learners call a smile — and the turning point is a MINIMUM. When a is negative, the parabola opens downward — a frown — and the turning point is a MAXIMUM. That single sign controls the entire mood of the graph, so read it first, always.

The SIZE of a decides how narrow or wide the curve is. Compare y equals two x squared with y equals x squared: at x equals one the first gives two instead of one, and at x equals three it gives eighteen instead of nine. Every y-value doubled, so the curve climbs faster and looks NARROWER. Now y equals one half x squared: at x equals two it gives two instead of four, climbing more slowly and looking WIDER. So a bigger than one in size stretches the graph vertically and narrows it; a between zero and one squashes it and widens it.

With a negative a, both things happen at once. For y equals negative two x squared the curve opens downward AND is narrow: at x equals one, y is negative two; at x equals three, y is negative eighteen. It is y equals two x squared reflected in the x-axis.

What a does NOT change is worth stating. With q still zero, the turning point stays at the origin and the axis of symmetry stays at x equals zero whatever a is — changing a pivots and flips the curve about that fixed point, never moving it sideways or up. And the range follows the sign of a: for y equals two x squared it is y greater than or equal to zero; for y equals negative two x squared it is y less than or equal to zero.

Stop for this section's questions now — the sign of a read first, the size of a interpreted as narrow or wide, and the range adjusted to match.

## Subtopic: The Effect of q — Shift, Range and Intercepts

The parameter q performs one job, and it does it cleanly: it shifts the whole graph VERTICALLY by q units. Positive q lifts it; negative q drops it. Nothing else about the curve changes — not its width, not its direction, not its axis of symmetry.

That gives you three consequences you should be able to state instantly. First, the turning point moves from the origin to zero comma q. Second, since x equals zero gives y equals q, the y-intercept IS q — so the value of q can be read straight off the graph as the height at which it crosses the y-axis. Third, the axis of symmetry remains x equals zero, because the shift is purely up or down.

The range shifts with the graph. For a positive a, the range becomes y greater than or equal to q. For a negative a, it becomes y less than or equal to q. Write it in y, and use the correct inequality direction for the shape.

Now the x-intercepts, which q controls in a way examiners exploit. Set y equal to zero and solve. For y equals x squared minus nine: zero equals x squared minus nine, so x squared equals nine, so x equals positive or negative three. Two intercepts, at negative three comma zero and three comma zero. That plus-or-minus is compulsory — writing only x equals three throws away half the answer and half the marks.

But now consider y equals x squared plus four. Setting y to zero gives x squared equals negative four, and no real number squares to a negative. This parabola has NO x-intercepts. Read that off the shape instead: it opens upward with its lowest point at zero comma four, four units above the axis, so it never reaches the axis at all. The rule to carry: when a and q have the SAME sign, the graph does not cut the x-axis; when they have OPPOSITE signs, it cuts twice; and when q is zero, it touches the axis once, at the origin.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — q as the vertical shift and the y-intercept, the range rewritten, and the sign test for how many x-intercepts exist.

## Subtopic: Sketching y Equals 2x² Minus 8, and Reading an Equation Off a Graph

Sketch y equals two x squared minus eight, showing all intercepts and the turning point.

Step one — shape. a is two, positive, so the parabola opens upward and has a minimum. Step two — turning point and axis. q is negative eight, so the turning point is zero comma negative eight and the axis of symmetry is x equals zero. Step three — y-intercept. Substitute x equals zero: y is negative eight, the turning point itself, as it always is in this form. Step four — x-intercepts. Set y to zero: two x squared equals eight, so x squared equals four, so x equals positive or negative two, giving negative two comma zero and two comma zero. Step five — domain all real x, range y greater than or equal to negative eight.

Now describe the finished sketch for someone drawing it. A smooth upward-opening curve whose lowest point sits on the y-axis at negative eight. It rises from there, crossing the x-axis at negative two on the left and at positive two on the right, and continuing steeply upward on both sides. A useful extra point: at x equals three, y is two times nine minus eight, which is ten — so the curve passes through three comma ten, and through negative three comma ten by symmetry.

Now the reverse task, asked just as often. A parabola of the form y equals a x squared plus q has its turning point at zero comma negative three and passes through two comma five. The turning point gives q immediately: q is negative three, so y equals a x squared minus three. Substitute the other point: five equals four a minus three, so four a is eight and a is two. The equation is y equals two x squared minus three.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: the range written in x instead of y. Two: the plus-or-minus dropped when solving x squared equals four. Three: q mistaken for a horizontal shift — in this form the graph never moves sideways. Four: a between zero and one described as "smaller" rather than WIDER; the graph is squashed vertically, so it spreads out horizontally. Five: forcing x-intercepts to exist when a and q share a sign — if the equation gives x squared equal to a negative number, the correct answer is that there are none.

The final questions of this part are with you now — the five-step sketch executed in order, and the reverse problem solved by taking q from the turning point before substituting.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same curve from a soccer ball in the air and a bowl on a kitchen table — two pictures that hold every rule in this topic.

## Subtopic: The Shape of a Thrown Ball

Kick a soccer ball high across a field and watch its path. Up, slowing, over the top, then down — and the way down is a perfect mirror of the way up. That shape is a parabola. Water arcing from a hosepipe makes one; a cricket ball thrown from the boundary makes one.

Its most important feature is the turn. There is exactly one moment when the ball stops climbing and starts falling, and that point is the turning point. Everything else is arranged around it: the left side and the right side are identical, folded across a vertical line through that point, like a face folded down the nose.

Now build the curve from numbers instead of a kick. The recipe: take a number and square it. Zero gives zero, one gives one, two gives four, three gives nine. Now go negative: negative one also gives one, negative two gives four, negative three gives nine. Same answers on both sides — which is exactly why the picture is symmetrical. Plot them and a smooth valley appears, sitting on the origin and climbing away in both directions, gently near the bottom and then very steeply: nine units high at only three units across.

Two words to keep apart, because a test will ask for both. What you are ALLOWED to put in is the domain — and you can square any number at all, so anything goes. What COMES OUT is the range — and squaring never gives a negative, so nothing below zero ever appears. Answer that second one using y, not x. It is about the heights, not the widths.

Quick check before we carry on — a few questions on the ball's path are coming to you right now. Watch out for the difference between what goes in and what comes out.

## Subtopic: Turning the Bowl Over and Lifting It

Picture a bowl standing on your kitchen table. That is your basic curve, smiling upward, lowest point at the bottom of the bowl. Two things can be done to that bowl, and they are exactly the two numbers in the equation.

The first is the number multiplying the squared part. Its SIGN turns the bowl over: positive and it stays the right way up, holding soup, with a lowest point; negative and the whole thing flips, like a dish covering food to keep the flies off, with a highest point instead. So before anything else ask: soup bowl or fly cover?

Its SIZE decides the bowl's shape. Bigger than one and the sides rise faster, so the bowl becomes tall and narrow like a vase — doubling it doubles every height, so one unit up becomes two and nine units up becomes eighteen. A fraction like a half and the sides rise slowly, so the bowl spreads out flat and wide like a serving dish. Bigger number, narrower; smaller fraction, wider.

The second number, added or subtracted on its own at the end, does something completely different: it picks the whole bowl up and moves it straight up or down. Add four and it sits four units higher on a shelf. Subtract eight and it drops eight units into a hole. Same width, same direction — only the height changes. And it comes with a free gift: that number is where the curve crosses the up-and-down axis AND the height of the turning point, both at once.

Put the two together and you can predict something clever. A bowl that is the right way up AND lifted above the line can never touch the line — a soup bowl on a high shelf, nowhere near the floor, so no crossings at all. But drop that same bowl into a hole and the rim pokes through the floor on both sides: two crossings. Same shape, different height, completely different answer.

Your questions for this part are up now. For each one, decide first whether the bowl is upright or upside down, then how high or low it is sitting.

## Subtopic: Four Moves to a Sketch — and the Traps

Sketching looks like art. It is not. It is four moves, in order, and then you join the dots.

Move one: which way up? Look at the sign of the number in front of the squared part. Positive, it smiles. Negative, it frowns. Write that down before you calculate anything.

Move two: where is the turning point? Read the number added at the end. That is the height of the turn, and it sits on the up-and-down axis. So for two x squared take away eight, the turn sits at eight below zero, on the vertical axis.

Move three: where does it cross the flat axis? Set the height to zero and solve. Two x squared minus eight equals zero, so two x squared is eight, so x squared is four — and now the important bit: x could be two OR negative two, because both of them square to four. Two crossings: at two on the right and two on the left. Forgetting the negative one is the most expensive habit in this whole topic, because it is worth its own mark and it wrecks the symmetry of your sketch.

Move four: get one extra point for accuracy, and use the mirror. Put in three: two times nine is eighteen, take away eight, gives ten. So the curve passes ten units up when it is three across — and by symmetry it does the same on the left.

Now draw it: a smooth valley with its floor at eight below zero on the vertical axis, rising to cross the horizontal axis at two on each side, and climbing steeply past ten units high by the time it is three across. Never a sharp point at the bottom — the curve rounds off and turns.

Three traps to name out loud so they lose their power. First, the range must be written using y, because it describes how high the curve goes, not how wide. Second, that end number never slides the curve sideways — only up and down. Third, when the maths hands you "x squared equals negative four", do not panic and do not fudge it. Nothing squares to a negative, so the honest answer is that there are no crossings, and the picture agrees: an upright bowl already sitting above the line was never going to reach it.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: shape, turn, crossings, extra point — in that order, every time. Get that routine automatic and a parabola question becomes four quick lines and a curve.
