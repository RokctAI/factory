# Part 1 — Expert

Two graphs remain in the Grade 10 function set, and they share a feature the parabola never had: an asymptote — a line the curve approaches without ever reaching. This session builds the hyperbola, y equals a over x plus q, and the exponential graph, y equals a times b to the power x, plus q. For each: the basic shape point by point, the effect of the two parameters, the asymptotes, the restrictions, and a full sketch with every feature a memorandum expects.

## Subtopic: The Hyperbola — Branches, Asymptotes and Restrictions

Begin with y equals six over x, read as "y equals six divided by x".

Build the table on positive x first. When x is one, y is six. When x is two, y is three. When x is three, y is two. When x is six, y is one. When x is twelve, y is one half. So as x grows, y shrinks toward zero but never arrives — there is no x for which six over x equals zero. Now run x downward: when x is one half, y is twelve; when x is one tenth, y is sixty. As x approaches zero, y races off to infinity.

Now negative x, and the pattern mirrors with the sign flipped. Negative one gives negative six. Negative two gives negative three. Negative six gives negative one. Negative twelve gives negative one half.

The picture, described so it can be drawn. Two separate curves, called BRANCHES. One branch lives in the first quadrant, high up close to the y-axis on the left, sweeping down and flattening out toward the x-axis on the right, never touching either. The other branch is its point-reflection through the origin: it lives in the third quadrant, hugging the x-axis on the far left and plunging down close to the y-axis. The two branches never meet and never cross the axes.

Name the features. The ASYMPTOTES are the two lines the curve approaches without touching: the vertical asymptote x equals zero, and the horizontal asymptote y equals zero. The reason for the vertical one is a restriction, not a preference: x equals zero is forbidden, because division by zero is undefined. So the DOMAIN is all real x except zero. And since six over x can never equal zero, the RANGE is all real y except zero.

Two more facts the syllabus names. The graph has two axes of symmetry, the lines y equals x and y equals negative x. And it has no y-intercept at all — ever — because you would have to substitute x equals zero, which is not allowed. Learners who calculate a y-intercept for a hyperbola have broken the domain.

The sign of a decides which pair of quadrants the branches occupy. A positive a puts them in the first and third quadrants, as above. A negative a — take y equals negative six over x — puts them in the SECOND and FOURTH: at x equals one, y is negative six; at x equals negative one, y is positive six.

Pause here — the questions for this section are with you now. They test the two branches, both asymptotes, the domain restriction, and why there is never a y-intercept.

## Subtopic: The Effect of q on the Hyperbola

The parameter q does for the hyperbola exactly what it did for the parabola: it shifts the entire graph vertically by q units, changing nothing else about the shape.

But the consequence is bigger here, because the graph carries its horizontal asymptote with it. For y equals six over x plus q, the horizontal asymptote is the line y equals q, NOT y equals zero. The vertical asymptote stays at x equals zero, because the shift is vertical and the division by x is untouched.

Work y equals six over x minus two in full.

Asymptotes: vertical x equals zero; horizontal y equals negative two. Domain: all real x except zero. Range: all real y except negative two — the curve gets arbitrarily close to the line y equals negative two but never lands on it.

The x-intercept, which now exists. Set y to zero: zero equals six over x minus two, so six over x equals two, so six equals two x, so x equals three. The point three comma zero. Note that the original y equals six over x had no x-intercept; the shift created one.

The y-intercept: none, as always.

Two extra points to guide the sketch. At x equals one, y is six minus two, which is four. At x equals negative one, y is negative six minus two, which is negative eight.

Describe the sketch. Draw the vertical asymptote on the y-axis and a dashed horizontal line two units below the x-axis. The right-hand branch comes down steeply from high on the left of the first quadrant, passes through one comma four, crosses the x-axis at three comma zero, and then flattens out as it moves right, settling just above the line y equals negative two. The left-hand branch sits below that dashed line: far to the left it lies just under y equals negative two, and as x approaches zero from the left it plunges downward, passing through negative one comma negative eight.

The examiner's favourite question here is reading the equation off a graph. If a hyperbola of the form y equals a over x plus q has a horizontal asymptote at y equals negative two and passes through one comma four, then q is negative two immediately, and substituting gives four equals a over one, minus two, so a equals six. The equation is y equals six over x minus two.

Stop for this section's questions now — the horizontal asymptote as y equals q, the range that follows from it, and the x-intercept created by the shift.

## Subtopic: The Exponential Graph — Growth, Decay and Its Asymptote

The exponential function is y equals b to the power x, where b is positive and not equal to one. The variable is now in the EXPONENT — this is the crucial difference from y equals x squared, where the variable was in the base.

Take y equals two to the power x. The table: when x is negative three, y is one eighth. Negative two gives one quarter. Negative one gives one half. Zero gives one. One gives two. Two gives four. Three gives eight. Ten gives one thousand and twenty-four.

Describe the curve. Far to the left it lies just above the x-axis, so close that it is visually flat — but always above, never on it. Moving right it climbs, slowly at first, passing through zero comma one, then two, four, eight, and then rocketing upward. There is no turning point, no symmetry, and no maximum. The horizontal asymptote is y equals zero, approached on the left only.

The features. Domain: all real x — you may raise two to any power. Range: y greater than zero, strictly — two to any power is always positive, never zero. That strict inequality matters: writing y greater than or equal to zero is wrong, because the value zero is never attained. Y-intercept: substitute x equals zero and get b to the power zero, which is one, for every base. Every basic exponential graph passes through zero comma one. X-intercept: none, because the curve never reaches the axis.

Now decay. Take y equals one half to the power x. When x is negative two, y is four; negative one gives two; zero gives one; one gives one half; two gives one quarter. The curve falls from left to right: high on the left, dropping through zero comma one, then flattening toward the x-axis on the right. Same asymptote, same range, same y-intercept — mirrored in the y-axis.

The rule: when b is greater than one the graph increases and represents GROWTH; when b lies between zero and one the graph decreases and represents DECAY. And note why b may not equal one: one to any power is one, which is a horizontal line, not an exponential curve. Nor may b be negative, since a negative base raised to a half has no real value.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — growth against decay, the asymptote at y equals zero, the strict inequality in the range, and the y-intercept of one.

## Subtopic: The Effect of a and q, and a Full Sketch

The full Grade 10 form is y equals a times b to the power x, plus q.

The parameter q shifts the graph vertically, taking the horizontal asymptote with it: the asymptote becomes y equals q. The parameter a stretches the graph vertically and, if negative, reflects it in the horizontal asymptote — a negative a turns growth into a curve that falls away downward.

Sketch y equals two to the power x, minus four.

Asymptote: y equals negative four. Y-intercept: substitute x equals zero — two to the zero is one, minus four is negative three, so the point zero comma negative three. X-intercept: set y to zero — two to the power x equals four, and four is two squared, so x equals two, giving the point two comma zero. Domain: all real x. Range: y greater than negative four.

The sketch in words: a dashed horizontal line four units below the x-axis. The curve comes in from the far left just above that dashed line, rises slowly through negative three on the y-axis, crosses the x-axis at x equals two, and then climbs steeply — at x equals four it is two to the fourth minus four, which is twelve.

Now one with a negative a. Sketch y equals negative two to the power x, plus eight — that is, negative one times two to the power x, plus eight.

Asymptote: y equals eight. Y-intercept: negative one plus eight, which is seven, so zero comma seven. X-intercept: set y to zero — two to the power x equals eight, so x equals three, giving three comma zero. Range: y less than eight, because subtracting a positive quantity from eight always leaves less than eight. The curve comes in from the far left just below the line y equals eight, drifts down through zero comma seven, crosses the x-axis at three comma zero, and then plunges steeply downward.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: giving the asymptote as y equals zero when q is not zero — the asymptote moves with the graph. Two: calculating a y-intercept for a hyperbola, which has none. Three: writing the exponential range with "greater than or equal to" instead of strictly "greater than". Four: confusing y equals two to the power x with y equals x squared — in the first the variable is upstairs, and the graph never turns; in the second it is downstairs, and the graph does. Five: drawing a curve that touches or crosses its asymptote; the whole meaning of an asymptote is that this never happens.

The final questions of this part are with you now — the asymptote located from q, the two intercepts computed, and the range written with the correct strict inequality.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same two curves from a taxi fare being split and a rumour spreading through a school — two ordinary situations that behave exactly like these graphs.

## Subtopic: Sharing a Fixed Pot

Six people need to get somewhere and the fare for the whole trip is one hundred and twenty rand, fixed, no matter how many climb in. What does each person pay?

Two passengers: sixty rand each. Three: forty each. Four: thirty. Six: twenty. Twelve: ten. Notice what is happening — the more people, the less each pays, and the drop is fast at first and then gentle. Going from two to three saves twenty rand each. Going from eleven to twelve saves less than a rand.

Now push it both ways and two important things appear. Squeeze more and more people in and the share per person keeps getting smaller — but it never becomes zero. Somebody always pays something, however small. And go the other way: with only one passenger the fare is the full one hundred and twenty. With half a person... well, that is the point. You cannot divide the fare among ZERO people. That situation simply does not exist.

Those two impossibilities are exactly what the graph shows. Plot people across the bottom and rand-per-person up the side, and the curve sweeps down steeply and then flattens out, running closer and closer to the bottom line without ever settling on it, and shooting up higher and higher near the left without ever touching the side line. Those two lines it never touches have a name: asymptotes. One says "the share never reaches zero". The other says "you cannot share among nobody".

And because you can never put zero into this, the curve never crosses the up-and-down axis at all. A hyperbola has no y-intercept. Not a difficult one — none at all.

There is also a second copy of the whole shape, sitting on the opposite side, in the corner where both numbers are negative. That is what makes it a pair of branches rather than a single curve.

Quick check before we carry on — a few questions on the shared fare are coming to you right now. Think about the two things that can never happen.

## Subtopic: Doubling Every Time

Different story. Someone tells one learner a piece of news before school. In the first break that learner tells two people. In the next break each of those tells two more. And so on.

Count the tellers: one, then two, then four, then eight, then sixteen, then thirty-two. By the tenth round it is over a thousand. The numbers start almost boringly small and then explode. That is growth by MULTIPLYING instead of by adding, and it is what an exponential curve looks like: hugging the bottom for a long time and then rocketing upward almost vertically.

Now run the same idea backwards, which is where the second important feature lives. Go back one round from one teller and you get a half. Back another and you get a quarter. Then an eighth, a sixteenth. The numbers shrink and shrink and shrink — but they never hit zero. You can halve forever and always have something left. So on the left the curve creeps along just above the bottom line without ever landing on it. That bottom line is the asymptote, and it is why the answer to "what heights does this curve reach" must be written as strictly ABOVE zero, never "zero or more". Zero is the one height it never manages.

One point every one of these curves passes through, and it is free: when you have done no rounds at all, there is one teller. Anything to the power zero is one. So the curve crosses the up-and-down axis at one, every time, whatever the doubling number is.

And the same shape runs in reverse. Instead of doubling, halve: a cellphone battery losing half its remaining charge each hour, or the value of a bakkie dropping by a fixed fraction each year. The curve then starts high on the left and slides down toward the bottom line, still never touching it. Multiplying by more than one climbs. Multiplying by a fraction falls.

Your questions for this part are up now. Look for whether the story multiplies up or multiplies down, and remember the line the curve refuses to touch.

## Subtopic: Asymptote First — the Routine and the Traps

Both of these graphs are sketched the same way, and the order matters: asymptote first, then intercepts, then a couple of points, then the curve.

Move one: find the line the curve will not touch. For both of these, the number sitting on its own at the end tells you where that horizontal line is. Subtract four and the line is four units below zero. Add eight and it is eight above. Draw it dashed, before anything else, because the curve has to be drawn around it. And for the shared-fare curve there is a second one — the vertical line where you would be dividing by zero.

Move two: where does it cross the up-and-down axis? For the doubling curve, put in zero rounds: you get one, plus whatever was added at the end. So for two to the power x minus four, that is one minus four, which is three below zero. For the shared-fare curve, skip this move entirely. It never crosses.

Move three: where does it cross the flat axis? Set the height to zero and solve. Two to the power x minus four equals zero means two to the power x equals four, and four is two doubled twice, so x is two. For six over x minus two equals zero, six over x is two, so x is three.

Move four: one or two extra points, then draw. The curve must approach each dashed line and never touch it — that is the single most important thing about the drawing, and markers look for it.

Now three traps, named so they lose their teeth. First: the line the curve avoids is NOT always the bottom axis. It moves when a number is added or subtracted at the end. Writing "asymptote at zero" for a graph that was shifted down four is the most common lost mark in this whole topic.

Second: for the doubling curve, the heights are strictly above the line — never "on or above". The curve gets unimaginably close and still never lands. One little symbol, one mark.

Third, and this one causes real confusion in tests: do not mix up x squared with two to the power x. In the first, the x is downstairs on the ground floor, being squared, and the graph turns around and comes back. In the second, the x is upstairs in the exponent, and the graph never turns — it just keeps climbing. Say "ground floor or upstairs?" when you look at an equation, and you will never sketch the wrong shape again.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: dashed lines first, then the crossings, then the shape that leans in but never lands. Draw it that way three times and both curves become easy marks.
