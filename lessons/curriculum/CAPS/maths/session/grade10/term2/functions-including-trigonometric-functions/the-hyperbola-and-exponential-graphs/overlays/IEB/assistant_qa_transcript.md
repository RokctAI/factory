**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! First, the honest one everyone whispered: what IS an asymptote really? "A line the graph never touches" sounds like a rule someone invented, not a reason.

**Tutor:** It is a consequence, and the reason always lives inside the equation. Take the sharing curve, eight divided by x. Ask: can the height ever be exactly zero? That would need eight divided by something to equal nothing, and no matter how gigantic the bottom of the fraction becomes, the result stays a whisker above zero. The curve chases the horizontal axis forever and never catches it, because the arithmetic forbids the catch. Now ask what happens at x equals zero. Dividing by nothing is not a big answer or a small answer — it is no answer at all. So no point of the curve exists there, and the vertical line through zero becomes a wall the branches press against from both sides. The doubling curve tells the same story: three raised to any power is always positive, because multiplying positive numbers can never manufacture zero, so the curve floats above the horizontal axis however far left you go. The method is always the same — ask which value is impossible, then draw a dashed line there. And here is the practical part: those dashed lines earn marks on their own, before the curve is even drawn, so placing them correctly is the cheapest scoring in the topic.

***

**Thandi:** Next: someone in the class insisted the asymptote is always the x-axis. When is that true, and when does it stop being true?

**Tutor:** True only for the plainest, unshifted versions — and questions almost never leave them unshifted. The moment a number is added or subtracted at the end of the equation, the entire graph slides vertically, and the crucial point is that it carries its horizontal asymptote along, like a boat carrying its own waterline up a rising tide. For eight over x plus two, the graph lifts two units, so the untouchable line lifts to y equals two, and the range becomes every real value except two. For three to the power x minus nine, the line drops to y equals negative nine, and the range is everything strictly greater than negative nine. For negative three to the power x plus three, the line sits up at y equals three, and because something positive is always being subtracted from three, the curve lives below it — range strictly less than three. So build the reflex: read the lonely number at the end of the equation FIRST, write the asymptote down before touching anything else, and let the range fall straight out of it. One habit, several marks, every single time.

***

**Thandi:** A confession from the practice set: someone was asked about the y-intercept of a hyperbola, and they calculated one. Where exactly did it go wrong?

**Tutor:** At the very first pen-stroke, and the why matters more than the what. Finding a y-intercept means substituting x equals zero — that is literally what crossing the vertical axis says. But for a hyperbola, x equals zero is the precise value the domain throws out, because it would put a zero underneath the fraction. The substitution is not hard; it is illegal. So the honest answer is a sentence, not a number: this graph has no y-intercept, because x equals zero is excluded from the domain. And the picture backs the sentence completely — the vertical axis IS the asymptote, with the two branches leaning against it from either side, so there is no place an intercept could even sit. Writing that one sentence usually earns the mark that hunting for a number would have thrown away. Take the wider lesson with you too: before substituting any value into any function, ask whether the value is allowed in. Restrictions are not decoration — they decide which questions have answers at all.

***

**Thandi:** Last one, the classic mix-up: x squared and three to the power x look like cousins on paper. How do we keep them apart for good?

**Tutor:** One question and one check. The question: is the x on the ground floor or upstairs? In x squared, the x is on the ground floor — it is the base, and the exponent is a frozen two. Because a negative input squares to a positive output, the left side mirrors the right, and the curve comes down, turns at a lowest point, and climbs back up: turning point, axis of symmetry, a range with a floor. In three to the power x, the x has moved upstairs into the exponent, and the base is a frozen three. Each step of one in x multiplies the output by three — every step, forever — so the curve never turns around: it only climbs to the right and creeps along its asymptote to the left, no symmetry, no floor it can touch, range strictly above the line. Then the check, if doubt survives: feed in x equals ten. Ten squared is one hundred. Three to the power ten is fifty-nine thousand and forty-nine. That absurd gap is the real difference between the two kinds of growth — squaring grows by stretching, exponentials grow by relentless multiplying — and once you have seen the gap with actual numbers, the two graphs never blur again.
