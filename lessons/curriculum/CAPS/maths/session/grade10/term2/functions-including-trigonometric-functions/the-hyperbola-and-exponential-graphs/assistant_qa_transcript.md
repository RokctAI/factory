**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! The first one is the honest confusion: what IS an asymptote, really? People keep saying "a line the graph never touches", but that sounds more like a rule than a reason.

**Tutor:** It is a consequence, not a rule, and the reason is always hiding in the equation. Take the shared-fare curve, six divided by x. Ask whether the height can ever be exactly zero — that would require six divided by something to equal nothing, and no matter how enormous you make the bottom of that fraction, the answer stays a tiny bit above zero. So the curve approaches the horizontal axis forever and never arrives, purely because the arithmetic forbids arrival. Now ask what happens at x equals zero. Dividing by nothing is not a small answer or a large answer; it is not defined at all. So there is no point on the curve there, and the vertical line through zero becomes a boundary the graph presses against from both sides. Same story for the doubling curve: two raised to any power is always positive, because multiplying positives never produces zero, so the curve stays above the horizontal axis no matter how far left you travel. In every case, first ask what value is impossible, then draw a dashed line there. And the practical payoff is that asymptotes are worth marks in their own right — a sketch with correctly placed dashed lines earns before the curve is even drawn.

**Thandi:** Here's one that started an argument at the break. Somebody wrote that the asymptote of a graph is always the x-axis. Is that ever true, and when does it stop being true?

**Tutor:** It is true only for the plainest versions, and it stops being true the moment a number is added or subtracted at the end of the equation — which, in the examination, is nearly always. The parameter added at the end shifts the entire graph vertically, and the crucial thing is that the graph carries its horizontal asymptote up or down with it, like a boat and its waterline rising together on the tide. For six over x minus two, the curve is dragged two units down, so the line it presses against is two units below the horizontal axis: the asymptote is y equals negative two, and the range becomes every real value except negative two. For two to the power x minus four, the asymptote is four units down, at y equals negative four, and the range is everything strictly greater than negative four. And for negative two to the power x plus eight, the asymptote sits eight units up, at y equals eight, with the range everything strictly less than eight, because you are always subtracting something positive from eight. So the habit is simple and it protects several marks at once: read the number at the end of the equation FIRST, write the asymptote down before anything else, and let the range follow directly from it.

**Thandi:** This one is a confession from the practice questions. Someone was asked for the y-intercept of a hyperbola and worked out an answer. Where exactly did that go wrong?

**Tutor:** It went wrong at the very first step, and it is worth being clear about why, because the same reasoning protects you elsewhere. To find a y-intercept you substitute x equals zero — that is what crossing the vertical axis means. But for a hyperbola, x equals zero is precisely the value the domain excludes, because it would mean dividing by nothing. So the substitution is not merely difficult, it is not permitted, and the honest answer is that the graph has no y-intercept at all. Notice that the picture agrees perfectly: the vertical axis is the asymptote, and the two branches lean toward it from either side without ever crossing it, so there is nowhere for an intercept to be. What a learner should write is a short sentence — no y-intercept, since x equals zero is excluded from the domain — which is a statement of understanding and usually earns the mark that hunting for a number would have lost. And carry the general principle with you: before substituting any value into any function, ask whether that value is actually allowed. Restrictions are not decoration; they change what questions even have answers.

**Thandi:** Last one, and it is the mix-up that catches nearly everyone: how do we keep x squared and two to the power x apart when they look so similar written down?

**Tutor:** Ask one question — is the x on the ground floor or upstairs? — and then check the answer against what the graph does. In x squared, the variable sits on the ground floor as the base, and the exponent is a fixed two. Doubling x multiplies the height by four, and because a negative number squared is positive, the left and right sides mirror each other, so the curve comes down, turns at a lowest point, and goes back up. It has a turning point, an axis of symmetry, and a range with a floor at the turning point. In two to the power x, the variable is upstairs in the exponent and the base is a fixed two. Adding one to x multiplies the height by two, every single time, so the curve never turns around: it climbs relentlessly to the right and creeps toward the horizontal axis on the left, never symmetrical, never turning, with a horizontal asymptote and a strictly-greater-than range. There is also a numerical check you can run in seconds if you are unsure: at x equals ten, x squared is a hundred, but two to the power ten is one thousand and twenty-four — exponential growth outruns squaring badly once the numbers get going, and that gap is the real difference between adding-based growth and multiplying-based growth.
