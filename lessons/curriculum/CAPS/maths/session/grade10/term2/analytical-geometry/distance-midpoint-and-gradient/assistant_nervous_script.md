Hey, it's Thandi — three formulae in one lesson can feel like three chances to pick the wrong one. So let me shrink them for you, because they are much closer to each other than they look.

All three start the same way: you compare the two points. How far apart are the x's? How far apart are the y's? Once you have those two numbers, distance squares them and adds and square-roots. Gradient divides the up by the across. And midpoint — this is the only odd one — adds the coordinates and halves. Say it once: middle adds, the other two subtract. That single sentence prevents the most common mistake in the whole topic.

The second thing that catches people is minus signs. Five take away negative three is eight, not two. Write the brackets in — five minus, open bracket, negative three, close bracket — and let your own handwriting protect you. And in gradient, pick an order and stick to it: if the second point's y goes on top, the second point's x goes underneath. Swap them halfway and your line falls when it should climb.

One more, and it's a small thing that costs a full mark: a flat line has gradient zero, but a vertical line's gradient is UNDEFINED. Not zero. There is nothing to divide by. Flat is zero, wall is undefined — two words, keep them apart.

And please do not trust the picture. In this lesson four points made a shape that looked exactly like a rectangle, and the gradients came out to negative fifteen over sixteen instead of negative one. Not a right angle. The numbers decide; the sketch is just a friendly suggestion.

So tonight: plot two points on squared paper, and work out all three things for them — the distance, the middle, the steepness. Do that for two or three pairs and the formulae stop being a menu you have to choose from and start being one routine you just run. You've got this!
