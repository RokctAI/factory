**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! The one everybody asked: how do we stop mixing up the three formulae under pressure? They all look like the same letters shuffled around.

**Tutor:** They do, and the cure is to stop memorising three separate things and start with what all three have in common. Every one of them begins by comparing the two points — how far apart the x-values are, and how far apart the y-values are. For A at negative three, two, and B at five, eight, those two numbers are eight across and six up, and you will use them again and again. Now the three formulae are just three different things to do with that pair. Distance squares both and adds them and takes the square root, because it is Pythagoras: sixty-four plus thirty-six is one hundred, square root ten. Gradient divides the up by the across: six over eight, which is three over four. And midpoint is the exception, because it does not use differences at all — it averages, adding the two x-values and halving, then adding the two y-values and halving, which gives one, five. So the sentence to carry into the examination is short: middle adds, distance and steepness subtract. If you catch yourself subtracting for a midpoint, the answer will land outside the two points, and that impossibility is your own built-in alarm.

**Thandi:** Here's one that caused an argument during the break. Someone said a vertical line has gradient zero because it does not go across at all. Someone else said it is undefined. Who is right, and does it actually matter?

**Tutor:** The second person is right, and it matters for a mark in almost every paper. Work it from the formula. Gradient is the change in y divided by the change in x. On a horizontal line — think of a soccer field — the y-values never change, so the top of the fraction is zero, and zero divided by anything nonzero is zero. That gradient really is zero. On a vertical line — think of a wall — it is the bottom of the fraction that is zero, because both points have the same x-value. Division by zero is not a small number or a big number; it is not a number at all, because nothing multiplied by zero can give you a nonzero answer. So we say the gradient is undefined. The confusion comes from both cases having a zero somewhere, and the fix is to ask which zero: zero on top means flat, zero underneath means vertical. There is a knock-on effect too. Because a vertical line has no gradient, you cannot use the product-equals-negative-one test on a vertical and a horizontal line, even though they are obviously perpendicular. That pair you simply state by inspection, and examiners accept it.

**Thandi:** This one is a confession from the practice run. The four points made a shape that looked exactly like a rectangle, so someone wrote 'rectangle' and lost most of the question. What should they have done instead?

**Tutor:** They should have treated the sketch as a suggestion and the numbers as the evidence — and this is the single most important habit in analytical geometry. Our four points were A negative three, two; B five, eight; C nine, three; and D one, negative three. On squared paper they do look convincingly square-cornered. But a rectangle needs adjacent sides at right angles, and on a grid the only proof of a right angle is that the two gradients multiply to negative one. Gradient AB is three over four. Gradient AD is negative five over four. Multiply: negative fifteen over sixteen. Close to negative one, which is exactly why the eye is fooled, but not equal to it. So angle A is not a right angle, and the figure is not a rectangle. What the numbers DO prove is that opposite sides are equal, opposite sides are parallel, and the diagonals share the midpoint three, two comma five — three separate proofs that it is a parallelogram. So the answer is: parallelogram, and nothing more. Build the routine of always asking what you have PROVED rather than what you have SEEN, and this whole topic becomes reliable.

**Thandi:** Last one, and it is a why question. Why do we bother putting shapes on a grid at all? We already proved things about parallelograms in Euclidean geometry without any coordinates.

**Tutor:** Because the two methods have opposite strengths, and having both makes you far harder to stump. Euclidean geometry proves things about EVERY parallelogram at once — one argument, infinitely many shapes — but it needs you to spot the right construction and quote the right theorem, and if you cannot see it, you are stuck with a blank page. Analytical geometry proves things about ONE specific figure only, but it never requires inspiration: you substitute into three formulae and the answer arrives whether you felt clever that morning or not. That reliability is why it carries so many marks in the paper. There is a second reason, and it matters more later. Coordinates turn geometry into algebra, which means geometric questions become solvable by equation-handling — and that idea grows into the equation of a circle and of a straight line in Grade 11, and into calculus in matric, where a gradient becomes the rate at which something changes. So today's rise over run is not a small trick for classifying quadrilaterals; it is the first appearance of the most useful number in senior mathematics. Learn it properly now and you are paying yourself forward for three years.
