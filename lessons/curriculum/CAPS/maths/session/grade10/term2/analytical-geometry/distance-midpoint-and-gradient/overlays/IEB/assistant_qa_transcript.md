**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we wrap up! The one that came up over and over: the three formulae all use the same letters, so how do we stop grabbing the wrong one under pressure?

**Tutor:** By noticing what they share instead of memorising them separately. Every one of the three begins by comparing the two points: how far apart the x-values are, and how far apart the y-values are. For A at negative one, three, and B at five, eleven, that pair is six across and eight up, and it gets reused constantly. From there, each formula is just a different instruction for that pair. Distance squares both, adds, and square-roots, because it is Pythagoras: thirty-six plus sixty-four is one hundred, square root ten. Gradient divides the up by the across: eight over six, which is four over three. Midpoint is the one exception — it never takes a difference at all. It averages: add the x-values and halve, add the y-values and halve, giving two, seven. So the whole survival kit is one sentence: middle adds, distance and steepness subtract. And there is a built-in alarm — if you subtract for a midpoint by mistake, your answer lands outside the two points, which is impossible for a middle, and that impossibility tells you to go back.

***

**Thandi:** Here's one that started a real debate. Half the class says a vertical line has gradient zero because it never moves across. The other half says undefined. Settle it for us — and does the difference actually matter?

**Tutor:** It matters, and the undefined camp wins. Run it through the formula. Gradient is the change in y over the change in x. On a horizontal line — picture a netball court — the y-values never change, so the top of the fraction is zero, and zero divided by any nonzero number is zero. Horizontal really is zero. On a vertical line — picture a wall — both points share the same x-value, so it is the BOTTOM of the fraction that is zero. And division by zero is not a very big number or a very small one; it is not a number at all, because no number times zero can give a nonzero answer. So the gradient does not exist, and we say undefined. The confusion comes from a zero appearing in both cases; the cure is to ask which zero. Zero on top: flat. Zero underneath: vertical. There is a consequence worth knowing too — because a vertical line has no gradient, the product-equals-negative-one test cannot be applied to a vertical paired with a horizontal, even though they are obviously perpendicular. That one pair you justify by inspection, and that justification is accepted.

**Thandi:** A confession came in from the practice questions. The four points made a shape that looked exactly like a rectangle, so someone wrote rectangle and lost most of the marks. What was the right move?

**Tutor:** Treat the sketch as a rumour and the calculation as the testimony — that habit is the heart of this topic. Our vertices were A negative one, three; B five, eleven; C ten, eight; D four, zero. Plot them and the corners do look square. But a rectangle requires a right angle, and on the grid the one and only proof of a right angle is two gradients multiplying to negative one. Gradient AB is four over three. Gradient AD is negative three over five. Their product is negative four over five — close enough to negative one to fool the eye, which is precisely why the eye cannot be trusted, but not equal to it. So angle A is not ninety degrees and the shape is not a rectangle. What the numbers do prove, three separate ways, is a parallelogram: opposite sides equal, opposite sides parallel, and diagonals sharing the midpoint four comma five, five comma five. The safe answer states exactly what was proved and nothing beyond it: a parallelogram, and nothing more. Always ask what you have PROVED, never what you have SEEN.

***

**Thandi:** Last one, and it's a big-picture question. We already proved things about parallelograms in Euclidean geometry with theorems. Why learn a second, number-crunching way of doing the same job?

**Tutor:** Because the two methods fail in different places, and owning both makes you very hard to stop. A Euclidean proof is general — one argument covers every parallelogram that ever existed — but it demands insight: you must see the construction and recall the right theorem, and when the insight does not come, you are stuck. The coordinate method proves things about one specific figure only, but it asks for no inspiration whatsoever: substitute into three formulae and the truth arrives, whether or not you are having a good day. That reliability is worth a great deal in an exam. And there is a longer game. Coordinates convert geometry into algebra, so geometric questions become equation questions — and that idea grows straight into the equation of a line and of a circle in grade eleven, and then into calculus in grade twelve, where the gradient becomes the rate at which anything changes: speed, growth, cooling, cost. Today's rise over run is not a small trick. It is your first meeting with the most useful number in senior mathematics, so make friends with it properly now.
