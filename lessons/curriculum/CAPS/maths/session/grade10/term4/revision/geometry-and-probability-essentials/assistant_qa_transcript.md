**Thandi:** Let's take the questions this material always raises. First: quadrilateral proofs. People either prove too little and lose the conclusion, or prove everything and lose precious time. Where exactly is the line?

**Tutor:** The line is the minimum test, and it is worth learning as a short shopping list. For a parallelogram, any ONE of these closes the proof: both pairs of opposite sides parallel; both pairs of opposite sides equal; one pair of opposite sides both equal and parallel — the workhorse; or diagonals bisecting each other — the fastest on a coordinate grid, because it is two midpoint calculations. What does NOT close it: equal diagonals alone — an isosceles trapezium has those too — or one pair of sides merely equal while a different pair is parallel. Then the upgrades, each a single extra fact on top of a proven parallelogram: one right angle, or equal diagonals, buys rectangle; one pair of adjacent sides equal buys rhombus; and both upgrades together buy the square. The discipline runs in both directions. Proving less than the ticket leaves the conclusion unpurchased, and the marker cannot award what the logic never earned. Proving more than the ticket is legal but expensive — the marks were complete at the ticket, and November pays nothing for overtime. So before writing, name the target shape, name its cheapest ticket, and prove exactly that.

**Thandi:** Next: in analytical geometry, the formulas are given, yet marks still leak. What are the leak points?

**Tutor:** Three leaks, all mechanical and all sealable. Leak one: subtraction order inside the gradient. Change in y over change in x — and BOTH differences must run in the same direction, B minus A on top and B minus A below. Mixing directions flips the sign, and a sign flip turns a correct perpendicularity argument into nonsense two lines later. Leak two: the gradient inverted as run over rise. Anchor it with steepness sense: from one semicolon two to seven semicolon ten, the climb is eight for a run of six — steeper than one, gradient four thirds, and a quick mental picture of the two points confirms uphill and steep. If your number says gentle while your sketch says steep, the fraction is upside down. Leak three: distance without the square root — thirty-six plus sixty-four is one hundred, and stopping there hands in a squared distance. The seal for all three is the same: a quick rough sketch of the points before formula work. The sketch predicts the sign of the gradient, roughly where the midpoint lands, and roughly how long the distance should be — and any formula answer that contradicts the sketch gets caught in the room, not in the marking centre.

**Thandi:** The Venn diagram question: people fill the circles with the given numbers and everything sums wrong. What is the correct filling order and why does it matter so much?

**Tutor:** Overlap first, always, and here is the reason: the numbers a question gives are usually TOTALS for each circle, not the exclusive regions, and a circle's total already contains the overlap. Write twenty-five into the mathematics-only region and the ten overlap learners are now counted twice before any rule is applied — the diagram is corrupted at birth. The correct order rebuilds the counts from the inside out: place the ten who take both in the overlap; then mathematics-only is twenty-five minus ten, fifteen; science-only is eighteen minus ten, eight; and the outside region is the class total minus everything placed — forty minus thirty-three, seven. Now every learner sits in exactly one region, which is the entire point of the diagram: regions are exclusive, circles are not. And the finished picture self-audits: fifteen plus ten plus eight plus seven must return the class of forty, and a total like forty-three is not a rounding quirk — it is the diagram announcing a double count. The addition rule is the same medicine in formula form: add the two circle totals, subtract the overlap once, because addition counted it twice. Draw first, fill from the middle, audit the total: three habits, zero double counts.

**Thandi:** Last one: how should the statistics revision be structured so it holds up under exam pressure — what genuinely moves marks there?

**Tutor:** Statistics marks are procedural, so revise it as procedure. First, install the opening move as a reflex: order the data before anything else. Every quartile, median and box plot is positional, and a single out-of-order value poisons all of them — markers see this cost full questions, not just single marks. Second, drill the five-number pipeline until it runs without thought: minimum, lower quartile, median, upper quartile, maximum, in increasing order — and if your five numbers do not increase, something upstream broke; that built-in check is free. Third, practise the interpretation sentences, because grade ten statistics increasingly pays for words: which average and why — median when extremes drag, mean when the data is balanced; what the box says — where the middle half lives, which whisker stretches, which direction the data leans; and what a mean above the median implies. Write those sentences out in practice, complete and short, so they arrive pre-built in the exam. And across the whole paper, keep the totals audit running: Venn regions to the class size, five numbers increasing, probabilities between nought and one. Data work checks itself arithmetic-for-free — the candidates who collect those free checks are the ones whose answers survive marking.
