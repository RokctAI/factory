**Thandi:** Revision brings out the same worries every year, so let's meet them head on. First: patterns feel easy until the backwards question — is such-and-such a number in the pattern? — and then people freeze. What is the reliable route?

**Tutor:** The reliable route is to stop treating it as a new kind of question, because it is the same formula driven in reverse. Forwards, you feed the formula a position n and it returns the term. Backwards, you feed it the term and solve for the position: set three n plus two equal to three hundred and two, solve, n equals one hundred. Now the single decision that carries the marks: is that n a natural number? Position one hundred exists, so three hundred and two is in the pattern, and you can even say which term it is. But set the formula equal to one hundred and n comes out as ninety-eight over three — and there is no term ninety-eight thirds of the way along a sequence. A fractional or negative n is not a computational failure; it IS the answer: the number does not belong to the pattern, and writing that sentence earns the mark. The freeze happens when learners expect a yes and treat the fraction as their own mistake. Expect either outcome, let n testify, and the backwards question becomes two lines of algebra plus one sentence of verdict.

**Thandi:** Next: simple versus compound. The formulas are on the formula sheet, so nobody should lose marks here — and yet everybody does. Where does it actually go wrong?

**Tutor:** It goes wrong at the reading stage, not the calculating stage. The formula sheet cannot tell you WHICH formula the story wants — that decision lives in the wording, and the wording has reliable signal words. Interest calculated on the original amount, hire purchase, a flat rate per year: simple, the ladder, A equals P times one plus i n. Compounded, inflation, interest added to the total: compound, the snowball, A equals P times one plus i to the power n. Then two mechanical traps. The rate must enter as a decimal — eight percent is nought comma zero eight, and writing eight into the bracket wrecks everything silently. And hire purchase has its own rhythm: deposit off first, simple interest on the remaining balance for the FULL term even though you pay monthly, then divide the grown balance by the number of months. Six thousand rand television: six hundred deposit, five thousand four hundred grows to six thousand six hundred and ninety-six over two years, and twenty-four equal payments of two hundred and seventy-nine rand. Learners who lose marks here almost never misuse the formula — they pick the wrong one, or feed it a percentage dressed as a whole number.

**Thandi:** The two heights on a cone or pyramid — perpendicular and slant. Is there a way to stop mixing them up under pressure?

**Tutor:** Anchor each height to its job with one physical picture. Volume asks how much the solid holds, and holding is vertical stacking — so volume uses the inside, perpendicular height, the height a plumb line would measure. Surface area asks what the solid wears, and the triangular faces of a cone or pyramid lean against the solid — so the wrapping uses the slant height, the distance a droplet would travel sliding down the face. Inside height fills; leaning height wraps. Then remember the two are connected, not interchangeable: the perpendicular height, the base radius or half-base, and the slant height form a right-angled triangle, so Pythagoras converts between them. Radius three and inside height four force a slant of five — root of nine plus sixteen. Exam questions exploit exactly this: they hand you the height the formula does not want and watch who converts. So before substituting, ask which job am I doing — filling or wrapping — pick that height, and if it is missing, build it with Pythagoras. The triangle is always there, drawn or not.

***

**Thandi:** Last one: any advice for structuring the revision of these three topics in the final weeks — what genuinely moves marks?

**Tutor:** Three moves, in payoff order. First, revise by classifying before computing. Take a mixed set of past-paper questions and, before solving anything, label each one out loud: staircase, ladder, snowball, catalogue, scale factor. The topics themselves are short; the exam skill is recognising which engine a dressed-up question wants, and that reflex only builds through mixed practice — never through redoing one topic's exercises in a block. Second, drill the four traps by name: the fractional n read as a yes, the percentage fed in undecimalised, the slant height in a volume, and the scale factor applied once where the cube belongs. Write them on one page with one example each; most lost marks in these topics are those four wearing different numbers. Third, end every solution with its built-in check — substitute a known term into your formula, recompute one year of interest by hand, compare a cone's volume against a third of its cylinder. Each check costs seconds and converts an uncertain answer into a certain one, and that certainty compounds through the paper faster than any interest rate in it.
