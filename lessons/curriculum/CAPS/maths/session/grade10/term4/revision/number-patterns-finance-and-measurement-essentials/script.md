# Part 1 — Expert

This session is the revision sweep for three topics the other revision lessons leave untouched — number patterns, finance, and measurement. Each one runs on a tiny engine: patterns on one constant difference, finance on two growth formulas, measurement on a short catalogue of solids and one scale-factor law. Nothing here is new; everything here is the exam-day version — compressed, worked through the classic examples, and wired to the traps the markers see every single year.

## Subtopic: Linear Patterns and the General Term

A linear number pattern announces itself by one signature: a constant difference between consecutive terms. Take five; eight; eleven; fourteen. The gaps are three, three, three — constant, so the pattern is linear, and the constant difference is the whole personality of the sequence.

The general term follows in two moves. The coefficient of n is the constant difference itself, so the formula starts as three n plus something. To find the something, test the first term: when n is one, three times one is three, and the pattern says five — so add two. The general term: T n equals three n plus two. Check it against a term you did not use: n equals four gives twelve plus two, fourteen. Correct — and that check is not optional politeness; it is the tiny proof that saves the whole question.

Now the two exam directions. Direction one, forwards: find term fifty. Substitute: three times fifty plus two — one hundred and fifty-two. No listing, no counting on fingers; the formula is a teleport. Direction two, backwards: is three hundred and two a term of this pattern? Set three n plus two equal to three hundred and two: three n equals three hundred, n equals one hundred. A natural number — so yes, it is term one hundred. But ask the same about one hundred: three n plus two equals one hundred gives three n equals ninety-eight, and n is ninety-eight over three — not a natural number, so one hundred is NOT in the pattern. The position n must always land on a natural number; a fraction is the pattern's way of saying no.

One more for fluency: seven; twelve; seventeen. Difference five, so T n equals five n plus two, and term twenty is one hundred and two.

This section's questions are with you now — build the formula from the difference, and test it before you trust it.

## Subtopic: Finance — Growth, Hire Purchase and Exchange Rates

Two formulas run all of grade ten finance. Simple growth: A equals P, times, one plus i n — interest calculated on the original amount only, the same flat payment of interest every year. Compound growth: A equals P, times, one plus i, all to the power n — interest calculated on the growing total, so the interest itself earns interest.

Watch them race. Two thousand rand at eight percent for five years. Simple: two thousand times, one plus nought comma four — two thousand eight hundred rand. Compound: two thousand times one comma zero eight to the fifth — two thousand nine hundred and thirty-eight rand and sixty-six cents. Same deposit, same rate, same years — the snowball beats the flat ladder, and the gap widens every extra year.

Hire purchase is simple interest wearing shop clothes. A television costs six thousand rand. Deposit: ten percent, six hundred rand. The balance, five thousand four hundred, is charged SIMPLE interest at twelve percent for the full two years: five thousand four hundred times, one plus nought comma two four — six thousand six hundred and ninety-six rand. Monthly payment: divide by twenty-four months — two hundred and seventy-nine rand per month. The trap: hire purchase is always simple interest, always on the balance after deposit, and the interest runs over the whole term even while you are paying it down.

Inflation is the compound formula with a costume change: prices grow by the inflation rate. An item at one hundred rand with six percent inflation costs, in three years, one hundred times one comma zero six cubed — one hundred and nineteen rand and ten cents. Same machine, different story.

Exchange rates: the rate is the price of one unit of the other currency. If one dollar costs eighteen rand fifty, then two hundred dollars cost three thousand seven hundred rand. When the rand weakens, that price per dollar rises and imports hurt; when the rand strengthens, the price falls. Read the rate as a price tag and the direction sorts itself out.

Take this section's questions now — pick the formula by the story, and let the snowball and the ladder stay separate.

## Subtopic: Measurement — the Volume and Surface Area Catalogue

Measurement is a catalogue question: identify the solid, fetch its two formulas, substitute cleanly. The catalogue, with one worked example each.

The rectangular prism: volume is length times breadth times height — four by three by five gives sixty cubic units. Surface area: two times, the sum of the three different faces — two times, twelve plus twenty plus fifteen — ninety-four square units.

The cylinder: volume is pi r squared h — a circle of area pi r squared, stacked h high. Radius three, height ten: ninety pi, about two hundred and eighty-two comma seven four cubic units. Closed surface area: two pi r squared for the lids plus two pi r h for the label — together two pi r, times, r plus h: seventy-eight pi, about two hundred and forty-five comma zero four.

The sphere: volume four thirds pi r cubed. Radius six: four thirds of pi times two hundred and sixteen — two hundred and eighty-eight pi, about nine hundred and four comma seven eight. Surface area: four pi r squared.

The cone: one third of its matching cylinder — volume one third pi r squared h. Radius three, height four: twelve pi, about thirty-seven comma seven zero. Its surface needs the SLANT height: by Pythagoras, the slant is the square root of nine plus sixteen — five. Total surface: pi r, times, r plus slant — pi times three times eight, twenty-four pi, about seventy-five comma four zero.

The pyramid: also one third — volume one third, base area, times perpendicular height. Square base of side six, height four: one third of thirty-six times four — forty-eight. Surface area: the base plus the triangular faces, and the triangles use the slant height five: thirty-six plus four triangles of a half times six times five — thirty-six plus sixty, ninety-six.

Two catalogue rules that harvest marks. Perpendicular height in the volume, slant height on the surface — mixing them is the classic cone-and-pyramid error. And the one-third belongs to cones and pyramids only; prisms and cylinders are full-strength.

This section's questions are ready — name the solid, fetch its pair of formulas, and keep the two heights apart.

## Subtopic: The Scale Factor k and the Integrated Question

Multiply every length of a solid by k, and the three kinds of measurement respond in ranks: lengths multiply by k, areas by k squared, volumes by k cubed. One dimension each per power — that is the entire law.

Feel its violence with k equals three. A solid of volume fifty becomes fifty times twenty-seven — one thousand three hundred and fifty. Not three times bigger: twenty-seven times bigger. And the reverse trap: doubling every side of a box does not double the volume; it multiplies it by eight.

Partial scaling is the examiner's favourite twist. Double ONLY the radius of a cylinder: volume is pi r squared h, and the radius appears squared, so the volume multiplies by four — not eight, because the height never moved. The law reads letter by letter: each scaled length contributes its own factor, as many times as it appears in the formula.

Now the integrated question, where the paper chains topics. A cylindrical tank, radius three metres, height ten metres, holds ninety pi — about two hundred and eighty-two comma seven four cubic metres. Its price rises with inflation; its build cost compares across a currency; a stack of such tanks forms a pattern in the total volume. One question, three topics — and each topic keeps its own engine: the difference for patterns, the two growth formulas for money, the catalogue for solids.

The revision error museum, four exhibits. Exhibit one: reading a non-natural n as a yes — a fractional position means NOT in the pattern. Exhibit two: compound interest computed as simple, the snowball flattened into the ladder. Exhibit three: slant height in a volume formula. Exhibit four: doubling lengths and doubling the volume — forgetting that volume answers with the cube. Each exhibit is a mark the markers collect every November — from everyone except the candidates who rehearsed them.

The final questions of this part are with you now — powers matched to dimensions, and all four exhibits dodged.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same revision as three small machines — a staircase, two ladders, and a box factory — with the levers that make each one run.

## Subtopic: Patterns as Staircases

Picture a linear pattern as a staircase where every step has the same height. Five; eight; eleven; fourteen — a staircase with steps of height three. The constant step IS the pattern; everything else is bookkeeping.

The general term is just a description of the staircase: to stand on step n, start from the ground and climb n steps of three. Where is the ground? One step below the first term: five minus three is two. So the height at step n is three n plus two — the ground floor plus n climbs. That is why the formula test always works: step one should be three plus two, five. It is.

Forwards questions ride the formula up: step fifty is at height one hundred and fifty-two, no climbing required. Backwards questions ask which step sits at a given height: set the formula equal to the height and solve for n. Height three hundred and two: n is one hundred — step one hundred exists, so yes. Height one hundred: n comes out as a fraction — and a staircase has no step ninety-eight thirds. Fractional step number means the height is between steps: not in the pattern.

Every linear pattern question is one of those three: find the step height, ride up, or check whether a height lands on a step. Name which one you are in before you compute.

Your questions for this section are ready — find the step, find the ground, then ride the formula.

## Subtopic: Money on Two Ladders

Simple and compound growth are two ways to climb. Simple interest is a ladder with equal rungs: every year adds the same amount, because the interest is always calculated on the original deposit. Compound interest is a snowball rolling downhill: every year's interest joins the ball, and next year's interest grows on the bigger ball.

Two thousand rand, eight percent, five years. The ladder adds one hundred and sixty rand each year — five rungs, eight hundred rand, finishing at two thousand eight hundred. The snowball finishes at two thousand nine hundred and thirty-eight rand and sixty-six cents — ahead by more than a hundred rand, and pulling further ahead every year after. Short story: same rate, different machines.

Hire purchase is the ladder with a shop sign on it. Deposit first — ten percent of the six thousand rand television is six hundred. The remaining five thousand four hundred climbs the SIMPLE ladder at twelve percent for the whole two years, reaching six thousand six hundred and ninety-six, and the monthly payment is that total shared across twenty-four months: two hundred and seventy-nine rand. The formula never changes; only the story around it does.

Inflation is the snowball working against you: prices ride the compound formula uphill, so one hundred rand of groceries becomes one hundred and nineteen rand and ten cents after three years at six percent. And an exchange rate is nothing but a price tag on one unit of foreign money: one dollar costing eighteen rand fifty means two hundred dollars cost three thousand seven hundred rand — multiply price by quantity, like buying apples.

The reading skill: the words interest on the original amount, hire purchase, or straight growth name the ladder; the words compounded, inflation, or interest on the new total name the snowball. Choose the machine before touching the numbers.

Here are this section's questions — ladder or snowball first, arithmetic second.

## Subtopic: Boxes, Tins and the Final Sweep

Every volume in the catalogue is one idea: area of the base, times how high you stack it. A prism stacks a rectangle; a cylinder stacks a circle — pi r squared, stacked h high. Cones and pyramids are their pointed cousins, and pointed costs you two thirds: each holds exactly one third of the box or tin it fits inside. The sphere stands alone with four thirds pi r cubed.

Surface area is a different question entirely: not what it holds, but what it wears. Unfold the solid and add the faces — two lids and a label for the tin, a base plus triangles for the pyramid. And the triangles lean, so they use the SLANT height, while volume always uses the vertical, perpendicular height. Two heights, two jobs: the inside height fills, the leaning height wraps.

Then the scale factor, the one law that multiplies everything: lengths by k, areas by k squared, volumes by k cubed — one factor of k per dimension. A box doubled in every direction holds eight times as much; a photo enlarged three times covers nine times the paper. And when only ONE length changes, count how many times that letter appears in the formula: double a cylinder's radius and the volume quadruples, because r appears squared and h stayed home.

The final sweep — four traps, four antidotes. A fractional position n: the number is not in the pattern; say so and move on. Ladder arithmetic in a snowball question: circle the word compounded before you start. Slant height in a volume: whisper inside height fills, leaning height wraps. And a scale factor applied once to a volume: match the power to the dimension — square for area, cube for volume. Then the last habit that turns near-marks into marks: test the general term on a known term, recompute one interest line, and sense-check every volume against the box it fits inside. Every answer carries its own free proof.

The final questions of the lesson are with you now — stack the base, wrap the faces, and give every power its dimension.
