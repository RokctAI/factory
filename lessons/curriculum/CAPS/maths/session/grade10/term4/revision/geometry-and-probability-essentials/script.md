# Part 1 — Expert

This session is the revision sweep for the geometry and data half of the year — quadrilaterals and the midpoint theorem, analytical geometry, probability, and statistics. Four topics, four small toolkits: a family tree of shapes with their defining properties, three coordinate formulas, one addition rule that stops double counting, and a five-number portrait of any data set. Nothing here is new; everything here is the exam-day version — compressed, worked through the classic examples, and aimed straight at the traps that harvest marks every November.

## Subtopic: Quadrilaterals and the Midpoint Theorem

The quadrilaterals form a family tree, and each generation ADDS a property without losing the ones before. The parallelogram is the founder: both pairs of opposite sides parallel and equal, both pairs of opposite angles equal, and the diagonals bisect each other. The rectangle inherits everything and adds right angles — which forces its diagonals equal. The rhombus inherits everything and adds four equal sides — which forces its diagonals perpendicular, and makes them bisect the corner angles. The square sits at the top, inheriting from both: it is a rectangle and a rhombus at once. Off the main line live the trapezium, with just one pair of parallel sides, and the kite, with two pairs of adjacent equal sides and diagonals that cross at right angles.

The exam skill is choosing the MINIMUM test. To prove a parallelogram, one pair of opposite sides both equal AND parallel is enough — or diagonals that bisect each other. To upgrade a parallelogram to a rectangle, one right angle suffices, or equal diagonals. To upgrade to a rhombus, one pair of adjacent sides equal. Prove the cheapest property; the family tree delivers the rest free.

Then the midpoint theorem, the year's most elegant shortcut: the segment joining the midpoints of two sides of a triangle is parallel to the third side and equal to HALF of it. Third side twelve — midpoint segment six, and parallel, no measuring needed. The converse works too: a line through one midpoint, parallel to another side, must pass through the third side's midpoint. Spot the word midpoint twice in a triangle question, and the theorem is almost certainly the intended key.

This section's questions are with you now — cheapest test first, and let the family tree pay out the rest.

## Subtopic: Analytical Geometry — Distance, Midpoint and Gradient

Three formulas run all of grade ten analytical geometry, and each answers one question about two points. Take A at one semicolon two, and B at seven semicolon ten.

How far apart? Distance: the square root of, the difference in x squared plus the difference in y squared. Differences: six and eight. Root of thirty-six plus sixty-four — root one hundred — ten. Pythagoras in coordinate clothing: the differences are the legs, the distance is the hypotenuse.

Where is the middle? Midpoint: average the x's, average the y's. Half of one plus seven is four; half of two plus ten is six. Midpoint: four semicolon six. Averages, nothing more.

How steep is the line? Gradient: change in y over change in x — eight over six, four thirds. Uphill from left to right when positive, downhill when negative, zero when horizontal — and undefined when vertical, because the run is zero.

Now the two relationships the exam loves. Parallel lines: equal gradients — four thirds matches four thirds, no exceptions. Perpendicular lines: gradients multiply to minus one — the partner of four thirds is minus three quarters. Flip and negate: that is the perpendicular machine.

And the integrated classic: prove that a quadrilateral on the grid is a parallelogram. Fastest route — the diagonals. Compute the midpoint of each diagonal; if the two midpoints coincide, the diagonals bisect each other, and the family tree from the previous section certifies the parallelogram. One formula, used twice, plus one theorem: that is how the topics chain.

Take this section's questions now — three formulas, two relationships, and Pythagoras behind it all.

## Subtopic: Probability — Venn Diagrams and the Addition Rule

Probability begins with one fraction: favourable outcomes over total outcomes, when all outcomes are equally likely. A fair die: the probability of a six is one sixth — about nought comma one seven. That is THEORETICAL probability, computed before anyone throws. RELATIVE FREQUENCY is what actually happened: throw the die three hundred times, count the sixes, divide. The two disagree in small experiments and drift together as the trials grow — that drift towards the theory is the whole point of the comparison.

Now the double-counting problem, the heart of this topic. In a class of forty, twenty-five take mathematics, eighteen take science, ten take both. How many take mathematics or science? Not twenty-five plus eighteen — that counts the ten overlap learners twice. Correct count: twenty-five plus eighteen minus ten — thirty-three. And so seven take neither. The Venn diagram shows it instantly: fill the overlap FIRST with ten, then mathematics-only is fifteen, science-only is eight, and the outside region holds whoever is left.

As a law: P of A or B equals P of A, plus P of B, minus P of A and B. Subtract the overlap once, because addition counted it twice. Two special cases finish the toolkit. Mutually exclusive events cannot happen together — the overlap is zero, and the rule collapses to plain addition. Complementary events split the world in two: P of not A equals one minus P of A. If the probability of rain is nought comma three, the probability of no rain is nought comma seven — and complementary is stronger than mutually exclusive, because the two events must also cover everything.

This section's questions are ready — overlap first in every Venn, and subtract it once in every rule.

## Subtopic: Statistics — Centre, Spread and the Box Plot

A data set answers two questions: where is its centre, and how spread out is it? Take the ordered set: five; seven; eight; ten; twelve; fifteen; twenty — seven values.

Centre, three ways. The mean: total over count — seventy-seven over seven — eleven. The median: the middle value of the ordered list — fourth of seven — ten. The mode: the most frequent value — this set has none, and that is a legitimate answer. The mean uses every value, which is its strength and its weakness: one wild value drags it. The median ignores size and cares only about position, so it shrugs at outliers.

Spread, two levels. The range: maximum minus minimum — fifteen. Crude, because it hears only the two extremes. Better: the quartiles. The lower quartile is the median of the values below the median — of five, seven, eight — seven. The upper quartile: median of twelve, fifteen, twenty — fifteen. The interquartile range: fifteen minus seven — eight. The middle half of the data lives in a corridor eight wide, and no outlier can touch that statement.

The five-number summary lines them up — minimum five, lower quartile seven, median ten, upper quartile fifteen, maximum twenty — and the box-and-whisker plot draws them: a box from seven to fifteen with a line at ten, whiskers reaching to five and twenty. Read skewness from the picture: here the median sits left of the box's centre and the right whisker stretches long, so the data leans right — and the mean at eleven sitting above the median ten says the same thing in numbers.

The revision error museum, four exhibits. Exhibit one: quoting a minimum test that proves nothing — equal diagonals alone certify no parallelogram. Exhibit two: a gradient flipped as run over rise. Exhibit three: the overlap counted twice — forty-three learners in a class of forty. Exhibit four: quartiles taken from an unordered list. Each exhibit is a mark the markers collect every year — from everyone except the candidates who rehearsed them.

The final questions of this part are with you now — order the data, place the five numbers, and let the box tell the story.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same revision as three pictures — a family tree, a counting machine that never double-counts, and a data set drawn as a box — with the habits that make each picture automatic.

## Subtopic: The Quadrilateral Family Tree

Picture the quadrilaterals as a family, where every child keeps all the family traits and adds one of their own. The parallelogram starts the line: opposite sides parallel and equal, opposite angles equal, diagonals cutting each other in half. The rectangle is the parallelogram child who added right angles. The rhombus is the child who added equal sides. The square is the grandchild of both — everything true of rectangles and everything true of rhombuses is true of the square, automatically.

Inheritance is the exam cheat code, in both directions. Downwards: asked for the properties of a square, recite the parallelogram's list, then the rectangle's additions, then the rhombus's — nothing to memorise separately. Upwards: asked to PROVE a shape, prove only the cheapest entry ticket. Diagonals bisecting each other buys parallelogram. One right angle upgrades to rectangle. A pair of adjacent equal sides upgrades to rhombus. Never prove more than the ticket price — every extra property is time spent buying what inheritance gives free.

And the midpoint theorem is a family shortcut for triangles: connect two midpoints and the segment runs parallel to the third side at exactly half its length. Twelve becomes six, guaranteed, no ruler. When a question plants the word midpoint twice, it is showing its cards — the theorem, or its converse, is the door.

Your questions for this section are ready — read the family tree downwards for properties, upwards for proofs.

## Subtopic: Chance as Counting Made Fair

All of probability is careful counting, divided fairly. The probability of an event is the count of ways it can happen over the count of everything that can happen — six faces, one six, one sixth. Before the experiment, that fraction is theory. After the experiment, the results give relative frequency — and the more trials, the closer the experiment leans towards the theory. Twenty throws can look wild; three hundred settle down.

The one place counting goes wrong is the overlap. Count the mathematics learners, count the science learners, add — and every learner taking both has been counted twice, once in each list. The fix is a single subtraction: add the two counts, subtract the overlap once. Twenty-five plus eighteen minus ten: thirty-three, in a class of forty, leaving seven outside. The Venn diagram is that arithmetic drawn as circles, and its golden rule is: fill the overlap FIRST, then work outwards, and let the outside region catch whoever remains.

Two special layouts complete the picture. Circles that cannot overlap — an event and its rival that never happen together — are mutually exclusive, and their probabilities add cleanly. Circles that split the whole rectangle into inside and outside — an event and its opposite — are complementary, and the outside is one minus the inside: rain at nought comma three means no rain at nought comma seven. Complementary is the stronger claim: no overlap AND nothing left over.

Here are this section's questions — count, subtract the overlap once, and let the outside catch the rest.

## Subtopic: Data Stories and the Final Sweep

Every data set is a story, and the five-number summary is its plot: where it starts, where the middle half begins, the midpoint, where the middle half ends, where it ends. The box plot draws that plot in one line — box for the middle half, whiskers for the tails — so two data sets can be compared at a glance: whose box sits higher, whose is wider, whose whisker stretches suspiciously far.

Choosing the right average is part of the storytelling. The mean listens to every value — democratic, but one millionaire in the room drags the average salary into fiction. The median stands in the middle and ignores how extreme the extremes are — the honest centre for skewed data. When the mean drifts above the median, the story leans right; below, it leans left. Say which average you chose and why: that sentence is often a mark.

The order of operations for any statistics question: ORDER the data first — every quartile computed on an unordered list is wrong before it starts. Then locate the median, then the quartile of each half, then draw. The interquartile range — upper minus lower quartile — measures the corridor where the middle half lives, immune to outliers at either end.

The final sweep — four traps, four antidotes. Proving too much in a quadrilateral question: pay the ticket price, let inheritance do the rest. Rise and run inverted: gradient is change in y OVER change in x, always. The overlap added twice: subtract it once, and sanity-check the total against the class size — forty-three out of forty is the question marking itself wrong. Quartiles from an unordered list: order first, always. And the last habit that converts near-marks into marks: check totals — Venn regions must sum to the class, the five numbers must run in increasing order, and a midpoint must land between its endpoints. Every picture in this lesson checks itself, free.

The final questions of the lesson are with you now — order the data, count without double-counting, and let every total confess.
