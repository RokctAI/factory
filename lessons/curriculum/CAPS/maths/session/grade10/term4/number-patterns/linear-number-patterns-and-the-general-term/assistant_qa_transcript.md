**Thandi:** Let's take the questions that come up most with this topic. First: why build a formula at all? Adding three again and again feels easier than algebra, and for the first few terms it genuinely is.

**Tutor:** For the first few terms, agreed — counting up wins. The formula earns its keep the moment the question jumps far ahead, and exam questions always jump far ahead. Term fifty by counting means forty-nine additions, each one a chance to slip, with no way to check yourself halfway. Term fifty by formula is one substitution: three times fifty plus two, one hundred and fifty-two, done and checkable. But speed is only half the argument. The formula also unlocks question types that counting cannot touch: which position holds ninety-two demands solving an equation, and proving one hundred is never in the sequence cannot be done by counting at all — you would count forever and still not have a proof. The general term converts an infinite list into one line of algebra that answers questions about any position, forwards or backwards. That is why the first sub-question of every pattern problem asks for T n: every later mark flows through it.

***

**Thandi:** Next: the correction term trips people constantly. Some write d n plus the first term and it works for a moment, then collapses. Why is the correction first term minus d, and not just the first term?

**Tutor:** Test the tempting version and let it fail honestly. Suppose T n equals three n plus five for the sequence five, eight, eleven. Substitute n equals one: three plus five is eight — but the first term is five, not eight. The formula overshoots by exactly three, and that overshoot is the clue. The term d times n has already taken one step of size d by the time it reaches position one: three times one is three, not zero. Whatever you add must top up from three to the actual first term, so it must be five minus three, which is two. In general the jacket is first term minus d, because d n spots you one d before you even start. There is a picture that makes it permanent: the sequence is the d times table shifted, and the shift is measured from the FIRST multiple, d itself, up to the first term. And the protection against ever getting it wrong is the verification habit: substitute n equals one the moment the formula is written. Five seconds, and the formula either proves itself or confesses.

***

**Thandi:** A membership question puzzle: when we test whether one hundred is in the sequence and n comes out as a fraction, some people feel they made an algebra mistake and start over. How should we read that fraction?

**Tutor:** Read it as the answer itself, fully formed. The equation three n plus two equals one hundred asks a precise question: at what position would one hundred sit? The algebra answers honestly: at position ninety-eight thirds, about thirty-two comma seven. But the sequence only has whole-numbered positions — there is a thirty-second term and a thirty-third term and absolutely nothing between them, because positions count places in a queue. So the fraction is the proof that no position holds one hundred. Nothing went wrong; the algebra did exactly its job, and the verdict is the sentence the memo wants: one hundred is not a term of the sequence, since n equals ninety-eight thirds is not a natural number. Contrast the two outcomes so the reading becomes automatic. Whole n, like n equals thirty for ninety-two: member, and the n tells you where. Broken n: outsider, and the brokenness is the reason. The instinct to distrust a non-whole answer is good algebra hygiene everywhere else — this is the one question type where the untidy answer is the point.

***

**Thandi:** Last one: in the picture problems, people miscount the matchsticks and then every mark afterwards dies. Is there a defence?

**Tutor:** Two defences, used together. First: count three figures, physically and carefully, before writing any sequence. The classic traps are shared parts — the matchstick wall two squares have in common, the seats swallowed where two tables press together. Figure two is where sharing first appears, so a count of figures one and two alone can still miss the true rhythm; the third figure confirms it. Second defence: find the difference in the STRUCTURE, not just the numbers. Ask what physically arrives with each new figure: one new square brings three matches because it borrows a wall; one new table brings two seats because the join eats two. When the physical count of what each step adds matches the numerical gap in your sequence, the pattern is confirmed twice over, and you can build T n with confidence. As a final safety net, the formula must reproduce figure one exactly — three times one plus one is four matches, correct. If a formula disagrees with the very first picture, the miscount happened at the start, and better to discover that before term twenty than after.
