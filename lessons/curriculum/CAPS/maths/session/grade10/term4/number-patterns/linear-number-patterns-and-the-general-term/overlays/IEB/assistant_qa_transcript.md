**Thandi:** Time for the questions this topic always produces. First one: why bother with a formula when you can just keep adding five? For the early terms, adding honestly feels faster.

**Tutor:** For the early terms it genuinely is faster — nobody builds machinery to reach term four. The formula pays for itself the moment the question leaps, and pattern questions always leap. Term sixty by repeated adding means fifty-nine additions with no checkpoint anywhere; one slip at step twenty and every number after it is quietly wrong. Term sixty by formula is five times sixty minus one, two hundred and ninety-nine, done — and verifiable in seconds. But reach is only half the story. Some questions cannot be answered by adding at all. Which position holds one hundred and twenty-four requires solving an equation. And proving one hundred and sixty-three never appears cannot be done by listing — you would list forever and still have no proof. The general term compresses an infinite list into one line of algebra that answers questions in both directions. That is why the first sub-question of every pattern problem asks you to find T n: all the later marks flow through it.

***

**Thandi:** Next one: the adjustment term. People happily write d n plus the first term, and it looks so reasonable. Why is the adjustment first term minus d instead?

**Tutor:** Let the wrong version convict itself. Take four, nine, fourteen and try T n equals five n plus four. Substitute n equals one: five plus four is nine — but the first term is four. The formula overshoots by exactly five, and the size of the overshoot is the whole explanation. By the time d n reaches position one it has already taken one full step: five times one is five, not zero. So the top-up must bridge from five to the true first term — four minus five, which is minus one, giving T n equals five n minus one. In general the adjustment is first term minus d, because d n arrives at position one already carrying one d. There is a picture that locks it in: the sequence is the d times table shifted, and the shift is measured from the first multiple — d itself — to the first term. And the permanent safety net is the verification habit: substitute n equals one the instant you write a formula. Five seconds, and it either proves itself or confesses.

***

**Thandi:** Here's the membership puzzle: when the test for one hundred and sixty-three gives n equals thirty-two comma eight, a lot of people panic and redo the algebra. What should that decimal be telling them?

**Tutor:** It should be telling them the case is closed. The equation five n minus one equals one hundred and sixty-three asks one precise thing: at which position would one hundred and sixty-three live? The algebra answers truthfully: at position thirty-two comma eight. But positions count places in a queue — there is a thirty-second term, a thirty-third term, and absolutely nothing between them. So the decimal is a verdict of not present, delivered with proof attached. Nothing went wrong; the algebra did its job perfectly. What remains is to write the verdict in full: one hundred and sixty-three is not a term, since n equals one hundred and sixty-four fifths is not a natural number. Now contrast the two possible endings so the reading becomes reflex. Whole n, like n equals twenty-five for one hundred and twenty-four: member, and n names its position. Broken n: outsider, and the brokenness is the reason you cite. Everywhere else in mathematics an untidy answer suggests a slip worth checking — this is the one question type where the untidy answer is the prize.

***

**Thandi:** Last question: the diagram problems. One miscounted matchstick and the whole answer collapses. Is there a reliable defence against counting the picture wrong?

**Tutor:** Two defences, and they work best together. Defence one: count three figures, slowly, before writing any sequence — because the traps are shared parts, and sharing only begins at figure two. Two triangles share a side, so figure two has five matches, not six; two tables pressed together swallow a seat on each side. A rushed count of the first figure alone can never reveal that. Defence two: find the difference in the structure, not just in the numbers. Ask what physically arrives with each new figure: a new triangle brings two matches because it borrows a side; a new table brings four seats because the join eats two. When the physical account of each step matches the numerical gap in your sequence, the pattern is confirmed twice over. Then the final safety net: the finished formula must reproduce figure one exactly — two times one plus one is three matches, correct. If a formula disagrees with the very first picture, the miscount happened at the start, and it is far cheaper to discover that before figure thirty than after.
