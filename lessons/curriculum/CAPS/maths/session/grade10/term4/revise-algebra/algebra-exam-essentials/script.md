# Part 1 — Expert

This session is the algebra revision sweep for the final examination — the whole of Paper 1's core, reorganised into the four decisions that earn the marks: how to factorise, how to solve each equation type, how to treat an inequality, and how to run the exponent laws. Nothing here is new; everything here is the exam-day version — compressed, ordered, and drilled through worked examples chosen to expose the classic traps.

## Subtopic: The Factorising Decision Tree

Every factorising question yields to the same four questions asked in the same order — the decision tree. Question one, always first: is there a common factor? Take three x squared minus twelve. Both terms divide by three: three, bracket, x squared minus four. Taking the common factor first is not a style choice — the leftover bracket is simpler, and often only becomes factorable after the extraction.

Question two: is the leftover a difference of two squares? X squared minus four is exactly that: x minus two, times x plus two. Full answer: three, times x minus two, times x plus two. Remember the one-way rule here: a DIFFERENCE of squares splits; a sum of squares, like x squared plus four, does not factorise at this level. And the difference of squares pattern hides in bigger clothing: nine x squared minus twenty-five is three x minus five times three x plus five — squares of expressions still count.

Question three: is it a trinomial? For x squared minus five x minus fourteen, hunt two numbers that multiply to minus fourteen and add to minus five: minus seven and plus two. Factors: x minus seven, times x plus two. When the leading coefficient is not one, as in two x squared plus five x minus three, the hunt runs through the factor pairs of the outer product: the answer is two x minus one, times x plus three — and multiplying back out confirms it: two x squared plus six x minus x minus three, which is two x squared plus five x minus three. That multiply-back check is the professional habit: factorising is reversible, so reverse it and be certain.

Question four, for four terms: grouping in pairs. And for the special cubes: x cubed plus twenty-seven is a sum of cubes, x plus three, times, x squared minus three x plus nine — first bracket from the cube roots, second bracket square, flip, square.

The tree in one breath: common factor, difference of squares, trinomial, grouping or cubes. Run it in order, every time, and no factorising question can ambush you.

## Subtopic: Every Equation Type on One Page

Grade 10 owns three equation types, and recognising which one is in front of you is half the solution.

Type one: linear — the variable appears to the first power only. Five x minus seven equals two x plus eight. Gather x on one side, numbers on the other: three x equals fifteen, x equals five. One solution, always.

Type two: quadratic — an x squared term appears. The golden sequence: everything to one side, zero on the other; factorise; set each factor to zero. Solve x squared equals five x plus six. Do not divide by x, do not take square roots — move everything left: x squared minus five x minus six equals zero. Factorise: x minus six, times x plus one. Solutions: x equals six or x equals minus one. Two solutions, and the exam expects both. The trap the memo watches for: dividing both sides of x squared equals five x by x to get x equals five — that division throws away the solution x equals zero. Never divide by the variable; factorise instead: x times x minus five equals zero gives x equals zero or five.

Type three: exponential — the variable lives in the exponent. Three to the power x equals eighty-one. Strategy: force both sides to the same base. Eighty-one is three to the fourth, so x equals four. Dressier version: five times two to the power x equals forty. Isolate the power first: two to the x equals eight, so x equals three. The order matters — divide away the five before touching the exponent, because five times two to the x is not ten to the x. Coefficients and bases never merge.

The recognition drill: see the variable squared — zero, factorise, two answers. See it upstairs in an exponent — same base. See it plain — gather and divide. Classify first, then solve; the classification is where the marks begin.

Take this section's questions now — three types, three strategies, and the two forbidden moves avoided.

## Subtopic: Inequalities — the Flip and the Number Line

An inequality is solved exactly like a linear equation, with one explosive exception: multiplying or dividing both sides by a NEGATIVE number reverses the inequality sign. Minus two x plus six is greater than twelve. Subtract six: minus two x is greater than six. Now divide by minus two, and the sign flips: x is LESS than minus three.

Why the flip? Watch what negation does to order: three is less than five, but minus three is GREATER than minus five — multiplying by a negative mirrors the number line, and every left-right relationship swaps. The flip is not a convention; it is the mirror. Addition and subtraction never flip anything; only negative multiplication and division do.

Second exam skill: the compound inequality, a value fenced between two bounds. Minus three is less than or equal to two x plus one, which is less than seven. Work all three parts at once. Subtract one throughout: minus four is less than or equal to two x, which is less than six. Divide by two throughout: minus two is less than or equal to x, which is less than three.

Third skill: presenting the answer in the demanded format. Interval notation: from minus two to three, square bracket on minus two because it is included, round bracket on three because it is not. Number line: a filled dot at minus two, an open dot at three, shading between. Filled means included, open means excluded — the equal-to part of the sign decides the dot. And if the question restricts x to natural numbers or integers, the answer becomes a list: minus two, minus one, nought, one, two.

Read the sign language precisely: strictly less than excludes; less than or equal to includes. One pixel of a dot, one shape of a bracket — real marks.

This section's questions are ready — one flip defended, one fence solved, and the same answer written in every format.

## Subtopic: Exponents and the Integrated Question

The exponent laws, rapid-fire, with one example each. Same base multiplied: add the exponents — x cubed times x to the fourth is x to the seventh. Same base divided: subtract — six x to the fifth over two x squared is three x cubed; the coefficients divide separately. Power of a power: multiply — two x cubed, all squared, is four x to the sixth; the coefficient gets squared too. Anything nonzero to the power zero: one. Negative exponent: a reciprocal — x to the minus two is one over x squared.

The law-level trap: the laws serve same-base products and quotients only. X cubed plus x to the fourth does not become x to the seventh — addition obeys no exponent law, only like terms combine. Two to the x times three cannot become six to the x.

Now the integrated question — the exam's favourite way to test everything at once. Solve: two to the power, x plus one, equals sixteen. Same-base strategy: sixteen is two to the fourth. So x plus one equals four, and x equals three. Every exponential equation resolves into a smaller equation of a type you already own — the topics chain together, and the chain is the design of the whole paper.

One more chain, factorising inside a fraction. Simplify: x squared minus nine, over, x plus three. The top is a difference of squares: x minus three, times x plus three. Cancel the common bracket with the bottom: x minus three, provided x is not minus three, since the original fraction forbids a zero denominator. Factorising, fractions, restrictions — three topics, one expression.

The revision error museum, four exhibits. Exhibit one: cancelling terms instead of factors — in the fraction x squared minus nine over x plus three, the x squared and the x may not be cancelled; only whole brackets cancel. Exhibit two: the lost solution from dividing by x. Exhibit three: the unflipped inequality sign. Exhibit four: x cubed plus x to the fourth merged into x to the seventh. Each exhibit is a mark the examiners harvest every single year — from everyone except the candidates who rehearsed them.

The final questions of this part are with you now — laws applied, chains followed, and all four exhibits dodged.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same revision as a toolbox check — four drawers, one tool per job, and the labels that tell you instantly which drawer a question lives in.

## Subtopic: Four Drawers in One Toolbox

Picture your algebra as a toolbox with four drawers, and every Paper 1 question as a job that names its own drawer.

Drawer one: factorising — the take-apart tools. The job: rewrite an expression as things multiplied. The drawer has an order printed on the inside of the lid: common factor first, always; then check for a difference of squares; then the trinomial hunt for two numbers; then grouping or cubes for four terms and cube patterns. The order exists because each tool clears the way for the next — pulling out the common factor three from three x squared minus twelve reveals the difference of squares x squared minus four hiding underneath.

Drawer two: equations — the solving tools, one per species. Plain x: gather and divide. X squared: everything to zero, factorise, both answers. X in the exponent: same base on both sides.

Drawer three: inequalities — the same tools as linear equations, plus one red warning sticker: dividing or multiplying by a negative flips the sign.

Drawer four: exponents — the five laws, used only on products, quotients and powers, never on sums.

The skill being tested in November is not any single drawer — it is choosing the right drawer fast. So label-read every question before working: the word factorise names drawer one; the word solve with an equals sign names drawer two; an inequality sign names drawer three; powers everywhere name drawer four. Say the drawer out loud, then open it.

Your questions for this section are ready — name the drawer before touching the tool.

## Subtopic: Solving Equations Like Untying Knots

Every equation is a knot: x is tangled up with numbers, and solving means untying — undoing each operation in reverse, like taking off shoes before socks never works, so socks came off last and go on first.

The linear knot: five x minus seven equals two x plus eight. X appears on both sides, so first bring the family together: take two x from both sides, three x minus seven equals eight. Then undo the minus seven: three x equals fifteen. Then undo the times three: x equals five. Each move undoes one operation, and each move applies to BOTH sides — an equation is a balanced scale, and whatever one side receives, the other must too.

The quadratic knot needs a different first move, because x squared and x cannot be gathered into one pile. Instead: sweep everything to one side, leave zero on the other, and factorise. X squared minus five x minus six equals zero becomes x minus six, times x plus one, equals zero. Now the beautiful step, the zero-product idea: two things multiply to zero only when at least one of them IS zero. No other number has that power — things multiply to twelve in endless ways, but to zero only through a zero. So x minus six is zero, or x plus one is zero: x equals six or minus one. Both answers, always — a quadratic question expects two.

The exponential knot: two to the x equals eight. Rewrite eight as two cubed, and the equation reads: two to the x equals two to the three. Same base, same result — so the exponents must match: x equals three. If a coefficient blocks the view, like five times two to the x equals forty, clear it first by division, then match bases.

Here are this section's questions — untie in reverse order, keep the scale balanced, and let zero do its magic.

## Subtopic: The Final Sweep — Traps and the Last Check

Revision is mostly about the traps, because in November the mathematics is familiar but the traps are fresh. Here are the four that harvest the most marks, each with its antidote.

Trap one: cancelling into a sum. In the fraction x squared minus nine over x plus three, the urge is to cancel the x's and the numbers separately. Forbidden — cancelling is dividing, and you can only divide out a factor of the WHOLE top and the WHOLE bottom. Antidote: factorise first. The top becomes x minus three times x plus three, and now the whole bracket x plus three cancels legally, leaving x minus three.

Trap two: dividing an equation by x. X squared equals five x, divided by x, gives x equals five — and silently murders the second answer, x equals nought. Antidote: never divide by the thing you are solving for; bring everything to one side and factorise: x times x minus five equals zero, both answers alive.

Trap three: the unflipped sign. Minus two x greater than six becomes x greater than minus three if you divide carelessly — wrong side of the mirror. Antidote: circle the sign the moment you divide by any negative, and flip it: x less than minus three. Then test one value: x equals minus four gives minus two times minus four, eight, greater than six — true, confirming the flip.

Trap four: inventing exponent laws for sums. X cubed plus x to the fourth stays exactly as it is; only like terms add.

And the last check, the habit that converts near-marks into full marks: substitute your answer back. X equals five in five x minus seven: twenty-five minus seven, eighteen; and two x plus eight: ten plus eight, eighteen. Balanced — proven correct, in the exam room, by you, before the marker ever sees it. Factorised something? Multiply it back. Solved an inequality? Test a number from your interval. Every drawer in the toolbox comes with its own proof, and the proof is free.

The final questions of the lesson are with you now — four traps, four antidotes, and every answer checked before it leaves your desk.
