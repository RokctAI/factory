**Thandi:** Revision time brings the same questions every year, so let's take them head on. First: with the whole year's algebra in one paper, how do we know which method a question wants, fast, without reading it five times?

**Tutor:** Train the label-reading reflex, because Paper 1 questions name their own drawer if you listen. The instruction word comes first: factorise means rewrite as a product — no solving, no answers for x. Solve, together with an equals sign, means find the values of x that balance it. An inequality sign means the linear toolkit plus the flip rule and an answer that is a whole interval, not a single number. Simplify with powers everywhere means the exponent laws. Then, within a solve question, the shape of the equation picks the strategy: x squared present means everything to one side, zero on the other, factorise, two answers; x upstairs in an exponent means force the same base on both sides; plain x means gather and divide. That is two reads of the question — one for the instruction word, one for the shape — and both take seconds. The candidates who lose time are not slow at algebra; they are solving before classifying, and the wrong method costs far more time than the two reads ever would.

***

**Thandi:** Next: why does the factorising order matter so much? Common factor first, then squares, then trinomials — some people jump straight to the trinomial hunt and it mostly works.

**Tutor:** Mostly works is the trap, because the questions are built for the order. Take three x squared minus twelve. A learner who skips the common-factor check stares at it, finds no trinomial, no obvious squares, and stalls — yet dividing out the three reveals x squared minus four, a textbook difference of squares, and the full answer three times x minus two times x plus two arrives in two lines. The common factor is first because it simplifies whatever remains, and examiners deliberately wrap easy patterns inside a common factor to reward the disciplined and stall the hasty. There is also a marks reason: fully factorise means fully — an answer of three x minus six, times x plus two, has smuggled the three inside one bracket incompletely and typically forfeits a mark. So run the tree in order every time: common factor, difference of squares, trinomial, then grouping or cubes. And close with the universal proof: multiply your brackets back out. Factorising is reversible, so the check is total — either you recover the original expression or you know immediately something is wrong.

***

**Thandi:** The lost-solution trap: dividing x squared equals five x by x feels so natural, and it even gives a correct answer. Why is a method that produces a correct answer still wrong?

**Tutor:** Because it produces one correct answer while destroying the other, and a quadratic question is only fully answered with both. Look at what the division assumes: dividing by x is only legal when x is not zero, so the move silently declares zero out of bounds — precisely the value that happens to be a solution, since zero squared equals five times zero. The examiner cannot see your reasoning was almost right; the memo has two answers, nought and five, and a solution with one of them loses the marks for the other. The safe method costs one extra line: bring everything to one side, x squared minus five x equals zero, factorise out the x, x times x minus five equals zero, and the zero-product rule delivers both answers in daylight. Make it a lifetime rule: never divide an equation by the variable you are solving for — factor it out instead. The same discipline matters next year, when quadratics arrive with fractions and substitutions; the habit you set this term is the one under pressure in matric.

***

**Thandi:** Last one: any advice for structuring algebra revision itself in these final weeks — what actually moves marks?

**Tutor:** Three shifts, in order of payoff. First, revise by doing, not by reading. Reading worked solutions feels like progress and stores almost nothing; closing the book and reproducing the solution is what installs the method. Take a past paper's algebra section, work it cold, then mark yourself against the memo — the gap between the two is your genuine revision list. Second, drill the traps by name, because November mathematics is familiar but the traps are fresh: the incomplete common factor, the lost solution from dividing by x, the unflipped inequality sign, the invented exponent law for sums. Write the four on one page, with one example each, and rehearse recognising them — most lost algebra marks are these four wearing different numbers. Third, end every solution with its free check: substitute the answer back, multiply the factors out, test a value from the interval. The check converts an uncertain answer into a certain one while you are still in the room, and certainty compounds — each verified answer settles the nerves for the next question. Method practice, trap rehearsal, self-checking: those three carry more marks than any amount of rereading notes.
