**Thandi:** Time for the questions this revision always surfaces. First: five families of graphs feels like five separate topics to restudy. How is one framework supposed to cover a straight line AND a tangent graph?

**Tutor:** Because the framework never claims the shapes are the same — it claims the CONTROLS are. Each family keeps its own basic silhouette: the line's slope, the parabola's bowl, the hyperbola's two branches, the exponential's glide, the waves and the tangent's climbing branches. What repeats is what the equation is allowed to do to that silhouette in Grade 10: multiply it by a, add q. That is the whole vocabulary. So revision splits into two small jobs instead of five big ones. Job one: know the five silhouettes cold — including tangent's asymptotes at ninety and two-seventy, which is what makes it look wild while still obeying the same two controls. Job two: practise reading a and q off any equation and saying what they do — stretch or flip, lift and relocate the midline or asymptote. Even tangent submits: y equals tangent x plus one is the same climbing pattern, lifted. Study the silhouettes one evening, drill the controls the next, and the five topics collapse into one grammar with five nouns.

***

**Thandi:** Next: asymptotes. Students can compute them but keep asking what an asymptote actually IS — why does the graph get close forever and never arrive?

**Tutor:** Take the machine apart with numbers and the mystery dissolves. Look at y equals four over x, minus two, and feed it enormous x values: at x equals one hundred, four over x is nought comma nought four, so y is minus one comma nine six. At x equals ten thousand, y is minus one comma nine nine nine six. The fraction four over x shrinks every time x grows — but here is the point: four divided by SOMETHING can never be nought. A pizza cut into more and more slices gives thinner and thinner slices, never a slice of nothing. So y edges toward minus two eternally, forbidden from arriving: that unreachable height IS the horizontal asymptote, and it sits at q because q is what remains when the fraction has faded. The vertical asymptote is the same story told at the other end: x equals nought is banned because division by nought means nothing at all, so the graph flees along the y-axis rather than touch it. Same for the exponential: three to the x is always strictly positive, so three to the x minus one stays strictly above minus one. An asymptote is not a decoration — it is the graph's honest confession of a value it can never produce. Draw the confession dotted, first, always.

***

**Thandi:** Here's a favourite: the difference between sine and cosine. On a sketch with no labels, students freeze. What's the fastest tell?

**Tutor:** Look at the departure lounge — the value at nought degrees — and the graph identifies itself immediately. Sine departs from the midline: at nought degrees its value is nought, and it sets off climbing. Cosine departs from the summit: at nought degrees it is already at its maximum, one for the basic graph, and its first move is downhill. Same wave, different boarding time — cosine is sine's wave already ninety degrees along its journey. On a transformed graph the tell survives the controls: y equals minus two cosine x departs from minus two, a valley at the start, because the mirror switch turned the opening summit into an opening dip — still recognisably the cosine schedule, just flipped. If the y-axis is unlabelled, use the second tell: cosine is symmetric about the y-axis, a mirror image left to right, while sine has that twisting symmetry through the origin. And tangent can never impersonate either: the dotted vertical asymptotes at ninety and two-seventy give it away before you read a single value. Departure value first, symmetry second — no wave stays anonymous for long.

**Thandi:** Last one: the read-off questions — for which x is f above g, how many solutions, distance between graphs. Students say these feel vague compared to solving equations. How do we make them concrete?

**Tutor:** By translating each phrase into one mechanical act, because every read-off verb has exactly one. Take our pair, the parabola x squared minus four and the line x plus two. Phrase one: for which x is the parabola below the line? Translation: find where the graphs cross — we solved it, minus two and three — then answer with the interval between or beyond, checked by one glance at the sketch: between, here, so minus two to three, strict brackets because below is strict. The answer is an interval of x values, never a pair of y's. Phrase two: how many solutions does three sine x minus one equals one have? Translation: rule a horizontal line at one and count crossings on the sketch — two. No algebra needed; the count IS the answer. Phrase three: the length of the vertical segment joining the graphs at x equals nought? Translation: top value minus bottom value at that x — two minus, minus four — six. Notice every translation ends at either an interval, a count, or a subtraction. Nothing vague remains: read the phrase, pick its act, execute on the evidence of the sketch. Drill the three translations on any two graphs and the read-off marks become the most reliable marks on the paper.
