# Part 1 — Expert

This session is the functions revision sweep for the final examination: every graph of the Grade 10 year — straight line, parabola, hyperbola, exponential, and the three trigonometric graphs — pulled into one framework. The framework is a single sentence: every function you know is a basic shape, stretched or flipped by a, then slid up or down by q. Own that sentence and the exam's sketch questions, read-off questions and interpretation questions all fall to the same routine.

## Subtopic: The Family Album — a and q in Every Graph

Line up the five families and watch the same two letters do the same two jobs everywhere. The straight line: y equals a x plus q. The parabola: y equals a x squared plus q. The hyperbola: y equals a over x, plus q. The exponential: y equals a times b to the power x, plus q. The trig graphs: y equals a sine x plus q, and the same for cosine and tangent.

The letter q is the elevator. It lifts or lowers the entire graph without changing its shape. The parabola y equals x squared plus two is the basic bowl raised two units; its lowest point rides up from the origin to the point nought, two. On the trig graphs, q lifts the whole wave: the midline of y equals sine x plus one runs along y equals one instead of the x-axis. On the hyperbola and exponential, q moves the horizontal asymptote — the graph's forbidden height — from zero up to q.

The letter a is the volume knob, with a twist. For the parabola, a bigger positive a makes a narrower, steeper bowl. For the trig waves, a is the amplitude — the distance from midline to peak. And a negative a flips the graph upside down: the parabola becomes a dome with a highest point instead of a lowest, the sine wave starts by diving instead of climbing, the hyperbola swaps its quadrant pair.

Exam skill one is reading a and q straight off an equation before any calculation: y equals minus two sine x plus one announces a flipped wave of amplitude two, midline at one. Exam skill two is the reverse: given a sketch with labelled points, recover a and q. The elevator and the volume knob are the two dials every later question turns.

This section's questions are with you now — five families, two dials, both directions.

## Subtopic: The Straight Line and the Parabola Routine

The sketching routine for lines and parabolas has three moves: intercepts, special point, shape. Run it on the exam's favourite pair — the parabola y equals x squared minus nine and the line y equals x minus three.

The parabola. Y-intercept: set x to nought — minus nine, the point nought, minus nine. X-intercepts: set y to nought — x squared minus nine equals nought, difference of squares, x minus three times x plus three, so x equals three or minus three. Turning point: with no x term, the bowl sits symmetrically at its q value — nought, minus nine, which here is also the y-intercept. Shape: a is positive one, an upright bowl. Range, read from the lowest point: y is greater than or equal to minus nine. Sketch: bowl through minus three and three on the x-axis, bottom at minus nine.

The line. Y-intercept: minus three. X-intercept: x minus three equals nought, so three. Gradient one, rising. Two points, one ruler.

Now the interpretation question the examiners attach: where do the two graphs intersect? Intersection means equal heights at the same x, so set the formulas equal: x squared minus nine equals x minus three. Everything to one side: x squared minus x minus six equals nought. Factorise: x minus three, times x plus two. So x equals three or x equals minus two. Substitute back into the line: at three, y is nought — the point three, nought; at minus two, y is minus five — the point minus two, minus five. Notice the machinery: an intersection question turned into last section's quadratic equation. The paper is one subject, not ten.

Read-off skills complete the routine: for which x is the parabola below the line? Between the intersection points — minus two to three, strict. Length of a vertical segment between the graphs at a given x: top formula minus bottom formula, evaluated there.

Take this section's questions now — the full routine on both graphs, and the intersection recovered by algebra.

## Subtopic: Asymptotes — the Hyperbola and the Exponential

Two families own asymptotes — lines the graph approaches forever but never touches — and the exam tests whether you can find them, draw them, and state what they forbid.

The hyperbola: y equals six over x, plus one. Two asymptotes. Vertical: x equals nought, because division by nought is impossible — the y-axis is off-limits. Horizontal: y equals one, the q value, because six over x shrinks toward nought as x grows huge, leaving y just above or below one — never exactly one. The graph lives in two opposite quadrants of the asymptote cross. Anchor it with points: at x equals two, y is four; at x equals six, y is two; at x equals minus one, y is minus five. X-intercept: set y to nought — six over x equals minus one, so x equals minus six. Domain: all x except nought. Range: all y except one.

The exponential: y equals two to the power x, plus one. One asymptote, horizontal: y equals one, again the q. The power two to the x is always positive — it never reaches nought — so y stays strictly above one forever. Y-intercept: two to the nought is one, plus one — the point nought, two. Two more anchors: at one, three; at two, five. Going left, the graph glides down toward the asymptote: at minus two, y is one and a quarter. Range: y strictly greater than one.

The asymptote discipline for sketches: draw the asymptotes first, as dotted lines, then hang the curve on them. A hyperbola or exponential sketched without its dotted lines bleeds marks even when the curve is right.

And the recognition reflex, equation to family: x in a denominator — hyperbola, two asymptotes. X in an exponent — exponential, one asymptote. No asymptotes on lines and parabolas. The asymptote count is the family's fingerprint.

This section's questions are ready — asymptotes stated, dotted, and respected by every point you plot.

## Subtopic: Trig Graphs From Zero to Three Hundred and Sixty Degrees

The three trig graphs on their exam domain, nought to three hundred and sixty degrees — first their fingerprints, then the a and q machinery.

Sine: starts at nought, climbs to one at ninety, back through nought at one-eighty, dips to minus one at two-seventy, home at three-sixty. One full smooth wave. Cosine: the same wave shifted — it STARTS at its peak: one at nought, nought at ninety, minus one at one-eighty, nought at two-seventy, one at three-sixty. Tangent: the outsider — no wave, no amplitude; it climbs from nought through one at forty-five, shoots to infinity at its vertical asymptotes, x equals ninety and x equals two-seventy, and repeats. Sine and cosine are bounded between minus one and one; tangent is unbounded.

Now the dials. Y equals two sine x plus one: amplitude two, midline lifted to one. Maximum: midline plus amplitude, three, still at ninety degrees. Minimum: midline minus amplitude, minus one, still at two-seventy. Y-intercept: at nought, sine is nought, so y equals one. Range: minus one up to three, inclusive. The lifting never moves the peaks left or right — q changes heights, not timing.

A flipped example: y equals minus cosine x. The peak becomes a valley: minus one at nought, climbing to plus one at one-eighty, back to minus one at three-sixty. Reading a and q from a given trig sketch reverses the machinery: amplitude is half the distance between maximum and minimum; q is their average, the midline.

Read-off questions on trig graphs: how many solutions does sine x equals a half have on the domain? Draw the horizontal line at a half; it cuts the wave twice — two solutions. Where is the graph decreasing? Sine falls from ninety to two-seventy. The graph answers counting and interval questions that algebra alone answers only later, in Grade 11.

The final questions of this part are with you now — three fingerprints, two dials, and the read-off moves rehearsed.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the whole gallery again, hung on one wall — five shapes, two dials, and a routine that sketches anything.

## Subtopic: The Volume Knob and the Lift

Every function equation is a machine with two dials bolted on, and revision becomes simple the moment you see that the dials are the same on every machine.

Dial one: q, the lift. Whatever the basic shape does, plus q carries the whole picture upstairs, minus q takes it down. The basic parabola bowls at the origin; plus two and the identical bowl floats two units higher. The basic sine wave breathes around the x-axis; plus one and the same wave breathes around the height one. For hyperbolas and exponentials, the lift carries something extra: the forbidden height, the horizontal asymptote, rides the elevator too — it always sits exactly at q.

Dial two: a, the volume knob with a flip switch. Turn a up and the shape intensifies: steeper line, narrower bowl, taller wave. The wave case has a name worth using — amplitude, the distance from the midline to the peak, exactly the size of a. Turn a negative and the whole picture flips upside down: bowls become domes, rising lines fall, waves start downward.

The revision power move: read the dials aloud before doing anything. Y equals minus two sine x plus one — say it: flipped, amplitude two, midline one. Y equals six over x plus one — say it: hyperbola, asymptotes at the axes lifted to y equals one. Ten seconds of dial-reading and the sketch is half drawn, the range half stated, the interpretation half done.

Your questions for this section are ready — two dials, read aloud, on every family in the album.

## Subtopic: Reading a Graph Like a Story

Half the functions marks in the final paper need no sketching at all — they show a completed graph and ask you to read it. Reading is a skill of its own, with four verbs.

Verb one: locate. Intercepts are where the graph crosses the axes — on the x-axis the height is nought, on the y-axis the x is nought. Turning points are the bottom of a bowl or top of a dome. Asymptotes are the dotted no-go lines.

Verb two: compare. Where does the parabola sit below the line? Look between their crossing points — from minus two to three in our worked pair. Which graph is on top at x equals nought? The line at minus three sits above the parabola at minus nine. Above means higher y, below means lower — the phrase for which x is f above g translates to an interval on the x-axis.

Verb three: measure. The vertical distance between two graphs at some x is top minus bottom at that x. At nought, the line minus the parabola: minus three, minus, minus nine — six units. The examiners love this one because it quietly tests substitution and signs.

Verb four: count. How many times does a horizontal line at one and a half cut y equals two sine x plus one? The wave tops at three and bottoms at minus one, so the line at one and a half slices through the rising and falling arms — count the crossings on the sketch: two. Counting questions are Grade 11 equation-solving, previewed with your eyes.

The discipline under all four verbs: every feature you claim must be traceable to either the equation or a labelled point. The graph is the evidence; read from it, never from hope.

Here are this section's questions — locate, compare, measure, count.

## Subtopic: The Sketching Checklist

One checklist sketches every function in the Grade 10 gallery. Six lines long.

One: name the family from the equation's fingerprint — x plain, line; x squared, parabola; x in a denominator, hyperbola; x in an exponent, exponential; sine, cosine or tangent of x, a trig graph.

Two: read the dials — a for steepness, amplitude or flip; q for the lift and, where relevant, the asymptote or midline.

Three: draw the skeleton — dotted asymptotes for hyperbola and exponential, the midline for a lifted wave, the axis of symmetry for the parabola.

Four: compute the anchors — y-intercept by setting x to nought, x-intercepts by setting y to nought and solving with last lesson's algebra, turning point or endpoints where the family has them, and the standard angle points for trig: nought, ninety, one-eighty, two-seventy, three-sixty.

Five: sweep the curve through the anchors, respecting the skeleton — curves approach dotted lines, they never cross them; waves stay within midline plus and minus amplitude.

Six: state the passport lines if asked — domain, range, and any values x may not take.

Run the checklist mentally across the gallery you now own: the line and bowl through three and minus three; the hyperbola hugging its lifted cross; the exponential gliding above y equals one; the lifted sine wave peaking at three over ninety degrees. Same checklist, five families, every sketch mark in the paper collected along the way.

Functions return in Grade 11 with shifts left and right joining the lifts — the two-dial machine grows a third dial, and everything you rehearsed today carries straight across.

The final questions of the lesson are with you now — checklist from line one, family by family.
