**Thandi:** The questions that come up most often in functions revision — let's work through them. First: five families, each with intercepts, asymptotes, turning points, ranges... how is anyone supposed to hold all of that at once?

**Tutor:** By refusing to hold it as five separate stories. There is one story: a basic shape, times a, plus q. The basic shapes are genuinely few — a line through the origin, a bowl at the origin, the hyperbola's two branches, the exponential's glide, and the three trig waves — and each takes seconds to recall. Everything else is the two dials. The a dial stretches or flips; the q dial lifts the whole picture, and whatever the family's reference feature is rides along with it: the parabola's turning point, the hyperbola's horizontal asymptote, the exponential's forbidden height, the trig graph's midline — all of them sit exactly at q. So instead of memorising that y equals two to the x plus one has range y greater than one, you reconstruct it: the basic exponential hugs the height nought, the lift carries that to one, the graph stays strictly above its asymptote — range follows. Five families times many facts collapses into five shapes plus two dials, and the dials are the same everywhere. Revision then becomes practising the reconstruction, not rehearsing the list.

***

**Thandi:** Next: intersection questions. Two graphs, find where they meet — some people plot point after point hoping to spot it. What is the reliable route?

**Tutor:** Algebra, always, with the graph as the sense check rather than the method. An intersection is a point lying on both graphs at once, which means one thing: the same x produces the same y in both formulas. So set the formulas equal and solve. For the parabola x squared minus nine and the line x minus three: x squared minus nine equals x minus three, bring everything to one side, x squared minus x minus six equals nought, factorise to x minus three times x plus two, giving x equals three or minus two. Then substitute each x back — into the simpler formula, usually the line — to collect the y partners: three gives nought, minus two gives minus five. Two tidy coordinate pairs, no plotting marathon. Notice what the question really was: the quadratic equation from the algebra section, wearing graph clothing. That is the deep pattern of Paper 1 — the sections feed each other, and intersection questions are the clearest example. Plotting can confirm your two points look right on the sketch, but the marks live in the equation, the factorising, and both coordinates stated.

***

**Thandi:** A trig one: the values of sine and cosine at the special angles keep sliding out of memory by exam day. Is there a way to hold the whole wave without cramming?

**Tutor:** Hold the shape, not the table — the graph IS the memory device. For sine, keep one sentence: start at nought, peak at ninety, back to nought at one-eighty, trough at two-seventy, home at three-sixty. Five anchor points, evenly spaced, one smooth wave through them — that single sentence regenerates every value the sketch questions need. Cosine is the same wave with one difference: it starts at the top. Say it: one at nought, nought at ninety, minus one at one-eighty, nought at two-seventy, one at three-sixty. Tangent refuses to be a wave, so store its two facts instead: through nought at nought and one-eighty, and vertical asymptotes at ninety and two-seventy where it runs away to infinity. Then the dials do the rest without any new memory: amplitude a scales the heights, q lifts the midline, maximum equals midline plus amplitude, minimum equals midline minus amplitude — for y equals two sine x plus one, that is three and minus one, peaks still at ninety and two-seventy because lifting never shifts timing. Draw the basic wave once from the sentence, turn the dials, and the values are simply read off your own sketch.

***

**Thandi:** Last one: sketch questions feel like art — some drawings earn full marks and near-identical ones earn half. What are the markers actually looking for?

**Tutor:** Not artistry — labelled evidence. A functions sketch is marked as a list of features, each worth its own tick, and the curve between them is almost decoration. The list: the correct basic shape for the family; every intercept computed and written as coordinates at the right spot; asymptotes drawn dotted and labelled with their equations for the hyperbola and exponential; the turning point labelled on a parabola; the anchor angles and the maximum and minimum on a trig wave; and the curve respecting the skeleton — approaching dotted lines without ever crossing them, staying inside midline plus and minus amplitude. A beautiful freehand curve with unlabelled crossings earns sympathy and few marks; a modest curve with nought comma minus nine, three comma nought and minus three comma nought printed at the right places collects everything. So sketch in checklist order: name the family, read the dials, draw the skeleton, compute and LABEL the anchors, then sweep the curve. The labelling step is where the marks concentrate, and it is the step nervous candidates skip. Slow down for ten seconds at each anchor, and the sketch marks the paper practically awards itself.
