**Thandi:** Here are the questions that come up over and over with this topic. First: two heights, one cone. People substitute the slant height into the volume formula and lose everything after that line. How do we keep the two apart under exam pressure?

**Tutor:** Give each height a job description and interrogate the diagram before touching a formula. The perpendicular height lives INSIDE the solid — it is the vertical drop from the apex to the centre of the base, the height a plumb line would measure. Volume is about how much space the inside holds, so volume gets the inside height, always. The slant height lives ON the solid — it runs along the sloping outside from apex to rim, the path a raindrop would take down the surface. Surface area is about covering the outside, so it gets the outside height, pi r s for the cone, half base times slant for each pyramid face. The two are never interchangeable, but they are permanently connected: with the radius, or half the base edge, they form a right-angled triangle, so s squared equals h squared plus r squared converts one to the other on demand. The exam habit that makes this safe: the moment you read a cone or pyramid question, write both heights down, labelled h and s, computing the missing one with Pythagoras immediately. With both sitting labelled on your page, substituting the wrong one takes real effort.

***

**Thandi:** Next: why does halving a sphere NOT halve its surface area? The volume halves cleanly, so the surface behaving differently catches almost everyone.

**Tutor:** Because cutting creates something new. Slice an orange in half and consider what your eye sees that it could not see before: a flat circular face that was interior flesh a second ago. The volume question never notices — every drop of juice that was inside is still there, split into two half-shares, so a hemisphere's volume is honestly half of four thirds pi r cubed. But surface area is a bill for everything exposed, and the cut exposed a brand-new circle of area pi r squared on each half. So a closed solid hemisphere wears half the sphere's skin, two pi r squared, plus that new circle, making three pi r squared in total — more than half of the original four pi r squared. Then comes the reading-comprehension layer: whether the flat circle is billed depends on the object's story. A solid half-ball on a desk, yes. A dome standing on a building, no — its rim is attached, and attached is not exposed. A hollow bowl gets curved skin on the outside and possibly the inside too. The formula supplies the parts; the story selects which parts are real.

***

**Thandi:** This one decides marks in every combined-solid question: which faces disappear at the join, and how can we be sure we crossed out the right ones?

**Tutor:** Use the touch test: a face belongs on the surface-area bill only if something outside the solid could touch it. Where two solids are glued, the pair of faces pressed together at the join fail that test — in the ice-cream shape, the cone's base circle and the hemisphere's flat circle are pressed into each other, so both leave the bill. The reliable procedure is bookkeeping, not intuition. Write each solid's complete face list: the cone offers a base circle and a curved surface; the hemisphere offers a flat circle and a curved skin. Then find the join and cross out the two faces that meet there, one from each list. Add what survives — curved cone plus curved skin, sixty pi plus seventy-two pi, one hundred and thirty-two pi. Two extra reminders complete the method. Volume never plays this game: glued volumes add in full, joins included, because the inside space does not vanish where the solids meet. And the crossed-out faces come in pairs of equal circles — if your two buried faces have different areas, the solids you described could not actually be glued flush, and something in the reading went wrong.

**Thandi:** Last one: is there a strategy for learning all these formulas, or is it raw memory from here to the final paper?

**Tutor:** Far less memory than the formula page suggests, because most of the list is manufactured from two ideas you already own. Every prism and cylinder is base times height — that is understanding, not memory. Every cone and pyramid is the same thing with the one-third discount, and the discount itself is a real experiment: three cones of water fill the matching cylinder, exactly. Surface areas are nets: the box unfolds into rectangles, the cylinder's wall unrolls into a rectangle of circumference times height, the pyramid opens into a square and four triangles. The cone's curved piece, pi r s, is the one net worth committing to memory, and Pythagoras builds the s it needs. That leaves the sphere as the only genuine memorisation: four thirds pi r cubed and four pi r squared, kept straight forever by the powers — cubed fills, squared wraps. So the strategy is a hierarchy: derive what can be derived, memorise the sphere, and spend the saved effort on the true skill this topic tests, which is reading the object — which faces exist, which are buried, which height the formula wants. Formulas are the easy third of measurement; the object's story is where the marks live.
