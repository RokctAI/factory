# Part 1 — Expert

This session answers the question the examiners love because it tests understanding rather than arithmetic: what happens to volume and surface area when a dimension is multiplied by a constant factor k? The two exam tasks carrying the lesson: first, a box four by three by two has its height doubled, then all three dimensions doubled — predict both new volumes and surface areas without rebuilding from scratch. Second, the reverse gear: a container's volume grew twenty-seven times — what factor scaled its dimensions? By the end, k, k squared and k cubed will each know their place.

## Subtopic: Stretch One Dimension — Volume Follows It

Start with the box: four by three by two. Its volume is four times three times two, twenty-four cubic units. Its surface area, from the net: two times, bracket, twelve plus eight plus six, close bracket — fifty-two square units.

Now multiply the height alone by k equals two. The box becomes four by three by four. New volume: four times three times four, forty-eight — exactly double the original twenty-four. No accident. Volume is a product of three dimensions, and the height appears in that product exactly once. Multiply one factor of a product by two and the whole product doubles. In symbols: length times breadth times two h equals two times the original volume. The rule: multiply any single dimension by k, and the volume multiplies by k — provided that dimension appears once in the formula.

Surface area refuses to be so tidy. The new box, four by three by four, has surface area two times, bracket, twelve plus sixteen plus twelve, close bracket — eighty. The original was fifty-two, and eighty is not two times fifty-two. Why not? Because the height appears in some terms of the surface area formula and not in others: the top and bottom faces, four by three, never felt the stretch, while the four side faces doubled. Part of the formula scales, part stands still, so the total scales by no clean factor. The exam consequence: for a single-dimension change, predict volume with the k rule, but recompute surface area honestly from the new dimensions.

This section's questions are with you now — the doubled box verified both ways, and the reason surface area broke the pattern stated in full.

## Subtopic: The Radius Counts Twice

Now the cylinder, and the twist that separates an A-candidate from the rest. Take the can from the previous lesson: radius five, height twelve, volume pi r squared h — three hundred pi.

Double the height, keeping the radius: volume becomes pi times twenty-five times twenty-four, six hundred pi. Twice the original. The height appears once in the formula, so k on the height means k on the volume. Consistent with the box.

Now instead double the radius, keeping the height: the new volume is pi times ten squared times twelve — pi times one hundred times twelve, twelve hundred pi. That is FOUR times the original three hundred pi, not two. The reason sits in the formula: the radius is squared, so it enters the product twice. Multiply the radius by k and the volume multiplies by k squared. Triple the radius and volume grows nine times: pi times fifteen squared times twelve is twenty-seven hundred pi, and twenty-seven hundred is nine times three hundred.

The governing principle, worth writing in your summary notes: k acts on the volume once for every time the scaled dimension appears in the formula. Height in pi r squared h: once, so k. Radius: twice, so k squared. This is why a pizza place's large, only a few centimetres wider, holds so much more than the regular — width pays twice.

The exam asks this conceptually: by what factor does the volume of a cylinder increase if the radius is multiplied by three? No calculation needed — the answer is nine, because radius counts twice. Say the reason, bank the marks.

Work this section's questions now: height versus radius, k versus k squared, and the principle quoted with its reason.

## Subtopic: Scale Everything — k Squared and k Cubed

The headline case: every dimension multiplied by the same k. This is enlargement — the solid keeps its shape and changes only its size, like a scale model against the real building.

Back to the box, four by three by two, all dimensions doubled: eight by six by four. New volume: eight times six times four, one hundred and ninety-two. Original: twenty-four. Ratio: eight — which is two cubed. Each of the three dimensions contributed one factor of two, and two times two times two is eight. Multiply every dimension by k and volume multiplies by k cubed, always, for any solid whatsoever — box, cylinder, pyramid, or a statue of irregular shape. Volume is three-dimensional, so it collects three copies of k.

Surface area this time IS predictable — because now every face scaled the same way. New surface area: two times, bracket, forty-eight plus thirty-two plus twenty-four, close bracket — two hundred and eight. Original: fifty-two. Ratio: four, which is k squared. Every face is a flat two-dimensional patch; each collects two copies of k. Multiply every dimension by k and surface area multiplies by k squared, again for any solid.

The pattern deserves its full statement: lengths scale by k, areas by k squared, volumes by k cubed. One, two, three copies of k for one, two, three dimensions. This single line explains why an ant can lift many times its own weight while an elephant cannot, and why a baby cools down faster than an adult: as k grows, volume outruns area — k cubed beats k squared.

Percentages ride the same rule. Increase every dimension by ten percent: k is one comma one. Volume scales by one comma one cubed — one comma three three one — a thirty-three comma one percent increase, not thirty. Surface area scales by one comma one squared — one comma two one — up twenty-one percent.

The questions for this section are in front of you — the doubled box checked, the k squared and k cubed rules stated, and the ten percent trap defused.

## Subtopic: Reverse Gear — Finding k From the Multiplier

Examiners love driving this machine backwards: they give the change in volume or area and ask for k.

Task: a container is enlarged so that its volume becomes twenty-seven times bigger. What happened to its dimensions? The relationship is k cubed equals twenty-seven, so k is the cube root of twenty-seven — three. Every length tripled. And now a bonus question answers itself: the surface area multiplied by k squared, which is nine.

Another: a sphere-shaped balloon's surface area quadruples. What happened to the radius, and to the volume? Area rule: k squared equals four, so k equals two — the radius doubled. Volume rule: k cubed equals eight — the volume is eight times bigger. Notice the flow: from the given quantity, extract k first; from k, predict everything else. Never jump directly from area-change to volume-change without passing through k.

The direction of the root matters. Volume information: take a cube root to find k. Area information: take a square root. And a plain length change needs no root at all. A quick sanity habit: k should come out as a sensible scale — if your k for an enlarged box is nought comma five, you found a shrinking, and the question said enlarged; revisit the division.

The error museum for this topic, three exhibits. Exhibit one: doubling all dimensions and claiming volume doubles — it multiplies by eight; the ant and the elephant forbid the shortcut. Exhibit two: applying k cubed to a single-dimension change — one dimension gives one k on volume. Exhibit three: adding percentages — ten percent on each dimension is not thirty percent on volume, it is one comma one cubed, thirty-three comma one percent, because scale factors multiply, never add.

The complete method: count how many times the scaled dimension appears — once, twice, or all three; apply k to that power; run roots backwards when the change is given; and recompute surface area honestly whenever only one dimension moved.

This section's questions are with you now — reverse gear, both roots, and all three museum exhibits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the whole lesson again through two everyday objects: a brick and a tin of paint. Same rules, no formulas until the last minute.

## Subtopic: One Brick, Then a Wall of Doubles

Picture a brick: four long, three wide, two high, in centimetres if you like. Its volume, twenty-four, is the number of little unit cubes that fit inside — four rows of three, stacked two layers high.

Make the brick twice as tall and nothing else. You have simply put a second brick on top of the first: two layers where there was one. Twice the cubes, twice the volume — forty-eight. Stretching ONE direction by two doubles the count, because the other two directions never changed. That is the whole single-dimension rule: the volume follows the one direction you stretched.

Now make the brick twice as big in EVERY direction. Here is the picture that locks the rule in forever: to build that double-size brick out of original bricks, you need two along the length, two across the width, and two up the height. Two times two times two — eight original bricks. Not two, not four: eight. Double everything, and volume multiplies by eight, which is two cubed. If you tripled everything, you would need three by three by three — twenty-seven bricks. The multiplier is always k times k times k, one k for each direction of space.

The examiner's favourite trap says: the dimensions doubled, so the volume doubled. You now own the picture that kills the trap — nobody builds a double-size brick from two bricks. It takes eight.

Your questions for this section are ready — count bricks, not formulas.

## Subtopic: Paint Grows Slower Than Water

Same brick, two different questions. How much water fits inside if it were hollow? That is volume. How much paint covers the outside? That is surface area. Scaling treats them differently, and the difference decides real prices in the real world.

Double the brick in every direction. The water inside multiplies by eight — two cubed, as the brick-counting showed. But the paint on the outside multiplies by only four — two squared. Why? Every face of the brick is a flat patch, and a flat patch has just two directions, length and width. Each direction doubles, so each face gets four times bigger: two times two. Faces know nothing about the third direction, so they collect only two copies of k.

So the slogan: paint scales by k squared, water scales by k cubed. Areas are two-dimensional, volumes are three-dimensional, and each dimension claims one copy of k.

Feel the gap grow. Double size: water times eight, paint times four. Triple size: water times twenty-seven, paint times nine. The bigger the enlargement, the further volume runs ahead of area. That is why the giant tank holds vastly more than it costs extra to paint, and why a tiny insect, all surface and hardly any volume, can fall from a table unharmed.

And the ten percent question, one more time in this language: growing every side by ten percent means k is one comma one. Paint: one comma one squared, one comma two one — twenty-one percent more paint. Water: one comma one cubed, one comma three three one — thirty-three comma one percent more water. Percentages on scale factors multiply through the powers; they never simply add.

Here are this section's questions — for each one, ask first: is this a paint question or a water question?

## Subtopic: Working Backwards Without Panic

Exam questions often tell you the ending and ask for the beginning: the volume became sixty-four times bigger — what happened to the sides? Reverse questions feel harder, but they are the same brick wall walked in the other direction.

The routine has three steps. Step one: name the quantity that changed. Volume changed? The multiplier is k cubed. Area changed? It is k squared. A plain length? Just k. Step two: undo the power. Volume times sixty-four: k cubed is sixty-four, so k is the cube root — four; every side is four times longer. Area times twenty-five: k squared is twenty-five, so k is five. Step three: use the k you found to answer whatever else is asked. Sides times four means area times four squared, sixteen; means any length, like the diagonal, times four.

Try the full chain once. A water tank is scaled up and now holds twenty-seven times more water. How much more paint does the outside need? Volume information first: k cubed is twenty-seven, k is three. Then the paint: k squared is nine. Nine times the paint — and notice you could never reach nine from twenty-seven without stopping at k on the way. The k in the middle is the bridge; every reverse question crosses it.

Sense checks to finish. A k below one means the object shrank — fine if the question said reduced, alarm bell if it said enlarged. And the two multipliers must sit in the right order: for any enlargement bigger than one, the volume multiplier is always the larger — if your paint number beat your water number, the powers were swapped.

Everything in this lesson is one sentence long: one k per dimension — once for lengths, twice for areas, three times for volumes, and roots to travel backwards. The final questions are with you now; cross the bridge at k every time.
