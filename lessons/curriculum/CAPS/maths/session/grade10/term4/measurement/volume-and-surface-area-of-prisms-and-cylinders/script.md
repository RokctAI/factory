# Part 1 — Expert

This session rebuilds the measurement machinery the final examination expects on demand: volume and surface area of right prisms and cylinders. Two exam tasks drive everything. First, a closed rectangular juice box measuring eight centimetres by five centimetres by twelve centimetres — find its volume and its total surface area. Second, a closed cylindrical cooldrink can with radius five centimetres and height twelve centimetres — the same two quantities, exact in terms of pi and then rounded to two decimal places. One law covers both solids, and by the end you will own it.

## Subtopic: One Rule for Every Prism — Volume Is Base Times Height

A right prism is a solid with a flat base that is copied, unchanged, all the way up: a rectangular box, a triangular glass prism, a hexagonal pencil stub. A cylinder is the same idea with a circular base. Because the cross-section never changes, every one of these solids obeys a single volume law: volume equals the area of the base times the perpendicular height.

Why does that work? Picture the solid built from identical thin layers, like a stack of sheets of paper cut to the shape of the base. Each layer contributes the same floor area, and the height counts how many layers are stacked. Area of one floor, times how tall the stack is — that is all volume ever is for these solids.

Run it on the juice box. The base is a rectangle, eight by five, so the base area is forty square centimetres. The height is twelve. Volume: forty times twelve, four hundred and eighty cubic centimetres.

Now the can. The base is a circle of radius five, so the base area is pi times radius squared: pi times twenty-five. Multiply by the height of twelve: three hundred pi cubic centimetres exactly. On the calculator, that is 942,48 cubic centimetres, rounded to two decimal places. Notice the discipline: the exact form, three hundred pi, is written first, and the decimal only at the end. Rounding early plants an error that grows through every later step.

One warning before we move on: the height in the formula is the perpendicular distance between the two identical faces — the direction the base was copied in. If a prism is lying on its side, the "height" of the formula might run horizontally across the page. Identify the repeated face first; the height is whatever direction is left.

This section's questions are ready for you now — the stacking idea in your own words, the box volume, and the can volume kept exact until the final line.

## Subtopic: Surface Area — Unfolding the Net

Surface area answers a different question entirely: not how much the solid holds, but how much material covers its outside. The reliable method is to unfold the solid flat into its net and add up the faces one by one.

The juice box unfolds into six rectangles, in three matching pairs. Top and bottom: eight by five, forty each. Front and back: eight by twelve, ninety-six each. The two sides: five by twelve, sixty each. Total surface area: two times the sum of forty, ninety-six and sixty. That bracket is one hundred and ninety-six, doubled gives three hundred and ninety-two square centimetres. The formula version says the same thing: two times, open the bracket, length times breadth plus length times height plus breadth times height, close the bracket.

The can unfolds into three pieces, and the middle one is the surprise. Top and bottom are circles: pi r squared each, twenty-five pi each, fifty pi together. The curved wall, sliced down its seam and rolled flat, becomes a rectangle. Its height is the height of the can, twelve. Its width is the distance around the circle — the circumference, two pi r, which is ten pi. So the curved surface is two pi r times h: ten pi times twelve, one hundred and twenty pi. Total: fifty pi plus one hundred and twenty pi, one hundred and seventy pi square centimetres exactly, which rounds to 534,07.

Hold on to the reason the wall is a rectangle: the label on a real can peels off as a flat rectangle whose width wraps exactly once around the rim. That single mental picture regenerates the formula two pi r h whenever memory fails.

And note the units. Volume came out in cubic centimetres; surface area comes out in square centimetres. Two different questions, two different units — an answer carrying the wrong unit is wrong even when the number is right.

Take on this section's questions now: the six faces of the box paired correctly, the rolled-out rectangle explained, and both totals reproduced.

## Subtopic: The Triangular Prism — a Full Worked Example

Now a complete exam-style solid with a base that is not a rectangle. A triangular prism: the base is a right-angled triangle with the two shorter sides six and eight centimetres and hypotenuse ten, and the prism is fifteen centimetres long.

Volume first, same law as always. Base area: half times base times perpendicular height of the triangle — half of six times eight, which is twenty-four square centimetres. The prism copies that triangle along its length of fifteen. Volume: twenty-four times fifteen, three hundred and sixty cubic centimetres.

Surface area by the net. The prism unfolds into two triangles and three rectangles. The triangles: twenty-four each, forty-eight together. Each rectangle has the prism's length, fifteen, as one side, and one edge of the triangle as the other: six by fifteen is ninety, eight by fifteen is one hundred and twenty, ten by fifteen is one hundred and fifty. Here is the professional shortcut: those three rectangles together make the perimeter of the triangle times the length — six plus eight plus ten is twenty-four, times fifteen is three hundred and sixty. Total surface area: forty-eight plus three hundred and sixty, four hundred and eight square centimetres.

Two traps live in this solid. Trap one: forgetting the half in the triangle's area — six times eight is the rectangle around the triangle, not the triangle. Trap two: using the hypotenuse as the triangle's height. The perpendicular height belongs with the base it is perpendicular to; in a right-angled triangle the two legs are that pair, and the hypotenuse is neither.

The questions for this section are with you now — the half defended, the perimeter shortcut used, and both final numbers checked.

## Subtopic: Units, Capacity and the Error Museum

Measurement marks are lost less to formulas than to housekeeping, so this section is the housekeeping.

First, capacity. One thousand cubic centimetres is one litre. The juice box's four hundred and eighty cubic centimetres is therefore 0,48 litres — divide by one thousand. The can's 942,48 cubic centimetres is roughly 0,94 litres. If an exam question asks how many litres a tank holds, the volume in cubic centimetres is only the halfway mark.

Second, mixed units. If a prism's base is given in centimetres and its height in metres, convert before multiplying, never after. A height of one comma two metres is one hundred and twenty centimetres; feed the formula centimetres throughout and the volume arrives honestly in cubic centimetres.

Third, open versus closed. A can with no lid loses one circle: surface area becomes one circle plus the curved wall — twenty-five pi plus one hundred and twenty pi, one hundred and forty-five pi. A pipe open at both ends is only the curved wall. Read the question's story: a tank being painted on the outside, a box with no top, a gutter — each story selects which faces exist.

Now the error museum, four exhibits. Exhibit one: diameter used as radius — a can described by its diameter of ten centimetres has radius five, and squaring the wrong one quadruples the base area. Exhibit two: rounding pi too early — carry pi as a symbol to the last line. Exhibit three: volume units squared instead of cubed, or the reverse. Exhibit four: the missing half in any triangular base.

The complete method, in order: name the solid and its base; compute the base area; multiply by height for volume; unfold the net for surface area; check which faces the story includes; finish with the right unit, exact first, rounded second.

This section's questions are in front of you now — capacity conversions, an open cylinder, and every museum exhibit recognised on sight.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same two answers again — four hundred and eighty and three hundred and ninety-two, three hundred pi and one hundred and seventy pi — but built from a stack of bricks and a roll of wrapping paper.

## Subtopic: Stacking Identical Floors

Think of a block of flats where every floor has exactly the same floor plan. If one floor covers forty square metres and the building has twelve floors, the total space inside is forty times twelve. Nobody needs a new formula for that — it is just counting floors.

Every right prism and every cylinder is that building. The juice box is a stack of identical eight-by-five rectangles, twelve layers tall: forty times twelve, four hundred and eighty cubic centimetres. The cooldrink can is a stack of identical circles, twelve layers tall. One circular floor covers pi times five squared, twenty-five pi. Twelve floors: three hundred pi, which the calculator turns into 942,48 cubic centimetres.

That is the entire theory of volume for this topic: find the floor, count the floors. When a strange prism appears in the exam — triangular, L-shaped, trapezium-based — do not hunt for a special formula. Ask two questions only. What shape is the floor, and what is its area? How tall is the stack? Multiply, and the volume is yours.

One habit protects the method: find the repeated face first. A prism lying on its side still has a repeated face; the stack might run left to right instead of bottom to top. The floor is the face that appears twice, and the height is the distance between those twins.

Your questions for this section are ready — for each solid, say out loud what the floor is before touching the calculator.

## Subtopic: Wrapping, Not Filling

Volume fills a solid; surface area wraps it. Different job, different tool, and the tool for wrapping is the net — the solid flattened out like a cardboard box opened for recycling.

Open the juice box flat and count the panels: top and bottom, forty each; front and back, ninety-six each; the two narrow sides, sixty each. Add the six panels: three hundred and ninety-two square centimetres of cardboard. If the exam gives a box with no lid, one forty disappears — the net always shows exactly which panels exist.

The can is the beautiful one. Peel the label off a real can: it comes away as a flat rectangle. Its height is the can's height, twelve. Its width is the distance the label travelled around the can — once around the circle, which is two pi times the radius, ten pi. So the label's area is ten pi times twelve, one hundred and twenty pi. Add the two circular ends, twenty-five pi each, and the total wrap is one hundred and seventy pi square centimetres — 534,07 once rounded.

Filling is measured in cubes, so its unit is cubic centimetres. Wrapping is measured in flat squares, so its unit is square centimetres. When you finish any measurement question, look at your unit and ask: did I fill, or did I wrap? If the unit disagrees with the job, something upstream went wrong.

## Subtopic: The Five-Step Checklist

Everything in this lesson compresses into five steps you can run on any prism or cylinder the paper offers.

Step one: identify the base — the face that repeats. Say its shape out loud. Step two: compute the base area. Rectangle: length times breadth. Triangle: half times base times perpendicular height, and the half is not optional. Circle: pi r squared, and check whether the question gave a radius or a diameter before squaring anything. Step three: volume equals base area times height, where height is the distance between the repeated faces. Step four: for surface area, unfold the net and add every panel the story allows — closed solids keep all faces, open tanks and pipes lose their lids. For a cylinder the wall is a rectangle: circumference times height, two pi r h. Step five: finish properly — exact answer with pi where relevant, then the rounded decimal, then the correct unit, cubic for filling and square for wrapping, and convert to litres by dividing cubic centimetres by one thousand if the question asks about capacity.

Practise the checklist against the numbers you now know by heart: the box at four hundred and eighty and three hundred and ninety-two, the can at three hundred pi and one hundred and seventy pi, the triangular prism at three hundred and sixty and four hundred and eight. If your run of the checklist reproduces those six numbers, the method is installed.

The lessons ahead will scale these very solids by a factor k and then curve them into spheres, pyramids and cones — every one of those lessons stands on base times height and the unfolded net.

The final questions of the lesson are with you now: run all five steps, in order, and let the checklist do the thinking.
