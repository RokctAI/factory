**Thandi:** Let's tackle the questions that always follow this topic. First up: the two heights. Perpendicular height, slant height — students mix them constantly. Is there a foolproof way to keep them apart?

**Tutor:** There is, and it starts with giving each height a job title instead of a letter. The perpendicular height is the inside height: it stands vertically within the solid, from the apex straight down to the centre of the base — you could drop a plumb line along it. Its job is volume, because volume asks how tall the stack of shrinking layers is. The slant height is the outside height: it lies along the sloping surface, apex to rim — a raindrop would run down it. Its job is surface area, because the sloping skin is what gets wrapped. The two are never interchangeable, but they are permanently connected: with the radius, or half the base edge for a pyramid, they form a right-angled triangle, hypotenuse on the slope. Our cone made it concrete: height twelve, radius five, slant thirteen. So the foolproof routine is a single question asked before every formula: does this height stand inside the solid, or lie on its skin? Inside goes to volume; on the skin goes to pi r s and the triangle faces. And when the one you need is missing, Pythagoras manufactures it — that conversion is often the very first mark of the question.

***

**Thandi:** Next: the one third. Students will use it, but plenty admit they don't believe it — why exactly a third, and not some messier fraction?

**Tutor:** Healthy suspicion, because the result is genuinely surprising. Here is the evidence trail. The pour test settles the fact physically: a hollow cone emptied into its matching cylinder fits exactly three times, whatever the size of the pair — three point nothing, every time. For the reason, look at how the layers shrink. In a cylinder every layer matches the floor. In a cone the layers shrink steadily to a point, and areas shrink with the SQUARE of the scale — a layer halfway up is scaled by a half in length but covers only a quarter of the floor's area. Summing layers whose areas fall away quadratically leaves exactly one third of the full stack; the squares are why the fraction is small, and the calculus you will meet in later grades turns that pattern into a two-line proof. For now the practical anchor is the pour, plus a sanity check the marker also uses: a pointy solid must hold noticeably less than its box — around a third feels right, a half is already suspicious. And the fraction is universal: square pyramid, cone, any base at all — one third, provided the solid rises to a point.

***

**Thandi:** The hemisphere question: half a sphere sounds like half of everything, yet the surface refuses to halve. Talk us through the trap.

**Tutor:** The trap lives in the difference between contents and packaging. Cut a sphere of radius three through the middle. The contents split fairly: volume thirty-six pi becomes eighteen pi each side — halving is exact for volume, always. But the packaging does something sneaky: the cut itself manufactures brand-new surface. Before the cut, the sphere wore only its curved skin, thirty-six pi. After the cut, each half wears half the skin, eighteen pi — plus a freshly exposed flat circle of pi r squared, nine pi, that did not exist a moment earlier. A closed hemisphere is therefore eighteen plus nine, twenty-seven pi — three quarters of the original skin, not half. Then comes the judgement call the story forces: is that flat circle actually exposed? A solid half-melon on a plate, yes — you could touch it. A dome seated on a wall, no — its rim is glued to the building, so the circle leaves the bill. The reliable habit: after any cut or join, ask what NEW faces appeared and what OLD faces vanished, then bill only what a hand outside could reach. That one question handles hemispheres, spinning tops and silos alike.

***

**Thandi:** Last one: combined solids. Volume adds, but surface area keeps tricking people into adding the two full surface areas. What's the working routine?

**Tutor:** The routine is an audit, and it runs in four lines. Line one: name the components — for the spinning top, a cone and a hemisphere; for the silo, a cylinder and a cone. Line two: volumes add with no ceremony — twelve pi plus eighteen pi gave the top's thirty pi, and no join can change that, because the grain or the sweet filling does not care where one shape ends and the next begins. Line three: for surface area, list every face of each component separately — cone: base circle, curved slope; hemisphere: flat circle, curved skin. Line four: strike out the faces at the join — the cone's base and the hemisphere's flat circle are pressed together, buried, invisible from outside — and add only the survivors: fifteen pi of slope plus eighteen pi of skin, thirty-three pi. Adding the two complete surface areas instead would have counted eighteen pi of buried cardboard that no paintbrush could ever reach. The mental picture that keeps the audit honest is painting: you can only bill surfaces the brush can touch. Volume is what fits inside, join-blind; surface is what the brush reaches, join-aware. Hold those two sentences and combined solids become the easiest marks in the topic.
