**Thandi:** Time for the questions that come up again and again on this topic. First: doubling all the sides feels like it should double the volume. Everyone's instinct says two, the answer is eight — why is intuition so wrong here?

**Tutor:** Because intuition tracks one direction at a time, and space has three. When you double every side, the stretching happens along the length, and along the width, and along the height — three separate doublings multiplied together, two times two times two. The brick picture makes it undeniable: to assemble a double-size brick from originals you need two end to end, two side by side, and two stacked up — eight bricks, no way around it. The mistake of answering two is really the mistake of adding scale effects instead of multiplying them, and it has a famous cousin: thinking ten percent growth on each side gives thirty percent more volume. It gives one comma one cubed, thirty-three comma one percent, because factors compound. So train the reflex: the moment a question scales an object, ask how many directions felt the change, and give k one multiplication for each. Three directions, k cubed. Every time your gut says double, make it prove itself against the brick wall.

***

**Thandi:** Next: the rule says one dimension times k gives volume times k. But then the cylinder's radius broke the rule and gave k squared. Which is it?

**Tutor:** Both, because the real rule is one level deeper than either statement. The honest version: k multiplies the volume once for every time the scaled measurement appears in the volume formula. A box's height appears once in length times breadth times height, so doubling it doubles the volume. A cylinder's height appears once in pi r squared h — same behaviour. But the cylinder's radius appears squared, which means it is in the product twice, so scaling it by k delivers k twice: k squared. Nothing broke; the radius is simply a dimension that does double duty, standing in for both directions across the circular base. That is also the practical reason a small increase in a pizza's radius changes what you get far more than the same increase in its thickness would — the radius pays twice. So before predicting any scaling effect, look at the formula and count appearances. Once, twice, or three times: that count is the power of k, and it never lies.

***

**Thandi:** A marks question: when only the height of a box is doubled, why does the memo recompute the surface area from scratch instead of using a k rule?

**Tutor:** Because a single-dimension change treats the faces unequally, and a clean scaling rule needs every part of the formula to feel the same stretch. Doubling only the height doubles the four upright faces — every one of them has the height as a side — but the top and bottom faces are length by breadth, and the height is nowhere in them, so they do not move at all. The total is part-doubled, part-frozen, and lands on some untidy factor that depends on the particular box: our four by three by two went from fifty-two to eighty, which is neither doubled nor any power of two. Contrast that with scaling every dimension: then each face, whatever its pair of sides, picks up k twice, so the whole total obeys k squared perfectly. The exam rule of thumb that falls out: all dimensions scaled, trust k squared and k cubed; one dimension scaled, trust k for the volume but rebuild the surface area honestly from the new measurements. Knowing when the shortcut is illegal is worth as many marks as the shortcut itself.

***

**Thandi:** Last one: in reverse questions people take the wrong root — square root of a volume change, cube root of an area change. Is there a way to keep the roots straight under pressure?

**Tutor:** Anchor the root to the dimension of the quantity, and say it before touching the calculator. Volume is three-dimensional, so a volume multiplier is k cubed, and undoing a cube needs a cube root. Area is two-dimensional, so an area multiplier is k squared, and undoing a square needs a square root. Length is one-dimensional and needs no root at all. One sentence covers it: the root matches the power, and the power matches the dimension. Then make the middle stop mandatory: whatever the question gives, extract k first, and only then compute what it asks for. Volume times twenty-seven: cube root, k is three; if the question wants area, square the three to get nine. Trying to leap from twenty-seven to nine directly is where the wrong roots creep in. And keep the free sense check: for an enlargement k must exceed one, and the volume multiplier must always beat the area multiplier. If your numbers violate either, a root went astray, and you caught it before the examiner did.
