**Thandi:** A few questions that come up a lot with this topic. The first one: prisms, cylinders, triangular prisms — is there really one volume formula for all of them, or are we secretly memorising three?

**Tutor:** One formula, genuinely, and it is worth understanding why. Every right prism and every cylinder is built the same way: a flat base copied straight upwards without changing shape. That construction means the solid is a stack of identical thin layers, so its volume can only be one thing — the area of one layer times how tall the stack is. Base area times perpendicular height. What changes between the box, the can and the triangular prism is not the volume law but the base-area calculation feeding it: length times breadth for the rectangle, pi r squared for the circle, half base times height for the triangle. So when a new solid appears, resist the urge to hunt for a new formula. Ask instead: what is the repeated face, and what is its area? Once that number exists, the rest of the question is a single multiplication. The learners who struggle in this topic are almost never weak at multiplying — they are hunting for special formulas that do not exist.

***

**Thandi:** Next one: where does two pi r h actually come from? People can recite it, but the moment a question changes the cylinder slightly, the recitation falls apart.

**Tutor:** It comes from an experiment you can do with any real can: peel the label off. It comes away as a flat rectangle. Its height is obviously the height of the can — the label ran from near the bottom to near the top. Its width is the distance the label travelled to wrap around the can exactly once, and once around a circle is the circumference, two pi r. A rectangle's area is width times height, so the curved surface is two pi r times h. That derivation is what survives when the question changes. Half a cylinder, like a rounded gutter? Half the label: pi r h. A pipe open at both ends? The label only, no circles. A closed can? Label plus two circles of pi r squared each. If you own the peeling picture, you never memorise those four cases — you rebuild whichever one the question describes, in one line, from the story it tells.

***

**Thandi:** This one costs marks every year: a question gives the diameter, someone uses it as the radius, and every number after that is wrong. Is there a habit that stops it?

**Tutor:** There is, and it takes one written line. The moment you read any circle measurement in the question, before touching a formula, write the letter r equals and the actual radius. If the question says diameter ten centimetres, your first line is r equals five. That line forces the halving to happen consciously, in the open, where you can check it — instead of silently inside a formula where the error hides. The damage from skipping it is severe because the radius gets squared: doubling the radius quadruples pi r squared, so the wrong choice does not nudge your answer, it wrecks it by a factor of four in the base area. There is also a quick sense check available afterwards: a can of radius ten and height twelve would be a fat drum wider than it is tall, and the question described a cooldrink can. When the picture in the question and the numbers on your page disagree, believe the question and re-read your first line.

**Thandi:** Last one: when do we leave an answer as three hundred pi, and when must it become nine hundred and forty-two comma four eight? Both appear in memos.

**Tutor:** Let the question's instruction decide, and hold both forms until it does. Three hundred pi is the exact answer — pi is an unending decimal, so any digit version of it is an approximation. The professional habit is to carry pi as a symbol through every line of working and only press the pi button at the very end. If the question says leave the answer in terms of pi, then three hundred pi cubic centimetres is the final answer and converting it throws away the mark. If it says round to two decimal places, then the final line becomes nine hundred and forty-two comma four eight, and the rounding instruction tells you exactly how many decimals to keep. What is never acceptable is rounding early — using three comma one four in line two, then multiplying three more times, lets the rounding error grow until the final answer misses the memo's tolerance. Exact all the way through, one rounding at the end, unit attached: that order earns full marks in every variant of the question.
