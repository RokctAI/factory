# Part 1 — Expert

This session completes the Grade 10 measurement toolkit: spheres, right pyramids, right cones, and the combined solids the examiners build from them. Two exam tasks anchor everything. First, a cone of radius six centimetres and perpendicular height eight centimetres — find its volume and total surface area, exact and rounded. Second, an ice-cream shape: that same cone with a hemisphere of radius six sealed on top — total volume, and the outside surface area. The whole topic rests on one new number, one third, and one old friend, Pythagoras.

## Subtopic: The One-Third Family — Pyramid and Cone Volumes

A right pyramid is a solid with a flat base — square, in the Grade 10 syllabus — rising to a single point, the apex, directly above the centre of the base. A right cone is the same story with a circular base. Neither is a stack of identical layers, so base times height overestimates them: the layers shrink as they climb. The correction is beautiful and exact: a pyramid or cone fills exactly one third of the prism or cylinder that shares its base and height. Volume equals one third times base area times perpendicular height.

That one third is a real, testable fact: take a hollow cone and a hollow cylinder with matching base and height, fill the cone with water, and pour. Three pours fill the cylinder — always, precisely, three.

Run the square pyramid: base edge ten centimetres, perpendicular height twelve. Base area: one hundred. Volume: one third times one hundred times twelve — four hundred cubic centimetres.

Now the cone: radius six, perpendicular height eight. Base area: pi times thirty-six. Volume: one third times thirty-six pi times eight — ninety-six pi cubic centimetres, which rounds to 301,59.

Vocabulary discipline before the questions: the perpendicular height is the vertical drop from the apex straight down to the centre of the base. It is not the slanted distance along the side. The formula wants the perpendicular height, always. The slanted one has its own name and its own job, and it owns the next section.

This section's questions are with you now — the one-third fact, both volumes, and the right height fed to the formula.

## Subtopic: Slant Height — Pythagoras Before Surface Area

Surface area of a pyramid or cone is a story about the sloping outside, and the sloping outside is measured by the slant height, written as a small letter s in most memos. For a cone, s is the distance from the apex straight down the sloping surface to the rim of the circle. For a square pyramid, s is the height of one triangular face, from the apex to the midpoint of a base edge.

Here is the move that separates prepared candidates: the slant height is almost never given. The question supplies the perpendicular height and expects you to build s with Pythagoras. Inside the cone hides a right-angled triangle: the perpendicular height standing vertical, the radius running horizontal, and the slant height as hypotenuse. So s squared equals h squared plus r squared. Our cone: eight squared plus six squared — sixty-four plus thirty-six, one hundred. The slant height is ten. For the square pyramid the horizontal leg is half the base edge, from the centre of the base to the midpoint of a side: s squared equals twelve squared plus five squared — one hundred and forty-four plus twenty-five, one hundred and sixty-nine. Slant height thirteen.

With s in the toolkit, the surface formulas assemble from flat pieces. The cone unrolls into a circular base, pi r squared, plus a curved sector whose area is pi times r times s. Total: pi r squared plus pi r s. Our cone: thirty-six pi plus pi times six times ten — thirty-six pi plus sixty pi, ninety-six pi square centimetres, 301,59 when rounded. A pleasing coincidence in this particular cone: volume ninety-six pi and surface ninety-six pi, same digits, different units — cubic for filling, square for wrapping.

The pyramid: a square base of one hundred, plus four triangular faces, each half times base edge times slant height — half times ten times thirteen, sixty-five each. Total: one hundred plus four times sixty-five — three hundred and sixty square centimetres.

Take this section's questions now — Pythagoras first, formulas second, and the perpendicular and slant heights never confused again.

## Subtopic: The Sphere — Two Formulas, One Radius

The sphere is the only solid in the syllabus needing genuine memorisation, because it has no flat net and no base to reason from. Two formulas, both driven by the radius alone. Volume: four thirds pi r cubed. Surface area: four pi r squared.

A memory anchor that never fails: volume is three-dimensional, and its formula carries r cubed; surface is two-dimensional, and its formula carries r squared. If you ever blank on which formula is which, the power of r announces it — cubed fills, squared wraps.

Worked example, radius six. Volume: four thirds times pi times six cubed. Six cubed is two hundred and sixteen; four thirds of two hundred and sixteen is two hundred and eighty-eight. Volume: two hundred and eighty-eight pi cubic centimetres — 904,78 rounded. Surface: four pi times thirty-six — one hundred and forty-four pi square centimetres, 452,39 rounded.

The exam's favourite variation is the hemisphere, half a sphere, and it holds a trap worth naming loudly. Volume halves cleanly: half of two hundred and eighty-eight pi is one hundred and forty-four pi. Surface does NOT simply halve. Half the curved skin is half of one hundred and forty-four pi, seventy-two pi — but slicing a sphere in half exposes a brand-new flat face, a circle of pi r squared, thirty-six pi. A closed hemisphere therefore wears seventy-two pi plus thirty-six pi, one hundred and eight pi. Whether that flat circle counts depends entirely on the story: a solid half-orange, yes; a hemispherical dome sitting on a building, no, the open rim is attached, not exposed. Read the object, then decide.

This section's questions are ready — both formulas anchored to the power of r, the numbers for radius six, and the hemisphere's new face handled with care.

## Subtopic: Combined Solids and the Hidden-Face Rule

Now the examiners' finale: solids glued from two shapes. An ice-cream cone with a hemisphere on top, a hut shaped as a cylinder with a cone roof, a capsule of cylinder and hemispheres. Two principles govern every combination.

Principle one, for volume: glued solids simply add. The ice-cream shape: cone of radius six and height eight, ninety-six pi, plus hemisphere of radius six, one hundred and forty-four pi. Total: two hundred and forty pi cubic centimetres — 753,98 rounded. Volume never cares about the join.

Principle two, for surface area: add only the faces that remain on the OUTSIDE. Where the two solids meet, a face from each disappears into the join — the cone's base circle and the hemisphere's flat circle are buried where they touch. The exposed surface is the cone's curved part, pi r s with slant height ten, sixty pi, plus the hemisphere's curved skin, seventy-two pi. Total: one hundred and thirty-two pi square centimetres — 414,69 rounded. If you had blindly added the two complete surface areas, the two buried circles, seventy-two pi of phantom area, would have poisoned the answer.

So the method for any combined solid: draw or imagine the join; list every face of each solid; cross out the faces that touch; add the survivors. For volume, skip the ceremony and add everything.

The error museum, four exhibits. Exhibit one: slant height fed into a volume formula, or perpendicular height into pi r s — each formula owns its height. Exhibit two: the buried faces counted in a combined solid. Exhibit three: the hemisphere's exposed circle forgotten, or included when the story attached it to something. Exhibit four: the one third dropped from a cone or pyramid volume — a factor-of-three wound, instantly visible to a marker.

The complete method: name each simple solid inside the combined one; volumes add; surfaces add minus the buried faces; Pythagoras manufactures every missing slant height; exact in pi, round last, unit checked.

The final questions of this part are with you now — the full ice-cream calculation, the hidden faces crossed out, and every museum exhibit identified.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same solids from the ground up — a funnel, a soccer ball, and a rondavel with numbers so friendly they almost work themselves.

## Subtopic: A Funnel, a Tent and a Soccer Ball

Three new shapes joined the family, so meet them as objects first. The cone: a funnel, an ice-cream holder, a party hat. The square pyramid: a tent with a square floor rising to a point. The sphere: a soccer ball.

For the funnel and the tent, one fact runs the whole volume show: a pointy solid holds exactly one third of the box or can it stands inside. Imagine the cone snug inside a cylinder with the same circular floor and the same height. Fill the cone with water and tip it into the cylinder: it takes three fills — not roughly three, exactly three. So the volume recipe is the old one with a discount: base area times height, divided by three.

Try it gently. The tent: square floor ten by ten, a hundred; height twelve; a hundred times twelve is twelve hundred; a third of that is four hundred. The funnel: floor is a circle of radius six, thirty-six pi; height eight; thirty-six pi times eight is two hundred and eighty-eight pi; a third is ninety-six pi.

The soccer ball plays by its own rules — no flat floor, no net to unfold — so its two formulas are simply learned, with one trick to keep them straight: filling is three-dimensional so the volume formula cubes the radius, four thirds pi r cubed; wrapping is two-dimensional so the skin formula squares it, four pi r squared. Cubed fills, squared wraps — say it twice and it stays.

Your questions for this section are ready — three objects, one discount, and the powers of r doing the remembering for you.

## Subtopic: Building the Rondavel

Now build something real: a rondavel — the round house with cylinder walls and a cone-shaped thatch roof. Walls: radius three metres, two metres tall. Roof: a cone of the same radius, four metres of perpendicular height from ceiling to peak.

Question one: how much air is inside? Glued solids add their volumes, join or no join. The walls are a cylinder: floor area pi times nine, times height two — eighteen pi. The roof is a cone: a third of, pi times nine times four — twelve pi. Together: thirty pi cubic metres, about 94,25. Done — volume questions on combined solids are pure addition.

Question two: how much outside needs covering — plaster for the wall, thatch for the roof? Here the join demands respect. Walk around the building and list only what a hand outside can touch. The wall's curved surface: circumference times height, two pi times three times two — twelve pi, about 37,70 square metres of plaster. The roof's sloping surface needs the slant height, the distance from peak straight down the thatch to the eaves. Pythagoras inside the roof: height four, radius three — hypotenuse five. Thatch area: pi times radius times slant, pi times three times five — fifteen pi, about 47,12 square metres.

Now notice everything we did NOT count. The cone's base circle? Buried — it is the ceiling, glued onto the walls. The cylinder's top circle? The same buried ceiling, from below. The floor? The ground — the question asked about plaster and thatch, and the story decides. Combined-solid surface area is a walk around the outside, touching each face and asking: exposed, or buried?

Here are this section's questions — build the rondavel again piece by piece, and let the story tell you which faces are real.

## Subtopic: The Trap List — Heights, Halves and Buried Circles

The shapes are learned; what remains is avoiding the four traps that harvest marks every November.

Trap one: two different heights live in every cone and pyramid. The perpendicular height stands vertically inside, apex to centre of base — it belongs to VOLUME. The slant height lies along the outside surface — it belongs to SURFACE AREA. They are teammates in one right-angled triangle, with the radius or half the base edge as the third side, so Pythagoras converts one to the other whenever needed. Before using any formula, touch the height in the diagram of your mind and ask: inside, or along the slope?

Trap two: the hemisphere's extra face. Halving a sphere halves the volume, clean. But the cut exposes a new flat circle, so a closed hemisphere's surface is half the skin PLUS pi r squared. Then read the story — a dome on a building keeps its rim attached, so the circle stays off the bill.

Trap three: buried faces in glued solids. Where two solids touch, one face from each vanishes. Volume ignores the join; surface area must cross out both buried faces before adding.

Trap four: the missing one third. A cone or pyramid volume without the one third is a cylinder or prism answer — three times too big. If your pointy solid ends up holding as much as its surrounding can, the discount went missing.

Run the closing checklist against the numbers you now own: cone ninety-six pi and ninety-six pi, pyramid four hundred and three hundred and sixty, sphere two hundred and eighty-eight pi and one hundred and forty-four pi, ice-cream two hundred and forty pi and one hundred and thirty-two pi, rondavel thirty pi inside with twelve pi of plaster and fifteen pi of thatch. Reproduce those and the toolkit is complete — every solid in the Grade 10 syllabus now answers to you.
