**Thandi:** Before we close, the class sent through some questions. The one that came up most: the marble bag has three colours, so why is the answer for purple 5 over 12 and not 1 over 3? Half the group went for the third.

**Tutor:** That half of the group has just met the topic's favourite trap, so this is the perfect moment to disarm it forever. The rule of theoretical probability demands EQUALLY LIKELY outcomes, and the colours are not equally likely — the bag does not hold one purple, one yellow and one orange; it holds five purples, four yellows and three oranges. The individual marbles are the equal candidates: with the bag shaken and your eyes closed, each of the twelve marbles has exactly the same claim on your fingers. Purple simply owns five of those twelve claims. So the denominator counts marbles, twelve, and the numerator counts purple marbles, five. Whenever a question lists group sizes, read them as a fast way of counting individuals — never as the outcomes themselves.

***

**Thandi:** Next one: if relative frequency needs hundreds of tosses to be trustworthy, how many trials are actually enough? Is there a magic number?

**Tutor:** There is no single magic number, but there is a reliable way to think about it. Ask what the estimate is going to be used for, and watch how much it is still wobbling. After five tosses of our drawing pin the estimate lurched around wildly — a rumour. By two hundred and fifty tosses it had steadied to 0,36 — a report you could quote. By a few thousand, the second decimal itself barely moves — an audit. The practical test is stability: keep adding trials, and when extra batches stop shifting the figure meaningfully, the evidence has done its work. What you must never do is stop early because the number looks like the answer you wanted — small samples will happily tell you flattering lies.

***

**Thandi:** A quieter question that I think is actually deep: why does the complement rule bother existing? If you can count the outcomes in "not A", why not just count them directly?

**Tutor:** For seven outcomes on a spinner, direct counting is fine, and the rule saves you only a little labour. Its real power appears where direct counting becomes a monster. Consider a question like "what is the probability that at least one of several draws shows a purple marble" — counting every way that can happen means chasing dozens of overlapping cases. But the OPPOSITE event — no purple at all — is often a single clean case, easy to compute. One subtraction from 1 then delivers the answer that direct counting would have made you sweat for. That is why I asked you to install the reflex now: the words "not" and "at least" should make your pen write 1 minus before anything else. In Grade 11 and 12 that reflex will feel like a superpower.

***

**Thandi:** Last one from me: if a fair coin can show 33 heads in 60 tosses without being crooked, how can we ever accuse any coin of being unfair? Where's the line?

**Tutor:** The line is drawn by sample size, and the settling behaviour draws it for us. Small samples are allowed to wander — 0,55 after sixty tosses sits comfortably inside normal wobble, so no accusation sticks. But the licence to wander shrinks as trials accumulate: by three thousand tosses, an honest coin has essentially settled onto its true value of one half. A coin still delivering seventy percent heads at that scale is not wobbling — it is confessing. So the working principle is this: judge no object on a handful of trials, and trust no object that defies theory across thousands of them. Between those two, gather more data. That instinct — demanding enough evidence before concluding — is not just good probability. It is good thinking, and it will serve you far beyond this classroom.
