**Thandi:** Before you move on — the questions that come up most often with this topic. The big one: why must the overlap be filled in first? Plenty of people start with the 22 soccer players and everything goes wrong from there.

**Tutor:** Because the numbers a survey gives you are not the numbers the diagram's regions want. The survey says 22 play soccer — but that 22 describes the WHOLE soccer circle, overlap included. The diagram's regions need finer information: how many play only soccer, and how many play both. Start by writing 22 in the soccer-only crescent and you have silently claimed that none of the soccer players also play chess — and the moment you try to place the 9 both-players, they get counted a second time, the regions total 49 instead of 40, and the diagram is broken. Fill the overlap first — the 9 — and every other region becomes a clean subtraction: 22 minus 9 gives 13 soccer-only, 15 minus 9 gives 6 chess-only, and 40 minus the 28 placed gives 12 in neither. Then run the check that certifies the whole structure: 13 plus 9 plus 6 plus 12 is 40, every learner placed exactly once. Overlap first, subtract outward, total to confirm — the order is not a preference; it is the method.

***

**Thandi:** The subtraction in the addition rule still feels arbitrary to a lot of people. Where exactly does the minus P of A and B come from?

**Tutor:** It comes from an honest double greeting. Think of the two club registers. The soccer register lists 22 names; the chess register lists 15. A learner who plays both appears on BOTH registers — one person, written down twice. Add the registers and you count 37 entries, but entries are not people: the 9 both-players contributed two entries each, so the addition greeted them twice. Only 28 distinct learners are in at least one club. The repair is surgical: subtract the shared names exactly once — 37 minus 9 is 28 — so that everybody ends up counted exactly one time. Divide everything by the class size and the same repair becomes the rule: P of A or B equals P of A plus P of B minus P of A and B. The subtracted term is not a penalty and not a convention — it is the overlap being counted back out after addition counted it in twice. And it gives you a built-in alarm: skip the subtraction on overlapping events and the answer inflates, sometimes past 1, which is arithmetic's way of reporting phantom people.

**Thandi:** Mutually exclusive and complementary get mixed up in almost every test. What is the cleanest way to keep the two ideas apart?

**Tutor:** Ask two separate questions of the pair of events, in order. Question one: can both happen on the same trial? If no — the events share no outcome — they are mutually exclusive. That is the ONLY thing exclusivity claims. Question two: do the two events together cover every possible outcome? Only if the answer is ALSO yes do the events graduate to complementary. So complementary is exclusivity plus completeness: no overlap AND no leftovers. The die makes it concrete. "Less than 3" and "at least 5" pass question one — no number is in both — but fail question two, because 3 and 4 belong to neither: exclusive, not complementary. "Six" and "not six" pass both questions: no overlap, nothing left out, so their probabilities must total exactly 1. The picture version: exclusive means the circles are pulled apart, but people may still stand on the grass outside; complementary means the two regions swallow the entire rectangle with nobody left over. When a theory question asks for the difference, give both halves — exclusive forbids sharing; complementary forbids sharing and leftovers.

**Thandi:** Last one — when is it legal to use the short rule, just P of A plus P of B, and how should that be shown in written working?

**Tutor:** It is legal in exactly one situation: when the overlap is empty, so the subtracted term is nought and drops away on its own. That happens when the events are mutually exclusive — and the discipline the memorandum wants is that you DECLARE it before you use it. Write the justification as its own line: the events cannot occur together, so P of A and B equals nought — and only then add. On the die: less than 3 and at least 5 share no outcome, so P of one or the other is 2 sixths plus 2 sixths, two thirds. What you may never do is reach for the short rule out of convenience. Soccer and chess had nine shared players; adding 22 fortieths to 15 fortieths without subtracting gives 37 fortieths, a wrong answer that LOOKS respectable — which is what makes it dangerous. So treat the full rule as the default that is always safe, and the short rule as a privilege that must be earned and stated. If you are ever unsure whether events overlap, use the full rule: when the overlap truly is empty, the subtraction subtracts nothing, and the full rule quietly becomes the short one.
