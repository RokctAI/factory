**Thandi:** A few class questions before we wrap. The most common one by far: how do you know the overlap number when the question only gives the two totals and the class size? Not every survey hands you the "both" figure.

**Tutor:** A superb practical question, because real questions often withhold exactly that number. The answer is that the addition rule is a four-variable machine: sample space, the two event totals, and the overlap — give me any three, and the fourth falls out. Suppose 50 learners, 24 play netball, 18 play tennis, and the question instead tells you that 32 play at least one sport. Then the overlap is 24 plus 18 minus 32 — ten. The double-counted total, minus what actually stands in the rings, exposes precisely the people counted twice. So never panic about which number is missing; write the rule in full, substitute the three you have, and solve for the one you do not.

***

**Thandi:** Next: a couple of us used the short rule — just adding — on the netball and tennis question and got 42 fiftieths. It felt so natural. How do we stop ourselves?

**Tutor:** Build a checkpoint into your hand: before any addition of probabilities, ask one question out loud — can these two events happen to the same person at the same time? If yes, even for one single learner, the full rule with its subtraction is compulsory, because that learner is sitting in both registers. Only when the answer is a certified no — no shared outcome exists at all — may the subtraction be dropped, and then you must write the certificate: mutually exclusive, so the overlap's probability is nought. And keep the alarm in mind: answers that creep past 1 are the rule itself telling you that phantoms were counted. Forty-two fiftieths did not break 1, but the ten phantoms were in it all the same.

***

**Thandi:** Here's a sneaky one from the break: "exactly one" and "at least one" — they sound nearly identical, and apparently they are not?

**Tutor:** They differ by precisely one region, and that region is the lens. "At least one" is generous: it accepts netball-only, tennis-only, AND the both-players — three regions, 14 plus 8 plus 10, which is 32 out of 50. "Exactly one" is strict: it accepts the two crescents but turns the both-players away — 14 plus 8, which is 22 out of 50. So when you meet either phrase, do not calculate immediately; first shade the regions the words allow. Language selects territory, and the territory does the arithmetic. Train yourself to translate words into shaded regions and these two phrases will never catch you again.

***

**Thandi:** Last one, and it's the one I'd want cleared up myself: mutually exclusive and complementary keep blurring together. Is there a picture that keeps them apart permanently?

**Tutor:** Keep the hall floor. Mutually exclusive is only about the ropes: pull the two rings apart until they share no floor — nobody can stand in both. That is the whole demand. But look around: there may still be learners standing on the open floor outside both rings, and mutual exclusivity does not mind them at all. Complementary makes the second, harsher demand: no shared floor AND no open floor — the two regions between them must swallow every last person in the rectangle. That is why complementary probabilities always total exactly 1, while merely exclusive ones can total anything up to 1. One phrase to carry out of here: exclusive bans sharing; complementary bans sharing and leftovers. Hold that sentence and the theory mark is yours every time.
