**Thandi:** Before you move on — the questions that come up constantly with this topic. The first is the counters trap: with 5 red, 3 blue and 2 green in a bag, why is the probability of blue not simply one colour out of three?

**Tutor:** Because the theoretical formula has a condition attached, and the condition is the whole game: the outcomes you count must be EQUALLY LIKELY. Ask what actually happens at the moment of drawing. Your hand meets ten counters, and each individual counter — not each colour — has the same chance of being grabbed. Red is not one candidate; red is five candidates wearing the same shirt. So the sample space has ten equally likely outcomes, and blue owns three of them: 3 over 10. The one-in-three answer secretly assumes the three colours are equally likely, which would only be true if the bag held the same number of each. The reflex to build: whenever a question lists group sizes, the equal candidates are the individuals, and the group sizes are just a fast way of counting how many individuals each group contributes. Count things, never types — that sentence alone defuses the most common wrong answer in the whole topic.

***

**Thandi:** Next one, asked in some form every year: if theoretical probability can be computed without ever touching a die, why does relative frequency exist at all?

**Tutor:** Because theoretical probability has a narrow entry requirement, and most of the real world fails it. The counting formula works only when symmetry guarantees equal likelihood — a die's six identical faces, a coin's two identical sides, counters identical except for colour. A bottle top has no such symmetry: cap-up and cap-down are shaped differently, and no honest counting argument makes them equal. For objects like that — and for weather, traffic, sport, whether a seed germinates — the only route to a probability is evidence: run the experiment many times and divide occurrences by trials. So the two engines split the world between them. Symmetric and idealised: count and divide, no experiment needed. Irregular and real: measure and divide. And they meet in the middle beautifully — flip a fair coin thousands of times and the relative frequency drifts toward the theoretical half, which is the experimental world confirming the counted one. When the two disagree badly over a large sample, you have not broken mathematics; you have discovered a biased object.

***

**Thandi:** People also worry about the wobble: a fair coin gave 27 heads out of 50, and half the class concluded the coin was unfair. What is the right way to think about results that miss the theoretical value?

**Tutor:** The right way is to make the sample size part of the verdict. Probability describes the LONG RUN, and short runs are noisy by nature — 27 heads in 50 flips is a relative frequency of 0,54, a whisker from 0,5, and entirely ordinary. Expecting exactly 25 heads misunderstands what a half means: it is a tendency, not a timetable. The discipline is to ask two questions of any experimental result. How far is the relative frequency from the theoretical value? And how many trials produced it? Small gap, small sample: nothing to see. Small gap, huge sample: the object is behaving as theory predicts. But a LARGE gap that survives a LARGE sample — 1 400 heads in 2 000 flips, a relative frequency of 0,7 — is exactly how bias is caught, because honest randomness does not miss by that much for that long. Small samples wobble; large samples testify. Sample size is the difference between gossip and evidence.

***

**Thandi:** Last one — the complement rule looks like the easiest line in the topic, so why does it deserve its own section, and where do the marks actually come from?

**Tutor:** It earns its section because it converts hard counting into one subtraction, and because examiners test whether you know WHY it works, not just that it works. The why: an event and its complement split the entire sample space between them with no overlap — they are mutually exclusive, since both cannot happen, and exhaustive, since one must. Two probabilities covering everything, sharing nothing: they must total exactly 1, so P of not-A equals 1 minus P of A. The marks come from three habits. First, the reflex: the words "not" or "at least" should trigger the subtraction before any counting starts — the probability of not drawing green is 1 minus 2 tenths, four fifths, one line. Second, the verification: when the direct count is easy, do both — eight non-green counters out of ten confirms four fifths, and agreement makes the answer unshakeable. Third, the theory sentence: when asked to explain, name both properties, exclusive and exhaustive, because the sum-to-1 conclusion stands on both legs. Master that reflex now and the "at least one" problems of the senior grades become one-line subtractions instead of nightmares.
