**Thandi:** Before you move on — a few questions that come up a lot with this topic. The big one first: how do you decide between sine, cosine and tangent when the clock is running and every ratio looks equally likely?

**Tutor:** Stop choosing by instinct and start choosing by inventory. Every right-angled triangle question gives you exactly two sides that matter: the one whose value you know, and the one you want. Name them both from the angle's point of view — opposite, adjacent or hypotenuse — and the pair picks the ratio with no decision left to make. Opposite with hypotenuse can only be sine. Adjacent with hypotenuse can only be cosine. Opposite with adjacent can only be tangent. The flagpole question knew the adjacent, wanted the opposite — tangent, no debate. The kite knew the hypotenuse, wanted the opposite — sine, no debate. If you catch yourself hesitating, it means you skipped the labelling step, not that you have forgotten trigonometry. Go back one step, label the sides, and the hesitation disappears.

**Thandi:** People keep drawing the angle of depression between the cliff face and the line of sight, and the whole answer collapses. Why does that error feel so natural, and what kills it?

**Tutor:** It feels natural because the cliff face is the thing you can see on the sketch, and the horizontal line is invisible until you draw it — the eye grabs the solid line instead of the imaginary one. The cure is mechanical: the moment the word depression appears, draw a small level line through the observer's eye before you mark anything else. The angle of depression hangs below that level line, always — depression means pressed down from level, and the word itself carries the definition. Then use the parallel-line fact to relocate it: the level line at the top and the ground at the bottom are parallel, the line of sight cuts both, so the angle below the level line up top equals the angle of elevation down at the boat or the car. Mark that equal angle inside the ground triangle and work from there, where your known sides live. Drawn level line, relocated angle — two moves, and the error has nowhere to happen.

**Thandi:** A question that comes up every year: the answer looks reasonable, the working looks right, but the memo disagrees — and the cause turns out to be the calculator. What is going on there?

**Tutor:** Mode. The calculator can measure angles in degrees or in radians, and it will happily compute tan of 32 in whichever mode it woke up in. In degree mode, tan 32 is 0,6249 and the flagpole is 25 metres. In radian mode, tan of 32 is a completely different number, and the flagpole comes out as nonsense — sometimes negative, sometimes enormous. The working after that point can be flawless and the answer is still wrong, which is what makes this error so cruel: it hides inside a correct method. So build the habit: before the first trig key of any paper, glance at the mode indicator and confirm the D for degrees. And back it up with the sense check — for angles between 0 and 90 degrees, sine and cosine must land between 0 and 1, and tan of anything below 45 degrees must be less than 1. If tan 32 shows you 0,66 or minus 0,04 instead of roughly 0,62, the mode is lying to you. Fix it before the damage compounds.

**Thandi:** Last one — in the two-triangle tower problem, why must d be the distance from the NEARER point? Half of us attached it to the further one and drowned in algebra.

**Tutor:** You can attach d to either point and the mathematics will still be true — but one choice keeps the algebra clean and the other invites a sign error. Set d as the nearer distance and the further point sits at d plus 20: both expressions for the height are built from addition, the equation gathers into d times the difference of the two tangents on one side, and the difference is positive because the nearer angle is steeper. Attach d to the further point instead and the nearer one becomes d minus 20 — now a subtraction travels through every line, and one dropped minus sign wrecks the answer. So the discipline is: let d belong to the nearer point, write the height twice, equate, factor out d, divide by the bracket. And spend the last moment verifying: push your d through the triangle you did not use to find the height, and confirm both triangles report the same number. Two triangles agreeing is the strongest proof a two-triangle problem can offer.
