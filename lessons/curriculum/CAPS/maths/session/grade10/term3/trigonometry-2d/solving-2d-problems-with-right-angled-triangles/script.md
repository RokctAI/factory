# Part 1 — Expert

Today's work turns the three trigonometric ratios you already own into a problem-solving machine. Heights of flagpoles, distances to boats, angles of ladders — every one of them is a right-angled triangle wearing a story. One flagpole runs through the whole session, a second triangle joins it near the end, and by the close you will convert any two-dimensional description into a labelled sketch and an exact answer.

## Subtopic: Angles of Elevation and Depression

Two definitions carry this entire topic, and both start from the same place: the horizontal.

The angle of elevation is the angle between the horizontal line through your eye and your line of sight when you look UP at something — the top of a flagpole, an aeroplane, a kite. Eye level first, then tilt upward; the tilt IS the angle of elevation.

The angle of depression is the angle between the horizontal line through your eye and your line of sight when you look DOWN — from a cliff top to a boat, from a balcony to a car. Horizontal first, then tilt downward; the tilt is the angle of depression. The commonest error in the whole topic is measuring depression from the vertical wall of the cliff instead of from the horizontal. Depression hangs BELOW the horizontal, always.

Now the fact that unlocks half the examination questions: the angle of depression from the top, down to an object, EQUALS the angle of elevation from that object, up to the top. Why? The two horizontals — one through the observer's eye, one along the ground — are parallel lines, and the line of sight cuts across both. Alternate angles between parallel lines are equal. So when a question gives you an angle of depression, you may immediately mark the equal angle of elevation inside the triangle at ground level, where the sides you know usually live. Write the reason — alternate angles, parallel lines — because the geometry mark is real.

Pause here — questions on this section are coming to you now. They test the two definitions and the alternate-angle link: answer from the horizontal, every time.

## Subtopic: The Three-Step Method — Sketch, Label, Choose the Ratio

The case study begins. A learner stands 40 metres from the base of a flagpole and measures the angle of elevation to the top as 32 degrees. Find the height of the flagpole.

Step one: sketch. Draw the vertical flagpole, the horizontal ground, and the line of sight from the learner to the top. The right angle sits where the pole meets the ground — vertical against horizontal is always 90 degrees. Mark 32 degrees at the learner's position, between the ground and the line of sight.

Step two: label the sides FROM THE ANGLE'S POINT OF VIEW. The side opposite the 32 degrees is the pole — the height we want; call it h. The side adjacent, running along the ground from the learner to the base, is 40 metres. The hypotenuse is the line of sight, and this question never mentions it.

Step three: choose the ratio that connects what you HAVE to what you WANT. We have the adjacent, we want the opposite — that is tangent territory: tan of the angle equals opposite over adjacent. So tan 32 degrees equals h over 40.

Solve it: h equals 40 times tan 32 degrees. Calculator in DEGREE mode — check the little D on the screen before anything else. The value of tan 32 degrees is 0,6249 to four decimals, and 40 times that is 24,99 and a bit. The height is 25,0 metres, rounded to one decimal place. State the unit; a bare 25 is an incomplete answer.

The discipline to memorise: sketch, label from the angle, choose the ratio by have-and-want. Three steps, every single problem.

Stop for this section's questions now — they walk the same three steps on the same flagpole. Execute exactly as demonstrated.

## Subtopic: Finding an Angle and Finding a Distance

Same machine, two new gears: sometimes the angle is the unknown, and sometimes a distance along the ground is.

First, the unknown angle. A 5-metre ladder leans against a wall with its foot 2 metres from the base of the wall. What angle does the ladder make with the ground? From the angle at the foot: the ladder itself is the hypotenuse, 5; the distance along the ground is the adjacent, 2. Adjacent and hypotenuse — cosine. Cos of theta equals 2 over 5, which is 0,4. Now the reverse move: theta equals the inverse cosine of 0,4. On the calculator, shift then cos, then 0,4 — and the answer is 66,42 degrees, so 66,4 degrees to one decimal. The inverse function is how you climb from a ratio back to an angle; you cannot divide your way there.

Second, the unknown distance, with a depression thrown in. From the top of a vertical cliff 30 metres high, the angle of depression to a boat is 25 degrees. How far is the boat from the base of the cliff? Use the alternate-angle fact immediately: the angle of elevation from the boat to the cliff top is also 25 degrees. In the triangle at the boat's corner: opposite is the cliff, 30; adjacent is the distance we want, d. Tangent again: tan 25 degrees equals 30 over d. Rearrange — d equals 30 over tan 25 degrees. The value of tan 25 degrees is 0,4663; thirty divided by that is 64,33 and a shade more. The boat is 64,3 metres from the base.

Notice the shape of that rearrangement: when the unknown sits in the BOTTOM of the ratio, you divide by the tan, you do not multiply. Write the equation first, rearrange second — guessing the operation is where the marks leak.

Pause now for this section's questions. One asks for an angle, one for a distance — let the have-and-want reading choose the ratio, and let algebra do the rearranging.

## Subtopic: Two Triangles, One Height

The distinction-level question: two observations of the same object from two different places.

From point A, the angle of elevation to the top of a tower is 38 degrees. Walking 20 metres straight towards the tower brings you to point B, where the angle of elevation is 50 degrees. Find the height of the tower.

The strategy in one sentence: the two triangles SHARE the same height, so write the height twice — once from each triangle — and set the expressions against each other.

Let d be the distance from B, the nearer point, to the base. From B: tan 50 degrees equals h over d, so h equals d times tan 50 degrees. From A, which stands d plus 20 from the base: tan 38 degrees equals h over d plus 20, so h equals the quantity d plus 20, times tan 38 degrees.

One height, two expressions — equate them: d times tan 50 degrees equals the quantity d plus 20, times tan 38 degrees. Expand the right side, gather the d terms on the left: d times tan 50 minus d times tan 38 equals 20 times tan 38. Factor out d: d times the quantity tan 50 minus tan 38, equals 20 times tan 38. Divide: d equals 20 tan 38 over the quantity tan 50 minus tan 38.

Numbers, full decimals held to the end: tan 50 is 1,1918; tan 38 is 0,7813. The numerator: 20 times 0,7813 is 15,626. The denominator: 1,1918 minus 0,7813 is 0,4105. Divide: d is 38,07 metres. Then the height: h equals d times tan 50, which is 38,07 times 1,1918 — 45,4 metres to one decimal.

Verify through the other triangle: 38,07 plus 20 is 58,07; times tan 38, which is 0,7813, gives 45,37 — the same 45,4. When both triangles report the same height, the answer is safe.

The compressed method: assign d to the NEAREST point, write h from each triangle, equate, factor, divide, and verify through the triangle you did not use. The final questions of this part are in front of you now — same structure, and the shared height is always the bridge.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same problems again now, built slowly from the ground up — from where your eyes actually point. Nothing new arrives in this half: the flagpole will still be 25 metres, the boat still 64,3 metres out. What changes is that you will feel WHY each step happens.

## Subtopic: Looking Up, Looking Down — Everything Starts Level

Stand outside and look straight ahead at the horizon — dead level, no tilt. That imaginary level line through your eyes is the starting line for every angle in this topic.

A kite is up in the sky. To see it, your chin lifts and your eyes rotate UP from that level line. The amount of turn — that is the angle of elevation. Small tilt, kite low; big tilt, kite nearly overhead. Elevation is just "how far up from level".

Now ride a lift to the top floor of a building and look down at a taxi in the street. Your eyes start level and rotate DOWN. That amount of turn is the angle of depression — "how far down from level". Not from the wall of the building, not from your feet: from LEVEL.

And there is a beautiful secret connecting the two. Your level line at the top of the building and the flat street below are parallel — two level lines never meet. Your line of sight to the taxi is one straight line cutting across both. Geometry says the angle it makes below your level line equals the angle it makes with the street at the taxi. In everyday terms: the angle you look DOWN at the taxi driver equals the angle the taxi driver looks UP at you. Perfectly symmetrical, like two people making eye contact along the same straight line. That one fact lets you move any depression angle down into the ground triangle, where the measurements live.

Quick check before we carry on — a few questions on the two angles are coming to you right now. Ask yourself one thing each time: up from level, or down from level?

## Subtopic: One Triangle at a Time

The flagpole again, in slow motion. Forty metres from the pole, looking up at 32 degrees.

Draw the picture in your mind — three lines only. Line one: the pole, standing straight up. Line two: the ground, flat, from your feet to the base. Line three: your line of sight, the slanted line from your eyes to the top. A triangle, and the corner at the base of the pole is a perfect square corner, because upright against flat is always 90 degrees.

Now stand INSIDE the 32-degree angle and look around the triangle. The side straight across from you, the one you would hit if you threw a ball through the triangle — that is the OPPOSITE. The pole. The side you are touching along the ground — the ADJACENT, 40 metres. The slanted one is the HYPOTENUSE, always across from the square corner, always the longest.

Which ratio? Say the memory phrase slowly — SOH CAH TOA. Sine is opposite over hypotenuse. Cosine is adjacent over hypotenuse. Tangent is opposite over adjacent. We know the adjacent, we want the opposite — the pair "opposite and adjacent" appears only in TOA. Tangent it is. So tan 32 equals the pole over 40, and the pole equals 40 times tan 32 — about 25 metres. A five-storey building, roughly, which sounds right for a tall flagpole seen at a gentle tilt — always ask whether the size sounds like the picture.

The kite gives us one more ratio for balance. Fifty metres of taut string, elevation 40 degrees, how high is the kite? The string is the slanted line — the hypotenuse, 50. The height is opposite the angle. Opposite and hypotenuse — that pair lives in SOH: sine. Height equals 50 times sin 40, which is 50 times 0,6428 — about 32,1 metres.

Your questions for this section are up now. For each one, do only this: find the square corner, name the three sides from the angle, and let the two sides in the story pick the ratio for you.

## Subtopic: Reading the Story Into a Sketch

Last skill, and it is the one that actually decides marks: turning words into the right picture. The examiner writes a paragraph; you answer with a triangle.

The reading habits, one by one. Habit one: something vertical, something horizontal. A pole, cliff, building or tower is your vertical line; the ground or the sea is your horizontal; the right angle lives where they meet. You never hunt for the right angle — the story builds it for you.

Habit two: the angle sits with the OBSERVER. "From a point 40 metres away" — the angle goes at that point, not at the top of the pole. "From the top of the cliff, the angle of depression" — the angle goes at the top, hanging below a little level line you draw through the observer's eye. Sketch that level line every time depression appears; it is the anchor the angle hangs from.

Habit three: translate the words into sides before touching the calculator. "How far from the base" — that is the horizontal side. "How high" — the vertical side. "The length of the ladder", "the string", "the line of sight" — the hypotenuse. Once each phrase has a side, the have-and-want pair chooses sine, cosine or tangent on its own.

And habit four: after the answer, read the picture back. A flagpole of 25 metres — sensible. A flagpole of 2 500 metres — the rate went in wrong or the mode was wrong; no flag flies higher than Table Mountain. A boat 64 metres out from a 30-metre cliff at a 25-degree tilt — gentle angle, boat further out than the cliff is tall; the picture agrees.

You now own the whole machine: level lines, the three-step method, the alternate-angle bridge, and the two-triangle trick waiting at the top of the topic. And here come the final questions of the lesson, right now — sketch first, label from the angle, and let the triangle do the heavy lifting. You have earned this.
