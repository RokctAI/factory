# Part 1 — Expert

Today's work is the pair of formulae that price every investment, every hire-purchase agreement and every year of inflation you will ever meet: simple growth and compound growth. One case study runs through the whole session — R5 000 invested at 8 percent per annum for 3 years — and by the end you will compute both answers, explain the gap between them, and know exactly which formula every exam scenario demands.

## Subtopic: The Cast of Symbols and the Two Kinds of Growth

Formula first, application second — that is the order that earns marks. Write both formulae down now. Simple growth: A equals P, times the quantity one plus i n. Compound growth: A equals P, times the quantity one plus i, raised to the power n.

Now the cast, precisely defined, because every substitution mark depends on it. P is the principal — the original amount, the starting value. A is the accumulated amount — the final value after growth. The letter n counts the periods — for us, years. And i is the interest rate per period written as a DECIMAL. That last phrase is non-negotiable: 8 percent enters the formula as 0,08 — eight hundredths — never as 8. Substituting 8 where 0,08 belongs inflates every answer by a factor of one hundred, and the error carries through the entire question. Convert the rate the moment you read it: divide the percentage by 100, write the decimal beside it, and only then proceed.

The two formulae encode two different agreements about where the interest lives. Simple growth pays interest on the ORIGINAL amount only, every period, forever: the interest is calculated once on P and merely multiplied by the number of years — that is why i n sits together inside the bracket. Compound growth pays interest on the ORIGINAL amount PLUS all interest already earned: each year's growth joins the principal, and next year's interest is calculated on the larger total — that is why the bracket is raised to the power n, applied again and again, once per year.

State the distinction in one sentence, because examiners award it: simple interest is calculated on the principal only; compound interest is calculated on the principal plus accumulated interest — interest on interest.

## Subtopic: Simple Growth — Executing A = P(1 + in)

The case study, simple version. R5 000 invested at 8 percent per annum simple interest for 3 years. Find A.

The exam layout, line by line — each line carries credit. Line one, the formula, quoted exactly: A equals P times one plus i n. Line two, the data, converted: P is 5 000; i is 8 over 100, which is 0,08; n is 3. Line three, the substitution, every value visible: A equals 5 000 times the quantity one plus 0,08 times 3.

Now compute with discipline — the bracket first, and inside the bracket, multiplication before addition. The product 0,08 times 3 is 0,24. Add the one: 1,24. So A equals 5 000 times 1,24.

Pause on that number 1,24, because reading it correctly is a mark of mastery: the investment has become 124 percent of itself — the original 100 percent, plus 8 percent claimed three times. The bracket IS the growth factor.

Multiply: 5 000 times 1,24 is 6 200. The accumulated amount is R6 200 — and the units are part of the answer; a naked 6 200 forfeits the final mark on most memoranda.

Decompose the answer to verify it: the interest earned is 6 200 minus 5 000 — R1 200 over three years, which is R400 per year, which is exactly 8 percent of R5 000. Every year paid the same R400, calculated on the original principal, untouched by the growing balance. That constant annual payment is the fingerprint of simple growth — linear growth, the same increment every period. When your simple-interest answer does not decompose into equal annual payments on P, the substitution was wrong. Verify this way every time; it costs twenty seconds.

## Subtopic: Compound Growth — Executing A = P(1 + i)ⁿ

Same data, compound version: R5 000 at 8 percent per annum compounded annually, for 3 years.

Layout again. Formula: A equals P times the quantity one plus i, to the power n. Data: P is 5 000; i is 0,08; n is 3. Substitution: A equals 5 000 times 1,08 to the power 3.

Note where the n went: it is an EXPONENT now, not a multiplier. The commonest structural error in this topic is writing 5 000 times one plus 0,08 times 3 under a compound heading — that is the simple formula wearing a compound label, and it surrenders every mark after line one. The power notation is the whole point: 1,08 cubed means 1,08 times 1,08 times 1,08 — the growth factor applied three separate times, each application growing an already-grown amount.

Compute the factor: 1,08 squared is 1,1664; times 1,08 again is 1,259712. Then A equals 5 000 times 1,259712, which is 6 298,56. The accumulated amount is R6 298,56. On the calculator, use the power key directly — 1,08, power key, 3 — and keep every decimal until the final line; rounding the factor early to 1,26 produces R6 300 and a lost accuracy mark. Round money to two decimals, at the end, only.

Now the comparison the examiner is really testing. Simple: R6 200. Compound: R6 298,56. The gap is R98,56 — and you must be able to say precisely where it comes from. Year one is identical in both schemes: 8 percent of 5 000, R400. But in year two, compound pays 8 percent of 5 400 — R432 — while simple still pays R400: the extra R32 is interest earned BY year one's interest. Year three compounds again: 8 percent of 5 832 is R466,56, another R66,56 ahead of simple's R400. Thirty-two plus 66,56 is the R98,56. Interest on interest — quantified, not just named. An answer that can point to the R32 and the R66,56 is a distinction answer.

The questions on this section will hand you the same comparison with different numbers — walk the same road: both formulae, both answers, and the gap explained by where the interest was calculated.

## Subtopic: Choosing the Formula and Working Backwards

The scenario decides the formula, and the syllabus fixes the conventions. Hire purchase — furniture, appliances, anything bought "on terms" — is calculated at SIMPLE interest: that is the CAPS convention, stated in the topic itself. Inflation, investments and savings accounts compounding annually, and population growth all use COMPOUND growth — quantities that grow on their grown selves. Read the question's language: "simple interest" or "hire purchase" sends you to A equals P times one plus i n; "compound", "compounded annually", "inflation" or "population" sends you to the power formula.

Two applications, executed. Hire purchase: a television priced at R8 000, bought over 2 years at 12 percent per annum simple interest. A equals 8 000 times one plus 0,12 times 2 — the bracket is 1,24 — A equals R9 920. The monthly instalment divides that total by 24 months: R413,33. Inflation: a loaf of bread costs R20 today; at 6 percent annual inflation, in 5 years it costs 20 times 1,06 to the power 5. The factor 1,06 to the fifth is 1,338226 to six decimals; the loaf costs R26,76. Same machinery, different costumes.

Now the reverse gear, because examiners run this formula backwards. What principal must be invested today at 6 percent compound interest to reach R10 000 in 4 years? The unknown is P. Write the formula, substitute what is known: 10 000 equals P times 1,06 to the power 4. The factor: 1,06 to the fourth is 1,262477. Isolate P by dividing both sides: P equals 10 000 over 1,262477, which is R7 920,94. Verify by running forwards: 7 920,94 times 1,262477 returns 10 000, to the cent. A reverse answer verified forwards is unassailable.

The method, compressed for the examination. One: classify the scenario — simple or compound — and quote the matching formula. Two: list P, i as a decimal, n. Three: substitute visibly. Four: bracket first, exponent before multiplication, full decimals to the end. Five: answer in rands, two decimals, units stated. Six: verify — decompose simple answers into equal annual payments; check compound answers year by year or run reverse answers forwards. The distinction-level takeaway: the bracket is a growth factor, and compound growth applies that factor to its own output — once you read the formulae as factors rather than recipes, every variation in Grade 11 and 12 finance, from depreciation to timelines, is the same two moves.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same two formulae now, built from the ground up — slowly, with the money visible at every step. Nothing new is coming: the answers will land exactly where the first part landed. What changes is that this time, you will see WHY.

## Subtopic: Two Pictures — the Wage and the Taxi

Forget formulas completely for a few minutes. Two pictures first.

Picture one. You have a weekend job that pays R400 every Saturday. Fixed deal. Work one Saturday, R400. Work three Saturdays, three times R400 — R1 200. The pay never changes, because it is calculated on the same thing every time: one Saturday of your work. Next month, same R400. That is the first way money grows: the same fixed amount added again and again. Steady. Predictable. You could work out ten years of it on your fingers.

Picture two. A woman in your neighbourhood owns one taxi. The taxi earns money. But she does something clever: instead of spending what the taxi earns, she SAVES it — and after a while, the earnings buy a SECOND taxi. Now both taxis earn. And their combined earnings, saved again, buy a third taxi sooner than the second one arrived. Her money is not growing by the same amount each year — it is growing FASTER each year, because the growth itself starts earning. The taxis are buying taxis.

Hold both pictures. The weekend wage is simple growth: the extra money never joins the team; every payment is calculated on the original deal. The taxi fleet is compound growth: every bit of growth joins the team and starts working. Same start, very different futures — and everything in this lesson is just these two pictures with numbers attached.

## Subtopic: The Same R5 000, Built Year by Year

Now the case study from the first part — R5 000 at 8 percent for 3 years — but no formulas yet. Just honest arithmetic, one year at a time.

The wage way first — simple interest. Eight percent of R5 000 is R400. That R400 is this deal's "weekend wage": it gets paid every year, calculated on the original R5 000 every time. Year one: R400. Year two: R400 again — the deal does not care that your balance grew. Year three: R400 again. Total interest: three R400s, R1 200. Final amount: 5 000 plus 1 200 — R6 200. Write those three equal R400s down and look at them: THAT is what simple means.

Now the taxi way — compound interest. Year one: 8 percent of 5 000 is R400, same as before. But here is the move: the R400 does not stand outside — it JOINS the account. You now have R5 400 working for you. Year two: 8 percent of 5 400 — not of 5 000 — which is R432. See the extra R32? That is interest earned by last year's R400. The interest got a job. Balance: R5 832. Year three: 8 percent of 5 832 is R466,56. Balance: 5 832 plus 466,56 — R6 298,56.

Put the two finals side by side: R6 200 the wage way, R6 298,56 the taxi way. The R98,56 difference is not magic — you just watched it arrive: R32 extra in year two, R66,56 extra in year three, both earned by interest that had joined the team.

Only NOW, the formulas — because they are just this arithmetic in a hurry. The wage way, three equal payments of i times P, is A equals P times one plus i n. The taxi way, multiplying by 1,08 three times over, is A equals P times one plus i to the power n. If you ever blank on which formula is which in a test, you can rebuild either one, year by year, exactly as we just did — slower, but bulletproof. The formula is a shortcut for arithmetic you can already do. That is worth celebrating: you just did compound interest BY HAND.

## Subtopic: Which Deal Is in Front of You?

Last skill: recognising which picture a question is showing you — because the question will never say "use the taxi way".

Listen for the deal's shape. Hire purchase — the fridge, the TV, the sofa "on terms" — is the WAGE deal: simple interest, by the syllabus's own rule. The shop works out the interest on the original price and spreads the total over the months. A savings account "compounded annually" is the TAXI deal — the bank literally adds your interest to your balance, and next year pays interest on the new total. Inflation is the taxi deal too: this year's prices rise on top of LAST year's already-risen prices — that is why your grandmother's bus fare sounds impossible. Population growth: babies grow up and have babies — the growth itself grows. Taxi deal.

Try the reflex on three quick ones. "A loan at 12 percent per annum simple interest" — the word is right there: wage deal, bracket formula. "An investment growing at 6 percent compounded yearly" — taxi deal, power formula. "The price of bread rises by 6 percent each year" — each year's rise stands on the last one: taxi deal.

And keep the one honest warning: the taxi deal is wonderful when the money is YOURS — savings, investments — and heavy when the money is OWED, because debts can compound too. Same mathematics, both directions.

You now own both pictures, both formulas, and — most importantly — the year-by-year arithmetic underneath them that no test can take away from you. Small win banked. The practice questions are yours.
